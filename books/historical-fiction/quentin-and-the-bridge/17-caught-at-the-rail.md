## 17. Caught at the Rail

The fireworks began at nine, and by half past the sky over the river was a lie of light.

Noor kept her eyes forward. Behind the grandstand the crowd roared at each burst — gold willows, red chrysanthemums, a finale that shook the planking underfoot — and every detonation covered another step of their flight along the maintenance catwalk beneath the span. Quentin moved ahead of her, one hand trailing the iron rail, his shoulders hunched in the borrowed foreman's coat that hung too wide across his back. He had not spoken since they left the boathouse ruins. Neither had she. Words were weight, and tonight every ounce mattered.

The atlas's route had held true. Elias's marginalia — *the third pier carries a stair no surveyor drew* — had delivered them past the workmen's abandoned tool sheds, down a ladder rusted to lace, along a service ledge no wider than a coffin, to this: the unfinished northern approach, where the bridge's bones rose out of the fog like the ribs of some drowned leviathan. Above them, ten thousand citizens cheered the first tested span. Below them, the river ran black and swollen with storm water, dragging branches and a broken rowboat toward the sea.

They had perhaps four hundred yards left. Four hundred yards of catwalk, then the far abutment, then the border post that tonight stood unstaffed because every soldier on both banks had been ordered to watch the celebration rather than the water. Elias had known. Five years dead, and he had still known.

"Almost there," Quentin breathed. Not turning around. His voice was strange to her after five years of silence — rougher, deeper, worn smooth only at the edges where he'd said her name.

Noor counted steps. She had been counting all night — steps, lanterns, seconds between fireworks. The gap between bursts was eleven seconds now; she could feel the rhythm failing, the show winding toward its finale, and when the finale came there would be nothing left in the world loud enough to hide them.

At three hundred yards, the fog thickened.

It came off the river in slow banks, fed by the storm's aftermath, swallowing the catwalk ahead until Quentin's coat was only a gray suggestion. Noor reached for him without deciding to, found his sleeve, held it. He did not pull away. For three steps they moved as one creature through the white, and she thought — with a clarity that felt like grief — *this is what it would have been like, if the ferry had never stopped running. If the borders had never closed. If we had simply grown older together instead of apart.*

Then the fireworks stopped.

Not the finale — something smaller. A misfire, perhaps, a delay in the fuse train, but the effect was a silence so sudden and complete that Noor heard her own heartbeat, heard the river chewing at the pilings below, heard — unmistakably — a boot on iron behind them.

Quentin heard it too. She felt him stiffen under her hand.

"Don't run," said Inspector Halvard.

He stepped out of the fog as if the fog had made him: gray coat, gray hat, the lamplight of the distant grandstand finding only the edge of his face. But Noor knew his hands, and she saw that the revolver in them was already drawn, already steady, its muzzle angled at the space between them — at neither, at both.

"I watched the crowd," Halvard said. "All evening. Ten thousand people cheering a bridge, and I thought — how many of them are watching anything else? Then I remembered you, Miss Nystrom." He tilted his head slightly toward her. "You always did watch the water."

"Halvard." Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"The letter gave you thirty days," he said. "You've used twenty-nine. Did you think I wouldn't be here tonight? The one night the bridge is open and unguarded? The one night a crossing would even be possible?" He took a step closer, boots ringing softly on the planks. "I've read your master's correspondence, Miss Nystrom. All of it. Except one page."

Behind her, Quentin shifted — she felt the movement through her grip on his sleeve, felt him gathering himself, and she squeezed hard enough to hurt. *Don't.* If he ran, Halvard would shoot. If he surrendered, he would hang. There was only one thing left on the bridge tonight, and it was the thing she had carried up from the workshop in the oilcloth satchel pressed flat against her ribs.

"You stole it," Halvard said. "From my evidence files, or from your master's effects before they reached me — it hardly matters which. A page of Elias Nystrom's letters, dated the week before he died. I've spent five years wondering about that page. Tonight I'm done wondering." The revolver did not waver. "Give me the boy, and give me the page, and perhaps the inquiry finds a way to be gentle."

The fog moved between them. Somewhere above, a workman shouted — testing the new span's joints, hammering, indifferent — and the sound fell away into the dark.

"Noor." Quentin's voice, low, at her shoulder. "Give him me. It was always going to end this way—"

"It ends the way I choose," she said, and she heard her own voice break open, all the safe silences of five years splitting along a seam she hadn't known was there. She turned to face Halvard fully, and she opened the satchel, and she took out the page.

She had expected fear. What came instead was calm — the same calm that came over her at the drafting table, when the coastline finally agreed with itself, when ink met paper and told the truth. She unfolded the page in the fog-damp air and held it up between two fingers, the way she might hold a finished chart.

"Do you know why my master died, Inspector?" she asked.

Halvard's eyes flicked to the page. Something moved in them — hunger, she thought, or its close cousin, dread.

"He died because he measured things," Noor went on. "That was his whole life. Measuring. And in the autumn before the declaration, he measured the third pier. Do you know what he found?"

Silence. Even the river seemed to listen.

"The caisson sits on gravel shoal," she said. "Not bedrock. The surveys were falsified — both governments knew, both governments signed, because stopping construction meant admitting the error, and admitting the error meant scandal, and scandal meant careers. So they buried it. And Elias wrote it down, with measurements and diagrams, because measuring things was his whole life, and he sent that page to—" she paused, let the name land like a stone, "—your ministry, Inspector. Your own ministry. Which buried it too. Which is why the page went missing from your files, and why no one ever asked where."

Halvard had gone very still. Stillness was his native language; she had learned it across two interviews in her workshop, the courteous predator's patience of a man who believed time always worked for him. But this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a man hearing a floor creak beneath him.

"That's a serious accusation," he said quietly.

"It's a measurement," Noor said. "Accusations can be argued with. Measurements cannot. There are copies, Halvard. In three places you'll never find them all, and each one names the engineers, the ministries, the dates. My master didn't die of fever. He died of what he knew, and everyone who touched what he knew has spent five years making sure it stayed dead." She took a breath. The fog was cold in her lungs. "If you take him — if you take us — the copies go to the newspapers within the week. Every one of your superiors who signed the false survey goes down. Including the ones who gave you your commission, Inspector. Including the ones who told you to find a conspirator and stop asking questions."

The revolver lowered an inch. Only an inch.

"You're blackmailing me," Halvard said. There was no outrage in it. Almost wonder.

"Yes."

"With the truth."

"Yes." Her hands were steady. She marveled at them. "I learned from the best liars in two governments, Inspector. They taught me that truth is only dangerous if someone is willing to carry it. My master was willing. Now I am."

For a long moment the only sounds were the river and the distant, resumed thunder of fireworks — the finale beginning at last, great blooms of gold opening above the fog, lighting Halvard's face in stuttering flashes. And in those flashes Noor saw everything pass across him: the officer who had once brought her books because he knew she loved maps; the investigator who had sat in her workshop asking soft questions with hard eyes; the man who had loved her, maybe, in the way that careful men love — silently, and from a distance, and never at the cost of duty. Until now. Until duty and love arrived at the same rail on the same night and demanded opposite things.

"You could have shown me this weeks ago," he said at last. "In the workshop. You could have ended the investigation with a word."

"If I'd shown you then, you'd have had to bury it again. That's what you're paid for." She softened her voice, and it cost her more than the threat had. "But you read Elias's letters, Halvard. All of them. You told me so. You knew him — the way he thought, the way he refused to round a number to make it comfortable. When you read that missing page existed, some part of you already knew what it said. Some part of you has known since the day the surveys were signed off, and it has been waiting five years for someone to say it aloud."

"Stop," Halvard said. Very quietly.

"Because that part of you became an inspector instead of a mapmaker," she went on, "and it has spent its whole career looking for the flaw in the official drawing. Haven't you? Isn't that why you're good at what you do?"

The finale crashed overhead — white light, then red, then a silence between shells long enough for a heart to break in. In that silence, Halvard looked at the page in her fingers for a long, long moment. Then at Quentin, standing gray-faced in the fog with the foreman's coat hanging off him like a shroud. Then at Noor.

And he lowered the gun.

All the way down, until the muzzle pointed at the planks between his boots, and his arm hung at his side as if the weight of it had suddenly become more than a careful man could carry.

"Walk," he said. "Now. Before the finale ends and the posts come back on duty." He did not look at Quentin again. He looked at Noor, and his voice was low enough that the river nearly took it. "The copies. If this is a bluff, Miss Nystrom, I will spend the rest of my life proving it, and I am very good at my work."

"So am I," she said. "Ask anyone who's tried to read me."

Something crossed his face that was not quite a smile — the ghost of one, the kind men wear at funerals for people they admired. "Your master would have been proud of you," he said. "God help me, I hate that he would have."

He stepped aside. One step, two, until his back was to the rail and the way north lay open — four hundred feet of fog-wrapped catwalk leading to the far bank, to the other nation, to the rest of Quentin Vasse's life.

Quentin hesitated. She saw him weigh it — the old vigilance, the counting of days, the instinct of a hunted thing that does not believe in open doors. Then he looked at Halvard, and Halvard looked back at the river, granting him the courtesy of not being seen, and Quentin understood that this was the only mercy the man had left to give: to make it impersonal.

"Thank you," Quentin said anyway.

"Don't thank me," Halvard said. "Thank her. And don't come back. If you cross this bridge again, I'll arrest you, and I won't hesitate, and we'll both pretend tonight never happened. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

Quentin turned to Noor. For a moment — one shell-burst, one held breath — the fog thinned and she saw him whole: twenty-one years old, five years of exile carved into his face, the boy from the ferry gone and a man standing in his place who had crossed a river for her and would have surrendered himself for her and was now leaving her, because leaving her was the only way either of them survived.

"Noor—"

"Go." She pushed the page into his coat pocket, closed his fingers around it. "Take it across. It belongs to the living, and so do you. Go."

He kissed her forehead, brief and burning, the way he had at fourteen on the last ferry deck, and then he was moving — into the fog, along the catwalk, his footsteps fading into the rhythm of the river until she could not tell them from the water anymore.

Noor did not follow.

She stood at the rail beside Halvard while the last shells climbed and burst, gilding the fog from above, and together they listened to the footsteps go, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the bridge itself had not already said.

When the finale ended and the darkness came back down like a curtain, Halvard holstered the revolver and straightened his coat, and when he turned to her his face was the inspector's face again, composed and gray, though the eyes were not quite the same.

"The inquiry convenes Thursday," he said. "You'll testify."

"I know."

"Not against the boy. About the pier." He paused at the head of the catwalk, half-turned into the fog. "If you testify about the flaw, Miss Nystrom — if you stand in front of both governments and read my master's numbers aloud — the conspiracy charges dissolve. No one hunting a traitor wants the world reading what the traitor knows." His jaw tightened. "That was your plan from the beginning. The letter, the thirty days, tonight. All of it built toward a courtroom."

"Elias taught me that a map is only worth what it costs," Noor said. "I'm prepared to pay."

Halvard studied her a moment longer — the appraiser's look, the last audit of a debt he would never name. Then he nodded once, a small precise motion, and walked away into the fog toward the grandstand and the cheering crowds and the career he had just chosen to endanger, his footsteps fading the way Quentin's had, until Noor stood alone at the rail of the half-built bridge with nothing in her hands but the memory of a page, and the whole dark river running beneath her toward morning.

Thursday, she thought. Two days.

She turned south, toward home, and began to walk.
