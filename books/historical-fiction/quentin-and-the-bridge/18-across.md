## 18. Across

The fog took the bridge away behind him one plank at a time.

Quentin ran with his hands out in front of him like a blind man, because that was what he was now — a man who had traded every sense but touch for speed, who had surrendered sight and sound to the white wall of river mist so that only his feet could be trusted to tell him where he was. The planks were cold and wet beneath his boots, slick from the storm two nights past, and each one struck up through his knees with a knock he felt rather than heard. Somewhere below him the river moved in the dark, swollen with rain, dragging its debris toward the sea with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

He did not look back.

That was the discipline. He had made himself learn it on the scaffolding, three years of walking iron above open air: you did not look down, you did not look back, you looked only at the next handhold, the next plank, the next breath. Looking was how men fell. Looking was how men froze. He put Noor's last words into the place where fear tried to live — *run, don't wait, don't you dare wait* — and he let them carry him the way current carries a leaf, fast and without argument.

Behind him — not too far behind him, he could not afford to think about how far — fireworks bloomed over the celebration city, and their light came through the fog as soft pulses, orange then green then white, like a heartbeat seen through closed eyelids. The crowd noise reached him as a single sustained roar, wordless, enormous, the sound of ten thousand people cheering a span of steel they believed was safe. *They don't know,* he thought, and his legs pumped harder. *They're standing on Elias Nystrom's grave and applauding.*

A whistle cut the fog behind him. One note, high, urgent — then another voice shouting, words shredded by distance and mist until only the tone survived, and the tone was command. Quentin's stomach clenched around nothing. He counted in his head the way Noor had taught him to count lantern flashes, one-two-three-four, because counting kept the panic in a box with holes punched in it for air.

*Halvard.* It had to be Halvard. The inspector had been at the rail; Noor had said so with her eyes when they'd paused under the third pier's shadow, said it the way she said everything now, in glances and pressures of the hand, a whole language built in five years apart and thirty days of terror. She had stepped forward to meet him. She had told Quentin to go, and there had been something in her face —

He shut the thought. Ran.

* * *

The far bank came up at him out of the fog all at once, mud and stone and the black shapes of mooring posts, and he went down the last ladder with his palms burning on the frost-slick rungs and landed hard enough to drive the breath out of him in a white cloud. For a moment he simply crouched there, hands flat on the ground, feeling the earth — actual earth, his country's earth, the border soil he had been forbidden for seven years — and let himself shake. Just for a moment. He gave himself ten heartbeats, counted them honestly, and then stood.

The boathouse ruins were where Noor's atlas had promised they would be. Of course they were. Her master had charted everything, even the rotting bones of a ferry landing that official maps had erased the day the borders closed, as though forgetting a thing could unmake it. Quentin slipped between the collapsed ribs of the old roof and found the rowboat exactly where the margin notes said it waited, hull upturned under sacking, oars wrapped in oilcloth against the thwarts. Someone had cached it here long ago. Perhaps Elias himself. The dead mapmaker had been preparing this escape for years, Quentin realized — building an exit into the world the way other men built doors into houses — and the thought filled him with a gratitude so fierce it was almost grief.

He dragged the boat to the water. The river grabbed it eagerly, hungry after the storm, and he had to fight the first hundred meters with everything his arms had left, hauling against a current that wanted to sweep him downstream into the delta marshes where patrols wouldn't bother looking because no boat could live there. But he had worked iron above this river for three years. He knew its moods the way he knew the creak of a cable before it parted. He ferried across its temper, angle by patient angle, and the far shore — the true far shore, the home shore, the shore with sentries who wore his own nation's grey — resolved out of the mist in lamps and low walls.

He grounded the bow on shingle and stepped out, and his boots made a sound on his homeland's stones that nearly undid him.

There was a checkpoint ahead — he could see the brazier glow, two sentries stamping against the cold, a third with a lantern checking papers of some fisherman come in late. Quentin had prepared for this. He had a story laminated out of truth: a bridge worker, discharged early for the celebrations, drunk on free wine, crossing by the works boat because the crowds had swallowed the ferries. His false name sat in his mouth like a coin he'd bitten so many times it had gone smooth. He walked toward the fire with his shoulders loose and his eyes appropriately bleary, and the sentry with the lantern glanced at his work chit, glanced at his face, saw exactly what three years of scaffolding labor was supposed to make a man look like, and waved him through with a bored jerk of the chin.

"Mind the mud," the sentry said. "Whole river's trying to drown us this week."

"Aye," Quentin said, in the accent he had practiced in whispers into his pillow. "It nearly has."

And then he was through. Through. Walking up the lane past shuttered houses with smoke rising straight from their chimneys, past a dog that sniffed his trouser leg and lost interest, past a woman pulling laundry off a line in the dark because she'd forgotten it and feared the rain coming back. Ordinary life. Unremarkable life. The life he had been exiled from at fourteen, wearing it now like a coat that fit everywhere except the shoulders.

He made himself walk until the lane bent inland and the river disappeared behind hedges, before he stopped under an alder tree and let the enormity land on him all at once, the way the storm water had landed, all in one hour.

He was across. He was safe. He was alive.

Quentin Vasse stood under an alder tree in his own country and put both hands over his face and wept — silently, the way you learned to weep working scaffolding, soundlessly, tears running hot into the creases of his palms — and the tree dripped on his neck and the night smelled of wet leaves and woodsmoke and home, and none of it, none of it, felt like triumph.

Because she wasn't beside him.

* * *

He had known, in the part of himself that never stopped calculating loads and tolerances, that she might not come. He had known it at the rail. When Halvard's silhouette had risen out of the crowd-fog like a drawn line, Noor had done the arithmetic faster than either of them — one officer, two fugitives, one narrow window of fireworks and chaos — and she had turned to Quentin with her jaw set in the expression he remembered from the ferry, seven years ago, the last open crossing, when she had been fourteen and certain the world would always make room for promises.

*Run,* she had said. *Don't wait. Don't you dare wait.*

And then she had turned to face the inspector with her hands empty and her chin up, and Quentin had run, because disobeying her was the only obedience left to him.

Now, under the alder, he made himself replay it — the discipline again, the same one that forbade looking down, except this was worse, this was looking backward at a fall already in progress. What would Halvard do? An arrest on the bridge, during the opening ceremony, in front of half the capital's gentry? Or quietly, later, off the span, where the fog could keep secrets? And what leverage did Noor have — unless it was the thing she'd whispered about in the boathouse ruins, the thing Elias had died carrying: the flaw in the third pier, documented, dated, signed, proof that both governments had known their celebrated bridge was a lie dressed in rivets.

She had traded it. Quentin was almost sure of it. That was the shape of what he'd seen at the rail — not surrender, not pleading, but a merchant laying goods on the table. *You want the boy. I have the truth your superiors buried. Choose.*

But merchants needed to walk away from the table afterward, and Noor had stayed on the bridge.

The fog was thinning inland, and through the thinning he could see the far bank's lights — the celebration city, still lit, still roaring faintly across the water, the sound arriving now as if from the bottom of a well. Somewhere in that glitter, on that span, Noor Nystrom was either walking free or being marched, and the distance between those two fates was exactly the width of a decision made by a man who had once loved her and now carried a gun and a file of accusations with her family's name on it.

Quentin pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw stars that weren't the fireworks'.

*I should have stayed.* The thought arrived whole, fully armed, and he killed it the way you kill anything that beautiful and useless. If he had stayed, there would be two prisoners instead of one, or two corpses, and her sacrifice — whatever it had been — would be worth nothing. She had spent herself buying his passage. The only currency he had left was to make the purchase real: to live, to cross legally someday, to build the life she had bought at the price of her own safety. That was the load his shoulders had to bear now. He had spent three years learning to carry iron. This weighed more.

He took out the flower.

He had carried it since he was fourteen — a sprig of river lavender, pressed flat inside the oilcloth cover of his work papers, hidden among chits and wage slips where no searcher would think to look for anything but forgery. She had given it to him on the ferry deck with the wind tearing at both their collars, laughing, saying something foolish about how lavender grew on both banks, same flower, different flags, and hadn't that proved something. He had kept it through the declaration of enmity, through the flight across the border, through three years of a false name and iron dust in his lungs. It was brown now, weightless, more memory than plant. He held it up in the alder shade and the distant fireworks lit it briefly, green then gold, and it looked, for one pulse of light, the way it had looked in her hand.

When she crossed — *when*, he decided, refusing the other word — he would give it back to her. Returned to sender. That was the promise now. The old one, sworn on a ferry deck in peacetime, had been fulfilled; he had found her, and she had found him, and they had gotten one of them out. The new promise was smaller and harder: wait. Wait however long the inquiries and the interrogations and the slow grinding mercy of governments required. Wait at the railing where they'd been caught. Wait until the bridge that divided their nations became, as bridges eventually must, just a way across.

He wrapped the flower back in its oilcloth and put it away, and his hands were steady doing it, and he was grateful to them for that.

* * *

He walked inland through the sleeping town to the house his uncle kept behind the tannery — the address written in Elias's marginalia too, because of course it was; the old mapmaker had charted not just coastlines and piers but people, safe houses, sympathizers, the whole secret geography of loyalty. Quentin knocked the pattern he'd been taught: two, pause, three. The door opened on a chain, then wider, and his uncle — older, greyer, smelling of hides and lamp oil — looked at him for a long moment before pulling him inside by the sleeve without a word.

Inside there was soup, and a fire, and questions asked softly and answered carefully, and a bed in a room that smelled of cedar that Quentin lay in without sleeping while the house ticked and settled around him. Across the ceiling, in the shifting light of the banked stove, he mapped the bridge from memory — he couldn't help it, he had walked it, he was made of it now — every plank, every cable, the third pier's shadow where they had paused and her fingers had tightened on his wrist, the rail where Halvard had risen out of the crowd. He drew the whole span in his mind from bank to bank and then, helplessly, he placed her on it, at the midpoint, alone, and left her there, because that was where he had last seen her and his mind refused to move her until it got news.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stair woke him properly. His uncle came in carrying a folded newspaper and an expression Quentin had never seen on him before — awe and dread sharing the same face.

"You'd best hear this," the old man said, and unfolded the paper with hands that were not quite steady. "The morning edition. They stopped the presses for it."

The headline shouted across the page in letters tall enough to read by stove-light:

**BRIDGE INQUIRY ORDERED — CHIEF ENGINEER'S SECRET LEDGER SURFACES — CROWN ENGINEER HALVARD SUBMITS EVIDENCE AGAINST HIS OWN MINISTRY**

Below it, a smaller line, and Quentin read it three times before the words agreed to mean what they said:

*Testimony attributed to cartographer Miss Noor Nystrom, daughter of the disgraced Nystrom household, whose charges of conspiracy are suspended pending investigation.*

"She's alive," his uncle said quietly. "And she's burned her own name to the waterline to buy yours."

Quentin sat up in the cedar-smelling dark with the newspaper shaking in his hands, and outside the window the sky was turning the grey of the river at dawn, and somewhere on the far side of that river, in a cell or a hearing room or a street, Noor was paying the bill he could never repay. He looked at the date printed under the masthead, and he began, right there, in his head, to count the days until the bridge opened — really opened, all the way, both directions, for everyone.

It was, the paper said, a matter of weeks.

He started counting anyway.
