## 20. The Burning Page

The inquiry ended on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday the city had already begun to forget it.

Noor felt the forgetting happen in real time — in the way the newspaper boy no longer shouted the word *inquiry* from his corner, in the way the clerk at the records office handed back her deposition without ceremony, in the way Marta hummed again over her ledgers downstairs as though the past month had been nothing but weather. The world was eager to close its mouth around the whole affair and swallow. The flaw in the third pier would be repaired; the engineers would argue in journals nobody read; the conscription lists would be quietly amended. Justice, when it came at all, came the way water comes under a door — slowly, invisibly, and only because someone had left a gap.

She walked home from the courthouse with her hands empty for the first time in weeks. The atlas had been surrendered — all of it, every folio, every chart Elias had bled into — and she had signed the receipt herself, watching the clerk's stamp come down like a small execution. She did not regret it. That was the strange part. She had expected to feel flayed, walking out of that room without her master's work, and instead she felt lighter, as though she had set down a stone she'd mistaken for a heart.

But not everything had gone to the court.

There was one page left.

* * *

It lived where it had lived since the night of the span: inside the false spine of the drafting table's lower drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, flat as a held breath. Noor knelt before the drawer now, the workshop dark around her except for the single lamp she allowed herself, and drew it out.

She had not looked at it since Halvard stood across this same room with Elias's correspondence fanned in his gloved hands, one page missing, his eyes moving over her face like a surveyor measuring land he intended to claim. She had stolen that page months ago — before the letter, before Quentin, before any of it, on an instinct she hadn't understood until much later — and what it contained had been the fuse of everything that followed.

Now the inquiry was dissolved. The conspiracy charges were ash. Quentin was across the river, alive, safe, and by the terms of the armistice commission's own ruling, no longer prosecutable — the testimony about the flaw had reframed him, retroactively, from traitor to witness. On paper, he was free.

On paper.

Noor unwrapped the oilcloth.

The page was small — a single sheet torn from Elias's field ledger, covered in his cramped, slantwise hand, the hand she knew better than her own. And there it was, in the third paragraph, the sentence that had started everything and could still, if it ever surfaced in the wrong hands, end it:

*The boy Vasse assisted me at the pier survey, June last. He knows the flaw's true dimensions. If I do not return, see that he is moved beyond reach before the winter crossings begin.*

Elias had named him. Not Quentin Vasse, enemy's son, declared traitor — just *the boy*, but names traveled fast in this country, and *Vasse* was a name the crown had been hunting for five years. This single sheet, filed anywhere, remembered by anyone, could reopen every door the inquiry had closed. It could put Quentin's name back into the mouths of men who spoke in warrants.

Halvard knew such a page existed. He had said as much, in his cold, courteous way, standing in this very room: *"Your master was thorough, Miss Nystrom. Thorough men leave pages."* He had never found it. Perhaps he had stopped looking after the testimony — or perhaps he hadn't. Men like Halvard did not stop looking. They simply waited for the tide to go out.

Noor sat down at the drafting table with the page before her and the lamp turned low, and she made herself read it through, top to bottom, one last time.

That was the discipline of it. She had learned from Elias that a mapmaker's first duty was not to draw but to look — to look so completely that the thing became part of you, and then, only then, to choose what deserved ink. So she looked. She read Elias's account of the pier's flawed footing, the numbers she had already given to the court, the desperate addendum in his smaller, later script. She read the sentence naming Quentin twice, three times, until she could recite it with her eyes shut. She read the date. She read the watermark in the paper — a mill mark from the northern provinces, useless now, precious once.

And then she closed her eyes and let the page become part of her.

*The boy Vasse assisted me at the pier survey.*

She would keep it. Not the paper — the knowing. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point. A page could be found, subpoenaed, brandished, misread. A memory belonged to no archive. It could not be stamped or shelved or surrendered to a clerk. It could only be carried, the way she intended to carry everything now: across the bridge, in two hands, with nothing written down.

But carrying meant deciding, and deciding meant fire.

* * *

She prepared the way Elias had taught her to prepare anything that mattered — which is to say, without hurry.

First she opened the workshop shutters despite the hour, because a room should know the sky when something leaves it. The night beyond was clear and early-spring cold, the river running silver somewhere below the rooftops, and far off — she could just make it out, a darker line against the dark — the bridge itself, finished now, scaffolded down to its bones, waiting for its opening day. Lanterns burned along its length even at this hour. Workmen finishing the rails. In nine days it would open, and people would walk across it in both directions for the first time in seven years, and no one would think of the boy who had worked its scaffolding under a false name, or the girl who had burned a map to carry him over.

Second, she cleared the table. The brass lamp. The ink stones. The burnishing bone. Everything set in its place, because order was a kind of prayer and she needed to pray.

Third — and here her hands paused, hovering — she took down the tin from the high shelf. Inside, pressed between two panes of glass gone cloudy with years, lay the flower. Not her flower. Quentin's — the one he had kept since he was fourteen, since the last ferry, since a peacetime that felt like it had happened to other children. He had returned it to her at the rail, pressed into her palm while Halvard's shadow still fell across them both, and she had carried it home in her breast pocket like contraband of a better kind.

She set the tin beside the lamp. She did not open it. Not yet.

Then she lit the candle.

Not the lamp — a plain white candle in a dish, the kind used for sealing wax and small emergencies. She brought the flame close to the page's edge and stopped, and for a long moment she simply watched the light tremble against the paper, and she thought about what it means to destroy a thing on purpose.

There were easier ways. She could have buried it beneath the floorboards, where Elias's other secrets had slept for years. She could have dropped it in the river, let the current take it south past the piers and out of all jurisdiction. Fire was the most honest method and therefore the hardest — fire left nothing, not even the comfort of a hidden thing. Fire required her to watch the words go, all of them, including the ones that were innocent, including the ones that were beautiful, including Elias's own hand, the last original example of it she possessed.

That was the cost. She had counted it a dozen times and arrived at the same figure every time. The court had the atlas; the atlas would survive in the public record, corrected, annotated, attributed. But this page was hers alone, private as a heartbeat, and when it burned, a piece of Elias would exist nowhere on earth except inside her.

She found she could bear that. She had already learned, these past weeks, that the dead do not live in paper. They live in the habits they leave behind — the way she still sharpened pencils in eight strokes exactly, the way she still checked a margin twice before trusting it, the way she still, when frightened, stood with her weight on both feet and made her face into a map with nothing marked on it. Elias was in her hands. He would survive one fire.

Quentin might not survive the alternative.

She touched the flame to the lower corner.

* * *

The paper caught reluctantly, the way old paper does — a brown blush along the edge, a curl, a hesitation — and then it took, and the fire climbed the page with terrible gentleness, the way dawn climbs a coastline.

Noor held it by the unburned corner over the iron dish and watched the words go in reverse order. Elias's final lines vanished first — the plea, the instruction, the love disguised as logistics. Then the middle paragraphs, the measurements, the flaw rendered in numbers that had already done their work in the world and were free to die. Last of all, near the top, the heading: *Field Notes, Third Pier,* and above it the small sketch he always began with, the little compass rose drawn freehand, slightly crooked, the crookedness she had once teased him about and he had defended with the gravity of a man defending religion. *A perfect north is a fiction, Noor. Every honest map leans a little toward what it loves.*

The compass rose burned last.

She held the final corner until the flame touched her fingers — a small bright sting, entirely deserved, she thought — and released the page into the dish, where it collapsed into a shape like a black leaf, then into flakes, then into a grey bloom of ash that settled and went still.

The smoke smelled of cedar and ink. Of the workshop. Of seven years of apprenticeship distilled into one thin thread of air.

She opened the window and let the wind take it.

For a while she did nothing at all. She stood at the open window with her stinging fingertips pressed to the sill, watching the ash-motes the wind missed drift down toward the river, toward the bridge, toward the far bank where a man with a returned flower's twin memory of her was, at this exact moment, alive and unbewritten. Somewhere below, a late cart rattled over cobblestones. Somewhere across the water, a workman's hammer rang twice against new iron and fell silent.

*The boy Vasse assisted me at the pier survey.*

She said it once, silently, deliberately — the way one sets a stone in a wall — and then she said the rest of it too, all the words that had burned, top to bottom, until she was certain. Certain the way Elias had taught her to be certain: not hoping, not guessing, but knowing, with the whole weight of attention brought down upon a single point.

Then she let it loosen. Not forget — she would not insult the dead or the living by pretending forgetting was possible, or even desirable. But she let the words stop being a document and become a story, and stories, unlike documents, cannot be seized. They can only be told, and she would tell this one to no one. Some testimonies are kept best in silence.

She closed the window. The room felt different — emptier in the way a healed wound feels different, tender and clean. She looked around the workshop: the drafting table with its worn hollows, the shelves of instruments, the great empty case where the atlas had stood for five years like a sleeping animal. Tomorrow she would begin packing. Not much to pack. An apprentice's life folds down small.

Her eyes went to the tin.

* * *

She opened it sitting on the floor, her back against the drafting table's leg, because some ceremonies want to be low to the ground.

Inside, between the clouded glass, the flower lay exactly as he must have placed it seven years ago: a wild cornflower, blue gone violet with age, flattened to a whisper of itself but whole — every petal accounted for, the stem's fine hairs still visible, pressed and patient. It had crossed a river twice. Once folded inside a fourteen-year-old's coat pocket on a ferry, once inside a wanted man's palm at a bridge rail with an officer's lantern swinging closer. Two crossings. More than most maps could claim.

She lifted the upper pane and took it out, and it weighed nothing, of course. It weighed less than the ash. It weighed what a promise weighs, which is to say everything or nothing depending on whether anyone keeps it.

He had given it back to her at the rail — she understood that now, though in the moment she had only gripped it and run. Giving it back was his answer to the question she'd asked in the boathouse ruins, the question that had hung between them for five years and been answered finally in fireworks and fog: *what happens to us?* He had answered with the flower. Return to sender. Meaning: it was never mine to keep. Meaning: hold it until I can give it properly. Meaning: the bridge opens in nine days.

Nine days.

She pressed the cornflower gently between her palms, feeling its papery ghost of a body, and thought about the future with the calm of a woman who has finished burning things. There would be a crossing soon — hers, this time, alone, in daylight, among crowds, legal at last. There would be a railing on the far side where a man would be waiting, because he had waited seven years and nine days more would not break him. There would be no atlas in her bag, no letters, no evidence, no leverage. Just the flower going home, and whatever new blank book she bought on the far side, and whatever she chose to draw in it.

Elias had leaned his maps toward what he loved. She intended to lean her whole life that direction.

She returned the flower to the tin — carefully, pane to pane, the way you shelve anything irreplaceable — and set the tin beside the candle dish with its little grey bloom of ash. Side by side on the floor: what she had destroyed, and what she had kept. Between them, everything worth knowing about her.

Then she pinched out the candle, and in the dark of the workshop she began, silently, to count.

One. Two. Three.

Eight days now, if the wind stayed fair and the rails were finished on time. Eight days until the crowds, the bells, the first legal footsteps on the span. Eight days until she walked onto that bridge with empty pockets and full hands, and gave the past back to the person it belonged to.

Downstairs, faintly, Marta's humming had stopped. Noor heard the landlady's chair scrape, heard slow steps cross the hall, pause at the foot of the stairs — hesitate — and climb one step, then another.

A knock, soft, at the workshop door. And through it, in a voice Noor had never heard Marta use before — small, stripped of ledger-calm, afraid —

*"Miss Nystrom. There's a letter come. From across the river."*
