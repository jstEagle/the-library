## 21. The Bridge Opens

*Noor*

The morning of the opening, Noor woke before the bells and lay listening to the river.

It had been raining for two days — a thin, apologetic rain that washed the soot from the rooftops and left the cobbles shining like poured pewter — and now the clouds were tearing open in the east, letting down long blades of gold light across the water. From her window she could see the bridge entire: the seven arches marching across the strait, the new iron railing gleaming wet, the banners already hung along the tollhouse in the colors of both nations, sewn side by side for the first time in anyone's living memory. Workmen moved along its length like ants on a beam, stringing garlands, testing lanterns, sweeping the stone with brooms whose strokes she could not hear but could almost feel, the way one feels a heartbeat through a wall.

Five years ago she had stood at this same window and watched the scaffolding rise over the unfinished third pier, and had understood nothing of what it meant. Four months ago she had stood here with a sealed letter in her hand and understood too much.

Today the bridge would open, and people would walk across it as if walking across anything else.

She dressed carefully, in the grey wool she wore to funerals and to courtrooms, because this was both and neither. Marta was downstairs — of course Marta was downstairs; Marta had been up since four, Noor could hear the kitchen clock's argument with the stove — and when Noor came into the hall, the older woman was standing at the door with a shawl pinned at her throat and an expression Noor had never quite seen on her before. It took her a moment to name it. It was the look of a woman about to attend something she did not believe she deserved to see.

"They'll want everyone out on the embankment by nine," Marta said. "The procession starts from the customs house."

"I know."

"Noor." Marta's hands found each other and held on. "What I did. What I wrote. I've said it, and I'll say it again every day you let me, and if you ever tell me to stop saying it, I'll—"

"You don't have to say it today," Noor said. "Today we're just two women going to watch a bridge open."

Marta looked at her for a long moment, and then nodded, once, the way soldiers nod, and opened the door onto the bright cold morning.

The town had turned out in its whole body. Noor had seen crowds before — coronation days, the fleet's return, the terrible rallies after the declaration of enmity, when the same streets had been packed with men shouting themselves hoarse about the enemy across the water — but never a crowd like this one. This crowd was quiet in a way that was not solemn but *attentive*, the way a congregation is attentive, the way an audience is in the last minute before the curtain. Families stood shoulder to shoulder along the railings of the embankment. Old men who had ferried the river in peacetime wore their ancient brass buttons. A woman near the customs house steps held a child on her shoulders, and the child was waving at the bridge as though the bridge might wave back.

And perhaps it would. Noor thought of Elias, who had charted every stone of it, who had died with his survey books under his coat on a night of sleet, documenting the flaw in the third pier that both governments had buried beneath ceremony and concrete. The inquiry was three weeks old now. Engineers swarmed the pier even today, though they had promised the opening would proceed; the reinforcement work, they said, was sound. She chose to believe them. She had spent enough of her life choosing what to believe.

The atlas itself sat in the archives now, surrendered to the court, catalogued and stamped and read aloud in chambers while men in wigs argued about load tolerances. She missed it the way one misses a limb. But there were worse things to lose a hand to than honesty.

The band struck up — something martial at first, then, mercifully, something gentler — and the dignitaries began their slow progress toward the span's midpoint, where a ribbon waited, absurdly red against all that grey stone and grey water. Noor found herself searching the far bank without meaning to, the way a compass needle finds north, and caught herself, and made herself stop.

Not yet. Not yet. First the ribbon, first the crowds, first the hours of noise and confusion. Then the far bank. Then the railing.

She had written him a letter, finally — one page, delivered through channels she no longer asked questions about, because asking questions had nearly killed them all. One page: *The bridge opens on Thursday. I will be there. Look for me at the place where we were stopped.* And beneath it, because she could not help herself, because some habits outlive their fear: *I am bringing nothing. Come as you are.*

*Quentin*

Quentin had crossed the border eleven days ago, legally, with papers that said he was Tomas Vasse, engineer's assistant, seconded to the inspection commission — papers that were forgeries, and papers that were not, because the inquiry had a taste for irony and had hired, quietly, through intermediaries, the very man whose testimony had helped force it into being. He had walked across the old ferry landing where the boats rotted, past the checkpoint where a bored soldier glanced at his documents and waved him through, and had stood for one full minute on sovereign soil he had not touched since he was seventeen, breathing air that smelled of coal smoke and river weed and home-that-was-never-quite-home.

He had not come to stay. He had told himself that firmly. He had a life on the other side now — such as it was, a room, a trade, a name that was half true — and she had her own country, her own streets, her own griefs to keep. People crossed bridges. They did not have to move house across them.

But he had come back for the opening, and he had come back early, and every morning since he had walked to the railing at the bridge's far end — the railing, *the* railing, the one with the chipped granite post where Halvard had stood with his pistol lowered and his eyes full of something Quentin still did not have a word for — and he had waited, and watched, and gone back to his rented room, and slept badly, and come again.

Now the day itself had come, and the far bank was a sea of strangers, and Quentin stood among them in his borrowed coat with his heart going like a trip-hammer.

The ribbon fell at ten minutes past noon. He heard it rather than saw it — a roar going up from the crowd on both banks, doubling, trebling, echoing off the water until the whole strait seemed to be cheering — and then the dignitaries stepped aside, and the soldiers who had guarded this crossing for five years shouldered their rifles and *stepped back*, actually stepped back, and the first civilians set foot on the span.

An old woman went first. Quentin learned later that she was the widow of a ferry captain lost in the last year of the closed borders, and that the organizers had begged her to do it, but at the time he only saw a small bent figure in black walking alone into the middle of the bridge, stopping at the centerline, and standing there with her face turned up to the sky as though waiting for weather. Then the crowd behind her could bear it no longer, and flowed past her like a river around a stone, and the bridge filled end to end with people crossing — north to south, south to north, mothers with children, brothers carrying sisters on their backs, an old man in ferryman's buttons weeping openly and pretending he wasn't, two young soldiers of opposite nations meeting at the midpoint and shaking hands with the enormous awkward formality of boys who have been taught all their lives to be enemies and have not yet learned why.

History softening, his mother would have called it, if his mother had lived to see it. Quentin stood at the railing and felt it soften in him too — the vigilance he had carried so long it had grown into his skeleton, the counting of exits, the cataloguing of uniforms. There were uniforms everywhere today and none of them were looking for anyone. For one afternoon, astonishingly, no one was looking for anyone.

Except him. He was looking for one person, and had been looking for her for eleven days, and the crowd was a wall of faces, and none of them —

There.

Not coming from the embankment, where he'd expected her, but along the near pedestrian way, walking against the current of festival-goers with her chin up and her grey coat buttoned to the throat, moving through the crowd the way water moves through a crowd — finding the gaps, wasting nothing. Her hair was pinned back. She carried nothing. Of course she carried nothing; she had written it and she meant it, and something in his chest turned over at the sight of her hands empty at her sides, those hands that had held compasses and pens and burning paper and, once, at fourteen, on the deck of the last ferry, had gripped his sleeve hard enough to leave a crease.

She saw him at forty paces and stopped walking.

For a moment they simply looked at each other across the streaming crowd, the way they had looked across the water from the boathouse ruins, across the rail on the night everything nearly ended, across seven years and one burned page and an entire war that had never quite been declared. Then the crowd jostled her forward a step, and she let it, and then she was moving again, and then she was there, close enough to touch, smelling of rain and ink, her eyes brighter than he had ever seen them.

"You're late," she said.

"The queue at the checkpoint," Quentin said, "was tremendous."

And then neither of them said anything at all, because she had taken his lapel in her empty hand — the left one, the crease-hand — and pulled him in, and the crowd roared around them like the sea, and the bridge held.

* * *

*Noor*

They walked the length of it together.

It seemed the only possible thing to do. Everyone else was doing it — the crossing had become a kind of pilgrimage within the hour, people walking to the center and back merely to be able to say they had, touching the iron railing as if it were a saint's relic — and Noor wanted, suddenly and completely, to be one of them. To put her feet on every stone Elias had drawn. To stand at the third pier and know what lived underneath it, the flaw and the truth and the dead man's courage, and know also that it was repaired, reinforced, certified by men who had once wanted her hanged. To cross, and arrive, and turn around, and see her own country from the other side — smaller, she suspected. Everything looked smaller from across a river.

They stopped at the centerline, where a brass strip had been laid into the stone, engraved with the date. Quentin put his boot on it deliberately, one foot on either side, and grinned at her, and for a moment he was fourteen again, standing on the gunwale of the ferry, arms out, daring the river to knock him down.

"Do you remember," he said, "what you swore? On the ferry?"

"That we'd find each other if the borders closed." She looked down at the brass. "I remember thinking it was a childish thing to swear."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's the only promise I've ever kept properly."

A bell began to ring on the far bank — the cathedral, throwing its voice across the water, and the nearer cathedral answering, the two towers calling to each other like the halves of a sentence finally joined. Below them the river ran green-gold in the afternoon light, indifferent and beautiful, carrying its barges and its light and its reflections of flags. Noor leaned on the railing — on *the* railing, her fingertips finding the chipped granite post without looking for it — and watched the water, and felt the strangest sensation, which took her a long moment to identify.

It was quiet. Inside. For the first time in years, the workshop of herself was silent: no letter, no deadline, no code, no route, no page waiting to be burned or testimony waiting to be given. Just the river, and the bell, and the man beside her watching her with an expression she had spent five years learning by heart across a quarter mile of water and was only now seeing from arm's length.

"Come and see the workshop," she heard herself say. "Before you go back. It's not much. Half the shelves are empty now — the court took the atlas, and I sold most of the instruments to pay the fines. But there's a window that looks at the river, and a table where Elias taught me to hold a pen, and—" She stopped, surprised at her own voice, which had gone rough. "I'm making a new atlas. Not coasts and borders. People. Streets. Where the ferry captains lived. Where Marta's son is stationed, when he comes home. Things worth keeping."

Quentin was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the way voices are when they are carrying something fragile across a distance.

"And how long does an atlas take?"

"Years," Noor said. "The good ones take years."

"Then I'd better see the workshop," Quentin said, "before I go back. And after, too. As often as the checkpoints allow." He turned to face her fully, and the bells rang on, and somewhere below them the first barge of the new era blew its whistle, long and joyful and slightly off-key, and Quentin Vasse, enemy's son, bridge-walker, keeper of a pressed flower for seven years, reached into his coat and drew out a small flat shape wrapped in oilcloth, worn soft at the edges from handling.

"I believe," he said, "this is yours. Returned to sender."

Noor took it. Inside the cloth, pressed flat and brown and perfect, the cornflower she had picked for him at fourteen on the last open ferry had waited out the whole of the closed years — through the declaration, the scaffoldings, the letter, the fire, the fog, the rail. It smelled of nothing anymore. It weighed less than a coin. She held it in her palm and found, to her astonishment, that she was not crying; she was laughing, or something adjacent to laughing, and Quentin was wearing the expression of a man who has finally set down a weight he forgot he was carrying.

"Keep it," she said, closing his fingers back over the flower. "We'll call it collateral."

"Collateral for what?"

"For the years," Noor said. "Hold it until the atlas is finished. Then bring it back, and I'll press a new one, and we'll start the ledger again."

The bells were still ringing when they turned toward the southern bank together, two figures among thousands on a bridge that had been a border and was now, at last, only a bridge — and ahead of them, small in the golden haze, the far shore waited with its lit windows and its ordinary evening, patient as a blank page, ready to be drawn.
