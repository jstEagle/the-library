## 2. Seven Years of Careful Silence

Hazel did not let go of the letter until Quentin Marsh's knuckles had gone white around his cap.

"Give it back," he said. Not a demand — a request dressed as one. He had the voice of a man who had learned that requests were cheaper than demands and paid out the same.

"In a moment." She held the page flat against her skirt, as though the wind might steal it and carry it off to some archive where drowned women were not permitted to write. The ink was brown-black, the nib pressed hard at every downstroke. Seven years, and the hand had not changed. If anything it had grown more certain, as though death had been good for Edith's penmanship.

"You've read it," Quentin said.

"I read everything." She said it plainly because there was no use being otherwise now. "Every letter I've carried for Lady Wexford in eleven years. Bills, love notes, a confession once, from a man in Cheltenham who'd forged his brother's signature on a mortgage. I read them all." She looked up at him. "You may as well know what sort of person you're standing with."

He didn't step back. That was something.

* * *

Seven years ago, in this same house — then her father's, not Lady Wexford's — Hazel had stood at an attic window watching men drag the harbor with grappling irons, and she had known with a certainty that frightened her more than grief ever could: *there is nothing down there.*

She had known because of the night before. Because Edith had come to her room at half past eleven with her hair already loose, wearing the gray traveling coat she'd sworn she'd sold, and had sat on the edge of Hazel's bed and taken both her hands.

"He'll publish them," Edith had said. "The letters. He showed me copies. Father would rather bury three daughters than survive this."

The *he* needed no name. A married man, older, with a wife in Brighton and a taste for keeping trophies. The kind of scandal that in 1906 did not merely ruin a girl; it salted the ground behind her so nothing else in the family could grow. Her father's seat on the harbor commission. Her younger sister's engagement. Their mother's fragile, expensive respectability.

"I'm not going to die," Edith had said, and her grip had tightened. "Do you understand me, Hazel? Whatever they find or don't find — whatever they say at the funeral — you must understand that I am *alive*, and that this is the only way I stay so."

And Hazel, nineteen years old and terrified, had done the only brave thing available to her. She had nodded. She had helped fold a shawl into a carpet bag. She had watched from the window at dawn as her sister walked down to the water in her best coat, the coat they would later describe to the coroner, and stepped into the fog like a woman stepping behind a curtain.

They never found a body. They found a shoe. One shoe, left neatly on the lower steps of the north pier, laced, pointing out to sea — placed, Hazel understood even then, by a sister who thought in details. The town gave Edith a stone in the churchyard with a good Christian verse on it, and her father gave the family twenty years of silence bought at the price of his own slow graying, and Hazel gave the sea her hatred and kept her promise.

She had told no one. Not her father, dying, calling Edith's name at the end in a voice like a hinge. Not their mother, who took to her bed each year on the anniversary and rose from it thinner. Not the youngest, Clara, who had grown up praying to a sainted dead sister and built her whole gentle life upon the altar of that loss.

Hazel had carried the secret the way she carried letters — carefully, silently, sealed.

Until this morning, when the seal had begun writing back.

* * *

"This is the third," she said to Quentin now, laying the letters out on Lady Wexford's morning-room table like a hand of cards. "That I know of. There may be more."

The first had come six weeks ago, addressed to *Mrs. Amberdale, sen.* — their mother — care of the old house. Hazel intercepted it before breakfast. No threat in it, only a request: money, sent to a poste restante in Wexmouth, and *passage when I ask*. Signed with the single initial *E.* It might have been anyone. Except that no one else alive wrote that backward-leaning E, the crossbar sweeping under like a wave curling home.

The second letter had been less polite. The third, the one in her hand, named a figure — eighty pounds — and a date. Thursday's tide. And beneath it, in a different pressure of pen, almost an afterthought: *Tell no one. Others are reading.*

"Others," Hazel repeated. "You saw the man who hired you. Describe him again."

Quentin shifted. "Gloves. Good ones. A scar through the left wrist — old, healed crooked. He paid double to have it delivered by hand, today, before ten." He hesitated. "There was something else. He asked whether the household kept a maid who handled the post. Asked casual, like. But he asked."

The fire ticked in the grate. Out in the harbor, the fog was lifting in rags, and beneath the rags the masts stood black and patient.

"Someone has been reading my sister's letters," Hazel said slowly, "besides me. Someone who knew enough to hire a courier instead of trusting the mails." She turned the newest letter toward the light. "Which means either Edith is playing games with strangers —"

"— or someone's found her trail," Quentin finished, "and is walking along it."

Yes. That was the shape of it, and it curdled in her exactly as fear should: low in the stomach, cold at the edges. For seven years Hazel had believed silence was a wall she maintained. Now she understood it had been a door, and someone had found it standing ajar, and had been slipping through for weeks while she slept.

"And the money," she went on. "Eighty pounds. Where does a dead woman collect eighty pounds? She'd need a name. A paper. Something with her face on it." Her voice thinned. "Or she isn't collecting it herself."

Quentin looked at her. "Blackmail," he said. "If it's blackmail, the writer isn't your sister at all."

"No," Hazel agreed. "But whoever wrote it knows she's alive. And knowing that is worth more than eighty pounds. It's worth the inheritance. It's worth Clara's marriage. It's worth everything my father traded his life to buy." She gathered the letters, and her hands were steady, which surprised her. Grief had taught her hands steadiness; fear was only grief with its sleeves rolled up. "I need to know where these came from. Which boarding houses take in people without histories. Which rooms face the water."

"That's courier work," Quentin said. "Asking after addresses."

"It's the work of the one person in this town who can't be blackmailed," Hazel said. "Everything I have is already ruined. That's a kind of freedom, Mr. Marsh. You should try it sometime."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; the memory of one. "Wexmouth," he said. "The poste restante on the first letter. If your sister's trail runs anywhere, it runs south along the coast road. Harbor town. Cheap rooms. Fog most of the year." He put on his cap. "There's a boarding house there called the Saltings. Takes cash, asks nothing. I delivered a parcel there once, winter before last, to a man who paid me not to look at his face."

"Then look at faces," Hazel said. "Starting tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?" He paused at the door. "Why not tonight?"

Because she wanted one more night of being a woman whose sister was safely, decently drowned. Because tomorrow, Thursday's tide would begin building, and whatever was coming would arrive with it. Because she was afraid — but she had been afraid for seven years, and it had never once stopped her doing the next thing.

"Because tonight," Hazel said, folding the letters into her bodice over her heart, "I'm going to write a reply — in my own hand, unsigned — offering the eighty pounds. And whoever collects it will have to show me a face too."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment, this quiet carrier of other people's secrets, and she saw him decide something.

"The Saltings," he said. "Room at the top of the stairs. If I'm not back by Friday's dawn —"

"You'll be back," Hazel said. "Dead sisters write. Living couriers walk."

He went out into the thinning fog, and she stood at the window watching him shrink down the hill toward the water, toward the ships counting the hours until Thursday — and did not see, two gardens away, behind the bare hedge of the empty Prewitt house, a figure in gray turn away from the gate and walk quickly toward the coast road south.
