## 3. What the Tide Takes

The boarding house stood at the end of Rope Walk like something the sea had coughed up and refused to take back. Its shingles were black with salt rot, its windows fogged from within by kettles and grief, and above the door hung a sign so weathered that Quentin had to tilt his head to read it: *MRS. PENHALIGON — LODGINGS FOR RESPECTABLE PERSONS*. The word *respectable* had been painted over an older word, and in the right light he could still make out its ghost: *quiet*.

Quentin Marsh had been paid to ask no questions for eleven years. He knew every variety of silence — the silence of the guilty, the silence of the frightened, the silence of men who had simply stopped caring what happened to them. But the silence of a woman who had been dead for seven years and was writing letters anyway — that was new to him, and it itched like wet wool.

He had told Hazel Amberdale he would trace the hand. He had not told her why. The truth was simple and shameful: the money in his coat pocket, the advance for this last delivery, was the only money between him and the morning tide himself. A courier who asked no questions could still count coins.

* * *

Mrs. Penhaligon was a mountain of gray wool and suspicion. She looked at Quentin the way gulls look at unattended fish.

"Letters," she said, when he mentioned the name he'd lifted from the delivery ledger. "We get letters here same as anywhere."

"I'm looking for a Mrs. Edith Amberdale."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, involuntary, like a shutter catching wind. "No such person lodges here."

"No," Quentin agreed pleasantly. "But someone writes under her name. Third floor, back room. I'd guess the one facing the water, because whoever it is likes to watch the harbor." He set a coin on the counter and left his hand on it. "I'm not the police, Mrs. Penhaligon. I'm worse. I'm curious."

She stared at the coin as if it might bite her. Then, without quite deciding to, she took it.

"Room nine," she said. "But you'll want to knock soft. Mr. Fenn doesn't care for surprises."

*Fenn.* Not Amberdale. Quentin climbed the stairs two at a time, his heart keeping a rhythm that had nothing to do with exertion.

* * *

The door of room nine was unlocked, which told him more than any lock could have. Inside: a table, a chair, a bottle of ink gone thick as tar, a stack of cheap paper. And on the table, half-finished, a letter in the unmistakable hand of Edith Amberdale — the looping ascenders, the distinctive backward slant on the letter *d* that Hazel had described with her voice shaking.

Except now, seeing it fresh, Quentin saw what Hazel's love for her sister had kept her from seeing. The hand was good. It was very good. It was *too* good — practiced, studied, the slant on the *d* reproduced with the careful precision of a man copying rather than a woman writing.

A man came out of the water closet with a razor halfway to his jaw and murder already settled comfortably into his face.

"You're the courier," the man said. He was small and neat, with the pallor of someone who lived indoors and the hands of someone who counted money often. "Marsh, isn't it. I've seen you about. You've got an honest face, which is why they pay you, and I'll be honest with you because it saves time." He gestured with the razor at the letter on the table. "There is no Edith Amberdale. There never has been. There is a dead woman whose handwriting I learned from letters I bought off a housemaid in Torquay, and there is a family rich enough to keep paying to keep her dead. That's all. That's the whole of it."

"And the drowning?"

"A gift from God," Mr. Fenn said, wiping the razor with terrible calm. "A woman goes missing off a headland, a body washes up three days later too far gone for anyone but a grieving husband to identify — well. Grief sees what it needs to see. I merely discovered what grief had built, and I've been collecting rent on it ever since." He smiled. "You delivered my last letter this morning. Did the little sister like it?"

"She recognized the hand."

For the first time, something flickered behind Fenn's composure. "Recognized it how?"

"Like recognizing your own reflection in dark water."

Fenn set down the razor slowly. "Then we have a problem, you and I. Because a sister who recognizes the hand won't pay forever. She'll start asking where the hand lives. And I'll be needing passage out of this charming town before she finds me — which brings me to you." He produced a wallet, thick, cracked leather, and counted notes onto the table with the tenderness of a man dealing cards. "There's a ship leaving on the morning tide. One berth left, and the purser's a friend of mine. I need a final letter delivered tonight — to the Amberdale house — naming the terms. And I need you to forget room nine exists."

"How much?"

"All of it," Fenn said. "Every note. Enough to vanish properly, wherever you like."

Quentin looked at the money. He thought about the winter behind him, the pawned watch, the room in Stepney with the damp climbing the walls like ivy. He thought about how easy it would be. He had carried worse things than blackmail letters. He had once delivered a message that ended a marriage and another, he suspected, that ended a life, and he had slept fine afterward because asking questions was the one luxury he'd never been able to afford.

And then he thought of Hazel Amberdale standing in her employer's hallway with her sister's death certificate trembling in her hands, saying: *I have kept her secret for seven years. I have buried her every morning of my life.*

"Terms of what?" he heard himself ask.

"The last payment," Fenn said smoothly. "And a choice. Tell the family that either they pay double by dawn, or the truth does. That whichever Amberdale can prove she's real gets to exist, and the other one —" he smiled again, and it was a horrible smile, patient and small, "— the other one can go back to being drowned."

* * *

Quentin did not take the money. He walked out of room nine with his fists closed and his mind roaring, and he made it four doors down Rope Walk before a figure stepped out of the alley shadow between a chandler's store and a net-mending shed, and said his name.

Not *Mr. Marsh*. His name. *Quentin.*

She was wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too large, hood pulled low, and beneath it her face was thinner than the photograph Hazel kept hidden in her prayer book, lined where seven years should not yet have lined her, but the eyes were the same eyes — the ones from the portrait over the Amberdale mantel, the ones Hazel had inherited along with everything else.

Edith Amberdale looked at him the way people look at a door they've been afraid to open.

"You didn't take his money," she said. It was not a question. "I watched you leave. I've watched you since you first came off the London train, courier. You walk like a man carrying something heavier than parcels." She glanced back toward the boarding house, and her mouth twisted. "Seven years I let them believe I was at the bottom of the bay. Seven years I watched from far enough away to be safe. And then *that creature* found the shape of my life and started squeezing it like a lemon." Her voice dropped. "I came back to end it myself. Instead I find my sister knows I'm alive, a blackmailer owns my name, and there is exactly one berth on the morning ship."

"There are two of you," Quentin said. "One berth."

"Yes." She reached into the oilskin and drew out an envelope — real paper, good paper, sealed with wax, and when she held it up he saw the handwriting on the front and understood at last what he was holding. Not a copy. Not a forgery. The original hand, tired now, older, but true.

"One letter," Edith said. "Delivered before dawn. It names which Amberdale sails. My sister reads it and believes whatever it says, because it's in my hand and my hand is the only god she's ever trusted." She pressed it toward him, and her fingers were cold as harbor water. "Name your price, Mr. Marsh. Every man at this dock has one. I watched you refuse his. So name yours, and deliver this, and by morning one of us is free and the other is a story."

Below them, the tide was turning. He could hear it filling the harbor channel, patient and enormous, taking what it always took.

Quentin looked at the envelope. He looked at the woman who had been drowned for seven years and had come back to choose, at last, between herself and her sister — or perhaps to make him choose.

"One berth," he said quietly. "Which one does the letter name?"

Edith Amberdale smiled, and it was nothing like Fenn's smile; it was tired and human and terribly sad.

"That," she said, "is the price of reading it."
