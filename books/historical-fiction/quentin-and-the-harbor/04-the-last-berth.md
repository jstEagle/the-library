## 4. The Last Berth

The fog had thinned by the time Hazel reached the quay, but it still hung in rags between the masts like something reluctant to leave. The *Coriolis* rode low at her berth, her funnel breathing a slow grey plume into the pinkening sky, and along her rail the crew moved with the unhurried economy of men who had done this a hundred dawns and would do it again tomorrow without remembering this one.

Hazel remembered everything.

She had not slept. She had sat in the boarding house's narrow parlour with her hat in her lap and her sister across from her — Edith, seven years older than the drowned girl of the photographs, her hair streaked with grey she made no effort to hide — and they had spoken until the candles guttered and neither of them lit new ones, because there are things better said in the dark.

Now Edith stood beside her on the wet cobbles, and Quentin Marsh stood a little apart, holding two tickets as though they were live coals.

"One berth," he said. He did not look at either woman. He looked at the ship, because it was easier. "The purser will only take one name before the harbour office opens and someone starts asking why Miss Amberdale is alive."

"Then it must be you," Hazel said to Edith.

Edith smiled, and it was the smile from the old photographs, the one that had always meant she had already decided something and was merely permitting the world to be informed. "No," she said. "It must be you."

"I won't—"

"You will." Edith took her hands. Her fingers were cold; seven years of hiding lived in them. "Listen to me, Hazel, because I have rehearsed this speech all night and I shall be very cross if you ruin it. If I board that ship, I run again. And if I run again, then the drowning was real after all — just slower. Then everything Father built on my grave stays built on it, and Mother dies believing her daughter is at the bottom of the Channel, and you spend the rest of your life carrying letters for a ghost." Her grip tightened. "I have been a ghost long enough. It is the only honest work left to me to stop being one."

"And what does honesty cost?" Hazel heard her own voice crack. "They will call you mad or wicked. They will say the scandal was true. They'll dig up every—"

"They may." Edith released her hands and straightened her plain grey coat, and for a moment she was seventeen again, defying their father over a pamphlet, chin up, eyes bright with the particular courage of people who intend to be right and do not care who watches. "But I will be standing in the daylight when they say it. That is more than I have had since 1906. That is more, I think, than you have had, my dear, and you never even went into the water."

* * *

Quentin spoke then, quietly, from his place by the bollard. "There's another matter."

Hazel turned. In the growing light she saw him properly for the first time — the courier who asked no questions, whose questions had nearly ruined them all. There was something raw in his face that had not been there when he'd first handed her that impossible envelope at Lady Verrell's door.

"The man at the Anchor Inn," he said. "Fenn. He wasn't working alone. He showed me telegrams — he's been corresponding with someone in London. A solicitor's clerk who handles the family trust." He hesitated. "He wanted me to choose which of you sailed, so the other could be... managed. I was to report the name."

"Managed," Edith repeated softly. "A kind word for it."

"He's gone," Quentin said. "When I told him I'd take no commission, he packed. But Fenn is only weather. Someone paid for the storm." He looked at Hazel. "Your family's affairs have a reader besides you, Miss Amberdale. Whoever it is, they've known about the letters longer than I have."

Hazel felt the ground tilt — the quay, the fog, seven years of careful silence all tilting together. Of course. Every letter she had steamed open, every secret she had guarded alone — guarded so well that guarding had become its own architecture, walls inside walls. And somewhere behind those walls, someone else had found the same loose brick.

"Then let them read this too," Edith said.

She unpinned her hat, took out a folded paper — the letter Quentin had carried to her, the final demand — and produced a pencil stub from her pocket. On the back, in that unmistakable hand, in full view of the waking harbour, she wrote three lines.

*I am Edith Amberdale. I am not dead. I am coming home.*

She folded it, sealed it with a lick and a press of her thumb, and handed it to Quentin. "Deliver it. To the house. Not to the solicitor — to the house, to my mother's own hand. Let whoever has been reading our mail choke on it." A pause. "You'll be paid."

"No," Quentin said.

Edith raised an eyebrow.

"Not for that one." He tucked the letter inside his coat, next to his heart, where couriers were supposed to keep nothing. "Some things go free."

* * *

The gangway came down with a groan of wet rope and iron. The purser called the hour. Somewhere beyond the breakwater the tide was turning, vast and indifferent, taking and giving according to laws older than any Amberdale.

Quentin held out the ticket.

Hazel did not take it at once. "Why?" she asked. "One berth, two of us, and you chose—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "Seven years ago everyone chose Edith's silence. Last night you chose me. Why?"

Quentin considered the question with the gravity of a man who had clearly been considering it all night himself.

"Because she's going home," he said finally, "and you're not. Because a person can survive being dead — I think she's proof — but you were alive this whole time and living like a grave-keeper. Somebody ought to find out what the rest of the world looks like." His mouth twitched toward something that was almost a smile. "Besides. You read other people's letters. Consider this one delivered to yourself: *go.*"

The word landed in her chest and stayed there, warm, absurdly warm, like a coal passed hand to hand.

She took the ticket.

Boarding was a blur — the purser's stamp, the creak of the plank under her boots, a sailor's arm briefly steadying her elbow. Then the deck, the salt wind sharpening as the fog gave way, and the town falling away beneath her in terracotta roofs and rigging lines, smaller than she had ever seen it, small enough to hold in one eye.

Edith stood on the quay, not waving. Simply standing, upright as a mast, watching her sister go — and as the *Coriolis* gathered way, Hazel saw her lift one hand anyway, high above her head, and hold it there, the old signal from the nursery window, the one that had meant *all is well, come inside before dark.*

All is well. It was not true yet. It might never be entirely true. But it was, for the first time in seven years, possible.

Hazel gripped the rail until her knuckles whitened, memorising the harbour, the fog-burned morning, the small figure on the quay, the courier who had already turned away to carry a dead woman's resurrection through the streets in his coat pocket.

The tide ran strong beneath the hull, bearing her out.

And far ahead, past the breakwater, past the pale line of the horizon, the future waited — unguarded now, unwalled, wide open as the sea.
