## 1. The Weight of Small Hours

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Elodie would remember later, because Tuesdays were the day she oiled the regulator in her own little shop and Tuesdays were, therefore, sacred — and the war had never before been so rude as to interrupt a Tuesday.

The letter was delivered by a boy too young for his uniform, who stood in her doorway breathing through his mouth and holding out the envelope as though it might bite him. It bore the seal of the observatory, which was strange enough, and inside was a single line in an unfamiliar hand: *The master clock has stopped. You are requested to attend. Bring your tools.*

*You are requested.* Not invited. Requested, the way one requests grain or horses or the use of a churchyard for artillery. The requisitions had begun in September, first the carts, then the copper from the rooftops of the abandoned houses across the river, then the bells themselves — no, not the bells, thank God, though Tomas had taken to sleeping in the bell tower as if he expected soldiers to come for them in the night. The town had learned to read the grammar of requisition notices the way other towns read weather.

But this was different. This was the observatory. And the master clock had not stopped since before Elodie was born.

She stood in her doorway after the boy clattered away, turning the envelope over twice, three times, looking down the street toward the hill where the observatory sat above the town like a patient eye. The morning was grey and smelled of coal smoke and the last wet leaves of October. Somewhere far off — far enough that the townspeople had trained themselves to call it *the sea* — the guns kept their low, patient arithmetic. *There*, thought Elodie. *There is history, doing its work over the horizon, to other people.* She folded the letter into her apron pocket and went upstairs for her tools.

Her shop was small and warm and hers, three rooms below the flat where she slept, with a south window that caught the light until four o'clock in November. On the bench lay the mantel clock belonging to Madame Ferrand, its case open like a patient awaiting surgery, and beside it the parts tray with its neat rows of screws arranged by size, because Augustin Verneuil had taught her that disorder in small things was how large things came to ruin. She looked at that tray for a long moment. Then she took up her roll of tools — the good ones, the ones with whalebone handles that had been his — and locked the door behind her, and did not let herself think about why her hands were not quite steady.

* * *

The road up the hill was cobbled and steep and ran between walls that had once held vineyards and now held weeds and, in one place, a propaganda poster half peeled from the stone, its tricolour face faded to the colour of weak tea. Elodie walked fast, partly because of the cold, partly because walking fast left no room for thinking. The observatory grew above her as she climbed, its dome grey against the grey sky, its lantern room dark — the great lamp unlit now for years, useless since the war had blacked out every coast and hilltop within a hundred kilometres. A blind eye in a blind country, her master used to say, and laugh in a way that made you unsure whether he was joking.

Augustin Verneuil had been dead eleven months.

She still measured time that way. Eleven months, and before that, thirty-one years, because those were the two quantities her life had been divided into: everything before the winter he coughed blood into his handkerchief at the workbench and pretended he hadn't, and everything after. She had apprenticed to him at fourteen, a girl with a foreign father's name and no dowry and hands that were already steady, and he had taken her without ceremony, saying only, *Good. Steady hands are rarer than clever heads.* For seventeen years he had taught her the trade — the escapements, the jewelling, the patience — and for seventeen years she had believed she knew him the way one knows a clock: thoroughly, from the inside, every wheel accounted for.

Then he died, and left her the workshop and a silence she could not dismantle.

The observatory door stood unlocked. That was the second strange thing. She noted it the way she noted a tick that had gone irregular — filed it away, kept working. Inside, the entrance hall rose up cold and echoing, its marble floor scuffed by decades of students' boots, its notice board still pinned with announcements from 1914, curling at their corners. Someone had left a lamp burning at the foot of the stairs. Someone, therefore, was here.

"Hello?" Her voice fell flat in the stone. "I've come about the clock."

No answer. Only, from somewhere above, a sound she felt before she heard it — a deep, arrhythmic shudder in the building's bones, like a heart missing beats. The master clock. She knew its voice; everyone in town did, though few ever thought about it, the way no one thinks about their own pulse until it stumbles. Every quarter hour all her life, that mechanism had spoken from the hill, and the whole town had set itself by it without knowing. Bakers and bell-ringers and midwives and train conductors — they had all been living inside Augustin Verneuil's keeping of time, and none of them had ever given it a thought.

Neither, she realized, climbing, had she.

The clock room was behind the great pendulum gallery, and the door to it was heavy oak with a brass plate worn smooth by hands. She put her shoulder to it and stepped into the past.

* * *

It was warm.

That was the first thing, the thing that stopped her with her hand still on the latch. The room was *warm* — not heated, nothing had been heated in this building for months, but warm in the way a body leaves warmth in a chair. A low iron stove sat in the corner, its ash settled but its metal not yet cold, and above it hung a kettle, dry, waiting. And the smell — oil and graphite and beeswax and, underneath, faintly, pipe tobacco, though the pipe itself lay in its dish on the bench, cold, dead, its bowl still holding the grey ghost of last ashes.

Eleven months. No one had smoked that pipe in eleven months. But the room had not been told.

Elodie stood just inside the door with her tool roll under her arm and let the room happen to her. It was exactly as he had kept it. The benches along the wall, scarred and burnished by sixty years of elbows. The pegboard with its outline-drawn tools, every silhouette filled except one — the fine pliers he'd lost the summer she was sixteen and had blamed her for losing, and found two years later in the garden under a rosebush, and never once apologized for blaming her, only set them back in their outline and said, *Well. Things return when they're ready.* The jars of screws sorted by thread. The loupe on its stand. His coat on its hook, collar up, as if he'd only stepped out to see about the weather.

And in the centre of the room, tall as a coffin standing upright, the master clock's case stood open, its faceplate swung aside on its hinge, its interior exposed — the great going-train, the weight lines running up into darkness, the pendulum hanging motionless in a way that hurt to look at, like a man mid-stride turned suddenly to stone.

She made herself cross the room. Professional pride, she told herself. That was the feeling in her chest. Pride that the summons had come to her and not to some army technician or a clockmaker from the city. Pride that she was the one who knew this movement the way a daughter knows a face. Grief was for widows and mothers, and she was neither; she was a tradeswoman with a stopped clock and a summons, and she set her tool roll on the bench beside the cold pipe and opened it with fingers that were almost perfectly steady.

The fault announced itself before she reached the movement. There, on the floor beneath the case: a broken tooth from the escape wheel, small and bright as a fish scale. She picked it up and held it to the window's grey light. Steel, good steel, and the fracture clean — fatigue, not abuse. Wheels fail; it happens; steel remembers every stress ever put upon it and one day presents the bill. But the master clock's escape wheel had been replaced — she replaced it herself, with him supervising, complaining about the price of Swiss steel — nine years ago. Nine years was early. Nine years meant something else had been wrong, some misalignment, some wear elsewhere feeding stress into the tooth night after night, and a careful man would have noticed.

A careful man had kept this room for sixty years.

Unless, said a small cold voice in her, unless he hadn't been careful lately. Unless the last year — the year of the coughing, the year he sent her home early and worked alone, the year his letters to the observatory board grew shorter and stranger — unless that year the care had gone somewhere else entirely.

She knelt and looked up into the movement, and there she saw what she had been summoned to see, and also what she had not.

The broken wheel was the surface. Beneath it, wedged in the frame where no honest repairman would leave anything, someone had packed a wad of cloth — and wrapped inside the cloth, tied with waxed thread, a shape she recognized with a lurch that had nothing to do with clockwork. It was the size and shape of a ledger. Of *his* ledger, the workshop ledger, the one she had searched for after his death and never found, the one the lawyer had assumed was lost or burned, the absence of which had bothered her for eleven months like a word forgotten on the tip of the tongue.

She did not reach for it. Not yet. Seventeen years of training said: diagnose first. So she looked, instead, at the movement itself, and saw the rest of it — the thing that made her go cold in a warm room.

The escape wheel had not simply failed. Its arbor had been *filed*. Delicately, expertly, by someone who understood exactly what they were doing, the pivot had been scored with fine cuts so that it would wear, and wear unevenly, and fail — not today, not tomorrow, but weeks later, months later, at some unpredictable hour. Sabotage, in the language of gears. Done from inside this room, by someone with access and skill.

By him?

Or by whoever had last been here — whoever had left the stove's ash settled but not cold, the kettle dry, the pipe full of old ghosts. Eleven months the room should have been sealed and silent, and instead it was *waiting*, maintained, warmed, as if Augustin Verneuil had merely stepped out and someone expected him back.

Elodie sat back on her heels on the cold flagstones, the broken tooth bright in her palm, and heard the silence of the stopped clock pressing down around her like water. Far off, muffled by hill and stone, the guns went on with their arithmetic. History, doing its work to other people, over the horizon.

Except that someone had sabotaged this clock. Except that her dead master's secrets were wedged inside its skeleton like a swallowed key. Except that the summons had come in an unfamiliar hand from a building that was supposed to be empty, and the door had been unlocked, and the lamp at the foot of the stairs was still burning.

The kettle, she noticed then, was not quite empty either. At its bottom, a skin of fresh water. Drawn within the day.

She rose slowly, every sense open, and that was when she heard it — overhead, through the ceiling, soft but unmistakable: the creak of a floorboard bearing a careful weight. Then another. Someone was moving in the lantern room above her, directly above the stopped heart of the observatory, and had been there, she understood now, for some little while. Listening to her work.

Elodie closed her hand around the broken tooth until its edges bit her palm, and did not call out.
