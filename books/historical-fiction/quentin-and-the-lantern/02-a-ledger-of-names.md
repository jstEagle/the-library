## 2. A Ledger of Names

The faceplate came away at dawn.

Elodie had worked through the small hours by lamplight, the observatory's great room breathing around her — that particular silence of a stopped clock, which is not silence at all but a held breath. The master clock stood taller than she did, its case of black walnut gone soft-edged with a century of hands, and when she finally loosened the last of the four brass screws and lifted the plate free, it came with the gentle sucking reluctance of a thing that has not been opened in years.

Augustin had kept it closed. That was the first wrongness, though she did not yet know to call it that. Her master had oiled his movements the way other men said their prayers — faithfully, at fixed hours, without exception. She had watched him do it a thousand times from the doorway of his shop below, this same man who had taught her that neglect was the only true sin a clockmaker could commit. *A stopped clock is not dead, Elodie. It is waiting. There is a difference, and your work is to know which is which.*

She set the faceplate on the dust sheet and looked into the case.

The movement itself was magnificent even in its sickness — the great barrel, the going train with its wheels like frozen water-lilies, the escapement poised mid-thought. Dust lay over everything in a grey felt, thick as snow on a roof. But it was not the dust that stopped her hand. It was what the dust did not cover.

Behind the dial, bolted where no inspector would ever look, no apprentice ever think to look, there was a shallow drawer of oiled tin. It was fitted into the case's inner wall with the same loving precision Augustin gave to everything — flush, seamless, invisible unless you knew the case's anatomy as intimately as he had taught her to know it. She had known this clock since she was nine years old. She had helped him move it once, in the spring of 1911, when the observatory floor was relaid, and she remembered every panel, every screw, every secret of its body.

There had been no drawer then.

Or there had been, and she had never seen it, because Augustin Verne had spent seventy-one years teaching one lesson above all others: that the truest hiding places are the ones built in plain sight, inside things everyone believes they understand completely.

Her fingers were steady. Apprentice's discipline; ten thousand hours at the bench had given her that much. But her heart had begun a small hard knocking against her ribs, out of time with everything else in the room, and she thought distantly that if her master could have seen her pulse just now he would have frowned and told her to breathe before touching anything.

She drew the drawer out on its little runners.

It was heavier than it should have been. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed string, lay a book — no, two books, an older one swollen with damp and rebinding, and beneath it a newer ledger bound in green board, its corners worn round as river stones. On top of them, folded into a square gone soft with handling, was a single page torn from some third volume, its edges feathered.

She untied the string the way Augustin had taught her to untie all knots: slowly, giving the rope its history back, never cutting what patience could preserve.

The green ledger opened to a page dated March 1903, and Elodie Okafor forgot how to breathe properly for a long moment.

It was not accounts. Or rather, it was accounts, but not of money — or not only of money. The columns ran: *Name. Debt. Disposition.* And under *Disposition*, in her master's small upright hand, unvarying across decades like the tick of some internal mechanism, were entries such as these:

*Henriette Baudry, widow. Rent owed to Landlord Perrin, six months, 214 francs. Paid in full by A.V., recorded as forgiven by Perrin after "negotiation." Henriette need not know.*

*Lucien Marchal, cooper. Convicted drunkard by reputation, sober eleven months, needs employment above suspicion. Spoke to Girard at the mill. Lucien believes the position found him by luck.*

*Boy, name unknown, age perhaps fourteen, caught stealing bread from Fournier's cart. Paid Fournier the value three times over and asked him to say nothing. Boy has returned twice to sweep the shop step. I pretend not to see.*

Page after page after page. Years of them. She turned the leaves with fingertips gone suddenly clumsy, and the dates scrolled backward under her thumbs — 1899, 1894, 1888 — until the green book ended and the swollen elder volume took up the work, its ink browner, its entries sparser, reaching back to a year before Elodie's own mother had been born.

And threaded among the debts, growing more frequent as the decades turned toward war, were other kinds of entries entirely.

*September 1914. Young Rousset, deserter from the 2nd Colonial, hid three nights in the cellar behind the coal bin. Fed him at odd hours. Moved him out Thursday in the flour wagon to Marguerite's cousin at Vireloup. He wept when he left. So, quietly, did I.*

*January 1916. Letters from Madame Aubert's son at Verdun, four of them, carried past the censor by means I will not write down. She reads them aloud to me because she has no one else, and pretends they arrive by ordinary post. Her performance is better than mine.*

*June 1917. The Prefect's men came asking about strangers. There were strangers. There are always strangers. I showed them my books of honest accounts and my honest face, and they believed both, because the world does not suspect a clockmaker of anything except slowness.*

Elodie sat down on the floor of the observatory with her back against the cold walnut case and the ledger open in her lap, and she read until the light through the dome windows went from grey to gold, and she did not hear a single bell strike the whole morning through.

* * *

The town had always seemed to her a naturally kind place, the way a valley seems naturally green. People helped one another because that was simply what people here did — the way rain fell, the way bread rose, unremarkable, requiring no explanation. When the widow Baudry had somehow kept her rooms through the terrible winter of '03, Elodie had assumed charity, anonymous and diffuse, the town taking care of its own. When young men who'd quarrelled with their sergeants appeared again months later working quiet jobs in neighboring villages, she had assumed luck, or army bureaucracy, or the general fog of war into which so many things vanished harmlessly. When letters arrived from boys at the front that their mothers swore the censors must have missed, she had assumed clerical error, the post's famous mercy.

Kindness, she had believed, was weather. It happened to people.

But weather, she understood now, sitting on the dusty floor with her master's handwriting darkening her hands, weather has causes. Every warm wind comes off some warmer sea. For forty years — longer than she had been alive, longer than she had been able to imagine — Augustin Verne had been the sea beneath the town's climate, unseen, unnamed, moving everything.

He had done it out of the workshop. Out of the shop where she had swept the step and learned to file and been scolded for hurrying. He had done it between repairs, between lessons, while she was eating lunch, while she slept. He had built a hidden drawer into a public clock and filled it with the record of a second life, a life conducted in the margins of the town's attention, and the town had thanked God or fortune or the goodness of human nature, and Augustin had let them.

*A clockmaker's advantage,* he had written once, in an entry from 1915 that made her chest ache: *is that everyone assumes he deals only in time. In fact I deal in secrets, which keep better.*

She laughed. It came out of her strangely, half sob, and echoed up into the dome, and she pressed her wrist against her mouth.

Because awe was not all she felt. That was the shameful discovery of the morning, the one she turned over and over as the light moved across the floorboards. Beneath the wonder, in the place where her reverence for Augustin had lived undisturbed since she was nine years old, something had begun to shift and grind, like grit in a bearing.

He had lied to her.

Not cruelly — perhaps not even consciously. He had simply let her believe the world was simpler than it was. All those years of training, all those hours of his patient instruction in escapements and jewelling and the ethics of the bench — *never take credit, never cut corners, never let a lie live inside a movement* — and the whole time the man had run a machinery of deception so vast it had sheltered deserters and defied prefects, and he had never once, not once, shown her the drawer.

Had he doubted her? Had he thought her too young, too careful, too frightened? She was twenty-three; she had lived in his house since her father died; she had loved him the way you love gravity, without ever thinking to examine it. And he had looked at her across the workbench for fourteen years and decided — or failed to decide — that she could not be trusted with the truth of who he was.

Or worse, a voice whispered, the small precise voice she used for diagnosing faults: perhaps it was not doubt at all. Perhaps it was protection, and protection was its own kind of judgment — the judgment that she was someone to be shielded rather than enlisted, a daughter and not a partner, an apprentice forever.

She thought of all the evenings he had sent her home early. All the errands he had run alone, coming back with unexplained smudges of coal dust on his cuffs, coal-dust-smelling visitors ushered out the back before she arrived in the morning. All the times she had asked what he'd been doing and he had smiled and said, *clock business, Elodie, only clock business,* and she had laughed and believed him, because believing him was easier than being curious, and curiosity, she realized now with a hot flush, was the one tool she had never properly learned to use.

The torn page lay in her lap where she had set it apart from the ledgers, and she looked at it again.

Unlike the rest, it was not in Augustin's hand. The writing was hurried, slanted, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — someone writing fast and afraid. Most of it was numbers and abbreviations she couldn't parse, a column of figures, a date in October of this year, and phrases half-legible: *...convoy... requisition... Major R— ... signed receipt...* At the bottom, a name she did not recognize: *Luc Duret*, and beside it, underlined twice, the words *not guilty — proof exists.*

Someone had torn this page out of a third ledger — a ledger she did not have — and hidden it here. Someone besides Augustin had known about the drawer. And whoever it was had wanted this page kept safe badly enough to trust it to a dead man's clock.

The light was high now. Down in the town she could see the market square, absurdly peaceful, women queuing at Marguerite's window, a cart of cabbages, the ordinary morning of an ordinary place that had never, until this hour, existed for her at all. Somewhere beyond the eastern hills, she knew without knowing how she knew it, the war had begun to lean toward them like weather.

She should close the drawer. She should put the ledgers back, screw down the faceplate, and spend the day repairing the escapement as she had been summoned to do, and tell no one, and decide later — when grief had finished with her — what a person ought to feel about discovering that her whole life had taken place inside someone else's design.

Her hands were already moving, wrapping the green ledger, when her eye caught the last entry in the final book. It was undated. The ink was fresh enough to shine.

*If you are reading this, then I did not finish. Look for what I could not write down. And forgive me, Elodie — I always meant to teach you everything.*

He had known she would find it.

She sat very still for a long time, the entry burning in her vision, the dome ticking faintly overhead with the sounds of heat and settling, the town below her going about its innocent morning. Then, from the street far beneath the tower, came a sound that did not belong to any morning she knew: running footsteps, uneven, dragging, and a heavy stumbling blow against the workshop door below — as if someone had fallen against it, or thrown themselves upon it, or fallen out of the sky altogether.

The knock, when it came, was weak, and desperate, and repeated.
