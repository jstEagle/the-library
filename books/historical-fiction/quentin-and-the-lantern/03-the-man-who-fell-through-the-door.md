## 3. The Man Who Fell Through the Door

The road into Sainte-Aubin-le-Clocher smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone, and Quentin Marchal walked it the way a man walks when every step is a negotiation with his own body — slowly, favoring the left leg, counting doorways. He had counted forty-one since the crossroads. Forty-one doors he could have knocked on and didn't.

He had learned, over four days of walking away from the garrison at Verzy, that a door was the most dangerous thing in France. A door meant questions. A door meant a face deciding whether you were a soldier or a coward, whether you were worth bread or worth reporting. He had slept in hay barns, in a drainage culvert, once in the lee of a collapsed chapel where the Virgin's head had rolled into the nettles and stared at him all night with her plaster eyes. He had bound the wound in his calf with a shirt torn into strips, then with sacking, then with strips of the sacking, because the wound had begun to weep through everything he gave it.

And now, in the last grey light of an October evening, with rain beginning to come down in long silver threads and the wound in his leg sending up its slow red pulse of pain like a telegraph tapping out one word over and over — *fool, fool, fool* — he stood before the forty-second door.

It was not remarkable. A workshop door, wide and dark oak, set beneath a lintel carved — he leaned closer to make it out — with a lantern. The carving was old, softened by decades of weather, but someone had recently rubbed it with oil or wax so that the little flame shape caught the last light and seemed almost lit. Above it, painted letters flaking at their edges:

**A. VERNIER — HORLOGERIE & RÉPARATIONS**

Quentin put his hand against the wood. It was warm.

That was the thing that undid him, later, when he tried to explain it to himself: the door was warm, as though the house behind it were a living body, as though something inside were still breathing. He had been cold for four days. Cold down in the marrow, cold in a way that had stopped feeling like temperature and started feeling like a verdict. And this door, this ordinary door in a half-empty town on the edge of a war that everyone said was nearly finished, was warm under his palm like the flank of a horse.

He knocked. Or rather — his fist rose, hovered, and fell against the wood twice before his courage could reconsider.

From inside: footsteps. Light ones. A woman's tread, quick across boards.

Every instinct he owned screamed at him to run. His leg voted otherwise. He stayed, swaying slightly, rain running off his chin, and heard the scrape of a bolt, and the door opened onto lamplight and the smell of machine oil and beeswax and, underneath it all, faintly, absurdly, coffee.

The woman who stood there was young — younger than him by some years, perhaps twenty-two, twenty-three — with dark skin and close-cropped hair and eyes that went from his face to the bloodied wrapping on his leg and back again with a speed that told him she had already made three decisions in the space of a breath.

"You're bleeding on my step," she said.

"I'm sorry." His voice came out cracked. He hadn't spoken aloud since morning, except to beg a farmer's dog to stop barking. "I'll — I only wanted to ask—"

"Ask standing in the rain, or ask inside?"

He stared at her. In the garrison, in the army, in the whole machinery of France at war, no question was ever asked plainly like that. Every question was a trap wearing a uniform. He searched her face for the trap and found instead a kind of exhausted, wary curiosity, and behind that — he would swear to this later — something that looked like recognition. As if she had expected someone. As if she'd been leaving a lamp in the window for years on the chance of it.

"Inside," he said. "Please."

She stepped back. He crossed the threshold into warmth and light, and the door closed behind him, and the bolt slid home with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

The workshop was larger than its front suggested, deep as a church nave, and it glowed. Not brightly — the lamp on the workbench was shaded, the windows curtained against requisitioned darkness — but warmly, in the way of places tended by patient hands. Benches ran along both walls, crowded with tools hung on outlines painted directly onto the boards so that each pliers and file sat in its own pale ghost. Clocks covered every other surface: carriage clocks, mantel clocks, a tall case clock stripped to its skeleton, a wall of pocket watches each ticking at its own small insistence, so that the whole room hummed like a hive of very orderly bees.

"You can sit there." She nodded at a stool by the stove. "Don't touch anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"Everyone says that. Everyone touches something." She was already moving, pulling a basin from under a shelf, a bottle of something amber, clean cloth from a drawer that she unlocked first with a key on a ribbon around her neck. She worked quickly, competently, without tenderness, and Quentin understood that this was its own kind of mercy. Pity would have slowed her hands.

"Sit," she said again. "Trousers up. Let me see it."

He lowered himself onto the stool with a grunt he couldn't suppress, and unwound the filthy wrapping. The smell that rose made her mouth tighten but did not surprise her. The wound was ugly — a shell fragment, or part of one, had gouged across his calf two days before he deserted, and the field dressing the medics had slapped on it had done more harm than good. The flesh around it had gone the color of old parchment, streaked with red lines climbing toward the knee.

"That's infected," she said flatly.

"Yes."

"You need a surgeon."

"The surgeon is with the army I just walked away from."

Her hands stopped. She looked up at him, and the lamplight caught her eyes, and Quentin watched the arithmetic happen behind them: deserter, wounded, in my shop, asking nothing but hiding. He braced for the bolt sliding open again, for her voice calling into the street.

Instead she poured the amber liquid — spirits of wine, sharp enough to make his eyes water from a foot away — directly into the wound.

His whole leg seized. He bit down on his own wrist to keep from shouting, and the taste of his own sleeve filled his mouth, wool and gun oil and four days of road dust, and lights bloomed behind his eyes.

"Breathe," she instructed. "Again."

She cleaned it twice more, packed it with something bitter-smelling from a jar labeled in her master's handwriting — *for inflammation; use sparingly; A.V.* — and bound it in clean linen with neat, even turns. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at him properly for the first time.

"You're a soldier," she said. "But not on leave."

"No."

"And not on a mission, or you'd have shown papers."

"No papers."

"Deserter, then." She said it the way she might have said *carriage clock* or *broken mainspring* — diagnosing, not judging. "From the garrison. They requisitioned the eastern villages last week. You've come a long way on that leg."

"Three days."

"Why here?" The question came sharp now, the scalpel under the diagnosis. "Out of every door in Sainte-Aubin, why mine? Why this one?"

And here it was, the moment he had rehearsed in culverts and barns, the speech about being passing through, needing directions, having money — he had eleven francs and a button — and none of it came. Because the workshop was warm, and his leg was throbbing in time with his heart, and the clocks ticked around him like a congregation murmuring through a service, and he was so tired of lying that the truth simply stepped forward and offered itself.

"I'm looking for the lantern keeper," he said.

Something happened to the woman's face. It was small — a stillness, a kind of listening — but Quentin had spent months learning to read faces across a mess table, watching men decide whether to report a friend or cover for him, and he knew stillness when he saw it. That was not the stillness of confusion. That was the stillness of a lock recognizing a key.

"What lantern keeper?" she asked. Too evenly.

"The man who kept the lantern." His voice was failing him now, fraying at the edges. "In the tower. My friend Luc — Corporal Luc Ferrand — he grew up near here, in the valley south. Before they took him, before—" His hands had started shaking and he let them; there was no one left to impress. "Before the tribunal, he told me, if it ever went wrong, if they ever turned on him, go to Sainte-Aubin and find the lantern keeper. He said the lantern keeper keeps accounts. He said—" Quentin pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "God help me, he said, *he writes down who really does the things people get blamed for.*"

Silence. Even the clocks seemed to lean in.

When he took his hands from his eyes, the woman was standing very straight, and she was holding something in her closed right hand — he hadn't seen her pick it up, hadn't seen what it was — and her expression had gone somewhere he couldn't follow.

"Luc Ferrand," she repeated.

"He's condemned." The words tore coming out, four days of pressure behind them. "They say he let a supply convoy be stolen. They say he signed for it and lost it. They're going to stand him against a wall on Thursday and shoot him for it, and he didn't do it, and I know he didn't do it, and the proof—" He fumbled at his breast pocket, at the inner pocket he had sewn himself out of a flour sack because he trusted no issued pocket, and drew out the thing he had carried against his heartbeat for three days and nights.

A piece of paper. Torn, roughly, down one side. Yellowed at the edges, soft as cloth from handling. On it, in a fine, upright, beautiful hand — a hand that belonged in a different century, a hand that had never hurried — a column of entries in faded brown ink.

He unfolded it on the workbench between them with the care of a priest laying out relics.

"*Fourteenth of May, 1917,*" he read, haltingly, because his eyes were swimming. "*Convoy 31, flour and fodder, taken at the Verzy cut. Signed receipt recovered from the mill at Dombasle. The signature is not Ferrand's. The hand is known to me.*"

Below that, the page ended. Torn. The name — the hand that was known, the thief, the whole salvation of Luc Ferrand — was on the other half, wherever the other half had gone, and had gone years ago.

"I got this from his mother," Quentin said. "Luc sent it to her last winter, tucked in a letter. Half a page. He said the rest was kept safe here. He said the lantern keeper promised him." He looked up at her, and his voice finally broke altogether, and he let it. "Please. I've nowhere else. He's all I have, and they'll kill him Thursday, and I don't even know your name."

For a long moment she said nothing. The clocks ticked. Rain fingered the curtained windows. Somewhere far to the east, too far to be thunder, something rumbled and settled, the way the earth had learned to clear its throat.

Then she opened her closed right hand. In it lay a key — old, heavy, black iron — on a ribbon much worn.

"My name is Elodie Okafor," she said. "I'm the apprentice here. Augustin Vernier was my master." Her jaw tightened around the past tense. "The lantern keeper was my master. And he has been dead since the spring."

The word went through Quentin like a bayonet. Dead. The word rang in the warm room among all the ticking clocks, and he heard, distantly, his own breath going wrong.

"But the ledger," he managed. "The accounts. If he wrote things down—"

"They exist." She was looking at the torn page on her bench with an expression he couldn't read — grief and anger and something fiercer underneath, something that looked almost like inheritance. "Somewhere in this shop, they exist. He hid them well enough that I haven't found them yet. But I will." She folded the fragment with both hands, gently, and gave it back to him, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — steadier, lower, with an edge of steel he hadn't heard in it before. "You'll stay tonight. There's a cot behind the storeroom and soup if the stove cooperates. Tomorrow we start looking properly."

"Elodie—" He didn't know how to hold this, this sudden shelter, this stranger turning her dead master's secrets toward a condemned man she'd never met. "Why? You don't know me. You don't know Luc. The army comes looking for deserters, they shoot people for less than this—"

She carried his torn page to a drawer, thought better of it, and instead slid it into the breast pocket of her apron, over her heart, the same place he had kept it.

"Because whoever tore that page," she said quietly, "tore it from a book that was hidden inside my master's clock. Which means someone knew it was there. Which means someone wanted a name forgotten badly enough to come into this shop and do it." She blew out the lamp, and the room filled with the softer glow of the stove, and the clocks went on ticking in the dark like a thousand small hearts refusing to stop. "So sleep while you can, Quentin Marchal. Because tomorrow, one way or another, somebody is going to come knocking on this door — and I intend to know which of us they're looking for first."

Through the curtain of the window, far off across the dark fields, the sky flickered — once, twice — a silent, patient lightning, keeping its own time.

And in the workshop, above the benches and the sleeping tools, Augustin Vernier's lantern hung unlit on its hook, waiting.
