## 4. Requisition

The soldiers came on a Tuesday, which felt obscene to Elodie because Tuesdays were for winding.

She had learned the town's rhythms through its clocks: the bakery's regulator set two minutes fast so Marguerite would never burn a Tuesday batch; the church bell that ran four minutes slow and had done so for so long that the whole parish arranged its souls around the delay; the observatory master above them all, correcting itself at noon like a schoolmaster clearing his throat. Tuesdays she wound the smaller pieces in the workshop before opening the shutters — eleven of them, each with its own temperament, each demanding the particular pressure of her thumb. It was the hour when the shop smelled most of Augustin: oil and brass dust and the faint sweetness of the shellac he mixed himself, the recipe he had refused to write down because, he said, *a thing written down stops being yours*.

She was on the fourth clock — the fat carriage clock with the chipped enamel face that had belonged to no one and therefore belonged to everyone — when the sound came up the street.

Not guns. Not yet. Boots.

Elodie set down her tweezers and went to the door, and what she saw in the rue des Horlogers made her understand, in one cold drop of comprehension, everything her master had tried to teach her without ever saying it plainly. History did not arrive like weather, gradual and deniable. It arrived like this: in columns, in grey-green wool, in the measured tread of men who had been told the street was theirs now. Four hundred boots, perhaps more, striking cobbles that Augustin had walked for forty years, and every one of those footfalls saying the same word in a language older than any ledger: *ours, ours, ours.*

The town had been expecting it for a week. The requisition notices had gone up on the mairie door with their wax seals and their bureaucratic tenderness — *the army requires lodging, provisions, and use of municipal structures* — and the town had read them the way people read diagnoses, quietly, each family folding the paper into itself. But reading about a flood is different from hearing the water come up the stairs.

They moved through the square like water too, finding the low places. A signals section took the telegraph office. An ambulance column parked itself against the arcade by the café, its canvas roofs beaded with the morning's damp. Two officers stood before the observatory steps with a map spread between them, and even from across the square Elodie could see the way one of them tapped the tower — twice, decisively, the gesture of a man claiming furniture.

The observatory. The tower. Her tower, if anything could be said to belong to anyone now, with Augustin three months in the churchyard and the master clock still holding his silence in its chest.

"You'll want your coat," said Tomas from the doorway of the bell-house opposite, where he had materialized the way old men do, as if they had always been standing there. His hands, scarred from fifty years of rope-work, hung loose at his sides. "And you'll want to be polite. Politeness is a kind of armor they haven't invented a shell for."

"They're taking the tower."

"They're taking everything." He said it without bitterness, which frightened her more than bitterness would have. "Question is what we let them carry away versus what they only stand inside of. Those are different losses, girl. Learn the difference early."

* * *

By noon the observatory had become a signal post.

Elodie watched it happen from the workshop window with the helpless attention of someone watching a surgeon work on a patient who cannot be saved. Soldiers carried wire spools up the spiral stair. A mast went up beside the lantern room, lashed to the stonework with a casualness that made her flinch as though the lashings were going through her own ribs. Antenna wire trailed from the cupola down the tower's flank like a hair fallen across a beloved face. Inside — she could not see it, but she could feel it, the way she could feel a mainspring under tension through a case wall — men were moving through the instrument rooms, the chart rooms, the little round chamber where Augustin had kept his tea things and his star almanacs and the tin of sugared almonds he refilled every December for no child in particular.

And beneath all of it, behind the great dial, in the dark of the clock's movement where she had found it nine days ago wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone soft as skin: the ledger.

She had not told anyone. Not Tomas, though he had known Augustin longer than she had been alive. Not Marguerite, who brought bread on Thursdays and gossip on every other day. Certainly not Quentin, who lay in the back room with a fevered leg and half a torn page and a story that had cracked open her understanding of her dead master like a hairline crack in a balance staff — invisible until the whole escapement shuddered.

Nine days she had kept the secret, and she had believed, with the comfortable vanity of the young, that the secret was safe as long as she held it.

Then the knock came at the workshop door, and the knocking was military: three blows, evenly spaced, struck by a man who had never once in his life knocked hoping someone was home.

"Open, please."

* * *

Captain Vasseur was younger than his rank, or perhaps the war had simply eaten the years off him the way damp eats a clock case. He stood in the workshop doorway with his cap under his arm — a courtesy she would later understand was calculated, every courtesy of his was calculated — and looked around the room the way she herself looked around an unfamiliar mechanism: identifying the parts, assessing the wear, deciding what could be stripped for salvage.

His eyes went over the benches, the lathe, the wall of small tools hanging in their painted outlines like a congregation of ghosts. They went over the carriage clocks waiting for repair, over the smell of shellac, over Petit's abandoned slate still propped against the vise with a half-finished sketch of a gear train on it. They did not linger anywhere, which was how she knew he was cataloguing rather than admiring.

"Mademoiselle Okafor." He had her name already. Of course he had. "I'm Captain Vasseur, Fourth Section, Signals and Requisitions. You're the horologist."

"I'm the apprentice."

"The master is?"

"Dead since July."

Something moved behind his eyes — not sympathy exactly, but the registration of a fact that might prove useful or inconvenient. "Then the workshop answers to you. Good. That simplifies matters." He produced a folded paper, official cream stock, and laid it on the bench with the deliberation of a man laying a card face-up. "The army requires this building. The ground floor will serve as a field office and store; the upper rooms as billets for my officers. You may continue your trade in the rear workshop until—" he consulted nothing, the date simply lived in him— "until further notice. Which means," and here he looked at her directly for the first time, and she saw that his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of a sky after gas, "that you will continue your trade, because I am given to understand the observatory clock has been failing, and my superiors have taken an unaccountable fancy to the idea that the tower clock should run true while we occupy this town. Order, mademoiselle. Symbolism. The men are tired of towns where everything has stopped."

"The clock isn't failing," Elodie said. "It stopped the night my master died, and I've been rebuilding the escapement since September. It will run again. It runs to my schedule, not yours."

A pause. Somewhere outside, a sergeant's voice rose and fell, counting crates.

"You mistake me," Vasseur said mildly. "I wasn't asking permission, and I don't require your schedule. I require compliance, and I find that compliance goes best when both parties understand each other completely." He turned a slow half-circle, taking in the workshop again, and this time his gaze was not cataloguing. It was hunting. "Which brings me to the second matter. Records. In a requisitioned municipality we inventory everything — grain, livestock, vehicles, and documents. Every commercial register, every account book, every ledger in private hands must be presented for inspection within forty-eight hours. Trade continues under occupation; trade leaves traces; traces must be accounted for." His eyes came back to her and settled there with the gentle weight of a fly landing on meat. "You are a business. Businesses keep books. Where are yours?"

The word *ledger* went through her like a needle finding the exact center of a jewel bearing. She had spent nine days learning to think of it as *Augustin's book*, as *the names*, as *the debt of the dead* — anything but the ordinary, confiscatable, accountable word. She had been careless with language, and language, she understood now, standing in the smell of her master's shellac while an officer waited for her answer, was the first thing the war confiscated.

"We have a daybook," she said. "Orders, repairs, parts purchased. My master kept it. I've continued it."

"And other records? Correspondence? Accounts going back through the years?" He smiled — a small, correct, terrible smile. "Forty years of business in a town like this, mademoiselle. There will be paper. There is always paper. And paper," he added, almost tenderly, "has a way of telling stories the living would prefer stayed quiet. Better it sits in a regimental crate where no one reads it, than in a cellar where everyone imagines what it says. You'd be surprised how much trouble imagination causes."

From the back of the shop, behind the curtain that divided the workroom from the storeroom where Quentin's cot stood, came a single muffled sound — the creak of a man shifting weight off a bad leg onto a good one. Elodie's heart performed a maneuver she would afterward have admired in a mechanism: it skipped, recovered, and locked into place, running smooth.

"My storeroom," she said, pitching her voice flat and bored, "contains forty years of broken clocks. If your inventory wants them, you're welcome to the lot. The daybook I'll bring to the mairie myself, tomorrow, properly entered. I keep professional standards, Captain. Dead masters or not."

He studied her for a moment longer than comfort allowed. She had the sensation of being weighed on a precision scale, gram by gram, and understood that whatever he decided she weighed, he would remember the number.

"Tomorrow evening, then. The daybook, and any other papers of account." He put on his cap, transforming himself from a tired man into an institution. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and glanced back at the great regulator clock on the wall — the shop's own timekeeper, ticking faithfully through the invasion with the serene indifference of its kind.

"They tell me your master repaired clocks for half this town and charged half of them nothing," he said. "That's in the reports too. Generosity gets noticed, in wartime. People wonder what it buys." The pale eyes rested on her one last moment. "Keep your standards, mademoiselle Okafor. But keep them quiet."

Then the door shut, and his boots went down the rue des Horlogers, four hundred boots' worth of history marching behind them, and Elodie stood in the middle of the workshop with her hands trembling so badly she had to press them flat against the bench to stop them.

* * *

She pulled the curtain aside.

Quentin was sitting up, gray-faced, one hand still pressed to the wall where he had steadied himself, his breath coming shallow through his teeth. The fever had broken in the night and left him looking like something excavated. On the blanket beside him lay the torn half-page, its ink faded to the brown of dried blood, Luc's name visible in Augustin's careful upright script.

"He knows," Quentin said. Not a question.

"He suspects. It's worse." She sat down on the stool by the cot, suddenly unable to hold her own weight, and told him all of it — the signal post, the billets, the demand for records, the forty-eight hours, the last soft sentence about generosity getting noticed. When she finished, Quentin was silent for a long time, staring at the floorboards as though reading something written between them.

"In my battalion," he said finally, "we had a saying. The war doesn't take your house. It takes the rooms inside you, one at a time, and then it billets itself in what's left." He looked up. "This workshop was the last room. I'm sorry. I led them here."

"You fell through the door half-dead. Don't make yourself a map for their artillery." But her voice shook, because the truth was she had thought the same thing, lying awake: *the intruder brought the war with him, the way a nail brings rust.* She pushed the thought down where she pushed all her unworthy thoughts, into the case-back of herself, to be dealt with some other century. "Listen to me. Tomorrow I surrender the daybook. Real orders, real invoices, forty years of boring honesty — Augustin was scrupulous on paper, it was only off paper that he—" She stopped. Only off paper that he forgave debts, sheltered deserters, smuggled letters across a border for strangers whose names filled a hidden ledger like a congregation roll. "Only off paper that he was Augustin."

"And the ledger itself?"

Elodie rose and went to the tall-case clock at the workshop's end — Augustin's finest work, the one piece he had never sold, its hood carved with suns and moons chasing each other around a gilt face. She opened the case door. The pendulum swung in its long arc, patient as a priest, and behind the bob, screwed into the case's inner wall, waited the oilcloth packet, flat as a psalm.

"Paper tells stories the living prefer stay quiet," she repeated. "So we'll give it a quieter room." She closed the case gently, and the pendulum went on swinging, keeping its count, indifferent to empires. "Inside the clock. Where the whole town's history has been ticking all along, and no one ever thought to look."

Through the wall, faintly, came the sounds of the observatory changing hands — hammering, the rattle of wire, a voice calling measurements — and beneath it, steady as ever, the master clock's tick, slowed now, wounded, counting down toward some hour none of them could yet name.

Quentin watched her, and in his hollowed face something shifted — the look of a man deciding, again, to trust.

"Forty-eight hours," he said. "Then we find out what else they know."
