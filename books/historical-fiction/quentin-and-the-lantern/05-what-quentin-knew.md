## 5. What Quentin Knew

The pain in his side had settled into something almost companionable — a dull, insistent throb that rose and fell with his breathing, like a second heartbeat that hated him. Quentin Marchal lay on the cot in the back room of the clockmaker's workshop and listened to the sounds of a house deciding whether to trust him.

Water being drawn. A kettle set down too hard. Footsteps — hers — crossing and recrossing the floor above like a thought pacing its cage. And beneath it all, everywhere, the ticking. Dozens of clocks, no two keeping the same time, so that the whole building seemed to murmur in disagreement, a parliament of small brass voices arguing about what hour it truly was.

He should go. That was the sane arithmetic. He was a deserter with a bullet-graze under his ribs, half a page of stolen paper sewn into his tunic lining, and a name — *Marchal, Q., 4th Compagnie* — that three gendarmes would pay good money to hear spoken aloud. Every hour he stayed made the clockmaker's apprentice an accomplice. Every hour she stayed kind made her one faster.

But then he thought of Luc, and sanity became a luxury he could not afford.

Luc Beaumont, who had taught him to shave by candlelight in the first week of the war because Quentin's hands shook too badly to hold the blade himself. Luc, who carried letters for the whole company and never once read them, though he could easily have done. Luc, who stood in the mud at Rouvres with a sergeant's hand on his shoulder and a court's sentence already written behind his eyes — *theft of military stores, abandonment of post, complicity* — and looked at Quentin across the parade ground as if to say: *don't do anything foolish.*

Quentin had done nothing but foolish things since.

The door opened without a knock. Elodie Okafor came in carrying a tray — bread, a wedge of pale cheese, chicory coffee in a cup with a chip in its rim. She set it on the stool beside him with the precise, unhurried movements he was beginning to recognize as her signature. Everything she did was exact. It occurred to him that she probably repaired watches the way other people prayed.

"You've been awake all night," she said. Not a question.

"I slept."

"Your eyes say otherwise." She sat on the crate across from him, knees together, hands folded, and regarded him with the level patience of someone accustomed to diagnosing faults in mechanisms that could not speak. "You fainted in my doorway yesterday. You have a wound I dressed with the last of my iodine. You asked for a man named Augustin, who has been dead since spring. And you have not told me a single true thing since you arrived." She tilted her head slightly. "I'm going to ask you now, plainly. Who are you, and why does a ledger matter enough to bleed for?"

The bread smelled wonderful. He found, absurdly, that this nearly undid him — more than the wound, more than the fear. Warm bread in a room full of ticking clocks. The last kitchen in the world.

"If I tell you," he said, "you're in it. There's no coming out again."

"I have been in it," Elodie said quietly, "since the moment you fell through my door. The question is only whether I know what *it* is."

He looked at her hands. Small, ink-stained at the cuticles, a burn scar across the left knuckle from solder or flame. Honest-looking hands. But he had been lied to by honest-looking people before — a lieutenant with a kind face had sent his whole section into a ravine at Verdun and wept beautifully at the funeral service.

And yet. She had hidden him. She had not gone to the mayor, the priest, the police. She had stitched him up with the last of her iodine, which meant there would be none for herself if he infected, and she had done it anyway, muttering about his carelessness as if his flesh were a mechanism she resented having to fix.

"His name is Luc," Quentin said. "Luc Beaumont. We were friends before the war — he apprenticed with a cooper in Dijon, I worked my father's farm outside Auxonne. We got called up the same month. Same regiment, same compagnie, two years in the same mud." He stopped. Started again, slower. "In September, a supply convoy went missing between Rouvres and the rail head. Four wagons. Rations, boots, morphine, rifle cartridges. The sort of thing men kill for, out here."

"The sort of thing men also steal for," Elodie said.

"Yes." He met her eyes. "Yes, exactly that. Someone stole those wagons, Madame Okafor. Someone with access to manifests, authority over drivers, and a place to sell four wagonloads of army goods across a border. That is not a private's crime. That requires a uniform with braid on it."

"And they blamed your friend."

"They blamed whoever was easiest." The old fury climbed his throat; he swallowed it down like bile. "Luc was on escort duty that night. His papers were in order except for one — a movement order he'd signed for, countersigned by nobody, dated wrong. Later we learned the original order had been altered. Altered, Madame. Somebody changed the date on a piece of paper, and my friend was standing next to four empty wagons when the audit came through. The tribunal took eleven days. Eleven. They spent longer choosing the officers' mess menu."

The chip in the coffee cup caught the grey window light. Neither of them drank.

"He'll be shot," Quentin said. "After the offensive. They're holding the executions until after — bad for morale to shoot your own while the men are needed elsewhere. So there is time. Days, maybe a week. And there is exactly one way to save him, which is proof. Proof that the convoy didn't vanish. Proof that it was *taken*, and by whom, and sold where. Proof with names in it."

"And you believe Augustin kept such proof."

"I know he did." He leaned forward despite the pain, and the motion pulled the graze under his ribs tight, and he welcomed it — pain was honest, pain kept a man sharp. "Because I've seen his ledger. Half a page of it. It was brought to our company by a chaplain doing rounds — a man Augustin had helped years ago, a deserter from the last war's mobilization, sheltered right here in this town. Before he died, Augustin gave him pages torn from his own records. Names. Dates. Debts forgiven, men hidden, letters carried past censors. A whole secret history written in a clockmaker's careful hand."

Elodie had gone very still. Stillness, he was learning, was her version of another person's gasp.

"The page I carry names three men hidden in the chapel crypt during the retreat of '14," Quentin went on. "And at the bottom, in the margin, Augustin wrote a note to himself — I've read it fifty times, I know it by heart. He wrote: *"The convoy business is not finished. R—— knows I keep accounts. If anything happens to me, the full record lies where the light is kept."*"

"*Where the light is kept*," Elodie repeated.

"That's all it says. I thought — a lantern shop, a lamp works, some phrase that would mean something in this town. I came here asking after the lantern keeper because it was the only thread I had. And instead I find his apprentice, and a workshop untouched since his death, and—" He gestured helplessly at the walls, the ticking, the whole patient machinery of the house around them. "—and a town where every kindness seems to have had his fingerprints on it."

She rose abruptly. Went to the window. Stood with her back to him, and he watched her shoulders and understood that whatever she was feeling, it had arrived too large to show face-forward.

"He repaired the church clock for free," she said at last, not turning. "Everyone knew that. And he forgave debts — he'd tear up an account book page and call it a clerical error. I thought he was merely... careless with money. Generous in a disorganized way." Her voice tightened. "There is a ledger in this workshop. Behind the master clock's faceplate. I found it two nights ago. Forty years of names, Monsieur Marchal. Men hidden. Letters smuggled. Fines paid anonymously. My master was not a careless man. He was an *architect*. And I am only now discovering the building."

"Then you understand why I can't leave without it."

"No." She turned. Her eyes were dry and fierce. "I understand why you *think* you can't. But listen to me. Captain Vasseur requisitioned the observatory yesterday. He demanded all records — ledgers, registers, correspondence. Everything. When he comes here, and he will come, he will turn this workshop inside out looking for exactly the book you're describing. If he finds it in your possession, you hang. If he finds it in mine, I hang beside you, and the book hangs with us. So the question is not whether you get it. The question is where it survives."

The word *hangs* sat in the air between them. Somewhere overhead a clock struck the half-hour, off-key, apologetic.

"Where the light is kept," Quentin murmured. "The lantern. The observatory has a lantern room — the signal tower. That's why the army wants it, for heliograph work, signalling."

"Then the book stays out of both our hands until we know more." She crossed to the corner where a tall case stood under a dust sheet, and pulled the sheet away with a single decisive motion, revealing the workshop's great regulator clock — a magnificent, sober instrument, walnut case taller than a man, its pendulum hanging motionless behind glass. "Augustin built this case himself. Double-walled, he told me once, against damp. There is space inside a wall that doesn't officially exist." She looked back at him. "It's the one place in this town where a book can hide inside the very thing the army most needs me to repair. Vasseur wants the master clock working. No officer dismantles the machine he depends upon."

"It's clever," Quentin admitted.

"It's desperate," she corrected. "Which is better than clever. Clever fails gracefully. Desperate fails loudly, and loud is sometimes what saves you." She opened the case door with a key from her apron pocket — of course she had the key; of course everything in this house answered to her hands alone — and unlatched the back panel. Inside, the case was hollow as a confessional. She lifted the ledger from the drawer where she'd concealed it: a thick volume bound in oxblood leather, its corners worn round, its spine cracked with use.

For a moment she simply held it. And Quentin watched her face and saw the whole argument she was having with herself — reverence and betrayal, grief and doubt, the disciple discovering the religion was larger and darker than its scripture.

"He never told you," Quentin said gently. "Any of it."

"He told me stories. I thought they were stories." She knelt, fitted the ledger into the cavity between the case's inner and outer walls, and began securing the panel. "A man who came at midnight and left before dawn, saying Augustin had saved his life. A woman who wept over a torn page. I thought my master collected strays." The screws went in, small and final, each one a tiny act of conspiracy. "Perhaps I did too. Perhaps that's what this workshop is. A trap for strays, disguised as a trade."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She closed the case, straightened, and dusted her palms as though she'd merely finished a routine adjustment. "Tell me everything about this Major — the one you think stole the convoy. Names, dates, regiments. If we're going to find his name in forty years of Augustin's handwriting, I need to know what shape it takes."

"Renard," Quentin said. "Major Renard. Supply command, third division."

Something crossed Elodie's face — quick, involuntary, quickly mastered. But not quickly enough.

"You know him," Quentin said, sitting up too fast, the wound flaring white at the edge of his vision. "Madame Okafor. You *know* that name."

"He dined at the observatory last night," she said slowly. "With Captain Vasseur. He is on Vasseur's own staff, Quentin. He inspected the workshop. He stood in this room." Her hand came to rest, quite unconsciously, on the walnut flank of the clock case — on the wall where the ledger now breathed in the dark, an inch from the man's own fingerprints. "And tomorrow morning, I have been ordered to begin repairs on the master clock. In the observatory. Under the supervision of the army. Under the supervision, if he wishes it, of *Renard*."

Outside, far to the east, the guns resumed their low conversation, and every clock in the house seemed to tick a little faster, as if they too understood how little time remained.

* * *

They worked by lamplight with the shutters closed, because lamplight through shutters was legal and lamplight through open windows was a shell-target, and because what they were doing had begun to feel illegal even when it wasn't.

Elodie spread Augustin's tools on the bench with liturgical care — files, pliers, the jeweler's loupe, the box of escapements sorted into paper twists labelled in his cramped hand. Quentin watched from the cot, too weak to help and too restless to lie still, and catalogued the room the way he'd once catalogued terrain: exits, cover, lines of sight. Two doors. One window, shuttered. A cellar hatch beneath the rug. A town that would be full of soldiers by week's end.

"Why did you really stay?" Elodie asked suddenly, not looking up from the mainspring she was cleaning. "When I gave you every reason to run. Most men would have run."

"Most men would have run straight into a patrol." He flexed his bandaged side experimentally. "And besides." He hesitated. The true answer felt too large and too soft to handle with words.

"Besides?" she prompted, still not looking up.

"Besides, you hid the ledger before I even asked you to. You didn't weigh it, or bargain, or make me swear oaths. You just — decided. Like pulling a lever." He laughed shortly, without humor. "Do you know what that's worth to a man who's spent two years watching everyone calculate? Sergeants calculating how many men a position costs. Lieutenants calculating glory. Courts calculating whether my friend's life balances against an officer's inconvenience. Everyone calculating, always. And then I fall through a stranger's door bleeding, and within a day she hides a book that could hang her, and doesn't even charge me for the iodine." He shook his head. "I stayed because for the first time since the war started, I'm in a room where the arithmetic includes mercy."

The mainspring stilled under her fingers. For a long moment the only sound was the parliament of clocks, arguing their small eternal arguments.

"My mother was from Accra," Elodie said at last, quietly, to the lamplight rather than to him. "My father a French railway engineer. When she died, there were people in this town who would not serve me in their shops. Augustin hired me anyway. Told anyone who objected that good hands had no color, only competence." She resumed her work, briskly, as if the confession were merely another component to be seated. "So you see, I already owed the ledger a debt before I ever read it. Hiding it isn't courage, Monsieur Marchal. It's payment."

"Quentin," he said. "If we're going to conspire, it shouldn't be as strangers."

She glanced up. Something almost like a smile touched the corner of her mouth — brief, unpracticed, quickly reassembled into professional composure. "Quentin, then. Elodie." She nodded toward the covered clock case. "Now sleep. At dawn I go to the observatory, and I will need to walk in there looking like a woman with nothing heavier on her mind than a broken escapement. Which means one of us has to actually rest."

He lay back. The pain throbbed its slow accompaniment. Above him the clocks ticked on, dozens of hearts in walnut and brass, keeping their separate times — and somewhere inside the tallest of them, forty years of rescued lives waited in the dark for the light to be kept.

Sleep came for him at last, shallow and dream-riddled. And in the dreams, as in the waking hours, one phrase recurred, turning over and over like a gear with a broken tooth:

*Where the light is kept.*

By morning he would learn what it cost to go looking.
