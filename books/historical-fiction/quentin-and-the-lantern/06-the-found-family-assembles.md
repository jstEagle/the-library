## 6. The Found Family Assembles

By the third day of the requisition, the town had learned to walk differently.

Elodie noticed it on her way back from the observatory that morning — the way the few remaining civilians kept to the walls of buildings rather than the centers of streets, as though the cobblestones themselves might betray them. Soldiers moved in pairs now, their boots striking a rhythm too disciplined for these lanes built by mule-drivers and millers. Somewhere beyond the eastern ridge, where the hills folded down toward the river, the guns had begun again at dawn, and their sound arrived not as thunder but as a kind of pressure, a hand laid flat against the chest.

She carried bread under her shawl. Marguerite had pressed it into her hands at the bakery door without a word, which was itself remarkable, because Marguerite had never in her life given away bread without a word. The loaves were dark and heavy, baked with the last of the good flour before the army's quartermasters had inventoried even that, and they smelled of woodsmoke and patience. Elodie held them against her ribs like something living.

The workshop door stood open when she reached it. That was wrong. She always latched it; Augustin had taught her that a workshop left open was a workshop already half-looted. Her hand went to her apron pocket, to the little brass rule she kept there more for comfort than use, and she stepped inside ready to find a soldier's hands in Augustin's drawers.

Instead she found Marguerite, already seated at the workbench nearest the stove, and old Tomas standing by the window with his cap in his hands, and Petit — the boy-soldier, sixteen at most, with a messenger's armband and mud to his knees — perched on an upturned crate near the door, watching the street through the gap in the shutters.

"We let ourselves in," Marguerite said, in the tone of a woman who considered permission a formality between friends. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, with flour worked so deeply into the creases of her knuckles that no washing ever quite reclaimed them. "I hope you don't mind."

"I do mind," Elodie said, setting down the bread. "But I notice you've brought your own chairs, so I suppose you intended to stay regardless."

Tomas smiled at that — a slow, creased unfolding, like paper left too long folded. He was seventy if he was a day, bell-ringer of Saint-Aubin since before Elodie was born, with forearms like mooring rope and a silence about him that was not emptiness but fullness, the way a bell is full after it has been struck. "Marguerite says we're having a meeting," he said.

"A meeting," Elodie repeated. "Of what?"

"Of us," said Petit from the crate. He had a boy's voice still, unbroken at its edges, and he said it with such simple certainty that Elodie found herself without an answer.

* * *

She made coffee from the grounds she'd stretched three times already, because there was nothing else to make, and because making it gave her hands something to do while she tried to understand what was happening in her own workshop. Marguerite had brought sugar — actual sugar, hoarded, illicit — and dropped two lumps into each cup with the defiance of a woman committing small treasons daily. Tomas drank his black and held the tin cup in both palms as though warming himself at it, though the stove was going.

Outside, a column of trucks ground past toward the ridge. The windowpanes shivered in their frames. Nobody spoke until the sound had gone.

"They posted notices this morning," Marguerite said finally. "At the mairie and the fountain. All civilian stores to be registered by Friday. Tools, stock, fuel — everything listed, everything subject to seizure for army use." She looked around the workshop, at the benches and the lathe, at the wall of small drawers each labeled in Augustin's upright hand. "Including this place."

"The captain told me," Elodie said. "Vasseur. He wants the master clock running for their signal work, and he wants—" she paused, choosing, "—he wants all records turned over. Ledgers, correspondence, anything written."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That a clockmaker keeps accounts of parts. Springs, escapements, mainsprings sold and fitted. Nothing else."

Marguerite nodded slowly, and Elodie understood, with a small cold drop in her stomach, that Marguerite knew exactly which ledger was meant. Not how — she could not know how — but that it existed. That it had always existed, the way you know a house has a cellar even if you've never been down the stairs.

"You knew him," Elodie said. "You knew what he kept."

"I knew what he *did*," Marguerite corrected. "The ledger is just the shadow the doing casts." She turned her cup on the bench, half a turn, another. "In 'seventeen, my husband Henri was taken up for desertion. Except he hadn't deserted — he'd been granted a harvest furlough by a lieutenant who then died at Verdun, and the paperwork died with him. They were going to shoot him for it. A formality, really. Six men, a wall behind the powder magazine." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb pressed white against the tin cup. "Augustin Okafor walked into the colonel's office with a document in his hand. Where he got it, or who wrote it, I never learned. But it was a furlough authorization, countersigned and dated correctly, and it was enough. Henri came home on a Thursday and died of the influenza the following winter, and I have thought every day since that God's mercy runs strange channels, but they run."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, faint through the ceiling, the great wheels of the observatory clock turned through their patient arithmetic.

"He never asked me for anything," Marguerite went on. "Never once. But he'd come into the bakery on cold mornings and I'd give him the end loaf, the one that wouldn't sell, and he'd accept it like it was payment. Like we were settling accounts." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "And now soldiers are going to go through his things with bayonets for letter-openers, and I will not sit in my kitchen and let it happen. So. What do you need, Elodie? Bread, flour, a hiding place, a loud voice, a quiet one? Name it."

* * *

It was Tomas who spoke next, after a silence long enough that Elodie had almost begun to frame an answer.

"She'll need the tower," he said.

Everyone looked at him. He stood by the window still, cap turning slowly in his rope-hard hands, and he did not look at any of them but out at the square, where the fountain threw its thin autumn water and a sentry paced his twenty steps.

"The clock in the observatory is the captain's concern," Tomas said. "But Saint-Aubin's bells are mine, and mine alone — they haven't requisitioned an old man's bells yet, because an old man's bells are beneath requisitioning. If there are things that must move, or messages that must pass, or times when folk must simply *know* something without a word being spoken—" he tapped his temple, "—there are ways. There have always been ways. The bells speak a grammar older than any of these officers' grandfathers. Three strokes and a pause means grief. Two quick and one slow means fire. And there are combinations the army has never heard, because I invented them myself during the Prussians, God rot them, and I remember every one."

"You'd ring false signals," Elodie said. "For us."

"I'd ring true ones," Tomas said. "That's the difference. Their signals are the false ones. Mine say what's actually happening." He settled his cap back onto his head with the finality of a man closing a door. "Augustin rang the bells with me twice in my life. Once for the armistice rumor in 'sixteen that turned out to be lies, and once — quietly, at four in the morning, just the two of us and the ropes — when the first list of dead boys came in 'fourteen, before the town crier was allowed to read it. He wanted the town to hear it in bronze before it heard it in print. Said grief should get the news first, so it would be sitting down waiting for them instead of jumping out at them." His jaw worked. "I owe him the same courtesy now, whatever comes."

Petit shifted on his crate. He had been silent so long that Elodie had nearly forgotten him, except that forgetting him was impossible — he had that quality of boys in uniform, of being simultaneously too young and too old, his face still soft and his eyes already filed flat by what they'd seen. Now he pulled from inside his tunic a folded paper, worn soft at the creases, and unfolded it on the bench with the care of a priest handling relics.

"My orders," he said. "From Captain Vasseur's adjutant. I'm to report movements. Civilians leaving, civilians gathering, strangers, unusual stockpiles." He swallowed. "Strangers in workshops."

The room went very still. Even the stove seemed to hold its crackle.

"And have you seen strangers?" Marguerite asked carefully.

"No," Petit said. Then, after a breath: "That's the first lie I've ever told anyone wearing this armband. I wanted to practice saying it out loud, in front of people, so it would be easier the next time." He looked at Elodie directly, and his chin trembled once and steadied. "Because I know there's someone here who shouldn't be. I'm not stupid. There's bread for six and I've counted five of us. And the shutters on the back room were re-hung backwards — the hinge pins are on the outside now, and they weren't before, I notice things like that, it's why they made me a runner. I haven't looked. I don't want to look. I only want you to know that whatever I'm ordered to write in my reports, I'll write what keeps everyone safe. Because—" his voice cracked, and he pushed through it, sixteen years old and choosing his side in front of witnesses, "—because my name is in the book."

Elodie felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs.

"You've read it," she said quietly.

"He read it to me," Marguerite said. "Years ago. After Henri. He got me drunk on plum brandy and read me the page with my name on it, and I cried, and he said, 'Don't cry, Marguerite, it's not sentimental, it's accounting. A town is just debts and credits, and most people never learn which column they're in.' " She laughed, wetly. "He was insufferable. I miss him so much I can hardly breathe some mornings."

"Tomas too?" Elodie asked.

The old man shook his head. "Not me. He said my page was blank because I'd never needed rescuing." A pause, and something flickered behind his eyes, old and private. "Though he wrote letters for me sometimes. To my son. In the prison camp. I couldn't write myself, and the censors at the post office were thieves of everything with feeling in it. Augustin's versions got through when mine never would have. He'd take my stumbling words and make them walk straight. So maybe there's a line about that somewhere, if lines exist for small kindnesses. Maybe that's what the whole book is — small kindnesses, entered in columns, compounding interest for fifty years." He looked around the room, at the flour-white baker and the boy-soldier and the apprentice clockmaker, and something in his seamed face softened into wonder. "Look at us. Look what the interest bought."

* * *

They worked out the arrangements as afternoon came down gray over the rooftops.

Marguerite would bring bread daily — ostensibly delivering to the army mess, whose route passed the workshop door, a cover so ordinary it was nearly invisible. Tomas would keep his bells and his grammar of strokes, and would climb to Elodie's window ledge each evening at dusk to leave a chalk mark: a single line if all was well, a cross if it was not, and they agreed without discussion never to ask what the cross would mean. Petit would continue his reports, bland and useless, a stream of nothing that would teach the adjutant that the town was empty and dull and unworthy of attention. "Boredom," the boy said gravely, "is the best blanket. Nobody searches under a blanket they think is just a blanket." He said it as though quoting someone; perhaps he was quoting Quentin, though none of them said the deserter's name aloud, not with the shutters open to the street.

And the ledger — Elodie confirmed to them only its existence, not its location, and they had the grace not to press — would stay where it was, in the dark and the oil-smell and the tick of brass, until they could decide what it was for.

"It's not enough to hide it," Marguerite said, wrapping herself back into her shawl as the light failed. "Hiding is just losing slower. Augustin didn't keep that book hidden. He kept it *ready*. There's a difference. Hidden things are for cowards; ready things are for whoever comes next."

"Who comes next," Tomas murmured, looking at Elodie.

"Don't," Elodie said. "Don't put it on me. I'm an apprentice. I mend escapements. I don't—"

"You walked into the observatory and found his book," Marguerite said simply, pulling on her gloves finger by finger. "Out of everyone in this town, dead and living, the book chose you. Or he chose you, which comes to the same thing. We're not putting anything on you, girl. We're telling you you're not carrying it alone. That's different. That's the whole difference."

They left in ones and twos, spaced apart like conspirators in a bad play, Marguerite first with her empty basket, then Tomas shuffling off toward the belfry, then Petit last, pausing at the door to touch his cap brim with a solemnity that sat strangely and movingly on his young face.

"Mademoiselle Okafor," he said. "If it matters. In my report tonight I'm writing that the workshop contains one clockmaker, working late, unsociable. Unsociable is good. Unsociable people don't have visitors."

Then he was gone, small and straight-backed against the gray, and Elodie latched the door behind him and stood with her forehead against the wood.

* * *

The workshop in the lamplight, afterward, felt different than it had a week ago. It was the same room — the same benches, the same hundred labeled drawers, the same smell of oil and iron and the ghost of Augustin's pipe tobacco worked into the very grain of the wood — but it was warmer, and she knew the warmth wasn't the stove. For three days since finding the ledger she had felt like a trespasser in her own inheritance, a girl holding a stranger's secret. Now the secret had edges she could grip. It had weight she could share.

She took down the ledger and set it on the bench, and opened it not to Luc's torn pages, not to the dangerous middle of the book, but to the earliest entries, Augustin's young handwriting, decades old: *Paid the doctor for the Vasseau child, 1889. Told his father it was a legacy from his grandmother.* And below it, in the margin, a note in smaller script: *Some mercies must wear disguises, or the proud die of them.*

She ran her thumb along the ink and understood, finally, what Marguerite had meant. This was not a record to be buried. It was a tool to be finished. And tools, in this house, were meant to be used.

A knock — soft, coded, three and then two — sounded at the shutter of the back room, and Elodie closed the book and slid it home into the pendulum case before she crossed to answer it.

Quentin stood in the dark of the yard, hood up, one hand pressed to the bandage at his side, and even before he spoke she saw in his face that something had broken open in the hours since she'd last seen him.

"They've moved him," he said, barely above a whisper. "Luc. They've moved him to the guardhouse cell, and the court-martial convenes Monday. Elodie — we have four days."
