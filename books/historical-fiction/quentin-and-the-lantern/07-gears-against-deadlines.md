## 7. Gears Against Deadlines

The army arrived at the observatory each morning at seven o'clock precisely, which Elodie found, in her darker moments, darkly funny. They had seized a town, requisitioned its flour and its horses and its church bell rope, and yet they kept time the way Augustin had taught her to: with an almost devotional punctuality. Two signalmen climbed the tower steps at seven sharp, boots ringing on iron treads, and by seven-ten they were tapping their coded messages into the morning fog while she knelt below them in the clock room with her sleeves rolled and her hands in the movement's brass heart.

"Mademoiselle Okafor." Sergeant Brillier — the younger of the two, a farm boy from somewhere near Amiens with a permanent look of apology on his face — would duck his head into the clock room each morning. "Anything we can carry for you? Tools? Oil?"

"No, thank you, Sergeant."

"You're sure? The captain said anything you need."

What she needed was for them all to go away, but there was no form on which one could request that, so she smiled instead — the small professional smile Augustin had worn for customers who asked if a repair might be done *by Friday* — and said, "A steady hand is all I need, and I brought my own."

Brillier would laugh as though this were wit rather than truth, and withdraw, and she would be alone with the clock.

It had become a strange arrangement, this double life of hers. By day she was the army's clockmaker, restoring their "symbol of order," as Captain Vasseur had called it with that flat officer's voice that made even poetry sound like requisition paperwork. The master clock's great wheels rose around her like the ribs of some patient whale, and she worked among them under military supervision, oiling pivots, truing escapes, filing a new detent for the striking train. The signalmen above her head tapped out the war in dots and dashes. Sometimes the whole tower shivered when a shell landed far off to the east — a soft concussion felt more in the teeth than the ears — and the men upstairs would pause, count seconds, and resume.

By night she hunted.

* * *

The workshop below was hers again after dark, once the sentry on the square had made his rounds and the blackout shutters were drawn. She worked by candlelight shielded behind tin, the way Marguerite had shown her, because Marguerite knew about light and what it invited from the sky. On the bench, under her hands, lay the ledger.

She had learned its geography by now. Three decades of Augustin's small, upright script, so unlike his speaking voice — he had talked in flourishes, but he wrote like a man counting. Names in the left column. Debts in the middle. And in the right column, always, a resolution: *forgiven*, *paid in kind*, *carried forward*, or simply a date and a single word, *done*, whose meaning shifted with the entry. For some names, *done* meant money quietly returned. For others it meant a letter carried across the border inside a clock case. For one entry from 1915 — *Tomas B., bell-rope, done* — she still did not know what had been done, only that Tomas had gone very quiet when she'd mentioned it, and had looked at his own hands for a long moment before saying, "He never told you what for?" and refusing to say more.

But the ledger was not whole. She had known that since the first night Quentin had laid his torn half-page beside it and the edges had matched like broken bone. Someone had cut entries out. Not torn — cut, with a razor or a fine knife, close along the gutter, so cleanly that at first glance the binding looked intact. Only the ghost of ink transfer remained on the facing page: the reversed shadow of handwriting pressed through paper long removed.

Quentin sat across from her most nights now, his shoulder healing crooked under Marguerite's poultices, and together they read the ghosts. He held the pages toward the candle; she tilted them until the light raked sideways and the transferred strokes stood up like grass after frost. Then she copied what she could decipher into the margins of her own notebook — backwards letters, half words, the arithmetic of debts she could reconstruct from the surviving side.

"It's like listening through a wall," Quentin said the third night, frustration cracking his whisper. "You know someone's talking. You can't make out the name."

"We'll make it out." She surprised herself with how certain she sounded. Certainty was not something she had ever owned before this autumn. It had been issued to her, apparently, along with the grief and the ledger and the man bleeding on her floor.

They were hunting one entry in particular. Luc Marchand — Quentin's friend, the condemned man — had been charged in September with the theft of a supply convoy: two lorries of grain and morphine that had vanished between the railhead and the front. The court-martial had needed a thief, and Luc, whose signature was on the wrong manifest at the wrong hour, had been convenient. But Quentin swore — had crossed a country half-shot to swear — that Luc had been at the depot gate when the lorries rolled out, that he'd reported the discrepancy himself, that the report had vanished along with the convoy. Somewhere in Augustin's records, if Augustin had recorded what Augustin seemed to have recorded everything else, there would be the other name. The true one.

"He wrote down everyone," Quentin said. "You told me. Debtors, deserters, smugglers. A man like that wouldn't leave out an army major walking stolen grain through his town."

"A man like that also got dead," Elodie said, and the sentence sat between them, ugly and true, until the candle guttered and they relit it and went back to work.

* * *

The days acquired a rhythm that Elodie would later recognize as the rhythm of siege: work by daylight, hope by candlelight, sleep as a rumor. Mornings at the observatory under the eyes of the signalmen. Afternoons in the workshop, ostensibly servicing the army's confiscated stock of watches — Vasseur wanted every timepiece in town synchronized to the tower, "so the garrison moves as one instrument," and so Elodie sat amid dozens of open cases, regulating movements to a standard she privately considered barbarous. Evenings, the family gathered: Marguerite bringing bread that grew darker and denser as the requisitioning tightened, Tomas arriving with wood for the stove and news decoded from his own bells, Petit slipping in at dusk with messages from the few households that remained, his uniform too big and his face trying too hard to be older than sixteen.

And then the household would sleep, and the hunt resumed.

She began to understand Augustin differently in those hours. Before, she had loved him the way an apprentice loves a master — with reverence shaped like gratitude, gratitude shaped like debt. Now she read his hand night after night and saw the labor of it. This was not a diary. It was work, years of it, hours stolen from sleep the way she stole hers now. Every forgiven debt had been a decision. Every hidden deserter had been a night of fear exactly like these nights. He had built the town's kindness the way she was rebuilding the clock: gear by gear, in the dark, trusting that the whole mechanism would hold.

Her doubt from those first days — *what else didn't I know about him?* — had not vanished, but it had changed shape. It was no longer awe curdling into suspicion. It was more like standing in his workshop and realizing the tools were not relics. They were invitations.

One night near the end of October she found something.

She had taken to working through the ledger backward, from 1918 toward its beginning, on the theory that whatever had gotten Augustin killed lived near the end. The ghost-pages clustered in the last three years, six cut entries, maybe seven, each leaving its pale negative in the binding. She was tilting a page against the candle — the light low, nearly horizontal, the way that made the ink-shadows rise — when a word surfaced that was not a word but a shape she knew: *convoy*.

Her breath fogged the paper. She wiped it with her sleeve, furious at her own body, and held the page steady against the bench vise.

*Convoy.* Below it, fragmentary: *two lorries… gate log… R—*. The rest dissolved into the grain of the paper, letters like fish seen through ice. *R—*. A beginning. Not enough. Not nearly enough. But proof — the first hard proof — that Augustin had written down the convoy at all, that the story Quentin had carried across France was a story Augustin had already heard and already recorded, and that someone had wanted the recording destroyed badly enough to use a razor.

She woke Quentin. He came down the stairs with his coat over his shoulders, moving carefully around his wound, and bent over the page for a long time without touching it.

"R," he said finally. "Renard. Renard, Rousseau, Rivière—" He straightened, wincing. "Half the officers in this army start with R."

"Then we narrow it." She pulled her notebook toward her. "Augustin indexed. Look — the ledger has a key in the back cover, initials matched to trade and street. If the full entry named him, the index may have survived where the page didn't. Cutters take pages. They don't think about indexes."

The back cover's pocket held a folded card, yellowed, dense with columns. Her finger ran down it while Quentin held the candle. *L. Martin, wheelwright, rue du Four. H. Delacroix, curé. T. Bresson, bell-ringer.* And then, near the bottom, in Augustin's smallest hand, an entry with no street and no trade, only initials and a rank:

*R., Major, supply — see 1918.*

"See 1918," Quentin breathed.

"See the pages that aren't here anymore." She closed the notebook. Outside, far to the east, the artillery spoke twice, a question and an answer, and both of them flinched and pretended they hadn't. "We don't have the name yet. But we have a trail. Augustin left us a trail."

Quentin looked at her across the candle flame, and what was in his face was not gratitude exactly — it was fiercer than that, closer to hunger. "Why?" he said. "Why are you doing this? You could burn the thing. Give Vasseur the empty book. Keep your head down until the shooting stops. Everyone would understand."

Elodie thought about the honest answer, which was complicated, and gave him a piece of it. "Because for twenty-six years I believed history happened somewhere else. To other people. In capitals, in trenches, to men with ranks. My job was to keep the clocks running so the people history happened to would know what time it was." She turned the ledger gently, the way she'd turn a movement on the bench. "I'm done keeping time for other people's histories."

* * *

Captain Vasseur appeared at the observatory on Thursday, unannounced, as he preferred to do.

She heard his tread on the tower stair — unhurried, deliberate, a man who wanted to be heard coming — and had time to sweep her notebook under a cloth before he filled the doorway of the clock room. He stood a moment looking up at the great wheel she had trued that morning, and something in his posture, some tired tilt of the shoulders, suggested a man visiting a church rather than inspecting a worksite.

"The striking train," he said. "How long?"

"Three days for the escapement. Five for the strike, if the lift wires need replacing. They do." She wiped her hands on the rag, deliberately slowly. "You'll have your symbol of order, Captain. Clocks don't hurry any more than they delay."

"Everything hurries, Mademoiselle Okafor. Some things merely pretend otherwise." He came further into the room and touched the frame of the movement — lightly, with two fingers, the way men touch things they've been ordered to respect. "There's talk in the staff mess that the armistice is close. Days, perhaps. When it comes, every ear in France will be listening for bells. I intend for this town's bells to be ready." His eyes moved over her workbench, the tools, the cloth-covered notebook, and rested there perhaps a second longer than curiosity required. "You keep notes on the restoration?"

"Every clockmaker does. Parts replaced, adjustments made. Otherwise the next repairer inherits mysteries."

"Mysteries." He weighed the word. "Yes. Well. We have quite enough of those." At the door he paused, back still turned. "My adjutant tells me you requested lamp oil beyond your ration. Twice."

"For night work. The strike train won't adjust itself, and your signalmen have the tower by day."

"I approved the second request myself," Vasseur said, and was gone down the stairs, his boot-falls measured as a pendulum.

Elodie stood in the cold of the clock room, heart knocking oddly against her ribs. He had approved it himself. Which meant either the army's captain was generous, or the army's captain wanted to know what she did in the lamplight, and had decided watching was cheaper than forbidding.

That night she moved the ledger. Not far — there was nowhere far to move it, in a town turning into a barracks — but deeper. She opened the clock's tall case below the pendulum, the space Augustin had built with a false panel she'd discovered at fifteen and never asked about, and she laid the book inside on a bed of clock wool, and closed the panel, and hung the pendulum swinging above it like a slow silver heart beating over a secret. If Vasseur wanted her nights, let him watch her mend gears. Let the army fund its own surveillance of a woman doing precisely what they'd ordered her to do.

Petit found her latching the case, sent to fetch her for supper. He watched her with quick bright eyes and asked no questions, which was itself a kind of growing up.

"Marguerite says there's stew," he reported. "Real meat. She traded for it and won't say with what, and Tomas says that means it's best not to ask, and Quentin says he'll eat first and repent after."

"Then we'd better not keep it waiting." She blew out the candle. In the dark, faintly, the tower above them ticked — the great escapement she'd restored that afternoon, holding its count through the cold, patient as a promise. Three days to the escapement's finish. Five to the strike. Perhaps fewer than that to the armistice, if the staff mess gossip was worth anything.

And somewhere in the gap between those numbers — between the clock's readiness and the war's end — was the window in which a name could still save a man's life.

She took Petit's arm in the dark, and they climbed the stairs toward the smell of stew and the low murmur of the family's voices, while overhead, unseen, the wheels turned and counted and refused, as she had promised the Captain, to hurry.
