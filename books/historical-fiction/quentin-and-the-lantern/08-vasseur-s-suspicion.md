## 8. Vasseur's Suspicion

Captain Étienne Vasseur had learned long ago that a town told you everything by what it kept behind glass.

He stood in the workshop on the rue des Forgerons with his gloves off—unusual for him, even in November—and let his eyes move over the room the way he'd been trained to move them over enemy trenches: slowly, in sections, noting what was where and what was not. The army's requisition order had been posted three days ago, pasted to the door beside Augustin Okafor's old business sign, and since then the town of Saint-Aubin-le-Haut had begun the process of becoming something else—a garrison, a depot, a staging ground for the offensive that would end the war or end them all. The paperwork of that transformation passed across Vasseur's desk every morning in triplicate. Requisitions of grain. Requisitions of horses. Requisitions of beds, of bells, of time itself.

The clockmaker's shop had been on the list from the first week. Not for its contents—for its man. A town's conscience is a strategic asset, someone at division had written, without irony, in a memorandum Vasseur still carried folded in his breast pocket. And when the conscience died, the asset passed to whoever held the keys.

Elodie Okafor held the keys now.

She was at the workbench with her back to him, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her dark hands moving among the brass with a certainty that unsettled him more than any defiance would have. Defiance he knew how to answer. Competence in a woman of twenty-three, in an apprentice who should have been weeping into her apron or fleeing down the road like half the town, was harder to classify. She did not look up when he entered. She had not looked up yesterday either.

"Mademoiselle Okafor."

"Captain." Her voice was level. She turned a screw a quarter-turn and set the driver down with the precision of a surgeon laying aside a scalpel. "The escapement will take another week. Perhaps ten days. I told your sergeant."

"You told my sergeant about the clock. I'm here about the books."

Now she looked up. Good. He wanted her eyes. Eyes were documents too, and he had spent four years learning to read them under worse light than this.

"The books are on the shelf," she said, nodding toward the corner cabinet. "As you requested. Master Augustin's commercial ledgers, forty years of them. Invoices, repairs, purchases. Very dull reading, Captain. You'll find no troop movements in the margins."

"I read dull things professionally," Vasseur said, and took down the oldest volume.

* * *

He had begun with the ledgers two nights ago, after the men were asleep, in the observatory's signal room with a lamp turned low so the glow wouldn't draw fire. It was there, in the smell of cold iron and lamp oil, that he had found the thing that had brought him back to this workshop with his gloves off.

Augustin Okafor had been a meticulous bookkeeper. That much was evident within minutes—the entries ruled true, the sums carried forward without error, dates in a small unvarying hand that never hurried and never slanted. Forty years of repair invoices for the church clock, the convent, the mill, the mayor's watch. Forty years of a life conducted in columns.

And every hundredth page, Vasseur discovered, was numbered twice.

It was a small thing. Page 100 bore the numeral 100 in the corner, as expected—and beneath it, faintly, the ghost of another figure, struck through but not fully effaced, as though the number had been written once, corrected, and written again. He checked. Page 200 showed the same doubling. Page 300. Page 400. As though the bookkeeper had planned his pagination against some other count and then, somewhere along the years, quietly abandoned it—or renumbered around it.

He sat with that for a long while before he understood what he might be looking at.

A ledger that has been rebound. A ledger whose original contents have been thinned—pages removed and replaced, or removed and left absent, the remaining leaves redistributed so the seams wouldn't show. He counted signatures. The gatherings didn't match. Somewhere between page 90 and 110, the paper changed weight by a fraction—not enough for a casual reader, but enough for a man who had spent the war reading supply manifests and learning, bitterly, exactly how paper lied.

There were other absences. A repair dated March 1899 for a job he could find no corresponding invoice for. A gap of eleven days in October 1907 where the daily entries simply stopped, resuming as though nothing had happened, though the ink of the resumed entry was a slightly different black. And once—only once—an entry that made no commercial sense at all:

*Repaired lantern, no charge. Repaired again.*

No client named. No mechanism described. Just that, alone on its line, in the same patient hand.

Vasseur had closed the ledger and sat in the dark listening to the wind move through the observatory's broken louvres, and he had thought: *this man was keeping two sets of accounts.* One for money. One for something else. And whatever the second account recorded, someone had already begun erasing it—before the army ever came, before the war, perhaps years before. Someone had gotten to Augustin Okafor's secrets early, and carefully, and had missed almost nothing.

Almost.

Because this morning, going through the newer volumes with a magnifying glass and the patience of a man who had once spent six weeks identifying a forged ration chit, he had found a stub. Near the back of the 1916 ledger, low in the gutter of the binding where the blade had cut carelessly in the dark or the haste, a triangle of paper remained attached to the spine—a fragment no larger than a thumbnail, bearing the bottom halves of two lines of writing:

*…llet, convoy of 14 June, remitted…*

*…name entered at his own request…*

Convoy. The word went through Vasseur like a bayonet finding the seam in a vest.

He knew about convoys. Every officer above the rank of lieutenant knew about convoys, and knew which ones could be spoken of and which could not, and the convoy of 14 June 1916 was of the second kind—a supply train out of the railhead that had vanished between one way station and the next, and for which a private soldier had been tried and condemned while the officers whose signatures dotted the route had developed a collective, convenient blindness. Vasseur had not signed anything in that affair. He had merely been close enough to smell it, and had said nothing, and had carried the saying-nothing with him ever since, the way a man carries shrapnel too near the heart to remove.

He had told himself then what he told himself now: *the machinery is larger than you; you are a cog; a cog does not audit the engine.* He had been telling himself that for four years, and each time it required more force, like a door swelling shut in wet weather.

* * *

"You're staring at my shelf," Elodie Okafor said.

Vasseur realized he had been standing with the 1916 ledger open in his hands for the better part of a minute, and she was watching him now—had perhaps been watching him the whole time—with an expression he could not immediately name. Not fear. Something adjacent to fear but older, more settled, like the expression of someone who has watched storms come over the same ridge every autumn of her life and no longer runs for shelter.

"I'm admiring your master's hand," he said. "Forty years of accounts and not an error in the carrying."

"He'd have corrected you. He made errors. He just never let them stay."

"No," Vasseur agreed softly. "I imagine he didn't."

He turned the ledger toward her, open to the stub in the gutter. He watched her face with the whole of his attention, the way he had once watched a treeline for the flash of a lens. What he saw was small and fast and damning: the pupils contracting, the breath caught and released through the nose, the hands going still on the bench—not reaching, deliberately not reaching, the stillness of restraint rather than calm.

"This fragment," he said. "*Convoy of 14 June.* Your master repaired clocks. What business did a convoy have in his books?"

"A convoy delivers supplies. Supplies include oil, wire, brass. Clockmakers buy brass." Her voice was perfectly steady. It was a very good performance. It would have fooled a lieutenant. "Perhaps he sold some."

"In June 1916?"

"Brass keeps, Captain."

"It does," Vasseur said. "So do secrets. Better than brass, in fact. Brass tarnishes." He closed the ledger gently, respecting the binding as he respected all well-made things—it was one of the few decencies the war had left him—and set it on the bench between them like a card laid face-down. "Your master died in July. Sudden illness, the doctor wrote. A fall, the neighbors said. The priest recorded neither and buried him quickly."

"The priest was busy. There was a war on."

"There was indeed." He let the silence stretch until it creaked. Outside, somewhere beyond the rooftops, artillery spoke in the distance—not the sharp crack of guns firing near but the long rolling throat-clearing of a bombardment beginning its slow argument with the horizon, miles east. Both of them listened without meaning to. It was the reflex of the age; silence had become a sound one listened *through*, the way one listens through a wall. "I want you to understand something, mademoiselle, because I think you are intelligent enough to understand it and proud enough to pretend otherwise."

She lifted her chin a fraction. Waiting.

"I am not Renard."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. He saw it strike—he saw her recognize it, catalog it, file it away behind those steady eyes—and noted, with a sensation like cold water rising in his boots, that she recognized it *too easily*. Major Renard's reputation preceded him everywhere, of course; that explained nothing and reassured him less.

"Major Renard," Vasseur continued, "believes that a town is a warehouse and its people are inventory. He believes a ledger is either an asset or a liability, and liabilities are burned. If he had found what I suspect exists—the real ledger, the one behind this one—he would not be standing here discussing bindings with you. Do you follow me?"

"I follow that you've accused me of hiding something, and now you're telling me I should be grateful it's you doing the accusing."

"Yes," Vasseur said. "That is precisely it. You should be. I have read your master's accounts, and I have read between them, and I believe that man spent forty years doing quietly what the rest of us have failed to do loudly—which is to say, being just in a place where justice was inconvenient. I am not his enemy. But I am also not free. There are orders above me with names attached to them, and one of those names is Renard, and Renard has asked me—politely, in writing—to confirm whether any documents of historical value were recovered from this premises." He paused. "I have not yet answered him."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't yet know what I'd be answering *about*." He took out his pocket watch—an heirloom, his grandfather's, the only object he had carried through four years of mud without losing it—and consulted it, though he knew the hour. The habit of a lifetime; a clockmaker's town deserved the courtesy. "Here is what will happen. I will continue the inventory. I will report that the commercial ledgers contain nothing of military significance, unless I find reason to report otherwise. And you, mademoiselle, will finish the master clock—not because the army requires a symbol of order, though God knows the order needs symbols—but because that tower clock has kept this valley honest for two hundred years, and if it stops, something stops with it that none of my generals can requisition."

"That's generous," Elodie said, in a tone that suggested she believed generosity was simply suspicion wearing a better coat.

"It's arithmetic," Vasseur said. "You have until the armistice. Everyone does. Whether it comes in nine days or nineteen, it's coming, and when it comes, whatever is unfinished will be finished quickly and badly. I would prefer it finished properly. I suspect your master would have preferred that too." He pulled on his gloves, one finger at a time. "Good day, mademoiselle."

"Captain." She did not say it like a farewell. She said it like a sentence deferred.

* * *

He did not go straight back to the observatory. He walked.

Saint-Aubin-le-Haut in late afternoon was a town holding its breath. The baker's window was shuttered but the ovens were lit—he could smell the bread, that stubborn daily miracle, and beneath it the colder smells of straw and leather and horse from the transport company billeted in the schoolhouse. A boy in an undersized uniform went past him at a run with a message satchel flapping—Petit, they called him, the runner the garrison had inherited along with the town, sixteen years old at most, with a face that hadn't yet decided what it believed about the war. The boy saluted, badly, and Vasseur returned it, and watched him go, and thought: *there goes the entire future of France, running at top speed, carrying someone else's words.*

At the square he stopped. The observatory rose above the roofs, its dome catching the last copper light, and beside it the clock tower, and high in the tower's face the pale discs of the dials, motionless now these many weeks, their hands frozen at a quarter past four on some forgotten afternoon. A dead clock over a living town. Or a sleeping one. He had stood here the day he arrived and felt the stillness like a rebuke, and he felt it again now, more sharply, because he understood better what slept beneath it.

Renard wanted the tower. Vasseur had seen the memorandum that morning—*signal hazard, sightline obstruction, recommend demolition prior to offensive*—and had read in its careful staff-officer prose exactly what it was: not strategy but fear. Renard had heard the rumors too, the same rumors Vasseur had heard, that papers existed in this town, that a dead clockmaker had kept accounts that could hang a career. Men like Renard did not demolish towers because towers obstructed sightlines. They demolished towers because towers *witnessed*.

And Renard had asked, politely, in writing, whether any documents had been recovered.

Vasseur stood in the emptying square with the cold coming down off the hills and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since morning. If he reported nothing found, and Renard later learned otherwise—as Renard always learned otherwise—then Vasseur's signature became a lie on record, and men had been shot for less, and deservedly, and he had signed executions himself and knew the machinery worked. If he reported truthfully that the ledgers appeared incomplete, Renard would descend on this town like the bombardment everyone feared, and a young woman with brass filings under her nails would learn what happened to people who stood between a major and his past.

If. Unless. Either. The words of a man trying to build a bridge across a gorge with grammar.

His grandfather had been a judge, in the years before everything. Vasseur remembered him only as an old man in a garden, but he remembered one sentence, spoken over the grape arbor to a visiting cousin: *A man who signs what he knows to be false has already hanged himself; the rope merely arrives later.* Vasseur had been nine. He had not understood it. He understood it now, standing in a square full of requisitioned horses, holding a pocket watch that had never once lied to him about the hour.

He walked on, past the shuttered café, past the church with its bell silenced by army order—Tomas the bell-ringer had petitioned formally, in beautiful copperplate, and Vasseur had denied the petition in equally formal prose, and both men had known the denial was a lie about what mattered—and stopped at the corner where he could see the workshop's windows. Lamplight now. The silhouette of Elodie Okafor bent over her bench, working, the way her master must have worked, the way honest people worked while the world outside arranged itself for slaughter.

He watched for a long moment. Then he saw the second silhouette cross the lamp—a man's shape, tall, favoring one leg, passing behind her with the ease of familiarity, and bending to help her lift something heavy from the floor. A deserter. He was nearly certain of it. The garrison muster had a hole in it the size and shape of a man, and holes like that filled themselves in towns like this, in shops like this, under the protection of dead clockmakers and their unreadable ledgers.

Vasseur should have acted on it. It was, in the plain language of his orders, his duty. Instead he found himself standing in the cold thinking about a line in a dead man's ledger—*repaired lantern, no charge, repaired again*—and about the fact that he had not yet answered Renard's letter, and about the strange, corrosive discovery that the longer he delayed, the heavier the pen grew.

Tomorrow, he would tell himself. Tomorrow he would decide what kind of man his signature made him.

But tomorrow, he knew—even as he thought it, even as he turned up his collar and walked away from the lamplight—tomorrow the offensive schedule would arrive, and with it Renard, and with Renard the end of every quiet arithmetic a tired captain could perform. Behind him the lamplight burned on in the workshop window, steady as a struck match held against the wind, and Vasseur walked faster, as though he could outrun the knowledge of what he would be asked to do about it.

In the tower above, the dead clock waited with its hands at a quarter past four, keeping, in its silent way, the only appointment that mattered.
