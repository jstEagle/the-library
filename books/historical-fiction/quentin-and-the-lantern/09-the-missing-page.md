## 9. The Missing Page

The workshop at midnight smelled of oil and cold iron and, beneath it all, of a man who had been dead for eight months.

Quentin had learned to read rooms the way soldiers learned to read ground — for cover, for exits, for the places where danger pooled like water in a shell crater. This room, by every measure he knew, was safe: one door, one window shuttered against the blackout regulations, a cellar hatch that Elodie swore opened onto the baker's yard. And yet something in it made his skin prickle, some residue of attention, as though Augustin Fabre had never entirely left his workbench and was watching over Elodie's shoulder as she bent above the ledger with a lamp turned low.

"Here." Her finger stopped on a line near the bottom of the page. "March, 1916. *Convoy 44 — flour, tinned meat, fodder — lost between Virecourt and the railhead. Luc Marchal charged.*"

She read it twice, as though repetition might summon more words out of the ink. It didn't. The entry ended there, clean and final, in Augustin's small upright hand — the same hand that had recorded debts forgiven and deserters fed for forty years, each name given its dignity in a book no larger than a psalter.

"That's not enough," Quentin said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He hadn't slept properly since the night he'd fallen through this door, and the wound in his side had begun to speak to him again in its low insistent dialect of heat and ache. "It says what Luc was accused of. It doesn't say who did it."

"It wouldn't." Elodie sat back on her stool. In the lamplight her face looked older than her years, carved into planes of concentration. "Look how he writes everything else. A name forgiven — he gives the reason. A letter smuggled — he gives the destination. Master Fabre believed a record without reasons was just gossip bound in leather. If he wrote down that a convoy was lost, he knew why."

"Then where is it?"

She didn't answer immediately. She turned back through the pages instead, slowly, letting them fall under her fingers one by one, and Quentin watched her face change as she went — watched the small crease deepen between her brows, watched her lips move silently over dates. The lamp guttered once and steadied. Somewhere outside, far off toward the front, artillery spoke in its sleep, a mutter rather than a roar, the war turning over in bed.

"Elodie," he said. "Where is it?"

"The pages are wrong," she said. "Feel them."

He came around the bench and took the ledger from her, carefully, the way one handles anything that has survived too much already. She was right. He could feel it without knowing the craft behind it — a subtle difference in the body of the book, the way a coat hangs differently after a pocket is mended. Certain gatherings of pages were thicker than others. Near the spring of 1916, the spine dipped, the sewing loose where it should have been tight, as though the book had been starved somewhere in the middle of its life.

"Somebody's cut them out," he said.

"Not cut. Torn." Elodie took the book back and held it up to the light, tilting the spine. "A blade leaves a shadow at the root of the page, a thin dark line. There's nothing here. Whoever took them peeled them out slowly, close to the binding, so the stubs would hide in the fold. That takes patience. That takes—" she hesitated, "—that takes someone who didn't want it to look like theft."

Quentin felt the floor tilt slightly under him, though he knew perfectly well it was level. "When?"

"I don't know. Before he died. After. There's no way to tell from the paper alone — I'd need to see the binding under magnification, check whether the remaining pages have shifted, settled—"

"You're telling me my friend is going to be shot because somebody tore four pages out of a dead man's diary?"

"I'm telling you what the book says." She set it down flat on the bench between them, and her hand stayed on its cover, protective, the gesture of someone guarding a sleeping child. "And what it doesn't say."

* * *

They worked until nearly three.

Elodie approached the problem the way she approached a stopped clock: systematically, without drama, taking apart the silence piece by piece to find the broken tooth inside it. She brought out her master's tools — the loupe, the fine probes, a jeweler's saw worn bright at the grip — and Quentin understood that using them was a kind of conversation she was still having with Augustin Fabre. She handled each instrument the way one hands a tool to a teacher standing just behind the shoulder.

"He kept duplicates," Quentin said, pacing the narrow aisle between bench and wall. "Men like him always do. You don't spend thirty years keeping dangerous names in one book unless you've made arrangements."

"How would you know? Men like him."

"Because I grew up next door to one." He caught himself before the rest of it came out — the village, the priest who baptized him and then hid two Belgian boys in the confessional loft for eleven days while the patrols searched, the ledgers the old man kept wrapped in oilcloth under the sacristy floor. "There's a whole country of them. Invisible people. The ones who feed you when the law wants you hungry."

Elodie made a sound that might have been a laugh if grief hadn't gotten to it first. "He used to say that. *There are two governments, Quentin* — no, wait, you wouldn't know this — he said it to me my first year. *There are two governments,* he said. *The one with the stamps and seals, and the one with the soup pot. Only one of them keeps its promises.*"

For a moment neither of them spoke. The clock they had been repairing together stood open on its stand across the room, its pendulum stilled, its face bare — a patient skeleton waiting for them to finish giving it back its heartbeat. Outside, the requisitioned town lay dark under army order, and the observatory hill rose black against the faintly less-black sky.

"If the pages were torn," Quentin said slowly, working it forward the way he'd once worked through trench maps, "they were torn either by Fabre himself or by whoever killed him."

"We don't know anyone killed him."

"Don't we?" He stopped pacing. "Elodie. Think about it the way an officer thinks. A man keeps a record for decades — names, debts, deserters, smuggled letters. Half the town owes their lives to that book. Then in one year, right in the middle of the worst convoy losses of the war, four pages vanish and eight months later the man is dead. Heart failure, they told everyone. A quiet death, very convenient, very tidy. And now the army comes into town and the first thing Captain Vasseur asks for — before billets, before maps, before anything — is *all records.*"

Her face had gone pale in the lamplight. "You think Vasseur knows about the ledger."

"I think somebody knows about the ledger. Vasseur might be the dog or he might be the man holding the leash; I can't tell from here. But somebody tore those pages because the names on them could hang people. Not hang them in the street sense. Hang them in the court-martial sense. Officially. With paperwork." He heard his own voice harden and couldn't soften it. "Luc is scheduled for exactly that paperwork in nine days."

Elodie looked down at the ledger for a long time. When she lifted her head, something had changed in her expression — the doubt he'd seen there in the first days, her grief curdled into questions about the man she'd revered, had burned off and left something harder underneath.

"Then we need to know what was on those pages," she said. "And I know how to find out what shape they were."

* * *

What she did next, Quentin would remember for the rest of his life, in the way one remembers the exact quality of light during an earthquake.

She carried the ledger to the great clock and opened the case, and by lamplight she began to measure the ghost of what was missing. The book's stitching showed her the width of each removed gathering — she counted thread-holes, measured the span between surviving signatures with a brass caliper, murmured figures to herself. Then she turned to the entries themselves and began to read the book's margins, its cross-references, its habit of mind.

"He indexed himself," she said. "Look — here, January 1915: *see autumn entry re: supply irregularities, Virecourt road.* And here, later: *as noted re: the captain's cousin.* He cross-referenced. Every important matter got mentioned more than once, from different angles, because he didn't trust any single page to survive. Fire, flood, confiscation — he built redundancy into the thing like a watchmaker builds a fusee. One spring fails, another carries the motion."

"So the torn pages exist in pieces everywhere else in the book."

"Fragments. Echoes." She was turning pages faster now, her finger flicking down columns, and the excitement in her voice frightened Quentin more than despair would have, because excitement meant she was finding things. "Enough echoes and you can reconstruct the bell. Listen. *Autumn 1915: the Virecourt road again — third loss — and always on nights when the escort roster is known in advance.* Here: *the young officer dines at the Hôtel du Commerce, pays with money no lieutenant should have.* Here — God, here — *he asked me for the loan of my silence. I declined. Some silences cannot be lent.*"

"Does he give the name?" Quentin was leaning over her shoulder now, forgetting his wound, forgetting the cold, forgetting everything but the page.

"Not directly. Never directly — he protects himself even in a hidden book. But—" her finger stopped, trembled almost imperceptibly, "—here. February 1916. Three weeks before the convoy Luc was blamed for. *R. called again. R. believes money settles everything. I have told R. that my book does not sell. R. smiled and said books burn.*"

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that hadn't been there a moment before, or perhaps only seemed to. Quentin found that his mouth had gone dry.

"R," he said.

"Renard." Elodie said the name quietly, but it filled the workshop like smoke. "Major Renard. He came with the requisition — he stands at Vasseur's left hand at every inspection, he signs the requisition orders for this district, he—" She stopped. Quentin watched her arrive at it, watched the last step of the staircase appear under her feet. "He's on Captain Vasseur's own staff. The man who tore these pages — if he tore them — is the man whose signature is on the order demanding every record in this town."

"Or he ordered them torn years ago," Quentin said, "and then arranged to come back and make sure nothing else survived." He straightened up, and the movement pulled at his wound sharply enough to whiten his vision for a moment. He gripped the bench edge until the workshop solidified around him again. "Think like a soldier, Elodie, not like a clockmaker. Renard steals convoys. Someone keeps a book that proves it. The book disappears into a clockmaker's shop in a border town nobody's ever heard of. What does Renard do?"

"Waits," she said. Her voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening to it talk. "Waits for the war to bring authority to his doorstep. And then arrives wearing that authority, and asks politely for all records, and burns whatever frightens him."

"And Augustin Fabre dies eight months before he can get here. Of nothing. Quietly."

"You don't know that."

"No," Quentin admitted. "I don't. But you felt the same thing I felt when we said it. Don't pretend you didn't."

She didn't answer. She stood with both hands flat on the bench, head bowed over the open ledger, and in the lamplight Quentin saw her lips move — not reading, he realized, but speaking to the dead man. Asking him. *Is it true? Did they kill you for this? Did you know they were coming for you?*

After a while she said, without lifting her head: "My master taught me everything I know about honesty. He used to say a mechanism doesn't lie — a wheel either engages or it doesn't, a spring either holds or it breaks, and if your work fails it's because you failed, not because the iron deceived you. I built my whole idea of what's true on top of that." She raised her head. Her eyes were dry and very bright. "And now I'm supposed to believe the same man was murdered for keeping a list of kindnesses, and the murderer is downstairs in my town drinking the officers' wine, and the proof is four pieces of paper that no longer exist."

"They existed," Quentin said. "Paper leaves ghosts. Ink bleeds through onto the page behind it. Impressions stay in the paper below — you must know that, you work with fine surfaces all day. And men like Fabre keep duplicates. You said it yourself — he built redundancy into everything."

"In the book, yes. But a duplicate of *this*—" she touched the ledger's cover, "—wouldn't stay in the book. It would go somewhere else. Somewhere he trusted."

"Then we find where."

"With what? An hour of lamplight and a town full of soldiers?" But she was already moving, pulling the ledger closed, sliding it back into its hiding place inside the clock case with the tenderness of long habit, and Quentin recognized the transformation happening in front of him because he had seen it happen in men going over the parapet — the moment when fear stops being a wall and becomes fuel. "Tomorrow I service the observatory clock. Vasseur's orders. I'll be in the tower all day, alone, with every cabinet and drawer and floorboard that man ever touched."

"Elodie." He caught her wrist, gently. "If Renard killed him, then the tower is the most dangerous place you could stand tomorrow."

She looked down at his hand, then up at his face, and for a moment she was not the apprentice clockmaker but something older — the inheritor of a government with a soup pot, the keeper of a lantern not yet lit.

"Yes," she agreed. "Which is why it's the one place I have to go."

Above them, in the silent case of the master clock, the weights hung heavy and patient in the dark, holding their power in reserve, waiting — like everything else in that town — for the hour when it would finally be let fall.
