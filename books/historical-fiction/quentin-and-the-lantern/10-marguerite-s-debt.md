## 10. Marguerite's Debt

The bread was burning.

Marguerite knew it before she smelled it — knew it in the way the kitchen had gone too quiet, in the way her hands had stopped moving on their own and stood idle at the table as though they had been dismissed. The oven ticked behind her, patient as a clock, and inside it six loaves were turning from gold to ruin because she could not make herself care enough to open the door.

She had not baked like this since Henri died. Not with this attention, this stubborn insistence on doing everything by hand even when the whole town's flour ration came pre-milled from the army stores. Baking was how she thought. Kneading was how she prayed. And tonight she needed to think harder than she had ever thought in her life, because tomorrow morning Captain Vasseur's men would come through the square with chalk and paper and begin writing numbers on doors, and one of those numbers would be hers.

*Confiscated.* That was the word the lieutenant had used, flat as a coin on a counter. Confiscated for billeting. Her ovens, her flour, her cellar where old Tomas kept his spare bell-rope and Petit slept when his mother's house grew too loud with grief — all of it, signed away by men who had never smelled yeast in their lives.

And the workshop would go too. Elodie's workshop. Augustin's workshop.

Marguerite opened the oven at last, pulled out the ruined loaves, and set them on the stone shelf without looking at them. Their smell filled the kitchen anyway — burnt grain, bitter and dark, like something that had gone past saving. She stood there a long moment with her flour-whitened apron pressed against her mouth, and then she untied it, folded it over the chair back, and went to the little chest at the foot of her bed.

The key was wrapped in oilcloth, buried beneath Henri's letters, beneath the christening gown neither of their children had ever worn long enough to stain, beneath the photograph of the two of them outside the bakery the summer before the war took him. She unwrapped it with the same care she used for proofing dough in cold weather, slow and deliberate, letting nothing be rushed.

A brass key, small, older than the war. Older than the last war. Augustin Okafor had pressed it into her palm eleven years ago on a night of rain so hard the gutters ran white, and he had said only: *For the lantern room. When you need it, you will know.*

She had never used it. She had carried it through two moves and one funeral and four years of war the way one carries a debt — not resentfully, but with the constant low awareness that someday it would come due.

It had come due now.

* * *

She found Elodie in the workshop at an hour when no sensible person was working, which meant she found her exactly where she expected to. Lamplight spilled yellow across the cobbles of the rue des Forgerons, and through the window Marguerite could see the girl bent over the great clock's exposed heart, tweezers in hand, a jeweler's loupe screwed into her eye, her whole body curled around the mechanism like a mother around a feverish child.

The knock made her jump badly enough that the tweezers clattered onto the bench. When she saw who it was, some of the tension went out of her shoulders and some of it stayed, and Marguerite understood both halves. The girl had learned, these past weeks, that visitors were weather — you could not tell until the door opened whether it would be rain or lightning.

"Madame Roux." Elodie wiped her hands on her apron, leaving fresh streaks over the old ones. "It's late. Is it the bread? I can come help with the morning batch if—"

"It's not the bread." Marguerite stepped inside and closed the door behind her, and heard, above them both, the soft irregular stumble of the master clock's escapement — *tick... tick-tick... tick* — like a heart learning to beat again after a long faint. "Sit down, child."

"I'm not—"

"You are sixteen years younger than me and you have been carrying this house on your back since Augustin died. Sit."

Elodie sat. There was a stool by the bench, worn concave by forty years of a master clockmaker's weight, and the girl settled into it the way one settles into a grave one has dug oneself. In the corner, half-shadowed behind a rack of gear blanks, Quentin rose quietly from his cot — he had been pretending to sleep, Marguerite noted, and had been doing it well, which told her things about what kind of man he had been before the army taught him to hide — and she lifted a hand to stop him retreating further.

"No. Stay. What I have to say is for you most of all."

Quentin hesitated. He was thinner than when he'd first fallen through the door, his wound healing crooked because there had been no proper surgeon, only Marguerite's kitchen knowledge and Elodie's steady hands and a bottle of absinthe for the screaming. He looked at Elodie. Elodie, after a moment, nodded.

So he stayed, standing with his back to the wall like a man in a corridor waiting for verdicts.

Marguerite took off her coat, hung it properly on the peg — because a house falls apart the moment you let yourself hang things anywhere — and then stood in the middle of the lamplight and looked at the two of them, and found that all her rehearsed openings had deserted her.

"You know," she said finally, "that I owe Augustin my life."

"Everyone says that." Elodie's voice was careful. "Everyone in this town owes him something. Half of them don't know what for."

"Yes. Well." Marguerite laughed, short and dry. "That was rather the point of him, wasn't it."

She pulled the second stool from under the workbench and sat down across from the girl, close enough that their knees almost touched, the way women sit when the talk is going to take off its shoes.

"Henri was conscripted in '07. Not this war — the colonial drafts, the ones nobody talks about. He didn't want to go. He said the uniform would fit him like a lie, and he was right; it did, I saw the photograph. So he ran. Not far — just to his cousin's farm past the ridge, and everyone pretended not to know, because everyone loved Henri, because Henri was the sort of man who fixed widows' shutters without being asked and never mentioned it after." Her hands had found each other in her lap and were holding on. "But the gendarme they sent was not from here. A man named Delacour, who wanted a promotion, and a deserter caught in his district was a promotion."

The clock stumbled again overhead. *Tick... tick...*

"They found Henri in the barn loft on a Tuesday. They were going to shoot him Thursday morning, as an example, in front of the school if the schoolmaster allowed it. And Wednesday night there was a knock at my door, and it was Augustin — Augustin, whom I knew only as the man who repaired the church clock and charged less than he should — and he said, 'Marguerite, your husband is going to be moved to the gendarmerie cellars tonight, and between the barn and the cellars there is a stretch of road forty meters long with no lamps on it, and I am an old man who walks badly, and I will need someone to walk badly with me.'"

Elodie had gone very still. Not skeptical stillness — Marguerite had seen plenty of that in her time — but the stillness of a person hearing a story about their own family they had never been told.

"What did he do?" the girl whispered.

"He fell down." Marguerite smiled, and the smile hurt her face. "Right in front of the escort wagon. Clutched his chest, turned grey, collapsed into the mud like a dropped sack of meal. And while every gendarme in the escort was crowding around shouting about doctors and whether the old fool was dying, a figure got up out of the ditch beside the road — a figure in a gendarme's coat Augustin had obtained from God-knows-where, face blacked, walking with a limp that would have fooled the devil — and walked straight into the middle of them, took hold of Henri's arm as though he were another prisoner, and marched him off into the dark. And by the time anyone counted heads, there was one prisoner too few and an old clockmaker groaning in the road asking for water."

Silence in the workshop. Even the clock seemed to lean in.

"They searched for Henri for three weeks. Augustin hid him first in the observatory itself — in the lantern room, at the top of the tower, where no soldier or gendarme in this country would ever think to look, because who climbs a hundred steps to look for a baker? He brought Henri bread every day — my bread, Marguerite, he came to my shop and bought loaves and told me they were for his own table, and I sold them to him and wept over them after, not knowing. Then when the search cooled, he moved him south, gave him papers, gave him a name. Henri lived eleven more years under that name. He died in '18 — this spring — in a bed, of his heart, an old man, free." She looked up. "I buried him under the false name Augustin gave him, because even dead, the draft board might have wanted its due. Do you understand what that means, child? Every Sunday for eleven years I put flowers on a grave with the wrong name on it, and it was the truest grave in the cemetery."

Elodie's eyes were shining. "He never — he never wrote anything down about this?"

"Oh, he wrote it down." Marguerite reached into her pocket and laid the oilcloth bundle on the bench between them. "That was his way. He wrote everything down. Names, dates, debts, rescues — I've known since the year after Henri escaped, because I asked him outright, one night, and he showed me a single line in a ledger bound in green leather. *H. Roux — escorted. Paid in full.* That was all it said. He wrote everything and hid everything, and he trusted the hiding to people who loved him."

She unfolded the oilcloth. The brass key lay in the lamplight, small and patient.

"And he trusted the rest to this."

* * *

Quentin picked up the key first. His hands shook — they always shook since the shelling at Vaux, he'd admitted once, drunk on half a glass of Tomas's plum brandy — but they shook gently, reverently, the way a bell shakes in its yoke.

"The lantern room," he said. "That's the top of the observatory tower."

"The very top. Above the clock works, above the signal platform. It hasn't held a real light since before the war, but the army uses it now — Vasseur's signalmen run a lamp up there some nights." Marguerite watched the boy turn the key over. "Augustin kept a duplicate because the caretaker's key lived in a lockbox in the mayor's office, and he did not trust mayors. He gave copies to three people, he told me: himself, me, and one other who is dead. I am the last."

"Why you?" Elodie asked. It was not an accusation; it was wonder, and underneath the wonder, something rawer. "Why give it to the baker?"

Marguerite reached across and took the girl's flour-roughened hand, the hand of a nineteen-year-old apprentice who had spent the autumn discovering her dead master was a larger country than she'd ever mapped.

"Because I asked him once why he did it. All of it — the hiding, the debts forgiven, the letters carried through lines. We were sitting where you're sitting, and I said, Augustin, one day this catches up with you. One day a man you saved talks when he shouldn't, or a page goes missing, or someone powerful loses something and comes looking. And he said—" her voice caught, and she steadied it the way she steadied dough, with pressure and patience— "he said, *Marguerite, a town is like a clock. Everyone thinks the hands do the work. But it's the small gears inside, the ones no one sees, that keep the hours honest. If I write it down, and if the ones I trust know where it's written, then even if they break me, the clock keeps running.*"

She looked up at the ceiling, at the floorboards beyond which the great clock's pendulum swung its slow metronome through the dark.

"He built himself into the walls of this town, Elodie. And now they want to requisition the walls."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low. Marguerite told them what she remembered of the lantern room — the narrow iron stair behind the clock chamber, the hatch with its rusted hasp, the smell of cold brass and pigeon droppings and, always, faintly, of Augustin's pipe tobacco, which had soaked into the wood of that place over thirty years and never left. She told them about the loose panel under the parapet sill on the north side, where Augustin had once, laughing, shown her a cache no bigger than a bread box and sworn her to silence about its existence, though never its contents.

"He said, *If ever the town needs its conscience back, it's behind the north stone.* I thought he was being dramatic. He liked being dramatic; it was his one vanity." Her mouth trembled around the smile. "I thought we had years."

Outside, a mile east, the artillery muttered — not the sharp crack of nearby fire but the long rolling grumble of a front shifting its weight, a giant turning over in bed. They had all grown fluent in that language. This was distant thunder. Distant thunder meant the storm was deciding where to fall next.

Quentin set the key down on the bench between himself and Elodie, precisely centered, like a piece in a game whose rules were still being negotiated.

"If those pages are up there," he said slowly, "and if any of them touch Luc's case — the convoy, the theft, the officer who actually did it — then the army's own signalmen have been sleeping twenty feet above the proof for weeks."

"Then we climb quietly," Elodie said.

"With a wounded leg," Quentin said.

"With whatever leg God left you," Marguerite said, "because there may not be many nights left. Vasseur chalked my door today. Did he chalk this one?"

Elodie shook her head. "Not yet. But the survey party comes back through Thursday."

"Then you have two nights. Maybe three." Marguerite stood, took her coat from the peg, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch, looking back at the two of them in the guttering light — the girl at the bench, the deserter against the wall, the broken clock ticking its uneven heartbeat above them both.

"Tomorrow I bake double," she said. "Bread keeps climbers strong, and bread buys goodwill at checkpoints, and bread is the one thing in this war nobody questions a woman for carrying. You'll climb tomorrow night, after the signalmen change watch. I'll bring the loaves at dusk and stay to mind the shop while you're gone, in case anyone comes asking after the apprentice."

"Madame Roux," Elodie began, "you shouldn't—"

"Child." Marguerite's voice came out quieter than she intended, and truer. "I spent eleven years putting flowers on the wrong name because one old man walked badly in the rain for me. You think I'll sit in my kitchen and knead dough while his ledger rots twenty feet from an army lamp?" She pulled the door open onto the cold. "Gratitude isn't a feeling. It's a debt schedule. And mine just came due."

She stepped out into the dark of the rue des Forgerons, and behind her she heard the clock stumble, catch, and — for the first time in weeks — strike a single clean note, one clear silver stroke rolling out over the rooftops toward the observatory tower, where somewhere above the sleeping town a lamp burned in the lantern room, and behind a loose panel beneath the north stone, an old man's secrets waited in the dark for someone brave enough to climb.
