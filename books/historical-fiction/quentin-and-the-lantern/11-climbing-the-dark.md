## 11. Climbing the Dark

*Quentin*

The lantern Marguerite pressed into his hands was small enough to cup like a bird, its glass smoked with soot except for a slit no wider than a coin. "Augustin's own rule," she had whispered at the workshop door, pressing the cold handle against his palm as if handing over a relic. "Light only what you need to see, and nothing more."

Now that light was all he had, and even it felt like a betrayal.

The stairwell of the observatory tower wound upward through darkness so complete it seemed to press against his eyes. Each step groaned under his boot, and each groan was a small announcement: *here, here, here is where they are.* Somewhere below, Elodie climbed behind him — he could hear her breathing, steady and deliberate, the way she breathed when she worked at the clock, and he understood suddenly that this woman did not know how to be afraid without also being precise about it. He envied her that. His own fear was a loose animal in his chest, clawing to get out.

He counted the turns of the stair to keep himself sane. Twelve steps to the first landing, where a window had been bricked over during the second year of the war and now held nothing but the memory of a view. Twelve more to the second, where pigeons had colonized the sill and their droppings lay white as shell fragments in the dark. The air grew colder and older as he rose, smelling of stone dust and pigeon lime and, faintly, of oil — the same oil Augustin must have used on these stairs, on the great clock's chains, for forty years. The dead man was everywhere in this tower. Quentin had never met him and yet he climbed inside him, through him, up the vertebrae of his spine.

Above them — impossibly above them, though the ceiling was surely not far — the master clock ticked. Not the small domestic tick of a mantel piece but something vast and patient, a heartbeat in the bones of the building. *Tick. Tick.* It had been ticking since before the war. It would tick, Elodie had promised him, long after. Tonight he needed to believe that more than he had ever needed anything: that some things ran on past the reach of men with orders.

A sound rolled up from beyond the hills to the east — distant, muffled, like a door closing in a house two streets away. Then another. Then a longer silence, which was worse.

"They're walking it closer," Elodie said quietly from below him. Her voice came up the stairwell flattened by the stone. "Tomas says the guns moved again yesterday. He can tell by the echo."

"Can he?" Quentin kept climbing. "Tell?"

"He's rung these bells through three wars. He says the echo comes back thinner when the guns are near. Like the air has been used up." A pause, then, drier: "He also claims he once rang for Napoleon's funeral, which would make him a hundred and thirty, so we may take or leave his methods."

Quentin laughed despite himself — a short, startled sound that bounced off the walls and seemed to hang there, luminous. He clamped a hand over his mouth. But the laughter stayed somewhere in his ribs, warm, and he realized it was the first time since Luc had been condemned that anything in him had laughed.

They reached the landing beneath the lantern room. Here the stair ended at an iron door, painted over so many times that the paint stood out from the metal like scar tissue. A lock, black with age. Quentin raised the lantern to it and saw, scratched into the paint beside the keyhole, a mark he recognized from the ledger's cover: a small circle with a line through its heart, like a coin seen edge-on. A moon. Or a lamp.

"The duplicate key," he said. "Marguerite's."

"She gave it to you?" A rustle below; Elodie came up beside him on the narrow landing, close enough that he could see her face in the lantern's thin light — smudged with machine grease, jaw set, eyes moving over the door the way they moved over a broken movement, reading it. "She told me she'd lost it years ago."

"She lied," Quentin said. "To protect you, I think. In case I was lying about everything."

Elodie took the key from his hand without comment. She turned it, and the lock opened with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

*Elodie*

The lantern room smelled of rain that had fallen years ago.

It was an octagon of glass and iron at the very crown of the tower, built for the observatory's true purpose — the great lens had been removed to Paris in '14, carted away like a wounded man, and the room had stood empty ever since, a glass skull at the top of the town. Moonlight came through the panes in pale sheets. Below them the rooftops of the town lay silvered and still, chimneys like stubbled beard, the square a dark coin. Beyond it, the fields. Beyond the fields, the invisible line where France stopped being France.

And above them, separated only by the clock's floorboards, the mechanism breathed. Elodie felt it through her boots — the slow swing of the pendulum, sixty beats to the minute, forty thousand a day, the patient arithmetic that Augustin had spent his life keeping honest. *Tick. Tick.* It steadied her. A clock did not care about wars. A clock cared only about now, and now, and now.

"Where do we look?" Quentin whispered. He set the lantern on the floor, shielding its slit of light with his body, and in the gloom he looked less like a soldier than like a boy caught out after curfew — thin, hollow-eyed, one arm still bound from his wound. She had dressed it again that evening with proper linen and Marguerite's comfrey salve, and the sight of her own neat bandaging on a stranger's arm had done something strange to her chest that she refused to examine.

"Augustin hid things the way he built things," she murmured, turning slowly, reading the room. "Where no one would think to look, but where the structure itself does the hiding. Watch." She crossed to the central column that supported the clock above — a massed pillar of iron, bolted plate over plate — and ran her fingers along the bolts at its base. Four of them were newer than the rest. Brighter. Turned within living memory.

She looked back at Quentin. "Give me your knife."

He hesitated — a half-second, no more, but she saw it, the reflex of a man who had learned that everything handed over might not come back. Then he drew the knife from his boot and put it in her palm without a word, and she thought: *there. That is trust. It takes half a second and costs a whole life.*

She worked the four bolts loose. They came stiffly, crying a little, and then the lowest plate of the column swung outward on hidden hinges, and there — packed into the iron heart of the tower, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed string — lay a cache.

Not papers alone. A pistol, oiled and wrapped. A canvas bag that clinked with coins. And a packet of letters tied with black thread, dozens of them, their edges soft with handling.

Elodie knelt and lifted the packet as if it might go off. By the shielded lantern-light she read the topmost envelope. The address was in Augustin's hand, unmistakable, the same upright script that filled the ledger: *M. le Capitaine Delorme, Dépôt de l'Intendance, Verdun.* The date: March 1915. And beneath the address, folded into the seal, a slip of paper covered in figures — convoy numbers, crate counts, weights.

"Convoy manifests," she said. Her voice came out flat with shock. "These are supply records. Official ones." She untied the black thread with fingers that wanted to shake. Letter after letter, all in the same hand, all addressed to the same captain, all containing the same kind of slips: dates, numbers, signatures copied out. Evidence. Years of it, gathered secretly, sent secretly, to someone who might have acted on it.

"Delorme," Quentin breathed. He crouched beside her and pulled a letter free, angling it to the light. "That name — Luc said it. Delorme signed the transfer order for the convoy that never arrived. The one they hanged the driver's mate for losing." His voice cracked down the middle. "The one they're going to shoot Luc for."

"Then Augustin knew." Elodie heard herself say it and could hardly bear how small her voice sounded in the great glass room. "He knew who stole it. He wrote to Delorme — look, the same figures, the same names — he tried to give it to the army through the front door. For three years."

"And?" Quentin's hands were trembling as he shuffled the letters. "What did Delorme answer? Is there—"

She turned the packet over. At the bottom, separate, wrapped twice in oilcloth, lay a single envelope different from the rest — heavier paper, an official stamp. She opened it. Inside was one sheet, brief, bearing a staff signature and a date from the previous winter.

She read it twice. Then she read it aloud, because Quentin's eyes were failing him in the bad light and because saying it made it real:

*"M. Okafor. Your correspondence has been received and reviewed. The matter falls outside the competence of this office. No further correspondence will be answered. Signed —"* she stopped.

"Signed who?" Quentin grabbed the page. His finger found the signature block. "*R. Renard, Major, Intendanture.*"

The name fell into the lantern room like a dropped gear. Neither of them spoke. Above them, indifferent, the clock ticked on — *tick, tick* — counting out seconds toward an hour neither of them could yet imagine.

"He didn't just ignore it," Quentin said at last. His voice had gone very quiet, the way quiet goes when a man is standing at an edge. "Renard read every word. He knew Augustin had the proof. He knew what it proved. And then—" He couldn't finish.

"And then Augustin died," Elodie finished for him. "In autumn, when the frost came early. A fall on the ice, they said. On the flat stones by the canal, where the frost lies thinnest." She had buried the thought a year ago with the man himself; now it climbed out of its grave and stood up in front of her wearing a major's uniform. "I repaired the church clock the week after the funeral. I remember thinking the escapement had gone slack, as if the whole town's time had slipped."

Quentin began gathering the letters with quick, desperate hands. "This is enough. This is more than enough — manifests, signatures, Renard's own reply admitting he reviewed it — Vasseur can't bury all of this. Even an officer can't—"

The world went white.

Not light — pressure. A detonation rolled through the tower's iron bones, and the glass panes sang in their frames like struck bells, and dust sifted down through the moonlight, and somewhere south of the square a house stopped existing. Elodie was on the floor without remembering falling, the letters crushed against her chest, Quentin's hand hard on her shoulder. The clock above them faltered — one beat, two — and resumed. *Tick. Tick.* As if even the great mechanism had flinched.

"That was close," Quentin said. His lips were gray. "Closer than last night."

"Closer than the night before, too." Elodie got her knees under her. Her ears rang; through the ringing she made herself count the clock's beats until they came even again. Sixty to the minute. Good. Whatever else burned, time itself was still running true. "They're ranging on the tower. Tomas said the shells walk toward whatever the army values most. The observatory is a signal post now. That makes us a target with a flag on it."

"Then we take this downstairs tonight. All of it."

"No." She heard her own voice come out with Augustin's firmness in it, and let it. "Soldiers at the door, remember? Petit counted six patrols around the square. If you walk out of this tower carrying an officer's correspondence, you'll be shot on the steps and the papers burned as contraband before your body cools." She repacked the cache with hands that had begun, remarkably, to obey her again — the pistol, the coins, the letters, Renard's reply laid on top where a searching hand would find it first and understand nothing. She swung the plate closed and seated the bolts. "It stays here, in the iron heart of the tower, where it's already survived a year of war. What we take downstairs is knowledge. Names. Dates. Enough to convince Vasseur when the moment comes — and enough to find it again if we don't live through the night."

Quentin stared at her. In the moonlight his face was young and furious and terrified, all three at war across it. "You speak like a quartermaster."

"I speak like a clockmaker. Everything has its place, and everything must stay in place until the hour strikes." She gathered the few pages she had allowed herself — the manifest of the lost convoy, Renard's reply, two letters naming the crates' true destination — and slid them inside her bodice, against her stays, where a search would have to become indecency to find them. Let them try. Let Captain Vasseur weigh his dignity against his suspicion. "Come. We climb down while there's still a town left to climb down to."

Another detonation, nearer — the windows flashed orange from the south — and the tower shuddered around them, and the clock above stumbled, caught, stumbled —

—and stopped.

The silence was enormous. After a year of that heartbeat ticking through every wall of her life, Elodie felt the absence like a wound. She looked up through the floorboards, into the dark where her master's greatest work hung motionless, dead-weight on its broken escapement.

"Tomorrow," she said aloud, to Quentin, to the tower, to the dead man whose staircase they stood on. "Tomorrow I fix it. Whatever else happens — however close they walk their guns — that clock strikes eleven on the eleventh day, or I die with my hands inside it."

Below them, faint through the glass, a bell began to ring — wrong, ragged, off-pattern. Old Tomas, ringing something urgent in the bell-code only five people in the town could read.

Elodie froze, the letters burning against her ribs, and translated the strokes with a coldness spreading through her chest.

*Raid. Workshop. Now.*
