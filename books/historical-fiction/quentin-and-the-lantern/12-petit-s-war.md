## 12. Petit's War

Petit had carried messages through worse weather than this, but never through a worse silence.

The rain came down in thin grey needles over the rue des Tanneurs, and he moved along the gutters with his collar up and his cap pulled low, counting doorways the way he counted everything now — by how long it would take a shell to reach them if one fell there. He was sixteen years old and he had been running messages since before the requisition, when a message was only bread from Marguerite's oven or a note from the notary about someone's hay. Now every message felt like it was made of glass. Now he ran with his shoulders up around his ears.

The town had emptied the way a bucket empties — slowly at first, then all at once, then just a sloshing remnant that couldn't get out. The army had posted its notices on the church doors: *All civilians of military age to report. All property subject to requisition. All movement after curfew forbidden.* The ink had been so fresh it smeared when Tomas touched it, and the old bell-ringer had wiped his fingers on his coat with an expression Petit had never seen on him before, something between disgust and grief, like a man touching a wound to see how deep it went.

But some people couldn't leave. Marguerite wouldn't abandon her ovens. Tomas wouldn't leave the bells. And Elodie Okafor had looked at Captain Vasseur himself and said the master clock would stop without her, and something in her voice — that flat, stubborn, clockmaker's certainty — had made even an officer with requisition papers blink and let her stay.

So the workshop on the rue des Tanneurs still had lamplight in its windows after curfew. Still smelled of oil and metal and Marguerite's smuggled loaves. Still held Quentin, who slept in the space behind the coal bin with a bandage on his shoulder and a name — Luc — that he said like other men said prayers.

And still held Petit, who was, as of this morning, a liar.

* * *

It had happened at the garrison headquarters, in the room that used to be the schoolmaster's office, where the maps were pinned now instead of the boys' penmanship exercises. Petit knew the room. He'd sat in it for three years getting sums wrong while the schoolmaster rapped his knuckles. There was still a dent in the floorboards where he'd once been made to stand for a whole afternoon.

Major Renard stood where the schoolmaster used to stand.

He was a tall man built like a folded knife, all clean angles and pressed cloth, with a face so smooth and unlined it seemed less like a face than a surface — polished, reflective, giving nothing back. His uniform fit him the way a glove fits a hand that has never done work. When he spoke, his voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. Everything about Renard was pleasant, the way ice is pleasant until you're under it.

"You're the messenger boy," Renard said. "Okafor's errand runner."

"Petit, sir." Everyone called him Petit because his real name — Pierre-Antoine Vasselet, which he hated — took too long to shout. "Yes, sir."

"Deliveries to the workshop. Who comes there?"

"The baker, sir. Bread. Old Tomas sometimes. The clockmaker works alone."

Renard smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which stayed flat and grey as slate roofs. "A deserter came through this quarter two nights ago. Wounded. Somebody fed him, somebody stitched him. Somebody hid him." He picked up a paperweight — the schoolmaster's old brass globe, worn shiny at the north pole by decades of bored thumbs — and turned it slowly. "Strangers in the workshop would be of great interest to me, Petit. You understand? A boy who sees things. A boy who reports them accurately could find himself well provided for. Extra rations. A dry billet. Perhaps, when this is all over, a position."

The globe turned. North Africa slid into view. Somewhere out there was the country half of Petit's blood came from, though no one at headquarters needed to know that; his mother's name was on none of their papers, buried the way she'd taught him to bury things that mattered.

"I haven't seen any strangers, sir," Petit said.

And there it was.

The lie came out of him smoothly, easily, in a steady soldier's voice he didn't know he owned, and it frightened him more than the shells did. Not because lying was hard — lying, he discovered, was terribly easy, like stepping off a curb. But because he understood, in the moment the words left his mouth, that he had crossed a line that could not be uncrossed, that somewhere inside him a small honest creature had died quietly and he had not even stopped to bury it. He was standing in the schoolmaster's office telling a major of the Republic that he had seen nothing, when what he had seen was Quentin's face at the window two nights ago, pale as tallow, and the lamp hurriedly shuttered, and Elodie's hand pressing him down behind the bench with a grip that left bruises.

"Nothing?" Renard said softly.

"Nothing, sir. The workshop's quiet. She works on the clock all day. Sometimes she swears at it."

That got a flicker of amusement from the staff officer at the desk by the window — Corporal Devaux, red-faced, who laughed too easily — and Renard's attention shifted just enough for Petit to breathe again. The major set the globe down.

"Very good. If you see anything — anyone — you'll come to me directly. Not to Captain Vasseur. To me." He said the captain's name with a faint distaste, like a man finding a hair in his soup. "Do we understand each other?"

"Yes, sir."

"And Petit." The pleasantness sharpened, became a blade laid flat against skin rather than drawn across it. "Boys who lie to me have a way of being conscripted early. There's a battalion going up next week that takes them at fifteen. I can arrange things. I'm very good at arranging things."

"Yes, sir," Petit said again, and saluted, and walked out of the schoolmaster's office with his legs feeling like borrowed legs, like stilts operated by someone else.

* * *

He didn't go straight to the workshop. That was the first rule of carrying secrets, and his mother had taught him rules the way other mothers taught prayers: you don't walk home the way a watched man walks home. So he delivered three legitimate messages first — a chit to the quartermaster, a note to the telegraph post, a request from the cookhouse to the butcher whose shop had been stripped bare weeks ago — and he let his feet carry him the long way round, past the fountain with the chipped dolphin, past the house where Madame Ferrand had lived before she packed her piano and her parrot and left, past the burned-out lorry that the children used to play on before there were no children left to play on it.

The town in November was a photograph of itself. That was how Petit thought of it. All the life developed out of the paper, leaving shapes.

By the time he reached the rue des Tanneurs the light was failing and the rain had thickened, and he slipped through the alley behind Marguerite's bakery, where the warm breath of the ovens poured out through a vent like a living thing. Marguerite saw him come and put a heel of yesterday's loaf in his pocket without a word, the way she'd been doing since he was nine, and squeezed his shoulder, and her hands were floured and strong and they were the closest thing to his mother's hands that existed in this world anymore.

"She's waiting for you," Marguerite murmured. "Been pacing since noon."

"Tell her—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I'll tell her myself."

Through the connecting yard, in the side door, down the familiar smell of oil and cold iron. Elodie was at the bench with her sleeves rolled, a loupe screwed into her eye, working on something smaller than his thumbnail with tweezers that trembled slightly — whether from the work or from waiting, he couldn't tell. The great shadow of the observatory clock's escapement occupied half the workshop, dismantled into its patient bronze anatomy, each piece laid out on flannel like surgical instruments. Above them all hung Augustin's lantern, dark, kept lit only when needed now. Fuel was counted like blood.

"You're late," Elodie said, not looking up. Her voice was rough with the hour and the fear she pretended not to feel. "Where—"

"He asked me about strangers."

The tweezers stopped. She looked up, and the loupe made her eye huge and strange, a whole dark planet of an eye, and then she took it out and her face was just Elodie's face, tired and young and older than tired.

"Who did. Vasseur?"

"Renard." Saying the name aloud made it more real than he wanted it to be, a thing with weight and edges. "The major. In the schoolmaster's office. He knows a deserter came through the quarter. He said wounded, hidden, fed. He wanted to know who comes to the workshop." Petit's voice cracked on the last word and he hated it and pushed on. "He offered me rations. A billet. He said boys who lie get conscripted early. There's a battalion that takes them at fifteen."

Elodie set down the tweezers very carefully, the way she handled anything fragile, and Petit realized with a jolt that she was handling *him*, that to her he was as delicate as a balance spring, and that she was afraid — not for herself. For him.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told her—" He stopped. Started again. "I told him nothing. I said the workshop was quiet. You, the clock, swearing at it. Marguerite's bread. Tomas sometimes." He swallowed. "I lied, Elodie. First time. Whole lie, right to a major's face."

For a moment she said nothing. Then she came around the bench and put both her hands on his shoulders, and her grip was firm and warm and slightly oily, smelling of clock grease, and she looked at him the way she looked at a mechanism that has surprised her by working better than it should.

"Then you're one of us properly now," she said. "No taking it back. You know that."

"I know that."

"It gets heavier. Lying. Every time. It doesn't wear smooth like a pivot, it wears rough. And someday someone will ask you something and the truth and the lie will both cost, and you'll have to pick which price to pay." Her thumb brushed the collar of his coat where Renard's gaze had seemed to sit all afternoon. "You picked right today."

From behind the coal bin came a rustle, and Quentin emerged into the lantern-light, moving carefully around his bandaged shoulder, his face hollowed and hungry and sharp. He'd heard everything; of course he'd heard everything; the coal bin had ears like a confessional.

"He's going to search eventually," Quentin said quietly. "Men like that don't ask questions unless they already suspect the answers. Your lie bought days, not safety. Maybe hours."

"Then we use the hours," Elodie said.

* * *

They used them the way poor families use candle-ends — carefully, completely, wasting nothing.

Petit ran messages until full dark and then kept running ones that didn't exist, circuiting the town to check the army's sentry posts, learning their rotation the way he'd once learned the times of the market carts. Sentry at the fountain changed at six and ten. Sentry at the church door dozed between rounds, always, a boy barely older than himself with chilblains and a rifle older than either of them. Patrol along the river road at irregular intervals but never, Petit noticed with a messenger's hunger for pattern, within the half-hour after the change at the fountain.

He reported all of it back in the workshop by lamplight, whispering over the open case of the clock while the pendulum — rehung, tested, swinging with its slow bronze heartbeat — ticked above them like the town's own pulse. Marguerite brought bread and news. Tomas brought kindling and, tucked inside his coat, the small brass key to the belfry that he was no longer officially permitted to carry, since the army had claimed the bells for signal work. Petit sat among them with his back against the warm flank of the clock case and felt the strangest thing settle over him, which was belonging. He had lost a mother and a father and a country-of-the-heart and a schoolroom, and here in a condemned workshop full of contraband kindness he had found a family assembled out of debts and damage, and he would burn the whole town down before he gave it up.

That was what war had made of him, he thought, watching Elodie fit a pallet stone with breath-held precision. Not a soldier. A keeper. His mother had told him once, in the language she only used indoors, behind shutters: *We are the people who keep lamps lit for others, little one. It is dangerous work. It is the only work.* He hadn't understood her then. He was beginning to understand her now, and understanding it hurt like teeth coming in.

Near midnight, footsteps on the cobbles made everyone freeze — the new instinct, the animal instinct, the one that lived now underneath everything like water under ice. But it was only Vasseur, alone, out of uniform cap, standing in the doorway with rain running off him, looking at the lamplit scene with an expression Petit couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Mademoiselle Okafor," the captain said. "The major has requested a progress report on the clock. Tomorrow. In person." His eyes moved over the workshop — over the flannel-laid mechanisms, the covered coal bin, the boy sitting too casually against the case — and lingered a half-second too long on each. "I would advise that everything... extraneous... be elsewhere by morning. He counts things. It's his nature. He counts everything."

He turned to go, then paused, his back to them, his voice dropping to something almost private.

"For what it's worth, I served under Renard in 'fourteen. I have been counting things ever since too." A pause. "Good night."

His boots faded up the street. No one spoke. Above them the pendulum swung, and swung, and the lantern flame bent slightly in the draft from the opened door, and Petit thought: *tomorrow*. Tomorrow Renard would come to count, and tomorrow Petit would stand in this room and lie again, a second time, a deeper time, and each lie would spend something in him that he could never earn back — innocence, his mother would have called it, spending itself coin by coin to buy a family's breathing.

He looked at his hands in the lamplight. They were a child's hands still, nicked and grimy, and they were shaking.

Outside, far off toward the front, the guns began their nightly mutter — and then, strangely, stopped. All at once. A silence rolled over the town so complete that the pendulum's tick suddenly sounded enormous, filling the workshop, filling the world.

Tomas lifted his head like an old dog scenting weather. "They've gone quiet," he said. "Armies don't go quiet for nothing. Something's coming."

Quentin was already pulling on his boots.
