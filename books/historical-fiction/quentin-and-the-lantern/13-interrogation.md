## 13. Interrogation

The summons came at half past six in the morning, delivered by a corporal who did not knock so much as announce himself through the door with the flat authority of a man reading a railway timetable.

"Mademoiselle Okafor. The captain requests your presence at the observatory. Nine o'clock. Bring your tools."

He was gone before Elodie could ask which tools, or why, or what a man meant by *requests* when he sent soldiers to say it. She stood in the doorway of the workshop in her nightdress, watching his boots recede through the grey light, and felt the cold of the threshold seep up through her bare feet like water rising in a cellar.

Behind her, from the back room where he had slept on a pallet of sacking for eleven nights now, Quentin's voice came low and careful: "Elodie?"

"He wants me at the observatory." She closed the door and turned the key out of habit — a habit that had become something else entirely since the ledger went into hiding inside the clock's pendulum case. "Nine o'clock."

"Then we have two hours."

"Two hours to do what? He could be summoning me to arrest me."

Quentin emerged, buttoning the borrowed shirt that hung loose on his shoulders, his face still creased from sleep but already working, already turning over possibilities the way she turned over gears. "If he wanted to arrest you, he'd send more than one corporal. Arrests are loud. Summons are quiet. Quiet means he doesn't want the town watching." He crossed to the window and eased back the curtain a finger's width. "Quiet means he knows something, and he wants to know how much you know about what he knows."

"That sentence should not make sense, and yet it does."

A ghost of a smile touched his mouth and vanished. It was strange, Elodie thought, how quickly fear could teach two strangers each other's rhythms — three weeks ago she had not known this man existed, and now she could read his silences the way Augustin had taught her to read the tick of an escapement, feeling for the hesitation that meant a tooth was worn, that something was about to slip.

She dressed in her work clothes because it was the only armour she owned. In the glass above the washbasin her face looked pale and set, older than her twenty-three years, and she thought of Augustin again — as she had thought of him every hour for days now — and wondered whether he had ever stood before powerful men wearing this same expression, this careful blankness, while the ledger's secrets sat heavy in his pockets like stones.

*History happens elsewhere,* she had believed, all her life, in this small town pressed against the border like a thumbprint on soft wax. She had believed it right up until the morning she found his ledger behind the clock's faceplate, and every day since had been a lesson in how wrong she had been. History had been here all along. It had simply learned to be quiet.

* * *

The observatory hill was slick with October frost, and the town below it wore the look of a place holding its breath. Since the requisition, whole streets had emptied; shutters nailed, carts gone east toward the interior with families and furniture stacked together like refugees from their own lives. Only the army's grey-green presence gave the lanes any motion now, and even the soldiers moved with the listless economy of men waiting for orders they suspected would kill them.

At the observatory door, two sentries flanked the entrance where once there had been only Tomas's ancient bell-pull and a hand-lettered sign: *RING ONCE AND WAIT.* The sign was gone. One of the sentries held out his palm for her toolbag, examined its contents with theatrical slowness — files, pliers, the brass oiler, a rag — then handed it back with a jerk of his chin toward the stairs.

Captain Vasseur had established himself in the observatory's main chamber, beneath the great dome, and he had arranged the room the way a man arranges a trap he is not yet certain has sprung. A table stood in the centre under the dome's grey light, and on it lay objects laid out with a precision that told Elodie everything before he said a word: a lantern, unlit; a folded map of the sector; and, square and damning in the middle of the table, three pieces of paper.

Three pieces of paper. Torn edges. Faded ink. She knew them without looking closely, the way one knows one's own heartbeat.

*He has pages. Real pages. Not the torn scrap Quentin carried — these were cut cleanly, carefully, from somewhere.*

Vasseur stood at the tall south window with his back to her, hands clasped behind him, watching the fog burn off the valley. He was perhaps forty-five, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had been soldiers too long for it to be anything but the truth of them, and there was grey at his temples that made him look less like an officer and more like a tired schoolmaster forced to examine a pupil he suspected of genius and mischief in equal measure.

"You are punctual," he said, without turning. "Your master was punctual also. I am told the clocks of this town have kept better time than the railways for fifty years."

"Augustin Rey took pride in his work, Captain."

"And you take pride in his." Now he turned, and his eyes — pale, unhurried, terribly patient — moved over her face as if reading a dial. "Sit down, Mademoiselle Okafor."

There was one chair. She did not sit. Standing, she decided, kept her hands free, and her hands were the most honest part of her; if they began to tremble she wanted to know it immediately, to hide them behind her back before it showed.

Vasseur regarded her refusal for a long moment, and something flickered across his face that might have been approval. Then he picked up the first page and held it out.

"Do you know what this is?"

She took it. Her fingers were steady. Later she would wonder at that; at the time she felt nothing at all, the way one feels nothing standing on a high ledge until one looks down.

It was a ledger page, ruled in Augustin's neat hand, dated March 1915. A name she did not recognise. A sum forgiven. And beside it, in the margin where Augustin wrote his small coded annotations, a phrase: *delivered through the miller, Tuesday.*

"I believe it comes from a book of accounts," she said. "My master kept meticulous records. Debts owed to the workshop, materials purchased—"

"Mademoiselle." Vasseur's voice did not rise. That was the frightening thing about it; it never rose. It simply continued, level as a spirit-gauge. "I have read four hundred pages of your master's accounts in the last six days. I know precisely what kind of records he kept. I know that in thirty years, Augustin Rey forgave debts to two hundred and eleven people whose names appear nowhere in any parish register of this town — people who, according to every document this garrison possesses, have never lived here at all." He lifted the second page. "Deserters. Sheltered. Fed. Moved along the river road at night. And letters—" the third page, "—smuggled past three separate censorship offices, written by prisoners of war to families the army had declared unreachable."

The dome echoed faintly with the words, giving them a terrible resonance, as though the building itself were testifying against the dead man.

"The ledger is evidence in a matter of military security," Vasseur said. "Where is the rest of it?"

"Destroyed," Elodie said. "When my master died, his papers were burned. It's customary. Old account books, client lists — no one keeps them after."

"No one keeps them." He repeated it softly, tasting it. He walked around the table slowly, and Elodie tracked him without turning her head, the way one tracks a cat near a birdcage. "And yet, Mademoiselle Okafor, someone kept *these*. These pages were found — I will not insult us both by pretending you'll believe whatever provenance I invent — these pages were found folded inside the lining of a coat belonging to a deserter apprehended ten days ago in the river district. A young man named Luc Ferreix, condemned for the theft of a supply convoy he did not steal. And on the reverse of this third page—" he tapped it, "—is a partial entry. A date. An amount. The initials *M.R.* Nothing more. Someone tore the rest away."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below in the town a cart rattled over cobbles, absurdly domestic, a sound from another century.

"M.R.," Vasseur said again. "Major Renard arrived with the requisition staff eight days ago. Before that, Major Renard was responsible for supply convoys on this entire sector. Before that, Major Renard filed the report that condemned Private Ferreix." His pale eyes settled on her and stayed. "You see my difficulty, mademoiselle. Either your dead master was keeping records that implicate an officer of the French Army in theft and in the judicial murder of a soldier — or someone is manufacturing forgeries of extraordinary quality to destroy that officer. Both possibilities require me to find the source. And every road I walk leads, eventually, to this observatory, to that clock, and to you."

*He knows,* Elodie thought, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful. *He may not know where, but he knows.* Lying further was like bailing a flooded boat with a thimble.

So she did not lie. She did the thing Augustin had done, she suspected, all his life: she changed the shape of the conversation until it fit through a narrower door.

"Captain," she said, "may I ask you a question?"

His eyebrows rose a fraction — surprise, or its performance. "You may attempt."

"When this offensive ends — and it will end, soon, everyone says within days — what happens to this town?"

"The town returns to civilian administration. The garrison withdraws."

"And the observatory?"

"The observatory—" He paused. For the first time, something moved behind the patience in his face, something that looked uncomfortably like weariness. "The observatory is slated for demolition. Its tower presents an obstruction to the artillery lines. It was to be cleared next week."

"Demolished." She let the word stand in the dome's cold air. "The oldest building in the valley. The tower my master climbed every night for thirty years to check the regulator against the stars. The tower that has given this town its time through two wars." She stepped forward, to the table, and laid her palm flat beside the torn pages — deliberately, deliberately not touching them. "You called it an obstruction, Captain. You're wrong. It's a symbol. Symbols are the only things left standing when everything else is rubble, and men need symbols more than they need roads."

Vasseur watched her with an expression she could not parse. "What is it you are proposing?"

"Give me the clock." Her heart was going hard now, but her voice held; years of speaking to customers about repair bills had trained her for exactly this, the negotiation conducted over a thing broken and beloved. "The master clock is seized, yes — I understand, requisition is requisition. But let me restore it. Fully. Escapement, striking train, the works. Your army wants order; you've said so — you said it the day you took the town, standing in my workshop with your boots on Augustin's floor. Well. There is no order in this valley, Captain, except the order this tower gives it. Every farm for forty kilometres sets its day by this clock. When the armistice comes — and it is coming, everyone whispers it, even your own officers — the first sound this region hears should be this bell, ringing true, rung by the French Army that restored it. Not a ruin. Not a demolition. A symbol of order, exactly as you wished the town to see you."

The words poured out of her and she heard, distantly, how good they were, how well-made — and hated herself a little for it, because they were also true, and the truest lies were the ones Augustin must have told all his life.

Vasseur was silent for a long time. He walked to the window again. Below, the fog was lifting off the river, and the ruined bridge stood up out of it like a rib. When he spoke, his back still to her, his voice had lost its examination-hall flatness.

"You think I don't know what Renard is," he said quietly. "You think I haven't read these pages and felt the floor move under me." He turned. Up close, in the dome's grey light, she saw that he had not slept either; there were shadows under his eyes as deep as Quentin's. "I have served thirty-one years, mademoiselle. I have carried out orders that sat in my stomach like lead shot, because that is what an army is — a machine for making men do things they will spend the rest of their lives explaining to themselves. But there are orders, and there is *this*." He gestured at the pages. "A man hanged for another man's theft while the thief wears a major's insignia and drinks the army's wine. If I act on this wrongly, I destroy myself and possibly the morale of an entire sector in the last week of the war. If I ignore it—" his jaw tightened, "—then I am the machine, and nothing else, forever."

"So what will you do?"

"What I always do. I follow procedure." Something bitter passed over his mouth. "Here is procedure, mademoiselle. You will restore the clock. You will have — I will be generous, because I suspect generosity costs me less than the alternative — you will have until the armistice, whenever it comes. Days, they say. Perhaps a week. When the clock strikes the armistice hour, I will come to this tower, and you will give me the complete ledger, and the name of whoever removed these pages. All of it. Every name, every rescue, every crime your master recorded — including, if it exists, the proof against Renard."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then when the tower is demolished, you will be in it." He said it without heat, almost gently, which was worse. "I am offering you a trade, Mademoiselle Okafor. Your master's secrets, bought with his clock's survival. I suggest you find that a fair price, because I am not certain I can protect this arrangement from other interested parties for longer than that."

*Other interested parties.* Renard. She kept her face still, but ice slid down her spine.

"The clock will strike," she said. "I promise you that much, Captain. On the armistice hour, this tower will speak."

"It had better," Vasseur said, "speak clearly."

* * *

She walked down the hill with the frost melting into mud under her boots and her mind moving faster than her feet. Two hours ago she had woken afraid. Now she was afraid in a different key — afraid and armed, because Vasseur had given her a deadline and a deadline, in her trade, was not a threat. A deadline was a specification. A specification could be worked to.

Until the armistice. Days, perhaps a week. In that time she had to finish the escapement, keep the ledger hidden, get the proof against Renard out of the tower's lantern room where Marguerite swore Augustin had cached it, and deliver it through channels that would not bury it — all while an officer who might hang her watched her work, and another one, worse, wanted the tower shelled into silence.

At the foot of the hill, Petit was waiting by the bakery wall, breath steaming, eyes wide with the news he was barely containing.

"Elodie — Madame Marguerite says come now. Quentin's gone up to the lantern room already, and Tomas found something. In the bell ropes." The boy's voice dropped to a whisper. "Someone's been in the tower. Before us. And they left a mark — Augustin's mark. The little lantern symbol, chalked on the beam." He swallowed. "But Elodie — the chalk's fresh."
