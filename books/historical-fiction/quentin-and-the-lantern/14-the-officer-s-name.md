## 14. The Officer's Name

The cache smelled of lamp oil and forty years of Augustin's patience.

Quentin sat on the floor of the lantern room with the papers spread around him like a card game he was losing, while outside the narrow windows the autumn dark pressed against the glass and, somewhere beyond the ridge, the artillery spoke its slow, patient language. Elodie had gone down to the workshop an hour ago — *someone has to be at the bench when they come asking* — and so it was Quentin alone who read the letter for the third time, alone who felt his hands begin to shake in a way they had not shaken even under shellfire.

It was a requisition docket. That was all. A requisition docket and a delivery note and a private letter, folded together in an oilcloth packet that had been sewn into the lining of a wooden box beneath the observatory's great lens, and together they said what half a page of Augustin's ledger had only hinted.

*Convoy 114, dispatched Verdun-adjacent sector, 14 March 1917. Grain, tinned meat, fodder, medical stores. Signed out under the seal of Major Étienne Renard, Office of Supply, Fourth Army rear echelon.*

The convoy had never arrived. Everyone knew that much; it had been the scandal that broke Luc's company apart like a dropped plate. Luc had signed for it at the transfer point — Luc, the most careful sergeant in the battalion, the man who counted cartridges twice and wrote everything down — and when the trucks turned up empty sixty kilometres away, stripped and burned in a farmyard that no one could quite locate on any map, it was Luc's signature they came for. Luc's neck they measured for.

But here, in Augustin's careful clerk's hand, was the rest of it. The delivery note bore two signatures: Luc's at the transfer point, and beneath it, added days later in different ink, a countersignature authorizing a *route amendment* — a diversion through a sector no convoy had business entering. The route amendment carried Major Renard's own seal. And the private letter — this was the thing that made Quentin close his eyes and press his knuckles against his mouth — the private letter was from a farmer outside Amiens, thanking God and the major for the *generous provisions* that had kept his family fed through the hard winter, provisions acquired, the farmer wrote delicately, *by means we will not speak of but will not forget.*

Renard hadn't stolen the convoy to sell it to strangers. He'd stolen it to buy goodwill, to salt the countryside with gratitude, to make himself beloved in the villages his own requisitions were stripping bare. A thief with a conscience. A thief building himself a legend out of other men's rations while a sergeant sat in a cell waiting for dawn and twelve rifles.

Quentin set the letter down very carefully, as though it were made of eggshell.

*There,* he thought. *There it is.* The thing he had crossed three hundred kilometres of war to find. The thing he had deserted for — and desertion, he reminded himself, looking down at the grey-blue uniform he'd stopped being able to wear honestly months ago, desertion was a thing they shot you for too, if they caught you, though they called it by a politer word. He had traded one rope for the chance to find another man's proof, and now he held it in both hands, and it weighed almost nothing at all.

Almost nothing. Four sheets of paper and a seal impression. Enough to hang a major, or enough to hang them all, depending entirely on whose hands it passed through next.

He heard the stair door below — the particular creak of the third step, which Tomas had promised to oil and never would — and a moment later Elodie's head appeared through the hatch, her face smudged with lamp-black, a wrench still in her hand as though she'd forgotten she was carrying it.

"You've been up here long enough to have read it twice," she said. "Your face says you read it four times."

"Three." He couldn't manage more than that. His voice had gone strange and flat.

She climbed the rest of the way into the lantern room and settled cross-legged opposite him among the papers, and she read, and he watched her read, watched her lips move slightly over the words the way she moved them over the fine print of a parts catalogue. When she reached the farmer's letter her jaw tightened. When she reached the route amendment with its double signatures she went very still, the way she went still over a movement that wasn't behaving, and he had learned by now that her stillness was worse than shouting.

"It's clean," she said finally. "It's damning and it's clean. Dates line up, seals match, the countersignature follows Luc's by four days. Any court—" She stopped herself. He saw her stop herself, saw the sentence die behind her teeth.

"Any court," Quentin finished for her. "Yes. If there were a court."

They looked at each other across the spread of paper, and neither of them said the obvious thing, because saying it would give it weight and it already weighed enough: there was no court. There was the Fourth Army's rear echelon, and there was the Fourth Army's provost, and somewhere above them both there were generals who needed the war to end cleanly, with tidy ledgers of their own, and a supply major who had spent eighteen months distributing bread to farmers' wives was not going to be fed to a firing squad on the testimony of a deserter and a clockmaker's apprentice. The papers would go into a channel. The channel had a name — Quentin knew the name of it, had heard it spoken with reverence in the mess tents: *the Bureau de Sûreté, liaison section*. And the channel ended in a drawer. Drawers were where truths went to die quietly in wartime, filed between a lost mule report and a requisition for boot polish.

"So we don't use the channels," Quentin said.

Elodie raised her eyebrows. "And what do we use instead?"

"I don't know yet." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "That's the honest answer. I've been carrying half a page for four months and telling myself that once I found the whole of it, once I put it in the right hands, everything would — resolve. Like a gear train. Tooth meets tooth and the whole mechanism turns." He picked up the route amendment and turned it over, although he had already memorized it. "But the right hands are Vasseur's hands, and Vasseur is the man who signed the order moving Luc's execution up a week."

"Vasseur is also the man who gave us until the armistice instead of shooting us on the spot," Elodie said. "I don't trust him. But I've noticed something about him. He reads everything. Twice. He read those torn ledger pages you saw him take, and he didn't burn them, and he didn't file them either — I checked the requisition log, they're not entered anywhere. He's keeping them *off* the record. A man burying evidence doesn't keep it off the record unless he intends to look at it again."

"That's not justice," Quentin said. "That's a man chewing on something."

"A man chewing on something can be made to swallow," Elodie said, and then flushed, as if surprised by her own voice. "Sorry. Marguerite's been teaching me to bargain. She does it with flour prices every market day. She says you never open with what you want. You open with what they're afraid of."

"And what's a captain afraid of?"

"Not the same things a private is afraid of." She gathered the papers into their oilcloth with a care that made his chest ache — she handled Augustin's cache the way she handled Augustin's tools, as if the dead man might still feel it. "A private is afraid of dying. An officer is afraid of *appearing* — appearing soft, appearing disloyal, appearing to have permitted disorder under his command. Renard made your friend Luc appear guilty because appearance was cheaper than investigation. So whatever we do, we can't hand these papers upward and hope. Upward is a drawer." She tied the cloth shut. "We hand them sideways. To the one officer who has already proven he can't stop reading."

"The armistice is coming," Quentin said. "Everyone says within days. Maybe tomorrow, maybe a week. And the moment it comes—"

"—the war ends and everything gets buried faster," she agreed. "A peace treaty is the biggest drawer of all. After the eleventh of November nobody wants to reopen anything. They'll want to go home. They'll want it to be over." She looked toward the window, toward the blacked-out town below where the last few lit windows of the workshop glowed faint gold. "Which means whatever happens has to happen before the guns stop. While the rules are still broken enough that a captain can bend them."

* * *

They came down through the tower in the dark, hand over hand along the rail, and found the workshop full of lamplight and argument.

Marguerite had brought bread and, miraculously, a jar of preserved plums that she guarded like ordnance. Tomas sat in the corner with his hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking, and Petit lay on his stomach on the floor with the boy's eternal pencil stub, copying — of all things — the serial numbers off a box of spare escapements, because Petit copied everything, as if writing the world down might keep the world from being taken away. The radio, or rather the rumor mill that served in place of one, had delivered its evening bulletin via the baker's cousin: the German delegation had crossed the lines; the talks were stalled again over the fleet; the fighting continued everywhere.

"They're stalling on purpose," Marguerite said, slicing bread with a knife that had seen better decades. "Every day they stall, another thousand men die, and every day our major gets another day to cover his tracks. That's how I read it, anyway. Men like that always need just one more day."

"Then we don't give him one more day," Quentin said.

He stood in the middle of the workshop, and the whole room turned to him, and he felt the strangest sensation — he, who had spent four months being hunted, being hidden, being a secret passed from cellar to cellar like contraband — he felt *listened to*. It was a dangerous feeling. It was also, he discovered, the first good feeling he'd had since March.

"We can't send this through the army's channels," he said, laying the oilcloth packet on the workbench beside Elodie's scattered files and pin-wires. "So we don't. Here's what we know about Captain Vasseur. He's not Renard's man — if he were, Renard wouldn't need to shell the observatory, he'd simply ask. Vasseur obeys orders he hates, and I think he hates them more each week, and I think the reason he hasn't reported the ledger pages he took from us is that some part of him is looking for permission. For a way to do the right thing that doesn't require him to commit career suicide in the process." He paused. "Or I'm wrong, and he's cleverer than all of us, and we hang ourselves."

"Cheerful," said Tomas, to no one in particular.

"So we don't give him the choice of believing *us*," Quentin went on. "Deserters lie. Townspeople under duress lie. We give him the papers themselves — seals, dates, the farmer's letter, all of it in Augustin's hand and the army's own ink — and we let him reach the conclusion before we say a word. And we give him a deadline. Not ours. *His*. We tell him: the moment the armistice comes, this goes into the drawers forever, and Renard retires a hero, and Sergeant Luc Berthier dies for it — because mark me, they'll push the execution through before the guns stop, they always do, the last executions of a war are the ones nobody asks questions about afterward."

Petit had stopped copying. The pencil hung motionless in his fingers.

"There's one more thing," Quentin said, and now his voice did shake, and he let it. "Vasseur signed Luc's death warrant. I've hated him for it since I learned his name. But a warrant isn't a hanging — not yet — and a man who signed a warrant can un-sign one, and the army respects nothing so much as a correction issued quietly. If Vasseur moves against Renard using our papers, Luc's conviction becomes *evidence tampered with*, becomes a stain *Renard* left on *his* command. Vasseur doesn't pardon Luc out of mercy. He pardons him out of pride. Marguerite's rule. Open with what they're afraid of."

Silence, except for the master clock's tick coming faintly down through the floor, that great patient heartbeat Elodie had been nursing back to health for weeks.

"He'll want something in return," Marguerite said at last. "Captains always do. What do we have that a captain wants?"

"The clock," Elodie said quietly.

Everyone looked at her.

"He told me. The day he searched my bench — he said an army runs on morale and morale runs on symbols, and a town whose clock strikes true on the day the war ends — he said it would look well in his report. He wants the eleventh hour to strike from *his* town, on *his* watch. Fine." Her chin lifted, and Quentin saw, with a jolt, how much of Augustin lived in her without her knowing it. "Let him have his symbol. Let him write his report. In exchange: the prisoners live, Renard faces a real inquiry — not a drawer, an *inquiry* — and these papers are read aloud somewhere they can't be unpicked. And when the clock strikes eleven on the eleventh day, I want the square full of people hearing it. Because a truth read aloud in front of a crowd is the one document no drawer can hold."

Tomas grunted, deep in his chest, the sound of a man who had rung bells through two wars and trusted few words but many sounds. "A crowd," he said, "is the loudest bell there is."

"And if Vasseur refuses?" Petit asked. His voice cracked in the middle of the question, sixteen years old and trying to be a soldier about it. "If he takes the papers and takes us all?"

Quentin looked at the boy, and then around the lamplit room — at the baker with her floured forearms, the old bell-ringer with his ruined hands, the girl clockmaker standing in her dead master's shadow — and he understood suddenly why Augustin had built all this, name by name, debt by debt, rescue by quiet rescue. Not a conspiracy. A *mechanism*. Every part shaped to bear its share of the load, so that no single tooth had to carry the whole weight alone.

"Then we'll know before we've handed over the originals," he said. "Elodie copies everything tonight. Every page, twice, in her own hand — she's better at forgery than she admits." Elodie made a small outraged noise. "We give him the copies. The originals stay inside the pendulum case, where the soldiers already searched and didn't think to look. If Vasseur keeps faith, he gets the originals when Luc walks free. If he doesn't—" Quentin shrugged. "Then Renard's name goes somewhere else. Over Tomas's bells. From a crowd. By whatever road is left."

"Justice outside the law," Marguerite murmured. She said it without horror, the way she might have said *bread without yeast* — a recipe for hard times, tested and known.

Outside, far off toward the eastern ridge, a single gun spoke, then another, then a ragged handful, probing. Everyone in the room did the same thing at the same instant: they glanced upward, involuntarily, toward the ceiling, toward the tower, toward the clock that stood above them all keeping its count of the shrinking hours.

Then Elodie pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her, uncapped Augustin's ink bottle, and began to copy.

Quentin sat down across from her to check each page as she finished it, line against line, and the lamplight burned low and the night wore on, and somewhere in the middle of the third page Petit fell asleep on the floor with his pencil still in his fist, and Marguerite covered him with her shawl without stopping her argument with Tomas about whether captains could be trusted further than majors.

Near midnight, boots sounded in the street outside.

Not one pair. A patrol's worth, unhurried, coming up the hill from the direction of the observatory — and stopping.

A fist struck the workshop door, three times, official and cold.

Through the frosted glass of the transom, silhouetted against the lamplight of the street, Quentin could see the peaked cap and the pale slash of a face he knew: Captain Vasseur, alone, having dismissed his escort at the corner.

He had come without orders, Quentin realized. Which meant he had come to listen — or to arrest them all before dawn.

Elodie rose, wiped the ink from her fingers with terrible calm, and reached for the latch.
