## 15. Tomas Rings False

The bells had been Tomas's hands for sixty years, and now a lieutenant was telling him what to do with them.

"You'll ring at four o'clock tomorrow," the young man said. He stood in the bell tower's lower chamber with his cap under his arm, as though politeness might soften the order. "Not the full peal. The bourdon only, struck nine times. Then silence. The officers want to see how the town reacts."

Tomas sat on the rope bench with his palms flat on his knees. His hands, even at seventy-three, were still good hands — broad, cracked like riverbed clay, with the permanent shine of brass polish worked into every crease that no soap would ever lift. They had pulled these ropes through two wars, three funerals of state, the wedding of Marguerite's parents, and one terrible morning in '71 when he had rung the alarm for fire and rung it wrong, too late, and had spent fifty years trying to ring better than that day.

"A morale test," he said.

"Yes, sir. The men have heard rumors the armistice is close. Command wants to measure the town's readiness. If we ring peace bells prematurely, we'll learn where the panic sits."

"And if the town believes it?" Tomas asked. "If they pour into the square weeping, if they open their cellars, if they kiss strangers in the street — and then the shells come again?"

The lieutenant looked at the floor. He was perhaps nineteen, with the soft jaw of a boy who still wrote home to his mother, and he shifted his weight from boot to boot. "That is not my concern, sir."

"No," Tomas said. "It never is, at your age. It becomes yours later, when there's nothing left to do about it."

* * *

He climbed the stairs after the lieutenant left, because he always climbed them when he needed to think, and thinking required altitude. Two hundred and six steps in a spiral worn smooth by his own boots and, before him, by his father's, and before his father by old Guillaume who had rung for the Prussians' first arrival and died on the top step with his heart burst open, which Tomas had always considered the correct way for a bell-ringer to die — in the tower, among the ropes, within reach of the thing he loved.

The bells hung above him in the dark like sleeping animals. There were five: the little treble, Claire; the second, Jeanne; the third, Marie-Élisabeth; the fourth, whose name nobody remembered anymore and whom Tomas privately called La Vieux; and the great bourdon, Anne-Marie, three tonnes of bronze cast in 1741, whose voice could be felt in the soles of the feet from one end of the rue des Tanneurs to the other.

Sixty years, and he knew them the way a man knows his wife's breathing in the dark. He knew that Anne-Marie went slightly sharp in cold weather and that Claire developed a buzz in her headstock when the wind came from the northeast. He knew the exact pressure of rope that made each one speak clearly and the lazy half-second between strike and sound that gave bell-ringing its peculiar quality of prayer — you set the thing in motion and then you waited, humbly, for the voice to arrive.

And he knew something the army did not know, something no officer with his maps and his signal posts and his requisition papers could ever know: that the bells of Saint-Aubin spoke a language older than any of their orders.

His father had taught him, and his father had learned it from Guillaume, and Guillaume had learned it — so the story went — from the smugglers who used to work the border passes before anyone drew lines on maps worth dying for. A code of strikes and silences, rung in the gaps between official ringing, buried inside ordinary hours so thoroughly that only an ear trained across generations could find it. Three quick strikes of the treble meant a stranger in town. The bourdon struck once, off-pattern, in the dead hours meant *all is lost*. And there were warnings — warnings for raids, for searches, for soldiers moving where soldiers shouldn't be.

It had been used perhaps a dozen times in Tomas's life. Once during the last war, to warn a family of a conscription sweep. Twice for fires. Never, in all those years, for anything like this.

* * *

That evening he walked down to the workshop on the rue des Forgerons, taking the long way past the bakery out of habit, though the bakery was shuttered now, Marguerite having been ordered to bake only for the garrison. The town had gone strange under requisition. Sandbags in doorways. Soldiers' bicycles stacked against the fountain like felled trees. The smell of latrines dug where flower beds used to be. And everywhere the new quiet — not peace, but held breath, the whole town listening for the next shell the way a man listens for the next word of a sentence he dreads.

At the workshop door he paused, as he always did, beneath the lantern.

Augustin's lantern. It still hung there, unlit per the blackout orders, but present — a black iron cage of a thing that had swung over that doorway for forty years. People said you could always tell when Augustin was working late because the lantern burned, and people in trouble learned to read it like scripture: lit meant sanctuary, dark meant come back tomorrow but come back. When Augustin died — quietly, in his chair, mid-repair, a gear still in his tweezers — the town had kept expecting someone to relight it, and nobody had, and Elodie Okafor had arrived two weeks later with her master's tools and her master's silences and had simply begun.

Tomas knocked the pattern he'd knocked for thirty years: two, pause, three.

Elodie opened the door. Behind her the workshop glowed with lamplight shielded by blackout cloth, warm as a hearth in a winter that had nothing else warm in it. He could see the clock case open like a patient on a table, its pendulum exposed, and Marguerite's flour-white forearms as she stirred something on the spirit stove, and young Petit cross-legged on the floor sorting screws by size with the fierce concentration of a child defusing a bomb.

"Tomas." Elodie stepped aside. "You look like a man carrying weather."

"I am." He came in and let Marguerite push him into the good chair, because refusing her hospitality had never once succeeded in forty years. She put coffee in front of him — real coffee, or whatever Marguerite's hoarded emergency supply passed for, bitter enough to strip paint — and stood over him with her arms crossed until he drank.

"An officer came to the tower today," he said. "Tomorrow at four o'clock I'm to ring the bourdon nine times. Alone. No peal. They're testing the town's nerves — ringing peace early to see who panics."

Marguerite spat into the stove, which was her way of commenting on most matters of policy. Petit looked up from his screws. Quentin, who had been mending a coat sleeve in the corner with his wounded shoulder propped against the wall, went very still — the stillness of a man who had learned that sudden movements attracted attention, and who now did everything slowly, deliberately, like a man wading through water he suspected was full of hooks.

"Four o'clock," Elodie said. "Why four?"

"Because it's plausible," Tomas said. "Rumors say the armistice talks are near. If the bells rang peace at noon, no one would believe it — too neat. At four, in the gray hour, tired people believe things. That's the arithmetic of it. Cold as a ledger." He heard himself use the word and saw it land — saw Elodie's hand move, just slightly, toward the drawer where she kept certain things, and stop.

"There's more," he said. "The lieutenant mentioned it casually, the way they mention things they want you to think they're ashamed of. After the ringing, there'd be a 'routine inspection' of the workshops along this street. This street particularly." He looked around the room slowly, letting each face register what his old ears had already catalogued: the illegal lamplight, the deserter in the corner, the ledger that lived somewhere in this building like a heart behind ribs. "I don't think it's a test of the town's nerves, children. I think it's a test of mine. They want me ringing bells so loud I can't hear boots on cobbles."

* * *

They talked until the candle burned low — plans, contingencies, the small desperate logistics of people with almost no pieces left to move. Quentin should leave tonight, some said. No — he couldn't walk, said others; his shoulder hadn't healed and the roads were crawling with patrols. Hide him in the clock case itself, Petit suggested, with the wild seriousness of youth, and Marguerite laughed for the first time in days, a short hard bark with no joy in it.

Tomas sat in the good chair and listened, and while they argued he turned the problem over the way he turned a stubborn escapement in his fingers, feeling for the tooth that would catch.

They could not hide Quentin by morning. They could not move the ledger without risk. They could not refuse the inspection without confirming every suspicion Captain Vasseur already carried like a stone in his pocket. Every road forward was mined.

But the bells were his. The bells were the one thing in this town the army had to ask permission to touch, because you cannot requisition a voice, and in sixty years no soldier had climbed those two hundred and six steps alone and understood what he found there.

"Gentlemen," he said, and the room quieted, because Tomas rarely spoke above the volume of the coffee cup. "Let them have their four o'clock. Let me ring the bourdon nine times for their morale test. But I will ring it my way, and after it, in the settling silence, I will ring something else — three seconds apart, treble and second alternating, the old smuggler's figure. Anyone in this street who grew up here will feel it in their teeth without knowing why. It means *soldiers coming, hide now, minutes matter.*"

Elodie frowned. "Will Vasseur know? Will they hear the difference?"

"The difference between nine solemn strokes and nine solemn strokes plus eleven quiet ones?" Tomas smiled, and felt his face make the expression his father used to make, the old dry crack of it. "Captain, yes, maybe, if he were standing in the tower with a score sheet. But Vasseur will be wherever officers stand, which is nowhere useful. And the common soldier hears church bells the way cows hear rain. It's the household that needs to understand, and the household will." He looked around at them — the baker, the boy, the deserter, the clockmaker. "Marguerite, you'll be at your ovens at four?"

"Where else."

"Then when you hear the figure after the bourdon, bank your fires and get your girls into the cellar and stay there. Elodie — the ledger goes where it goes, and quickly. Petit—" The boy straightened like a soldier. "You'll be on the street at four. You're always on the street. Listen for the figure and count the boots that follow it down this road. Come tell us the number."

"And me?" Quentin asked quietly.

Tomas regarded him for a moment. The man was gaunt, healing badly, and carried himself like a debt he didn't know how to repay. "You," Tomas said, "will go up into the tower with me. You can climb two hundred steps with a bad shoulder if you take them slowly, and I need a second pair of hands on the brake lever in case my hands remember 1871 instead of today. Besides—" he allowed himself the smallest kindness, "—a man in your position ought to spend an hour somewhere the army can't see him. There isn't a spot in France more invisible than a bell loft during a rehearsal."

Quentin looked at him for a long time. Something moved behind his eyes — gratitude, or grief, or the particular exhaustion of a man who has stopped being surprised by either. "Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet." Tomas drained his coffee and stood, joints announcing themselves one by one like a carillon warming up. "Thank me tomorrow night, if we're all still free."

* * *

Four o'clock came gray and windless, the kind of afternoon that seemed to hold its breath.

Tomas stood in the loft with the ropes in his fists, Quentin beside him at the brake, and below them the town lay flattened under the light — rooftops, sandbags, the distant glitter of bayonets at the crossroads. Somewhere east, so far away it was more memory than sound, the guns muttered in their sleep.

He thought of his father. He thought of Guillaume dying on the top step. He thought of every funeral, every wedding, every midnight Christmas peal, every small hour when these bells had spoken for a town that could not always speak for itself.

Then he rang.

Nine strokes of the bourdon, slow, solemn, spaced exactly as the lieutenant had instructed — Anne-Marie's great voice rolling out over the roofs, filling the streets, pressing against windows, and Tomas watched through the louvres as doors opened below, as faces tilted upward, as an old woman in the rue des Tanneurs fell to her knees in the middle of the cobblestones because after four years, four years, the bells were saying *peace, peace, peace* — and it was a lie, and he was the one telling it, and the shame of it went through him worse than any shell.

Nine strokes. Silence, as ordered.

And into that silence — into the trembling air where the whole town stood weeping and waiting — Tomas rang the truth.

Treble. Second. Treble. Second. Eleven quiet figures, three seconds apart, small bright voices threaded through the fading thunder of the bourdon, meaningless to every ear in the garrison, and unmistakable as a shout to the three households on the rue des Forgerons who had grown up on stories of smugglers and secret signs. *Soldiers coming. Hide now. Minutes matter.*

Below, he saw Marguerite step out of the bakery doorway, glance up at the tower once — once, no more — and turn back inside with unhurried, terrible calm. He saw Petit detach himself from the fountain and begin to stroll, a boy idling, toward the workshop street. He felt Quentin beside him grip the brake lever, knuckles white.

"They heard," Quentin whispered.

"They heard," Tomas said.

And then, far down the boulevard, right on schedule, came the sound he had rung to meet: boots. Not a patrol — a formation. Two squads, rifles slung, moving at route-step toward the rue des Forgerons, and at their head a figure Tomas recognized even from this height, even through the louvres, by the set of his shoulders alone.

Not Vasseur. Worse. Renard.

The major walked like a man going to collect something, and Tomas, watching him come, understood with the clarity of an old man who has rung bells for death and disaster his whole life and knows their footsteps by heart:

Whatever happened next, it was no inspection at all.
