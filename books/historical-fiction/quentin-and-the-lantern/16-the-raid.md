## 16. The Raid

The warning came as Tomas had promised it would — not in words, but in bronze.

Elodie was bent over the pendulum case, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a smear of clock oil along her jaw, when the observatory's bell struck a sequence that had no business existing in any liturgy of hours. Two strokes, a pause, three more, then a single deep toll that seemed to come up through the floorboards rather than down from the tower. She had heard Tomas ring the Angelus, the quarters, the alarms for fire and flood; she had never heard this. But the pattern had been written into the ledger's margins in Augustin's careful hand — *if the bells speak wrongly, listen anyway* — and Marguerite, who was kneading bread in the back room as cover for her vigil at the window, went still with her fists buried in dough.

"That's it," Marguerite said quietly. "That's his false peal."

Even now, even with what was coming, Elodie felt a flare of something close to wonder. Augustin had built warnings into the town itself. He had taught an old bell-ringer a language of rebellion decades before there was anything worth rebelling against, and the old man had kept it folded away in his hands like a coin saved for a day he must have half-dreaded and half-hoped would never come.

Then the wonder burned off, and what remained was cold.

"Where is he?" she asked, though she already knew. Quentin had gone out at first light to the chandler's on the rue Basse, because Petit could not carry lamp oil and watch the street at the same time, and because hunger did not pause for raids.

"The oil shop," Marguerite said. Her voice was steady in the way bread is steady — dense, warm, made to hold things up. "He'll be coming back by the lane."

"He can't come back by the lane." Elodie wiped her hands on her apron, already moving. "If they're coming here, the lanes will be full of them. Marguerite — the ledger."

They had rehearsed this. That was the terrible mercy of the last week: every contingency had been walked through like a dance, over and over, until the steps lived somewhere below thought. The ledger came out of its hiding place behind the loose brick where Augustin had once hidden letters for deserters — a place so obvious in hindsight that Elodie had laughed, the one time she'd laughed all week, at how many years she'd swept past it without knowing. Now she carried it across the workshop floor, past the bench where Augustin's loupe still sat in its velvet cradle, past the wall of drawers labeled in his handwriting — *escapements, springs, mainspring clamps, do not touch, E.* — to the great case of the master clock's smaller sister, the regulator clock that stood seven feet tall against the east wall.

She opened the case door. The pendulum hung motionless; she had stopped the regulator two days ago, telling Vasseur's sergeant it needed a new suspension spring, and the lie had been true enough to be safe. Behind the pendulum bob, screwed into the case's inner wall, was a bracket Augustin had installed years ago for a weight tray. She slid the ledger flat into the space between bracket and wall, wrapped in oilcloth, and swung the heavy brass pendulum bob down in front of it like a curtain drawn over a window.

A soldier searching a clock looked at the movement, the face, the weights. No one looked at the pendulum. A pendulum was the most honest thing in the world; it only knew how to swing.

"Again," Marguerite said. "Do it again. Your hands were shaking."

"My hands were not—"

"They were. Again."

So Elodie took the ledger out and put it back in, twice more, until her fingers moved without her permission and the oilcloth knot tied itself in the dark by touch alone. Marguerite watched her with flour to her elbows and an expression Elodie couldn't read — pride, perhaps, or grief, or the particular tenderness of a woman watching a girl become something harder and being sorry for it.

Outside, boots. Many boots, in step, coming down the rue de l'Horloge.

* * *

They knocked the way armies knock: once, formally, and then not at all.

The door splintered at the jamb and four soldiers came through it into the warm smell of bread and machine oil, rifles first, faces second. Elodie had imagined she would be afraid. What she felt instead, standing in the middle of Augustin's workshop with her heart going like a runaway train, was a furious, clarifying anger — anger at the mud on the floorboards, at the rifle barrel that clipped a shelf of finished watches and sent them ringing to the ground like a carillon of small catastrophes, at the young lieutenant who read from a paper in a bored voice as though he were reciting a laundry list.

"By order of Captain Vasseur, these premises are to be searched. All persons will remain where they are. All documents, correspondence, and written records are subject to seizure."

"All persons," Elodie repeated, before she could stop herself. "There are two of us. You can see the whole room."

"I can see a back room," the lieutenant said. "And a cellar stair."

Marguerite lifted her chin. "My oven is lit. If you want to stand between me and my ovens while my loaves burn, you'll answer to every hungry soldier in this garrison, and I will tell each of them personally whose fault it was."

It was magnificent and it bought them ninety seconds, no more. Two soldiers went down to the cellar, where Tomas waited among the coal bins — too old to run, refusing to leave, sitting with his hands folded as if he'd been expecting company. Another went to the back room and came out with a fistful of Petit's drawings, the boy's careful pencil sketches of gears and birds, which he dropped on the floor without looking at them. Elodie watched a heel come down on one and felt it in her chest like a cracked tooth.

And through all of it, the regulator clock stood against the east wall with its stopped pendulum and its secret, ticking nothing, saying nothing, holding its breath the way she was holding hers.

They pulled the drawers out. They emptied Augustin's tool chest onto the floor — his father's tools, some of them, handles worn to the shape of dead men's grips — and Elodie made herself watch, made herself catalog each indignity, because she understood suddenly that remembering was also a kind of resistance and someone would need to remember exactly what had been taken. They tipped over the workbench. They found the loose brick behind which the ledger had slept for a week and pried it free and found only dust and an older, emptier hollow, and the lieutenant frowned at it, and Elodie let her breath out one millimeter at a time.

"Whose bookkeeping is this?" He held up a scrap of paper — an invoice, harmless, for brass rod and gut line.

"Mine," Elodie said. "I keep the accounts now. My master is dead."

"So we've heard." His eyes came around the room and snagged, as she'd known they would, on the tall case of the regulator. "Open it."

"It's broken," she said. "Suspension spring. I told Sergeant Duret—"

"Open it."

Her body wanted to refuse — wanted it so badly her knees actually locked, and for one absurd moment she stood there like a child refusing a bath while four rifles rearranged themselves politely in her direction. Then Marguerite's hand settled on her shoulder, heavy and warm as a baking stone, and Elodie heard her own voice say, levelly, "Of course. It's heavy. The key is stiff."

She opened the case door with the steadiness of ten thousand repetitions. She stepped aside.

The lieutenant looked at the movement. He looked at the face, with its painted Roman numerals and its hairline crack running through the eleven. He looked at the weights, and he reached in and touched the pendulum bob — touched it, Elodie thought afterward, the way a man might touch a coffin lid — and gave it a small push.

The pendulum swung. Once, twice, three times, silver arcs through the lamplight, the escapement clicking softly beneath the floor of the movement, and the ledger rode behind it in its oilcloth shroud, invisible, unbothered, keeping its own time. The lieutenant watched it slow and stop, because there was no driving weight to feed it, and whatever he saw in that dying swing satisfied him, or disappointed him, or simply bored him.

"Broken," he agreed. He turned away. "Nothing here. Move to the chandlery next — and you two," he added, without looking at them, "stay where you can be found."

And that should have been the end of it. That was the shape of the relief she had rehearsed: the raid comes, the raid passes, the ledger sleeps on. Elodie stood in the wreckage of the workshop listening to boots fade down the rue de l'Horloge, and she let herself believe, for eleven entire seconds, that they had won.

Then she heard the footsteps in the lane behind the house. Unhurried ones. And Marguerite said, very softly, "Oh, no," and Elodie knew.

* * *

Quentin came in through the kitchen door carrying two tins of lamp oil and the particular looseness of a man trying to look ordinary and failing at it. He saw their faces and stopped.

"What," he said. "What happened."

"They searched," Elodie said. "It's fine. The ledger's safe. Quentin, they're still on the street, you have to go out the way you came—"

But even as she spoke she was reading him, the way she had learned to read movements and trains and the weather of other people's fear, and what she read made her stomach drop through the floor. He wasn't listening. His eyes had gone past her, past Marguerite, to the cellar doorway, where Tomas was climbing the stairs slowly with one hand pressed to the wall — and beyond him, framed in the shattered front door, two soldiers had appeared in the street outside, and one of them was pointing.

Later, she would understand exactly how it happened. The chandlery had been raided within minutes of the workshop; the oil seller, terrified, had mentioned a young man buying lamp oil who paid in army scrip and flinched at loud noises. A runner had been sent. The timing was nobody's failure and everybody's. War did not need anyone to make mistakes; it only needed everyone to be in two places at once, which no one ever was.

"Sergeant," the nearer soldier called into the house, conversationally. "Found your stranger. Been asking after him since Tuesday."

Quentin set the oil tins down on the kitchen table with exaggerated care, as if the world were still a place where objects deserved gentleness. Elodie watched his hands and saw that they were shaking, and hated herself for noticing, and noticed anyway.

"Quentin," she said. His name came out of her before she decided anything about it. "Tell them you're my apprentice. Tell them you're—"

"Elodie." He smiled at her, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, because it was kind. "You've got flour on your face and oil on your hands and a dead man's workshop full of soldiers, and I'm a deserter with half a page of contraband sewn in my coat. There isn't a story you can tell fast enough." He was already pulling the coat open himself, saving them the trouble, saving her the sight of it being done to him. "Besides. If they take me quiet, they may not look hard at what's left behind. If they take me after a fight, they'll turn this place inside out twice."

"That's not—" Her voice cracked and she hated that too. "That is not how we decided—"

"We never decided this part." He shrugged, and winced, and she remembered the wound in his side that had never properly healed, the reason he'd fallen through her door in the first place, bleeding and desperate and calling for the lantern keeper. "Nobody ever decides this part. It just arrives, like weather." He looked past her at Marguerite. "Madame, thank you for the bread. It was the first real bread I've had since Verdun, and I don't mean the flour. I mean—" He stopped, because the sentence was too big for the room. "Thank you."

Tomas had reached the top of the cellar stairs. The old man stood there in his coal-dusted coat, straightening himself joint by joint until he was as tall as age allowed, and looked at the two soldiers in the doorway with the expression of a man who had rung bells for forty years and knew precisely how much noise dignity could make.

"You'll want to walk in front of them, boy," Tomas said. "Never behind. Behind is for prisoners."

"I'm ahead of you, old man," Quentin said, and stepped toward the door.

And here is what Elodie did, though for weeks afterward she could not say whether it was courage or panic or simply the reflexes of a girl who had spent her life fixing things that broke at the worst possible moment: she moved. She crossed the workshop floor in six strides and caught Quentin's sleeve, and the soldiers tensed, and Marguerite's hand closed on her shoulder again — but gently this time, holding her back rather than holding her up — and Elodie said, low and fast, words that had been waiting in her since the night she'd hidden a stranger's page inside a stranger's clock:

"The lantern room. Augustin's papers are in the lantern room. Find a way to get word to me — Petit, the baker's boy, he runs messages for the garrison, they trust him — and I swear to you on my master's grave that what's in those papers will pull Luc out of that cell if it pulls the tower down doing it. Do you hear me? You are not leaving this unfinished. You are not *allowed*—"

"Elodie." He put his hand over hers on his sleeve. His palm was cold and rough and shaking slightly, and she thought, absurdly, of a mainspring wound too tight. "Eleven days," he said. "Eleventh hour. Listen for the clock."

Then the soldiers had his arms, and the door frame was empty, and the street took him.

* * *

Afterward there was a silence of the specific, ringing kind that follows a bell being struck — a silence shaped exactly like the sound that had filled it, so that everywhere Elodie turned she seemed to see him still: the loose way he'd stood, the terrible kindness of his smile, the tins of lamp oil sitting on the kitchen table like an offering no one would need now.

She picked up the nearest tin and threw it at the wall. It burst in a bloom of oil and clangor, and the sound was satisfying for exactly one second, and then she was on her knees in the wreckage of Augustin's workshop surrounded by spilled watches and scattered drawings and the smell of lamp oil, sobbing the way she hadn't sobbed at her master's funeral, because grief for the dead has a shape and grief for the taken does not — it just pours, everywhere, into everything, under every door.

Marguerite knelt with her. Tomas lowered himself onto the stairs like a man setting down a weight carried too far. Nobody said *there, there*, or *it will be well*, because they were not liars, and the four of them stayed that way in the ruins of the morning while somewhere above them the observatory clock ticked on, indifferent and exact, counting down the hours to an armistice that was rumored to be days away — hours in which a court-martial could convene, in which a firing squad could be scheduled, in which a man could be shot at dawn for the crime of having loved his friend too much to let him die alone.

It was Petit who finally broke the silence. He came in through the kitchen, white-faced, having seen the arrest from the corner of the rue Basse, and stood over them with his cap wrung in his fists like a killed bird.

"They're taking him to the citadel," the boy said. "To the cells with the other one. And Madame Okafor—" His voice failed and restarted. "Madame Okafor, the officer they gave him to. The one taking the report. I've seen him before. He was with Major Renard. He's Renard's man."

Elodie lifted her head. Through the broken front door, far off across the ruined roofs of the town, she could see the observatory tower, and the lantern room at its top where Augustin's papers waited in the dark — and beside it, impossibly patient, the clock face with its cracked eleven, still keeping the only honest time left in France.

"Then we have until dawn," she said, "to climb."
