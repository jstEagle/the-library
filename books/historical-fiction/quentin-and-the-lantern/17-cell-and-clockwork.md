## 17. Cell and Clockwork

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, a smell that got into the back of the throat and stayed there, so that Quentin could not tell after the first hour whether he was breathing air or rust. There were two cells in the requisitioned granary's cellar, separated by a wall of fieldstone laid down by some farmer's grandfather and never meant to hold men. The mortar had crumbled in places. Through one such gap, low near the floor, where the stones had shifted apart like teeth gone loose in an old jaw, he could hear Luc breathing.

That was how they spoke.

Not at first. The first hours there had been only the sound of a man trying to keep himself together in the dark — the small involuntary noises of it, the swallowed coughs, the shifting of a body against straw that some previous prisoner had left flattened and gray. Quentin had pressed his palm flat to the cold stone and said nothing, because what was there to say? *I deserted to find you. I found the proof. And now I am on this side of the wall and you are on that side, and between us stands a captain who may or may not hang us both.* He had said none of it. He had listened instead to Luc breathe, counting the rhythm of it like a clockmaker counts a pendulum, and when the breathing went ragged he tapped once on the stone, softly, with one knuckle. A single tap. *Still here.*

After a while, Luc tapped back.

They built a language out of almost nothing. One tap: I am here. Two taps: sleep if you can. Three taps, slow: tell me something true. That last one Luc used most often, and Quentin understood why — a condemned man does not want comfort; he wants witness. So Quentin told him things through the stone gap, his mouth close to the crumbling mortar, his voice pitched below the hearing of any guard who might pass overhead. He told him about the workshop, about the smell of oil and beeswax and the way Elodie Okafor bent over a movement the way other women bent over letters from home. He told him about Augustin's ledger, about the hundreds of names written in a hand so small and patient it looked like stitching. He told him about Marguerite's bread smuggled in a laundry basket, about old Tomas who could make a bell say more than a priest, about Petit, sixteen years old and lying for the first time in his life and probably lying badly, God help him.

And when he told him about the ledger, about the entry that should have existed and did not — the page torn away, the truth of the convoy stolen along with the convoy itself — Luc was quiet for a long time. Then three taps, slow. *Tell me something true.*

"The truth," Quentin whispered into the gap, "is that I came for you. Everything else came after."

There was no tap for a long while. Then, faintly, the sound he knew too well from their years in the same company: Luc laughing without any breath behind it, the laugh of a man saving his strength.

* * *

On the second day, or perhaps the third — the cellar had no windows and time pooled down there like water in a bilge — footsteps came down the stairs that were different from the others. The guards walked heavy and bored, boots striking carelessly. These steps came slow, measured, each one placed as though the stair might object. Quentin knew before the lantern light spilled across the cellar floor whose they were. He had heard Captain Vasseur walk exactly that way across the observatory yard, a man perpetually testing whether the ground would hold him.

Vasseur stopped outside the cell. The lantern threw his shadow long across the straw, and Quentin saw that he carried papers under one arm — folded, tied with string, the string knotted with the particular fussiness of a staff officer.

"You are awake," Vasseur said. It was not a question. In the dark, everyone is awake; sleep is a thing that belongs to men aboveground.

Quentin got up from the straw. His side still pulled where the shrapnel had caught him crossing the fields, the wound bound now with someone's careful bandaging — a nurse's work, or a doctor's, which meant someone in the garrison had decided he ought to live at least until whatever came next. "Captain."

"I am not here to interrogate you." Vasseur turned the papers over in his hands, once, twice. In the lantern light Quentin saw that the paper was old, cream-colored, ruled faintly — ledger paper. His heart began to knock against his ribs. "I have been reading," the captain continued, in the tone of a man confessing to a vice rather than announcing a discovery, "an account book. Or part of one. Pages recovered during a search of a certain workshop." He paused. "You know the document I mean."

Quentin said nothing. Silence was the only currency he had left, and he meant to spend it slowly.

"Forty-one years of entries." Vasseur's voice dropped, not to a whisper but to something adjacent to one, a voice ashamed of its own volume. "Debts forgiven. Men hidden. Letters carried through lines that no letter had any business crossing. A woman in 1904 whose husband was to be shot for striking an officer — and the officer, it seems, was persuaded to recant his account of the blow. Persuaded how, the ledger does not say. It only says *settled*, with a date, in a hand like embroidery." He looked up, and in that moment Quentin saw past the uniform entirely, saw a tired man in his forties with ink stains on his fingers and a war sitting on his shoulders like wet sand. "Do you understand what I am telling you, deserter? I have spent eleven days reading the private conscience of this town, and every page of it makes my own orders look like — "

He stopped. He straightened. The uniform closed back over him like water over a dropped stone.

"Like what?" Quentin asked quietly.

"Like the paperwork of men who will never be audited." Vasseur set the papers down on the floor, deliberately, just inside the cell bars' reach, then stepped back. "Major Renard has requested the remainder of the recovered documents. I have told him the search yielded nothing further. This is the last time I will be able to tell him that." He turned toward the stairs, then halted, his back to the cell. "Your friend in the next room. Luc Marchal. The charge is theft of army property and the sentence is confirmed. It goes up for review after the offensive. After the armistice, if the rumors hold."

"And if the rumors don't hold?"

"Then sooner." The lantern light trembled once — the captain's hand, Quentin realized, not the flame. "I read your name in those pages too. Not yours — your father's. 1892. A debt of four hundred francs forgiven, marked *paid in bread and silence*. I begin to think this whole country has been kept standing by men like the one who wrote that book, and none of us ever knew it, and now we are burning it all down around their ledgers." He climbed three steps. Stopped again. Did not turn around. "If you have anything worth trading, deserter, trade it soon. I cannot hold this door shut with my body forever."

Then the light went up the stairs and the dark came back, and Quentin stood with his heart hammering and the ghost of the captain's words hanging in the wet cold air.

He crossed to the wall gap and put his mouth to it.

"Luc. Are you awake?"

Two taps. Then, after a moment: three, slow.

"He's read it," Quentin breathed. "Vasseur. He's read Augustin's pages and he's wavering — you can hear it in him, Luc, he's a man standing on a cracking floor and pretending it's solid. But Renard wants the rest destroyed, and Vasseur can't stall much longer. We have days. Maybe less."

Through the stone came a sound that might have been a sigh. Then Luc's voice itself, hoarse, close to the gap, so quiet it was barely more than the mortar whispering. "Then we need the rest of it. All of it. The pages they didn't find."

"Elodie has them."

"Then she'd better bring them," Luc said, "before Renard brings the firing squad instead."

* * *

She came on the fourth day, under escort, carrying a toolbox.

Quentin heard her before he saw her — that quick light tread he would have known anywhere now, on stairs, in dreams — and then the guards were unlocking the cell and Elodie Okafor stood in the doorway with a leather case in her hands and an expression on her face that was pure professional composure stretched thin over something molten. Behind her, uninvited and unbothered, stood Petit, holding a lantern and wearing the studied blankness of a boy who has recently discovered lying and is terrified anyone can see it on him.

"The master clock in the granary tower," Elodie announced to the guard, in the crisp accent she saved for officers, "has a seized escapement and a cracked suspension spring. Captain Vasseur has ordered it restored as a symbol of order. To do that I require the prisoner."

The guard blinked. "Requires the — "

"The clock's strike train drives off the same weight line as the tower's signal mechanism," she said, with the serene confidence of a woman inventing horology at speed. "Its regulation affects the accuracy of every timing in this post. Do you want to explain to Major Renard why his artillery clocks are running four minutes slow?"

No one wanted that. No one in the entire garrison wanted that. The guard fetched a chair, and Quentin was brought up from the cellar in leg irons and seated beside the great clock's open case in the tower's ground chamber, where the movement hung exposed like a dissected animal — wheels and arbors and the long pendulum swinging its slow metronome of seconds. Elodie knelt at the case with her tools laid out in perfect order, and Petit stationed himself by the door, and for a few minutes there was nothing in the world but the tick of the escapement and the small bright sounds of her pliers.

Then, without looking up, without changing the rhythm of her work, she spoke.

"Vasseur came to the workshop yesterday. Alone, out of uniform coat, at dusk, like a man visiting a mistress or a confessor. He put the recovered pages on my bench and asked me if I knew what they were." Her voice stayed level, conversational, pitched for Quentin alone beneath the clock's voice. "I told him they were Augustin's. I told him I could prove everything in them, because I've spent three weeks learning how to prove it — matching entries to Tomas's bell records, to Marguerite's flour accounts, to the parish register. Every rescue in that book leaves a fingerprint somewhere else. That is the offer I made him: the full ledger, complete, authenticated, every name corroborated — in exchange for Luc's sentence commuted and yours quashed. Both of you. Alive. Before the armistice, not after."

Quentin watched the pendulum swing. "And what did he say?"

"He said justice doesn't work that way. He said a captain cannot barter prisoners for evidence." She fitted a new suspension spring into its slot with a click so soft it was almost tender. "Then he stood there for a long time looking at Augustin's bench, and he said — I wrote it down afterward so I wouldn't lose the exact words — he said, *'My orders come from Major Renard, and Major Renard's orders come from a war, and nobody in that chain has read a single honest page in three years.'* And then he put on his coat and left, and he took the pages with him, and he did not give them to Renard. Yet."

"Yet," Quentin repeated.

"Yet." She finally looked up at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "So we finish the clock. Because Vasseur gave me until the armistice — the real one, when it comes — and he said that when the hour strikes, whatever is true had better be ready to be spoken aloud, because there will never be a quieter moment and never a louder audience. He said it like a threat." Her jaw tightened. "It wasn't a threat. It was a man handing us the only weapon he's allowed to carry."

From the door, Petit spoke up, his voice cracking on the first word. "Marguerite says the bells code is ready. Tomas has it worked out. Whatever happens — whatever they do — the town will hear it. All of it. Every name."

Quentin sat in the iron bracelets and felt something shift in his chest that he had not felt since he fell through the workshop door half-dead: not hope exactly, which was a luxury, but the thing underneath hope, the load-bearing beam of it. Purpose with a deadline.

"Elodie," he said. "When you go back down to the cells — take a message to Luc. Tell him—" His voice caught. He made it steady. "Tell him the ledger forgave my father four hundred francs and called it *bread and silence*. Tell him I intend to pay our own account the same way. And tell him to hold on. The clock is nearly done. When it strikes, everything Augustin buried is coming up out of the ground, and Renard can't shell all of it."

Elodie closed her toolbox. Beneath the tower's floor, muffled by stone, they could both hear the guards changing, boots on stairs — and among them, lighter and quicker than the rest, footsteps descending toward the cellar that did not belong to any soldier of the garrison.

Petit frowned at the doorway. "Someone's down there," he whispered. "Nobody's scheduled for the cells this hour."

Elodie rose slowly, one hand still resting on the open clock case, on the pendulum's warm brass. Above them the great wheel train turned, patient and indifferent, eating the seconds toward an hour none of them could yet name.

"Whoever it is," she said quietly, "they're going to Luc first."

She was already moving for the stairs, toolbox in hand, when the first bell of warning began — wrong, ragged, one stroke too many — ringing out from Tomas's tower across the darkening square.
