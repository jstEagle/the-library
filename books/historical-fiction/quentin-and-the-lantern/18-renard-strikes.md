## 18. Renard Strikes

The order came at half past two in the morning, typed on a field telegraph form that smelled of machine oil and other men's sweat.

Vasseur read it twice by the light of a guttering candle in the requisitioned schoolhouse that served as his headquarters. The paper was ordinary enough — flimsy stock, the army's cheapest — and yet it seemed to weigh more than any dispatch he had ever held. *By direction of Major Renard, Signals Directorate: the observatory tower of Saint-Aubin-le-Bas constitutes an unregistered signal hazard within range of enemy observation. Recommend immediate reduction by artillery. Coordinate with 74th Battery for morning registration. Acknowledge.*

*Reduction by artillery.* The phrase was a masterpiece of staff language. Not *destroy*. Not *shell*. *Reduce*, as though the tower were an obstruction to be planed away, a splinter drawn from the flesh of some great operational body. As though the clock inside it were not the oldest working mechanism in the valley, older than the Republic, older than the war itself.

Vasseur set the paper down and looked at his hands. They were steady. They had been steady through Verdun, through the Chemin des Dames, through every ordered horror of four years, and he had told himself for four years that steadiness was the same thing as conscience. That if his hands did not shake, then whatever they did could be squared with duty.

He no longer believed it. He was not certain when he had stopped.

Outside, the town lay under its blanket of blackout, and beyond it the guns murmured their insomniac conversation along the ridge. Somewhere in that darkness stood the observatory, its lantern room dark now per regulation, and below it the workshop where the clockmaker's apprentice worked through the nights on an escapement she had promised him would be finished before the armistice came. She had bargained with him like a woman twice her age, offering him the master clock as a symbol of order, and he had taken the bargain because taking it had been easier than asking himself why a girl of nineteen should have to bargain with an officer of the Third Republic for the life of a deserter who had done nothing but love his friend too much to watch him die for another man's theft.

He thought of her hands, small and ink-stained, holding out the torn ledger page as though it were a live shell.

He thought of Major Renard's hands, which were always immaculate.

*Acknowledge,* said the form.

Vasseur did not acknowledge it. He sat very still in the cold schoolhouse and listened to the guns, and understood, with the clarity that comes only in the smallest hours, that he had arrived at the place toward which his whole war had been trending — the place where a man must finally decide what he is.

* * *

Renard arrived himself at seven, which was unusual. Majors did not come down from divisional headquarters to inspect the reduction of a signal hazard; they sent captains, or worse, lieutenants eager to please. But Renard came, riding in a staff car with mud to its axles, wearing a greatcoat too fine for the front and an expression of brisk administrative sorrow.

"Captain." He stepped into the schoolhouse without waiting to be invited, bringing with him the smell of petrol and cologne, an impossible combination this close to the line. "You've received my recommendation."

"I received it," Vasseur said.

"And?" Renard unwound a scarf with the care of a man removing bandages. "I confess I expected the acknowledgment hours ago. The battery commander has been asking."

"The battery commander can go on asking." Vasseur heard his own voice and did not recognize it. It was flat, unhurried. "Major, walk with me."

Renard's eyebrows rose a fraction — the infinitesimal affront of a man accustomed to rooms rearranging themselves around him — but he followed Vasseur out into the street, perhaps because refusing would itself have been a kind of acknowledgment.

The morning was raw and gray, the sky the color of dishwater, smoke standing up over the ridge in slow columns. Vasseur led him up the rue de l'Église until the observatory rose above the rooftops ahead of them, black against the gray, its dome catching the first thin light like a dull coin.

"There it is," Renard said. "Your hazard."

"Do you know when it was built?"

"I'm given to understand it predates the war."

"Seventeen sixty-one." Vasseur had asked Tomas, two days ago, standing in the bell loft while the old man pretended to oil machinery that had not needed oil since the Second Empire. "An astronomer built it — a royal appointment, before the Revolution took his head. The clock beneath it has struck the hour of this town for a hundred and fifty-seven years. Through 'seventy. Through fourteen. Every hour, Major. There are people in Saint-Aubin who set their lives by it the way other men set them by the sun."

Renard smiled the smile of a patient schoolmaster. "And there are men on that ridge who will set their sights by it, Captain, unless we remove the temptation. A tower is a tower. An observation post is an observation post. Sentiment is a luxury the General Staff stopped issuing in August of 1914."

"Is it a hazard? Genuinely?"

"Genuinely." Renard said it without hesitation, without the flicker of an eye that betrays invention, and Vasseur understood that the man was either telling the truth or so practiced a liar that truth and lies had long ago become interchangeable tools. "Enemy balloon observers have noted it. Whether they've ranged it yet I can't say. But it *will* be ranged, Captain, because everything will be ranged eventually — that is the nature of this war. We can reduce it now, on our own schedule, cleanly, or we can wait for the Boche to do it for us, messily, possibly with one of my own signal sections standing on its roof at the time."

"And the armistice."

"What of it?"

"They say it comes within days. Hours, some say. If the talks at Rethondes conclude—"

"If," said Renard, softly, and the word dropped between them like a stone into a well. "We do not conduct operations on the basis of rumors, Captain. We conduct them on the basis of orders. My order stands. Your acknowledgment is overdue." He turned to go, then paused, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its schoolroom warmth entirely, leaving something colder underneath, something with teeth. "You've been reading recovered documents. Papers seized in a raid on a civilian workshop. You'll want to be careful with those. Provenance matters. Some papers are worth exactly the trouble they cause, and the trouble they cause tends to land on the officers who mishandled them."

It was not a threat, formally. Formally, it was advice. Vasseur had been in the army long enough to know that the most dangerous orders were never written down, and the sharpest threats never spoken.

"I'll consider your recommendation, Major," he said.

"Do better than consider." Renard settled his scarf. "Consider what happened at Château-Thierry to the last captain who let a civilian's sentimentality compromise a firing solution. Then send the acknowledgment. By noon." He paused at the corner, and glanced back once, up at the tower, and for a single unguarded instant something moved behind his face — something quick and hungry and afraid — and was gone. "Noon, Captain."

Then he was gone, and the staff car coughed away through the muddy streets, and Vasseur stood alone in the cold with a hundred and fifty-seven years of hourly bells hanging over him like a question.

* * *

He went to the workshop.

He told himself it was an inspection. The fiction lasted approximately eleven steps.

Elodie Okafor opened the door before he knocked — she had developed, in these weeks, the uncanny awareness of a hunted animal — and looked at him with those level dark eyes that gave nothing away except, lately, a kind of exhausted contempt that she no longer bothered to disguise. Behind her, the workshop smelled of brass filings, lamp oil, and bread; Marguerite had been baking again, defiance expressed in flour. The boy Petit sat cross-legged on the floor sorting screws by size with the solemnity of a cardinal sorting relics.

"Mademoiselle. May I come in?"

"You're the army," she said. "You may come anywhere. That appears to be the arrangement."

He came in. The clock's pendulum case stood open against the wall — he knew precisely what was hidden inside it, had known since the interrogation, and had said nothing, and the not-saying had been accumulating in him like water behind a failing dam. On the bench lay the escapement, disassembled into its jeweled parts, each piece laid out on black cloth like surgery.

"How long until it runs?" he asked.

Her chin lifted slightly. "Why does the army care?"

"Because at noon I am expected to sign an order calling for the destruction of the tower it lives in."

Silence took the room. Even Petit's hands went still among the screws. Marguerite emerged from the back room wiping her arms, and old Tomas appeared in the doorway with the unhurried inevitability of a man who had spent seventy years listening for things.

"Shelling it," Elodie said at last. Her voice did not rise. "They mean to shell the observatory."

"Renard means to. He frames it as a signal hazard." Vasseur heard how the sentence sounded — how the word *frames* betrayed him — and found that he did not care. "There are legitimate arguments for it. There always are. That is the discovery of my middle age, mademoiselle: every crime I have watched this war commit was argued first, and argued well, by reasonable men with maps."

"And you?" Marguerite said quietly. "Are you one of the reasonable men?"

He looked at her. The baker's widow, whose husband had died at Charleroi, whose debt Augustin Ferrand had quietly forgiven year after year in a ledger none of them had ever seen. "I don't know anymore," he said, and was surprised to find it was the truest sentence he had spoken in years. "That is why I'm here. I have until noon. And I wanted—" He stopped. Recast it. "What is in that ledger, mademoiselle? All of it. Not the pages I've seen. All of it."

Elodie studied him for a long moment. Then she crossed to the pendulum case and lifted out the book — the real one, the master copy, wrapped in oilcloth — and set it on the bench between them as though laying down a card in a game whose stakes neither of them had fully admitted.

"Names," she said. "Thirty years of names. Men sheltered. Debts erased. Letters carried through lines. Prisoners fed. And pages torn out — torn recently, within the last year, I think, by someone who wanted particular names gone." Her eyes fixed on him, unblinking. "Pages about a supply convoy, Captain. About who really sold it."

The room seemed to contract around the words. Vasseur looked down at the ledger, at the columns of small precise handwriting, at the gaps where the paper stubs showed pale like broken teeth, and felt the full architecture of the thing assemble itself in his mind at last — Renard's arrival, Renard's urgency, Renard's hunger when he had glanced up at the tower. Not a signal hazard. A witness. The tower was a witness, and witnesses were to be reduced.

"He knows," Vasseur said slowly. "Renard knows what's in here. That's why the tower. It isn't the Boche ranging it. He needs it gone before the armistice, before any inquiry, before anyone reads aloud what your master wrote down." He laughed, briefly, without humor. "And I am the instrument provided for the purpose. A captain with a reputation for steadiness. Steady hands, steady conscience, signs where he's told."

"Will you sign?" Petit asked from the floor. The boy's voice cracked on it, sixteen years old and furious.

Vasseur did not answer immediately. He picked up the ledger, weighed it — it weighed almost nothing — and set it back down.

"When I was at Verdun," he said, "I commanded a battery that fired on coordinates passed down from divisional staff. Coordinates for a German ammunition dump. We fired for six hours. Afterward we learned the dump was a dressing station — mislabeled, or deliberately mislabeled, I have never learned which. Four hundred casualties. I fired the guns myself for two of those hours, and my hands never shook, and I slept that night, and I told myself it was the fog of war, and the fog of war is real, gentlemen, it is real, but." He looked at his hands, lying open on the bench beside thirty years of quiet mercies. "But there is fog, and there is choice, and a man who cannot tell them apart anymore has stopped being a soldier and started being a shell — aimed and fired by other men. I have been a shell for four years. At noon, I am invited to remain one."

Tomas made a sound low in his chest, almost a word, almost a prayer.

"So here is what will happen," Vasseur said, and now his voice changed, and they all heard it change, the officer draining out of it and something else arriving, something older and harder and cleaner. "At noon I will send divisional headquarters an acknowledgment. It will state that the tower cannot be safely reduced by indirect fire without collapsing the adjacent quarter — which happens to be true; I checked the survey maps at three o'clock this morning. It will recommend that any reduction wait upon a ground reconnaissance I will personally conduct. And it will request — citing signals security, citing the sensitivity of recovered documents — that Major Renard's involvement in any matter touching this sector be reviewed by the inspectorate, with supporting documentation to follow."

"Documentation," Elodie breathed.

"From the ledger. The torn pages, and whatever remains of the originals your master cached in the lantern room. Copies, certified. Names, dates, amounts. Enough that burying it becomes more expensive than facing it." He met her eyes. "It will cost me my career. Possibly more — Renard does not forgive, and men like him rarely fall alone. But I have begun to suspect, mademoiselle, that a career is merely a slower way of being a shell."

Marguerite put her hand flat on the bench, over the ledger, as though taking an oath upon it. "Augustin used to say the clock doesn't ask the hour whether it's convenient."

"No," Vasseur said. "It doesn't. That is rather the point of clocks." He straightened, and buttoned his coat, and the transformation completed itself — not into a good man, perhaps; he suspected he would never be permitted that simplicity — but into a man who had chosen, finally, which orders he would carry and which he would break. "The reconnaissance will begin tomorrow at dawn. Have your evidence ready. And mademoiselle — finish the clock. Whatever else falls, finish the clock. If this town is going to survive what's coming, it will want to hear the hour strike true."

He was at the door when Elodie spoke behind him.

"Captain. Why?"

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty. "Because a week ago a nineteen-year-old girl looked at me across this bench and offered me a trade — her work for two prisoners' lives — and I realized she had more conscience in her little finger than I had managed to accumulate in twenty-two years of service. It was humiliating." He almost smiled. "I find I would rather be humiliated than hollow. Good day, mademoiselle."

* * *

At noon he sent the acknowledgment. At twelve-forty, divisional headquarters acknowledged receipt. At one-fifteen, a second message arrived, and Vasseur read it standing at the window of the schoolhouse with the gray light falling on the page, and the cold walking slowly up his spine.

*Reference your 1200. Ground reconnaissance approved. Be advised: Major Renard has requested transfer of all recovered documentary material to divisional custody pending review. Request compliance by 1800 today. Further: be advised that 74th Battery has received independent instruction regarding registration of targets in your sector. Coordination procedures apply. — Col. Aubry, Chief of Staff.*

He read it three times.

*Independent instruction.* The colonel was warning him — in the careful, deniable grammar of staff correspondence, but warning him nonetheless. Renard had gone around him. The battery had its own instructions. And the ledger was to be surrendered by evening, into the hands of the very man it condemned.

Vasseur folded the message slowly, and outside the window the observatory stood black against the gray, patient, keeping its own time above the rooftops, and far along the ridge the guns had begun to speak again — not the tired mutter of the night before but something sharper, something deliberate, ranging.

They had already begun to register the tower.

He reached for his cap. There was no time left for careful grammar, or deniable warnings, or the slow machinery of the inspectorate. Renard had forced the endgame, and the eleventh hour was coming for all of them far sooner than the calendar promised.

Vasseur went out into the street at a run, toward the workshop, toward the ledger, toward whatever remained of the man he had decided, at last and too late and just in time, to be.
