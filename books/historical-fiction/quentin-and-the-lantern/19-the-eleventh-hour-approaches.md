## 19. The Eleventh Hour Approaches

The shells had learned the town's rhythm.

Elodie noticed it the way she noticed anything now — as a clockmaker notices intervals, the spaces between events, the patience of mechanisms. For three days the bombardment had come in waves: a burst at dawn, silence through the morning, then the long afternoon percussion that made dust sift from the observatory rafters like flour from a torn sack. The guns were searching for something. She understood, with a cold clarity that surprised her, what it was. They were searching for the tower. Renard's order had gone out under the cover of technical language — *signal hazard*, *obstruction to artillery observation* — and somewhere in the hills east of town the gunners had received their coordinates and begun, methodically, to walk their fire toward the only tall thing left standing in Sainte-Aurelle.

She worked anyway.

The workshop smelled of oil and scorched metal and the ghost of Marguerite's bread, which she had left on the bench that morning wrapped in cloth and a note that said only *eat something, both of you*. Quentin was asleep — or pretending to sleep — on the cot behind the partition where Augustin's old apprentice bed had been, his wounded shoulder propped on a folded coat, his face turned toward the wall as if the plaster could tell him whether Luc was still alive in the cell below the command post. Vasseur had kept his word so far. So far was not a word a clockmaker trusted. So far was a pendulum at the top of its arc, hanging in that weightless instant before gravity remembered it.

Elodie bent over the escapement.

It lay on a square of black velvet she had taken from the display case — Augustin's velvet, worn soft at the corners by forty years of resting delicate things upon it — and in the lamplight its steel parts threw small bright shadows like fish seen through dark water. The anchor escape wheel had survived the shell-blast that had cracked the tower's clock room two nights ago; it was the pallets that had failed, one chipped clean through where a splinter of stone had struck the movement, the other worn past tolerance by decades of service no one had thought to question until the whole town began counting on it again.

*No one thinks about the escapement,* Augustin used to say, holding one up between finger and thumb for her to examine when she was fourteen and all elbows and impatience. *Everyone loves the dial. Everyone loves the bells. But the escapement is the conscience of the clock, Elodie. It is the part that says: not yet. Not yet. Not yet — and then, at last, now.*

She filed the new pallet. The sound of steel against stone-dust-filled air was absurdly small, almost intimate, and twice she stopped because a shell had landed close enough to make the windows buzz in their frames and she needed the silence afterward to be sure she could hear the true note of the metal.

Craft as resistance. She had not understood the phrase when Quentin first said it, ten days ago, sitting where he sat now with his half-page of ledger evidence spread on the bench like a confession. She had thought resistance meant rifles, meant barricades, meant the things men did to one another in trenches with names she'd only seen on maps. But filing a pallet to a tolerance of a tenth of a millimeter while the world tried to tear itself down around you — that was resistance too. It insisted that some things would keep time regardless. That eleven o'clock would arrive whether the guns approved or not, and that when it arrived, someone had better be ready to mark it.

* * *

Tomas came up from the bell chamber just after noon, moving slowly, one hand on the rail, his face gray with dust and something older than dust.

"Third stair from the landing," he said without preamble, easing himself onto the stool by the door. "Cracked through. I wouldn't put my weight on it, and I'm light as a Lenten sermon these days."

"I'll shore it before tonight," Elodie said. "You'll need it if—"

"If we ring." He nodded. His hands, those great knuckled hands that had spoken the town's moods through bronze for sixty years, lay open in his lap. "Girl, I need to ask you straight. Will the clock strike? Truly?"

She looked down at the escapement rather than answer immediately. Honesty was a discipline like any other; Augustin had taught her that too, though she hadn't recognized it as watchmaking at the time. You did not let a movement leave your bench until it was true. You did not let a promise leave your mouth until it was certain.

"The escapement will be finished today," she said. "The striking train is sound — I checked every tooth yesterday. The weights are wound. What I can't guarantee is the tower standing."

"And if it stands?"

"Then at eleven o'clock tomorrow morning, this clock will strike eleven times, and it will strike them evenly, and everyone within three kilometers will hear it." She heard her own voice steady itself the way she steadied a wobbling gear — a slight pressure, deliberately applied. "And then, Tomas, I am going to stand in the square and read a dead man's ledger aloud until there is nothing left in it to read."

The old man was quiet for a moment. Outside, a shell fell somewhere near the eastern road, and neither of them flinched anymore; they had spent their flinching days ago.

"Marguerite's already baking," he said at last. "For after. She won't say for what, but I saw her carrying her good flour up from the cellar, the flour she's been hoarding since March, and that woman does not waste good flour on despair." He pushed himself up. "I'll check the bell hammers again. If the clock is going to speak, the bells had better answer properly. Augustin would never forgive us otherwise."

He paused at the door, an old man silhouetted against the gray light of a shelled town, and added softly: "Your master is in every screw of this place, you know. I feel him listening."

Then he was gone, and Elodie let herself feel, for exactly four seconds — she counted them off the ticking of the regulator on the wall — everything she had been refusing to feel since dawn. Fear. Grief. The specific loneliness of being the last person who understood how a machine worked, and therefore the last person responsible for its soul. Then she wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, took up the file, and returned to work.

* * *

Quentin woke — or emerged from pretense — in the middle of the afternoon, and without a word began doing what he had done every day since the raid: the small tasks that did not require his ruined shoulder. He trimmed lamp wicks. He sorted screws into Augustin's dented tins. He carried water up the tower stairs, using his left arm and his teeth and an ingenuity that would have made any sergeant proud. Today he sat across the bench from her and held parts while she fitted them, becoming, without discussion, her second pair of hands.

"Pallet's done?" he asked.

"Done and polished." She held it up. In the lamplight the steel gleamed like something alive. "Now the hard part. Setting the drop."

"Explain it to me again. You explained it Tuesday but I liked listening."

Despite everything — despite the shells and the cell below the command post and the torn pages hidden inside the pendulum case where even Vasseur's sergeants hadn't thought to look — she smiled. "The pallets have to catch the escape wheel teeth at exactly the right depth. Too shallow and the clock runs fast and wears itself to death. Too deep and it sticks. The drop — the little fall of each tooth onto the locking face — has to be equal on both sides. Otherwise the beat sounds wrong."

"The beat."

"Every clock has a heartbeat, Quentin. Tick and tock should be identical, like a man walking. When a clock limps, people feel it even if they can't name it. They say a room feels uneasy and they don't know why." She set the pallet assembly against the cradled movement and squinted. "Tomorrow, when this thing strikes eleven, it has to sound like a heart that has never doubted. Not like a machine that barely survived a war. The whole town will be listening. Half of them will be crying. And underneath the crying they'll hear this beat, and it needs to tell them the world hasn't ended."

Quentin was silent a moment. "Luc used to say something like that," he said finally. "About the men in his company. That you could tell who was going to hold by how they stood at attention. Not straighter — everyone straightens for inspection. By whether they were *level*. Like a spirit level." He turned the screw tin absently in his fingers. "Is it going to hold, Elodie? The truth, I mean. Vasseur has the pages. Renard knows Vasseur has them. Tomorrow morning, one way or another—"

"One way or another," she agreed, "the ledger gets read. Out loud. Every name. Including yours."

"I deserted, Elodie. Whatever happens to Renard, that doesn't change."

"No," she said. "But names in this ledger aren't only sins. Ask Marguerite. Ask Tomas. Ask Petit, whose father Augustin pulled out of a debtor's prison before Petit was born." She adjusted the pallet, a fraction of a degree, and felt the mechanism take the adjustment like a held breath. "Augustin wrote people down because writing them down meant they counted. You count, Quentin. We'll write it however it must be written, but you'll be in the book."

Above them, faintly, came the sound of Tomas testing the third bell — a single low stroke, checked, corrected, tested again — and beneath it, closer and less forgivable, the guns.

* * *

Night came early, as it did now, under blackout rules that the army enforced and the town obeyed with the sullen competence of people who had run out of fear and settled into stubbornness. Elodie worked by shaded lamplight, the kind of light that made a jeweler's world: a warm circle three feet wide, and beyond it darkness where the tower groaned and the wind carried smoke.

She assembled the escapement into the movement at nine o'clock.

Her hands were steady. She noted this detachedly, the way she'd once noted that her master's hands trembled only when he wasn't working. The anchor went in first, seated on its arbor; then the bridge, screwed down in the sequence Augustin had drilled into her — crosswise, always crosswise, never in rotation, or the plate would warp. Then the suspension of the pendulum, and the pendulum itself, lifted carefully from its cradle of rags where it had slept through the raid, through the interrogation, through the terrible night Quentin had been taken and the worse night he'd been given back.

She set it swinging.

The first tick was wrong. She knew it before her ear confirmed it — knew it in her wrists, in the tiny sympathetic vibration of the bench. Too much drop on the entry side. She stopped the pendulum, eased the pallet stone a breath's width, restarted.

Better. Nearly. Again.

The third adjustment brought the beat true, and the sound that rose through the clock room was so simple and so complete that she had to put down her tools and simply sit inside it for a minute. Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock. Level. Unhurried. A heart that had never doubted. Somewhere below, the town lay blacked-out and battered, and here above it this small iron certainty went on saying *not yet, not yet* to each falling second, and she understood suddenly why Augustin had built his life inside this sound. It was not an escape from history. It was an argument with it.

"You beautiful, stubborn thing," she whispered.

She wound the striking train next, cranking the great drum until the weight hung heavy on its cable, storing up eleven strokes' worth of intention. She oiled the hammer lift. She tested the release — once, letting a single trial blow fall against a wooden block instead of the bell, so that only she and the rafters would know the mechanism lived — and the *thud* of it was the most hopeful sound she had heard in weeks.

It was nearly midnight when Quentin climbed the stairs with two cups of chicory coffee and found her still sitting there, watching the pendulum.

"Well?" he asked.

"It will strike."

He exhaled, long and slow, and handed her a cup. For a while they drank in silence, two people keeping company with a heartbeat.

"Marguerite wants to know if you'll eat at dawn," he said eventually. "Before. She says a girl can't read a revolution on an empty stomach."

"It's not a revolution. It's a reading."

"Marguerite says revolutions are just readings that scare the right people." He smiled faintly, then lost the smile. "Elodie — if the guns find us before morning—"

"They won't." She said it more firmly than she felt, and then, because honesty was a discipline: "They might. But listen. Even if the tower falls tonight, the ledger survives. It's in the pendulum case, and the pendulum case comes down those stairs with me before sunrise and goes to the square in Marguerite's bread basket, under a cloth, next to her good loaves. The clock is the voice, Quentin. But the words go home with me either way."

He looked at her for a long moment in the shaded lamplight. "When did you become this person?" he asked quietly. "In October you were hiding in a workshop telling me history happened to other people."

"It did." She set down her cup and laid her palm flat against the warm wood of the clock case, against the vibration humming up through it like a second pulse. "And then history knocked on the door, bleeding, and asked for the lantern keeper. You don't get to choose the hour, Quentin. You only get to choose whether the clock is running when it arrives."

Below them, far off in the dark hills, the guns fell silent — one battery, then another, then, gradually, all of them, until the night held nothing but wind and the tick and the tock and, very faintly from the direction of the command post, the sound of a vehicle engine turning over and beginning to climb toward the observatory road.

Quentin stiffened. "That's early," he said. "Vasseur's courier doesn't come until dawn."

Elodie rose, blowing out the lamp, leaving them in the pendulum's unlit rhythm. Through the black window, down the scarred slope of the town, she could see headlights — two of them, hooded to slits, crawling upward.

"Someone couldn't wait for the eleventh hour," she said.
