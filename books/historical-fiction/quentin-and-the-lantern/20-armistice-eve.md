## 20. Armistice Eve

### Vasseur

The order lay on the desk in front of Captain Vasseur like a dead thing, and he could not stop looking at it.

*Signal tower to be designated a hazard to artillery observation. Recommend reduction by fire before dawn.* Signed in a hand he had come to know too well these past weeks — Major Renard's, neat as a ledger line, tidy as theft.

Vasseur had read it four times. Each time, his eyes went to the same place: the corner of the desk where a bundle of papers sat under a brass weight, pages he had taken from Elodie Okafor's workshop not as confiscation but as pretext, and then — shamefully, secretly, at two in the morning with a lamp turned low — had actually read. Supply requisitions. Receipts for grain that never reached the front. A column of figures in Augustin Okafor's careful clockmaker's hand beside a column in another hand entirely, and the other hand was Renard's; Vasseur would have known it anywhere now, having spent three nights comparing it against the major's signature on this very order. The convoy that Luc Marchand had been condemned for losing had never been lost at all. It had been sold, and the profit had flowed upward through channels that ended at Renard's desk, and the clockmaker who had noticed had died of a fall down observatory stairs that no one had witnessed and everyone had politely stopped asking about.

And now Renard wanted the tower shelled. The tower where those papers had been found, where the clockmaker's apprentice worked day and night on the master clock, where a deserter had been hidden for a week. Not because the tower was a hazard. Because it was evidence.

Vasseur thought about the orders he had carried out in four years of war without letting himself think about them. He thought about how easy it was: you signed, someone else fired, and the truth stayed buried under rubble along with everything else the town had ever been. He thought about the girl's face when she'd bargained with him across her own workbench, grease on her hands, terror in her eyes and iron underneath it — *you want your symbol of order, Captain; give me until the armistice and I'll give you a clock that strikes.*

Outside, the evening was unnaturally quiet. The guns to the east had been muttering all day but not aiming here, saving their breath, everyone said, for one last great effort — or for nothing at all, if the rumors were true. The rumors were always true eventually. Armistice within days. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps the eleventh.

Renard was coming at eight o'clock for the countersignature.

Vasseur took the bundle from beneath the brass weight, weighed it once in his hand — paper, so much lighter than what it held — and set it deliberately on top of the order. Then he poured himself the glass of wine he had been denying himself since September, and waited for his superior officer the way a man waits for a tooth to be pulled: knowing exactly where the pain would be, and determined to be done with it.

At seven minutes past eight, boots on the stair. Vasseur stood, buttoned his tunic to the throat, and put his hand flat on the ledger pages.

"Major," he said, when Renard entered. "Sit down."

"I haven't time—"

"You have time." It came out quieter than he intended, which somehow made it worse, better, both. "You have exactly enough time to sit down and explain to me why the man whose handwriting is all over these receipts wants my signal tower demolished twelve hours before an armistice we've been promised is already signed."

He watched the change come over Renard's face — the flicker, the recalculation, the smooth mask sliding up half a second too late. Four years of war had taught Vasseur to read faces in shell-light. There was nothing left to misread.

"Captain," Renard began, "those documents are—"

"Forgeries?" Vasseur said. "Yes. I considered that. But forgeries are usually forged by the party with something to gain, Major, and the only person who gains by burning that tower tonight is you." He slid the top page across the desk. "Your hand. Your figures. The convoy that Private Marchand is going to be shot for losing — sold out from under him while he was carrying its manifest. I've read it all. Twice."

Silence. Somewhere below, in the square, old Tomas was testing the bells — a single soft note, the E-flat, as if the town itself were clearing its throat.

"You're tired," Renard said at last, gently, horribly reasonably. "You've been under strain. This border posting, the civilians, the desertions—"

"The deserter," Vasseur said. "Yes. Let's speak of him. Quentin Aubry, taken in the raid on the watchmaker's. And Marchand, awaiting sentence in the cell below this office." He looked at his superior officer for a long moment, feeling something in his chest settle — not peace, exactly; more like a mechanism finding its seat after years of running loose. "Here is what happens now, Major. You will countersign nothing. You will go back to divisional headquarters and you will say nothing, because if you say anything at all, these pages go to the inspection board tonight, with a covering letter in my hand describing exactly how they came to me. Or—" and here he heard his own voice do something it hadn't done in years, bend toward mercy, "—or you resign yourself quietly to spending the armistice in custody pending investigation, and Marchand and Aubry spend it walking free."

"You would destroy your career."

"My career," Vasseur said, "is standing in that square right now listening to a bell-ringer practice. I intend to let it hear the armistice bells." He lifted the lantern on his desk. "Sergeant Delacour is outside the door. He has been there since six o'clock. He has read the pages too."

That was a lie — Delacour had read only the first page, at Vasseur's request, and had gone gray and silent and gone for coffee and come back and asked to be allowed to stay. But it was the kind of lie that becomes true the moment it's spoken, and Renard heard it become true, and his shoulders came down by a centimeter, and it was finished.

They walked him down past the cells. At Quentin Aubry's door Vasseur stopped, produced the key, and unlocked it, and did not look at Renard while he did it.

"Aubry," he said. "Get your coat. You're needed at the observatory. And send word to the watchmaker's — tell the girl she'll have her prisoner back, and one more besides."

### Quentin

The corridor smelled of straw and carbolic and four hundred years of stone cold, and when they put Luc Marchand's hand in his, Quentin held on the way a drowning man holds a spar — too hard, saying nothing, because anything he said would come apart into pieces.

Luc was thinner than the ghost of him Quentin had carried through three weeks of forest and hedgerow. His wrist felt like a bundle of kindling. But his grip answered, and his eyes, sunken as they were, were still Luc's eyes, still faintly amused at the world's expense even now.

"You look terrible," Luc rasped.

"You should see the other deserter."

They laughed — or made the sounds of laughing, cracked and wet, leaning against each other in the doorway while the sergeant pretended to study his boots — and Quentin understood that this was the whole of what he had crossed a war for. Not the ledger, not the proof, not justice with its clean white collar. Just this: his friend alive, his friend's hand in his, the sentence lifted off them both like weather passing.

"They gave me back your papers," Quentin managed, when he could speak. "From the raid. And there's a woman you have to thank, and a dead man you have to thank more, and a baker who will feed you until you can't walk. Luc — the name's clear. The convoy. All of it. It's written down. It's been written down for months, waiting."

Luc looked at him for a long moment. Outside, through the high barred window, the sky was going the deep bruised violet of November dusk, and somewhere beyond it the guns were quiet in a way that made everyone's skin prickle.

"Then we'd better not waste the night being grateful in a corridor," Luc said. "Take me to meet them."

They came out into the square as full dark fell, and the town rose around them black against a sky rinsed with stars — the first stars Quentin had seen unspoiled by flare-light in weeks. The observatory tower stood over everything like a patient teacher, and at its base, spilling warm yellow onto the cobbles from every window of the workshop and up the outside stair to the lantern room, lamplight. Lamplight everywhere, defiant, unshuttered for the first night since the requisition, as though the whole building had decided the blackout was over whether anyone official agreed or not.

Elodie Okafor met them at the door. She had ink on her fingers and a smudge of brass polish on her cheekbone and she looked, Quentin thought, like someone who had been awake for two days and would refuse to sleep for two more, and when she saw Luc she didn't hesitate or perform — she simply stepped aside and said, "There's soup. Marguerite made too much, which means she made it for you specifically, so don't insult her by leaving any."

Inside, the workshop blazed. Marguerite stood over the stove stirring a pot big enough to bathe in. Old Tomas sat by the window with his hands wrapped around a mug, nodding off and jerking awake, refusing on principle to go to bed. Petit — sixteen years old, wearing a uniform two sizes large and an expression ten sizes too old — was perched on the workbench turning the pages of a manual on escapements, and when Quentin came in the boy jumped down and gripped his hand with a fierce, wordless shake, and neither of them mentioned the raid, or the lie Petit had told, or the price of either.

And above it all, filling the room like a heartbeat, the tick of the master clock's movement spread across the long table in a hundred shining pieces, each one cleaned and labeled in Elodie's precise hand, waiting.

"We have until dawn," she said, "to have it striking again. Maybe less. Tomas says the armistice comes at eleven tomorrow — everyone in the market is saying eleven, the eleventh. If this town hears that clock fail at the hour it matters most..." She didn't finish it. She didn't need to.

Quentin rolled up his sleeves. "Tell me what to hold."

### Elodie

The night organized itself the way a difficult repair does: chaos resolving, piece by piece, into necessity.

Marguerite ruled the stove and the intervals between work, appearing at elbows with bread and soup and sharp words about posture. Tomas, who could no longer climb ladders but refused to admit it, appointed himself keeper of the parts tray and identified every gear Elodie hesitated over by memory alone — "the third wheel, girl, the one Augustin broke in 'ninety-one and swore at for a week; mind the tooth on the left, he never filed it quite true" — so that working beside him felt like working beside the dead man himself, and Elodie had to stop twice, once with her hand inside the going train, and breathe.

By midnight the escapement was assembled and beating, that quick light tick-tock like a pulse returning, and Petit cheered and was shushed and cheered anyway. By two the striking train was threaded, and Quentin — who had spent the war learning engines and had hands made for mechanisms — proved steady enough that Elodie stopped checking his work by nine o'clock and stopped admitting it ever.

It was near three, in the deepest quiet, that she climbed the tower stairs with the oil can and found Vasseur there.

He stood at the base of the lantern room's spiral stair, cap in his hands, looking up into the dark where the great lens hung. He had arrested his own major that afternoon. He had freed two prisoners. Tomorrow, if the rumors held, his war would end and his reckoning would begin, and he stood now in the one place in the tower he seemed to feel he had no right to.

"He kept it beautiful," Vasseur said, hearing her on the stairs. "Okafor. The lantern room. Even requisitioned, even dark. I came up here the first week. Everything was dusted."

"He dusted for forty years," Elodie said. "It doesn't stop just because the man does. Not immediately." She set the oil can down and took out her cloth. "You could help, Captain. There's thirty years of brass up there that needs polishing, and I'm given to understand you have a free evening."

For a moment she thought he would refuse — she saw the refusal assemble itself behind his eyes, rank and habit and the fear of being seen doing honest work — and then he set his cap on the rail, rolled his sleeves with the crisp economy of a man who irons his shirts himself, and picked up a cloth.

They worked without speaking for a long time, the captain and the apprentice, polishing the housing of the great lamp that would burn again tomorrow night, if the world allowed it. Once, near the end, he paused and said, to the brass rather than to her: "I read the ledger. Some of it. The pages I took." His jaw worked. "My name isn't in it."

"No," Elodie said. "But tomorrow it might be. That's rather the point of the thing. It isn't finished."

Vasseur looked at her then — really looked, the way one craftsman looks at another's work — and nodded slowly, and went back to polishing, and neither of them said anything more, because nothing more was needed, and because the clock below had begun, faintly through five floors of stone, its trial striking.

One. Two. Three.

They counted together in silence, and when it reached twelve and stopped, clean and true, Elodie pressed her forehead briefly against the cold lens housing and let herself, for exactly three seconds, believe they had done it.

### Petit

Dawn came gray and cold and smelling of frost, and nobody slept, and nobody minded.

Petit had been sent home twice and had simply taken the long way back both times, so by sunrise he was officially part of the crew, stationed on the tower's middle landing with a signal whistle and instructions to listen to the eastern road. Below him he could hear everything: Marguerite arguing cheerfully with Tomas about whether bells could be rung *too* joyfully; Quentin's laugh, roughened by prison and soup; the endless patient voice of Mademoiselle Elodie calling measurements down the stairwell, each number landing in the hush like a coin dropped in water.

He had lied for them. He still carried the lie inside him like a swallowed stone — *no strangers here, Sergeant, only the watchmaker and her aunt* — and he had expected, when he told it, to feel grown. He didn't. He felt like a door that had been opened and couldn't be shut again, and everything that mattered in the world was now walking through him, depending on him, and some days the weight of it made his knees strange. But this morning, sitting in the cold with the whole tower ticking beneath him like a heart, he found the weight had changed shape. It wasn't a stone anymore. It was more like a key.

Boots on the stair. He spun, whistle at his lips — and it was only Quentin, climbing with a coil of wire over one shoulder and a face gray with exhaustion and lit from inside.

"Any word?" Quentin asked.

"Roads are empty. Guns stopped an hour ago. Everyone's saying—" Petit lowered his voice, though there was no one to hear, "—everyone's saying eleven o'clock."

Quentin leaned on the rail beside him and looked out over the rooftops, where smoke rose straight and thin from chimneys that hadn't smoked in weeks, where people were already beginning to gather in the square below without any order to gather, drawn up out of their houses the way iron filings stand up around a magnet.

"Eleven o'clock," Quentin repeated softly. He touched the boy's shoulder. "Then we've got four hours to make sure that when this town listens, it hears something worth hearing."

Below them, faint and clear, the master clock began to strike the hour of eight — twelve strokes rolling out over the frosted roofs, each one true, each one landing on the cobbles of the square like a promise — and in the ringing silence afterward, Elodie's voice floated up the stairwell:

"Petit! Tell Quentin the pendulum's running four seconds fast, and if he's finished flirting with the view, I need his hands."

Petit grinned, and delivered the message exactly, word for word, including the flirting, and the whole tower laughed, and the day — the eleventh day, the last day, the day with eleven o'clock waiting at the end of it like a held breath — began.
