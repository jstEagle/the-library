## 21. When the Lantern Burns

The morning of the eleventh day came up grey and washed clean, as though the sky itself had been scoured overnight and could not yet decide what color to be.

Elodie stood in the workshop doorway with a broom she had forgotten to use, watching the light settle over the square like dust finding its level. The bombardment had stopped at some hour she had not been awake to mark — stopped not with the gradual faltering of a spent thing but abruptly, the way a clock stops when a hand lifts away from the escapement. The silence that followed had been so total that Tomas, half-deaf after forty years beside the bells, had come down into the workshop asking what was wrong with his ears.

Now there were men in the square. Not many. Not yet. A few soldiers without their rifles slung properly, standing in loose clusters as if they had forgotten why they'd gathered and were waiting for someone to remind them. A woman from the rue Basse picking her way through rubble toward the fountain, testing each step as though the ground might still be a lie. Somewhere a cart horse stamped and breathed great white plumes into the cold.

And above it all, through the broken faceplate of the observatory tower, the master clock ticked.

Elodie had worked on it for nineteen hours. Her hands remembered every minute of them — the raw crescents under her fingernails where the pivot polisher had slipped, the burn along her left thumb from solder taken too eagerly, the deep ache in her shoulders that felt less like pain than like ballast, like something holding her upright. She had rebuilt the escapement twice. She had re-cut the pallet faces by lamplight while Quentin held the lantern and Petit slept sitting up against the doorframe, his rifle across his knees out of habit even now that he had no orders to obey. Marguerite had brought bread at three in the morning and no one had eaten it until dawn, because eating felt too much like believing.

But the clock ran. It ran true. At nine o'clock it had struck the hour with a voice that rolled across the ruined roofs and made grown soldiers stop in the street and look up, and Elodie had stood inside the tower's throat with grease to her elbows and wept without making any sound at all, because grief and triumph had finally found each other and there was nothing left to do but let them hold on.

"Elodie."

Quentin's voice behind her. He moved quietly now; the week in the cell had taught him that. He came to stand beside her in the doorway, and for a moment neither spoke. He looked better — thinner, bruised yellow-green along the jaw where the sergeant's fist had landed, but steadied somehow, as if imprisonment had burned off the last of the deserter's flinch in him and left only the man.

"They're coming," he said. "Word went round at first light. Vasseur sent runners himself."

"All of them?"

"He couldn't say who would come. He said — " Quentin smiled faintly, without much humor in it. "He said he had spent a week reading a dead man's book, and the least he owed Augustin Okafor's memory was an honest attempt to let the town hear it."

Below them, in the square, Luc was setting out chairs.

It had been Marguerite's idea, and like most of her ideas it had arrived fully formed and refusing argument: if the ledger was to be read aloud, it would be read aloud properly, in the open air, with seats for the old and the lame and the ones whose nerves could no longer bear standing. So Luc — freed two days now, blinking like a man emerging from a cellar — had begun carrying chairs out of whatever houses still had intact doors, arranging them in rows facing the fountain, facing the tower. Petit ran messages between the houses. Marguerite baked as though the ovens themselves had something to prove, and the smell of it drifted over the square, sweet and absurd and unbearably human, so that one of the soldiers near the fountain took off his helmet just to breathe it better.

Elodie watched Luc set a chair down carefully, adjust its angle, go back for another. His hands shook. They had shaken since the cell — the doctor said they might always — but he set each chair as though precision mattered, as though a crooked row would dishonor everything the morning meant.

"He shouldn't be working," she said.

"He needs to," Quentin said. "You know how it is. You've been building clocks since you were small enough to hide inside the cases." He glanced at her. "A person needs to put their hands on the thing that saved them. Otherwise they can't believe it happened."

* * *

By ten o'clock the square was full.

Not packed — the town was half its former size, and grief sat heavily on those who remained — but full in the way that matters. Old Tomas came down from the belfry in his good coat, the one with the moth-eaten cuffs, and stood at the tower's base with his hat in his hands like a man waiting at a church door. Madame Ferrand from the milliner's, whose husband's name was in the ledger's third year, written in Augustin's careful hand: *Ferrand, Henri — debt forgiven in full; do not tell him I told you.* The schoolteacher. Two of the nuns. A cluster of soldiers whom Vasseur had released from duty for the hour, standing awkwardly at the edges because they did not know yet whether they were guests or witnesses or accused.

Vasseur himself arrived at quarter past ten, in uniform, unaccompanied. He crossed the square the way a man crosses a room where a difficult conversation is waiting for him, and he took up a position near the fountain, slightly apart, slightly forward — neither among the townspeople nor above them. Renard's arrest had cost him more than rank; Elodie saw it in the set of his shoulders, in the way his eyes went once, briefly, to the tower and then away. Whatever he had decided in those last days, deciding it had hollowed him. But he had kept his word. Quentin walked free. Luc walked free. And this morning the army's censors were conspicuously elsewhere.

Marguerite appeared at Elodie's side with a chair.

"You'll sit," she said.

"I'll stand."

"You'll sit until you stand, then. There's a difference, and the difference is my peace of mind." She set the chair down with the finality of a woman placing a loaf on a scale. Then, softer, looking out at the gathering crowd: "Do you have it?"

Elodie touched the satchel at her hip. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the ledger — Augustin's ledger, twenty-two years of names and quiet rescues, plus the pages recovered from the lantern room, plus the correspondence that had ended Major Renard's career somewhere between midnight and dawn. She had read it all twice through in the last two nights, by candle, learning it the way she learned a movement before servicing it: which gear drove which, where the wear showed, what needed saying aloud.

"There are four hundred and six entries," she said. "I counted. Some are only a line. Some go on for pages." She paused. "One of them is about your husband."

"I know." Marguerite's chin lifted, but her eyes glistened. "I've known for thirty years what he owed. Knowing it in public is different." She pressed Elodie's shoulder once, hard, and went back to her chairs.

At five minutes to eleven, Elodie climbed the tower steps.

The lantern room smelled of cold stone and old smoke. Augustin's cache had been emptied days ago, its papers carried down and copied and distributed to whoever needed them most urgently, but the niche where they had lain was still there, a dark rectangle in the wall like a mouth that had finished speaking. She lit the lantern — the great brass one from the workshop door, the one whose hanging had given the town its name for the place, *the lantern keeper's*, though few had known what he kept besides light — and she set it in the window facing the square, and its flame steadied in the still air and threw a warm circle onto the floor.

Then she waited.

She had thought, climbing the stairs, that she would be afraid. That the crowd below would feel like judgment, or like exposure, or like the weight of history doing exactly the thing she had spent her whole life believing happened only to other people, in other places. She had rehearsed her opening sentence a dozen times and hated all dozen versions.

But standing there in the lantern room with the clock's heartbeat beneath her feet and the town spread out below — the broken roofs, the scarred façades, the little rows of chairs, the people craning upward toward the light she had lit — she found instead a strange, settled calm. It felt, absurdly, like the inside of a clock case: enclosed, purposeful, every part in its appointed place. Even the grief. Especially the grief. Augustin had built all of this, name by name, entry by entry, and never once asked to be seen. The least she could do was see him now.

Below, the crowd had gone quiet. She heard Marguerite's voice, brief and carrying: "She's ready. Sit down, Armand, sit *down*—"

Elodie leaned out over the parapet.

"People of Saint-Aubin-le-Tour!" Her voice surprised her — clear, unhurried, trained perhaps by years of calling up stairwells after apprentices who wouldn't come to bed. "My master, Augustin Okafor, died eight months ago in this tower, alone, at his work. The town believed he was only a clockmaker. I believed it too. We were wrong, and it is time you heard how wrong."

She unwrapped the ledger and held it up, so the morning light caught its worn cover.

"This book holds twenty-two years of this town's hidden kindnesses. Every debt forgiven in secret. Every man sheltered from every war. Every letter smuggled, every child fed, every lie told gently to save someone from ruin. Four hundred and six names." She let that number stand in the air. "Some of you are in it. All of you were protected by it. I am going to read you every entry, beginning to end, because my master wrote it in secret and the least we owe him is to hear it openly — and because there are men in authority who would very much prefer it stayed buried. Well. It is eleven o'clock soon, and the armistice has been signed, and buried things have a way of rising when the ground stops shaking."

A sound moved through the square — not quite a murmur, not quite a gasp. Somewhere a baby cried and was hushed. Vasseur stood very still, his hands clasped behind him, his face giving away nothing except, perhaps, to someone watching closely, the smallest tightening around the eyes: the look of a man listening to his own complicity being read aloud and choosing not to leave.

Elodie opened the ledger to the first page.

"*Entry the first. March 1896. Widow Céleste Roux, four children, rent arrears of ninety francs to the landlord Delacour. Paid in full by anonymous hand. Delacour told the money came from her late husband's regimental fund. Let him believe it. Pride is also bread.*"

Her voice settled into a rhythm as she read — the rhythm of the clockwork itself, steady, metronomic, each entry a tick of a larger mechanism. Below her, the square changed shape by degrees. People began to turn and look at one another. An old woman near the fountain began to weep quietly and was not consoled, because everyone near her was close to weeping too. When Elodie reached the entry about Madame Ferrand's husband, the milliner cried out — actually cried out, one syllable, like a stitch pulled free — and covered her mouth, and the women on either side of her took her arms.

"*Entry the eleventh. The boy Tomas Aubry, apprentice bell-ringer, caught taking bread from the Ravel bakery during the hard winter. Paid the baker double and told him the boy had already offered restitution. The boy believes to this day that the baker relented out of Christian feeling. Good. Let him owe God rather than shame.*"

Tomas, at the base of the tower, laughed — a cracked, astonished laugh — and then pressed his fist to his mouth, and Elodie, reading, saw him and nearly lost the line, because she had known the man for fifteen years and had never once seen him cry.

* * *

She read for an hour.

She read through the deserters' entries, and here her voice slowed, because these were the dangerous ones, the ones Augustin had risked everything to keep: names, dates, hiding places, the false papers obtained from the printer in Metz, the money advanced and never recorded as debts. She read the entry about Marguerite's husband, Antonin — *condemned in absentia, 1915; hid him in the observatory cellar eleven weeks; forged his travel papers myself; if I am ever questioned, I am a fool and a sentimentalist and nothing more* — and Marguerite stood up straight in her chair with her hands folded like a woman at communion, and did not sit down again for the rest of it.

She read the entries about the convoy — the stolen supply convoy, the scapegoating of Corporal Luc Marchand, the slow accumulation of evidence Augustin had gathered against Major Renard across two years, patient as sediment. She read the torn page restored, the one Quentin had carried half across a war zone in his boot. When she named Luc aloud, the crowd turned toward him, and Luc stood, and for a long moment nobody spoke, and then somebody started clapping — Petit, she thought, sixteen years old and lying fluently to officers only a week ago — and the applause spread through the square like fire catching dry grass, and Luc stood in the middle of it with his shaking hands at his sides and his head bowed, taking it the way a man takes sunlight after a long illness: gratefully, painfully, unable to hurry it.

She read the small entries too, the ones that would have seemed trivial if you didn't understand that a town is built of trivia: the wedding dress bought for the girl whose father forbade the match; the piano tuned free for thirty years for the schoolhouse; the anonymous fifty francs every Christmas to the widow of the man who had once cheated Augustin at cards. Entry after entry after entry. The sun climbed. The light shifted on the broken rooftops and turned the rubble gold, and the crowd did not thin but thickened, latecomers pressing in from the side streets, drawn by the voice falling from the tower like weather.

Near the end, her voice went hoarse, and Marguerite called up, "Drink, girl! There's water right there!" and there was, set out on the parapet without Elodie remembering putting it there, and she drank and continued.

And then she reached the last page.

The last entry was recent — within the month of his death. Augustin's handwriting had gone thin and careful, the writing of a man conserving strength. Elodie had read it four times already and it still undid her.

"*Entry the four hundred and sixth. My eyes are failing and the clock wants service I can no longer give. I have taken an apprentice, Elodie Okafor, and I write this knowing she will find this book someday, because I have hidden it where only a clockmaker would ever look. She does not yet believe that history happens to people like us. She will learn otherwise, or the world will get worse, and either way she will need this book. To whoever reads this: the ledger is not finished. Ledgers of this kind are never finished. There is always another name. Add yours.*"

Silence, below. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Elodie closed the ledger and held it against her chest, and looked out over the square — at Marguerite and Tomas and Petit and Quentin and Luc, at Vasseur standing apart with his eyes closed, at the hundreds of faces turned up toward the lantern burning in broad daylight because no one had thought to extinguish it and no one wanted to — and she found she could not speak for a moment, and did not try.

Then, far above her, deeper in the tower than the lantern room, deeper than the works, in the great bronze throat of the hour bell that old Tomas had tended through two wars —

the clock began to strike.

Eleven strokes. Each one rolled out over the town and out past the town, over the fields, over the silent front, out into a Europe holding its breath, and each stroke fell on the square below like a physical thing, like a hand laid on every upturned face. People counted aloud without meaning to. By the seventh stroke, strangers were holding on to one another. By the tenth, the weeping was general and unhidden. On the eleventh stroke, the sound went out and out and out, and the town of Saint-Aubin-le-Tour stood in the ruins of its square and listened to its own heart beat, and knew, at last and together, exactly what it had survived, and who had kept it alive, and what it owed.

When the echo finally faded, Elodie leaned over the parapet one last time.

"The ledger," she called down, and her voice broke and she let it, "is not finished. There is always another name."

She lifted the ledger high.

"And mine starts today."
