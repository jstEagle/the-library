## 22. Inheritance

The frost came early that November, silvering the cobbles of the Rue des Horlogers before the sun had cleared the ridge. Elodie Okafor stood in the doorway of the workshop with her breath making small clouds and her hand flat against the doorframe — Augustin's doorframe, planed by his own hands thirty years ago, worn smooth at shoulder height by decades of his leaning there to watch the street wake up.

Three weeks since the armistice. Twenty-three days since she had stood in the ruined square with a ledger open against her chest and read aloud until her voice gave out, while the whole town stood in the cold and heard its own history spoken back to it. She could still feel the weight of the book against her ribs when she thought about it, though the ledger itself now lay upstairs on the workbench, waiting for ink.

But first, the lantern.

It hung inside by the bench, where it had hung through every year of her apprenticeship — a brass storm lantern with glass smoked amber at one corner where a spark had once kissed it, its handle wrapped in leather gone dark as chestnut from Augustin's grip. Marguerite had polished it two nights ago without being asked, saying nothing, only setting it back down with a look that said *well? What are you waiting for?*

Elodie took it down now. It was lighter than she remembered. Everything he had left behind was lighter than she remembered, as if grief had been carrying some of the weight for her all along and was only now setting it down.

She stepped out into the street. The town had repaired itself the way towns do after wars — badly, quickly, with mismatched mortar and borrowed nails and an air of defiant impermanence, as if beauty might be provisional again someday and there was no point getting attached. But the square had been swept. Someone had righted the fountain. And the observatory tower still stood above the rooftops, scarred along its southern flank where Renard's guns had found it, but standing, and every hour on the hour its clock spoke across the valley in a voice that made people pause mid-step and look up, the way they used to before the war taught them that looking up could cost you something.

She hung the lantern on its iron bracket beside the door. It turned gently in the morning wind, catching the low light, throwing a slow amber wheel across the cobbles.

There was a tradition here she had never understood until this autumn. Old Tomas had explained it the night they'd finished the repairs, sitting among the shavings with a cup of wine he was mostly holding for warmth: a workshop that hung its lantern meant its door was never truly shut. Not to debtors. Not to strangers. Not, if it came to it, to deserters. *Your master lit that lamp every night of the last war,* Tomas had said. *You think the town survived on bread alone?*

From somewhere up the street came the smell of Marguerite's ovens, the first batches of the morning, and beneath it the sound of Petit's boots skidding around the corner at a dead run because Petit did everything at a dead run now, even errands that didn't require it, even walking to the table. He arrived breathless with a basket and a grin.

"Marguerite says you're to eat before you touch anything sharp." He set the basket on the step: rolls still steaming through their cloth. "And she says—" he dropped his voice into a fair imitation of the baker's gravel "—'tell her the ledger won't write itself, but it'll wait ten minutes for breakfast.'"

"Tell her I heard every word."

"She knows. That's why she sent me." He hesitated, scuffing his boot against the stone, and for a moment the boy-soldier fell away and there was just a sixteen-year-old trying not to ask for something. "Is today the day? Quentin said — he said you were going to start the new page."

Elodie looked at him. Under the grime of the last months he had begun, impossibly, to look younger again rather than older, as though peace were letting him grow backward toward childhood, reclaiming what it could. His uniform had been traded for a baker's apron that was already too big for him. Marguerite had taken him in the way she took in everything — flour-dusted, unhesitating, permanent.

"It's the day," she said.

He grinned and bolted, then stopped three doors down and called back, "We'll all be here tonight! Tomas is bringing the good wine, the one he's been saving since 1914, which he says proves he always knew we'd win, which is a lie, but it's a nice lie," and then he was gone around the corner in a clatter of echoes.

Elodie laughed — actually laughed, out loud, alone in the street, and realized she had not done that in over four years — and went inside to begin.

* * *

The workshop smelled as it always had: oil and beeswax, walnut shavings, the faint mineral coolness of brass. She had cleaned it twice already and would clean it a hundred times more, not because it needed it but because cleaning it felt like conversation. Every drawer she opened was a sentence he'd left unfinished. The loupe in its velvet nest. The box of escapements sorted by hand into paper twists labeled in his cramped script. The chair by the window with its cushion worn to the shape of no one else.

She sat at the master bench — his bench, though he'd have bellowed at her for calling it anything so sentimental; he'd have said benches belonged to whoever was working them — and drew the ledger from its wrappings.

They had copied it. That had been Luc's idea, in the strange quiet week after Vasseur's arrest of Renard, when the army's paperwork moved at the speed of shame. A copy for the garrison archive, certified by Captain Vasseur himself, who had come to the workshop in civilian clothes to do it, standing awkwardly among the shavings with his cap in his hands like a schoolboy called before the headmaster. He had read every entry. She had watched him read them — watched the man who had demanded all records be surrendered turn the pages of the record he'd tried to take, and understand, entry by entry, what kind of town he had been sent to occupy. At the end he had closed the copy and said only, "My superiors will want to know why I'm recommending commendations for civilians and a deserter." Then, quieter: "Tell them whatever you like. You've earned the truth being inconvenient for someone other than yourselves."

Renard's court-martial had been brief and distant, conducted in a city whose name appeared in the newspapers between reports of coal shortages. The convoy theft, the scapegoating of Corporal Luc Marchand, the order to shell the observatory — all of it entered the record now, official and indelible. But Elodie knew, as everyone in that room had known, that the record would have stayed blank forever if a dead clockmaker hadn't written names in a book and hidden pages in a lantern room.

Official truth, it turned out, required an unofficial twin to survive long enough to be born.

She opened the ledger to the last written page. Augustin's final entries were dated June — six weeks before his death. She still did not know how he had died, whether it had been his heart or Renard's men or simply the accumulated weight of keeping a town's conscience running for forty years like a clock no one else knew needed winding. Perhaps she never would. The missing page had never been found, and Vasseur's investigation had ended in a shrug and a sealed file. Some debts, she was learning, were never settled. They were only inherited.

Below his last line, the page was empty. Below that, fresh paper she had sewn in herself, waiting.

She dipped the pen. Her hand, so steady with files and pliers and the hair-fine adjustment of a pallet stone, shook slightly at the first stroke, and she smiled at that, because Augustin had told her once that a shaking hand on important work was not a flaw but proof the work mattered. *A steady hand means you've done it too many times,* he'd said. *Tremble a little. It keeps you honest.*

She wrote:

*Quentin Aubry. Arrived wounded, October 1918, seeking a page torn from this book. Found shelter instead.*

She paused, pen hovering. There should be more. There should be the whole of it — the climbing of the dark tower under blackout, the raid, the cell, the night they'd repaired the escapement together by lamplight while shells walked their slow way up the valley. But the ledger had never recorded deeds. It recorded debts and their settling, and something in her understood, finally, the difference. Augustin had not written *hid the Duval brothers in the cellar for eleven days.* He had written *Duval — forgiven, 40 francs.* Small entries. The architecture hidden inside the masonry.

So she kept it small, and let the smallness hold everything:

*Debt owed to this house: one life, freely risked for another's name. Settled. No further payment accepted. Door open always.*

* * *

By afternoon the others began to arrive, drawn the way they always were now, by some gravity the workshop exerted on anyone who had ever been kept alive inside its walls.

Tomas came first, moving slowly up the street with his stick, pausing beneath the lantern to squint up at it and deliver his verdict to the town at large: "About time. Thought we'd have to hang you up there instead." Inside, he claimed his chair by the stove — a chair that had somehow become his chair in the space of three weeks, the way things became permanent in this room — and began telling the story of the false bells again, embellishing it slightly each telling, so that by Christmas the raid warning would involve six soldiers and a blizzard. Nobody minded. The town needed its legends, and Tomas rang them out daily now like practice peals.

Marguerite arrived with bread and her sleeves rolled, surveyed the workshop, pronounced it "still a mess, Augustin's ghost must be proud," and annexed the corner near the sink to begin preparing supper as if the kitchen were hers, which by now it functionally was. Her husband Henri — thin, quiet, with the watchfulness of a man who had once stood before a firing squad and been pulled back from it by a clockmaker's signature on a forged pardon — sat with Tomas and listened to the bell story as if he hadn't heard it nine times, because he understood better than any of them what it cost to be given your life back and how rude it would be to seem bored while someone described giving it.

Luc came at dusk, walking with the faint hitch that the prison had left in his hip, wearing civilian clothes that fit him like a promise he was still growing into. He carried a parcel, which he set on the bench with ceremony. Inside: a new clock face for the observatory's side dial, painted by hand, the numerals picked out in gold.

"For the south wall," he said. "Where the scar is. Let the thing that got shelled tell perfect time. Seems fair."

Quentin arrived last, as he seemed to arrive last at everything lately, lingering at thresholds as though still not quite convinced doors would stay open for him. He stood in the doorway under the lantern's glow — Elodie saw the moment he noticed it, saw him understand what it meant, saw him swallow — and then he came in, and Marguerite pushed a roll into his hand before he'd finished saying hello, and Tomas raised his glass to him, and Luc clapped his shoulder, and Petit appeared from wherever Petit lurked between arrivals to demand whether Quentin had heard about the wolf Tomas had allegedly rung the bells against in '92.

The workshop filled. Lamplight. Shavings. The smell of Marguerite's stew colonizing every corner. The great regulator clock in the alcove ticking its patient, unhurried heartbeat, the sound Elodie had grown up inside and nearly lost and now heard everywhere, even in her sleep, even in silence.

Later, when the plates were cleared and Tomas had reached the part of the bell story where he personally held off a sergeant with nothing but a hymnal, Quentin drifted to the bench where the ledger lay open. He read the new entry. Elodie watched him read it from across the room and pretended not to, the way you pretend not to watch someone you love receive something fragile.

His finger touched the ink, lightly, as if checking whether it was real.

"You wrote my name in a dead man's book," he said quietly, coming to stand beside her.

"I wrote your name in *the* book. There's a difference." She tapped the older pages. "These are his. This page onward is mine. That's what inheritance means, I've decided — not getting the furniture. Getting the next page."

"And what comes after me?"

"Whoever falls through the door." She nodded toward the lantern burning outside, its amber wheel turning slowly on the cobbles. "That's the whole system. Lantern, door, book. Augustin ran it for forty years and never told a soul, and the town thought kindness just happened to it, like weather." She looked at the faces around the room — Tomas mid-anecdote, Marguerite laughing at him, Petit hanging off Luc's arm, Henri smiling into his wine — the found family assembled in the lamplight, official now, inscribed. "I don't intend to keep it secret. Secrecy nearly got us all killed. But I intend to keep it going."

Quentin was silent a moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "I deserted, Elodie. Whatever the papers say now, whatever Vasseur smoothed over — there are men who'll always call me that."

"Yes." She met his eyes. "And this house calls you family. Both things can be true. The ledger's full of both kinds of true." She turned to a fresh line. "Now hold still. I'm not finished with you."

She dipped the pen again and wrote beneath his name a second entry, dated that evening:

*Quentin Aubry — apprenticed. Clockmaking. Terms: room, board, instruction, and the whole truth about escapements. Wages deferred until he stops breaking my files.*

"That's—" He stared at it. "I don't know the first thing about clocks."

"You knew enough to climb a dark tower and trust a stranger with the only proof your friend was innocent. That's most of it. The rest is patience, and you clearly have that, or you'd have strangled Petit weeks ago."

From across the room, Petit: "*I heard that!*"

"You were meant to!" she called back, and the room laughed, and Quentin laughed too — the real laugh, the one that had started coming back to him in the last week or two, the one that sounded like a door opening.

* * *

They stayed late. Tomas's wine proved worth the saving. Somewhere past midnight the others filtered out into the frost-bright street, each pausing under the lantern as they left — Tomas touching the bracket like a churchgoer touching a saint's foot, Petit saluting it with tremendous irony, Marguerite adjusting the wick a fraction higher because Marguerite believed in doing things properly — until only Elodie and Quentin remained, and then only Elodie.

She banked the stove. She wiped the bench. She hung the loupe on its hook and squared the tools in their places, and at the very end of it all she stood a moment in the middle of the room with the lamp turned low, listening to the tick.

Then she climbed the narrow stair to the little room that had been hers for five years and would be hers for fifty, and paused at the window. Across the roofs, the observatory tower stood black against the stars, its scarred flank catching the moonlight, and as she watched, distant but unmistakable, it struck the hour — two strokes rolling down over the sleeping town, over the rebuilt fountain and the swept square and the graves that were already becoming flowerbeds, over the whole battered, stubborn, forgiving world.

Two o'clock. The clock was right. The ledger was open. The lantern burned.

In the morning there would be work — commissions piling up, a town rebuilding, a letter from Paris she'd been avoiding from a ministry wanting to discuss the observatory's future, and a new apprentice to teach, who broke files. But that was morning. Tonight the page was still wet, the family was home, and history — the kind that happened elsewhere, to other people — could manage without her for a few hours.

Elodie Okafor slept, and the clock kept the hours, and under the eaves the lantern turned its slow gold wheel against the dark, lighting the door for whoever came next.
