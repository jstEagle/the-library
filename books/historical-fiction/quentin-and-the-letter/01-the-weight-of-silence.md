## 1. The Weight of Silence

There were forty-one ways to say nothing in Velloran, and my father had taught me every one of them before I was old enough to shave.

The first was the simplest: you simply did not speak. The second involved speaking, but only about weather, and only after the other person had spoken first, so that your opinion of the rain could never be construed as an original thought. The third through the fortieth were variations of the same trick performed with the eyes, the hands, the shoulders, and — on one memorable occasion that Father still cited as a masterwork — a strategically timed sneeze. The forty-first way was the one I was practicing now, alone in the shop at half past eleven, sorting type by candlelight: you said nothing so thoroughly, for so many years, that even the silence itself forgot you were there.

The candle guttered. I tipped more wax toward the wick and went back to the case.

It was good work, sorting type. I want to be honest about that, because everything else I have to tell you is dishonest in some degree, and it seems fair to begin with one true thing. There is a pleasure in it that I have never been able to explain to anyone who hasn't done it — the small leaden bodies of the letters clicking into their compartments, each one going home, the whole alphabet restored to order after a day of being shaken apart into sentences. *A* to its house. *B* to its house. Somewhere in the third row, an *e* worn nearly smooth from overuse, because *e* does all the work in any language and gets thanked for it least. My father said you could read a printer's soul in his *e*. He had never explained what he meant, and I had long ago stopped asking, because asking questions was not among the forty-one approved silences.

Outside, Velloran was doing what Velloran did on the eve of quarter-day: nothing, loudly. A patrol went down Copperlane with boots that wanted everyone to know they were boots. Two doors up, the Delacombes' shutters closed — early, even for them. From the direction of the university came the faint, stubborn sound of students singing something, the words lost in the distance but the tune unmistakably the sort of tune that got universities closed.

I sorted type and catalogued my silences, which is a habit I fell into around my seventeenth year and have never entirely broken, the way some men bite their nails.

*The pamphlet we refused.* That was September's entry. A man had come to the shop with wet eyes and a damp roll of paper under his coat — a student, or dressed like one, though the coat was too good and the eyes too frightened to be either. He wanted five hundred copies of a thing called *What Was Done at Corvel's Trial*, and he offered to pay in silver, real silver, the kind that rings when you drop it on the counter instead of clinking politely. Father looked at the manuscript for exactly as long as it takes to read a title, and then he said, "We are a jobbing shop. Wedding invitations. Bills of lading. Playbills for the Amphitheatre, when the censor approves them." And the man had looked at Father, and then at me, standing there with my apron and my clean hands, and whatever he saw in our faces made him put the silver away without another word. He was arrested eleven days later. I know because it was in the *Gazette*, between an announcement about drainage improvements and an advertisement for a tonic that cured melancholy, gout, and — I am quoting — "excessive hopefulness."

*The neighbor we no longer greet.* That was Madame Ostrevant, two doors down, who had taught Maren her letters and once pulled me out of the canal by my collar when I was nine. She had begun leaving her lamp lit in the front window past curfew, which in Velloran was either a signal, a protest, or very bad housekeeping, and we could no longer afford to find out which. So when she stood at her step in the mornings, I studied the lintel above her door as though it were the most fascinating lintel in the empire, and she studied mine, and between us we maintained two silences of excellent quality.

*The question we did not ask.* That one belonged to Maren, and it was only three weeks old. She had come home from the charity ward with coal dust on her cuffs — coal dust, in a hospital nowhere near the river — and when Father asked how her evening had gone she said, "Full," in a voice with a door already closing behind it, and he had nodded, and I had watched both of them not-ask with such professional smoothness that I almost applauded.

You will notice that none of these silences felt like cowardice at the time. That is the genius of them, and I mean *genius* sincerely, because whoever designed this system understood people better than any philosopher ever has. Each silence arrives wearing the costume of prudence. You do not refuse to greet Madame Ostrevant because you are afraid; you are merely busy. You do not decline the student's pamphlet because five hundred copies would kill your family; you are merely a jobbing shop. The fear is real, but it works backstage, in the dark, and out front the whole performance looks like ordinary life, tidy and reasonable, a man sorting type by candlelight because that is what printers do.

The comfort of it was suffocating, and I loved it, and I am not proud of either verb.

* * *

Father came down near midnight, as he always did, in his dressing gown with the burned cuff, carrying two cups of chicory that tasted the way chicory always tastes, which is to say like a lie coffee tells when it cannot remember being itself.

"You'll ruin your eyes," he said, which was his way of saying *I see you're awake, and I'm glad of the company, and neither of us will admit it.*

"You'll ruin yours reading over my shoulder."

"I wasn't reading over your shoulder." He set the cups down among the type cases with the untroubled confidence of a man who has knocked ink onto every surface in the shop at least twice and considers the surfaces lucky. "I was checking your distribution. Your *e* is in the wrong compartment."

"It's worn. I gave it the corner slot so I can find it."

"That's sentiment, not system." But he didn't move it, and I noticed he didn't, and filed that away among the small mercies.

We drank our chicory in companionable silence — silence number seven, the comfortable kind, the kind that is actually a form of speech between people who have run out of things they're permitted to say. Through the floorboards I could hear Maren turning over in her room, and the clock in the hall clearing its throat, and beneath all of it the great held breath of the city, the one it had been holding since the trial, since the lists, since the word *Corvel* became the kind of word you wiped off your hands after touching.

"Quarter-day tomorrow," Father said.

"Mm."

"The barge comes down from the coal yards. Salt Quay will be a madhouse by noon." He turned his cup a slow circle on the bench. "Your mother used to say quarter-day was the one day Velloran told the truth about itself. Everything changes hands. Everyone counts."

"She also said the censor had a face like a wet Thursday, so I'd take her aphorisms generally."

That earned me the ghost of a smile, there and gone, like a misprint caught just before the press runs. Then the smile finished whatever arithmetic it had started and left his face, and he said, lightly, in the tone of a man discussing drainage improvements: "Whatever happens tomorrow — whatever you hear in the streets — the shop opens at eight, as always. We take the Amphitheatre playbills. We deliver the Harrowgate wedding order. We are a jobbing shop."

"We are a jobbing shop," I agreed.

"And Orsen—" He stopped. This was unusual enough that I looked up. My father did not stop mid-sentence; stopping was for men who hadn't rehearsed. Whatever he had been about to say, he weighed it against the forty-one silences and found it wanting, because what he actually said was: "Mind the *k* case. The ligatures stick."

He took the candle stub and went up to bed, and I sat in the dark he left behind, listening to the shop settle.

* * *

Here is the thing about a hairline crack: it does not announce itself. No trumpet, no thunderclap. It is found later, usually by accident, usually by lamplight, by someone running a thumb along a surface that was supposed to be sound and feeling the seam give. At the time, it feels like nothing at all. It feels like a Tuesday.

For me it was the *e*.

I had gone back to sorting, because sleep and I had reached our usual impasse, and I picked up the worn letter — my father's *e*, cut by his own graver twenty years ago when he bought the license and the press and the whole respectable coffin of a quiet life — and I turned it in the candlelight, and I thought: *this letter has printed ten thousand wedding invitations and four hundred bills of lading and not one true thing.*

Not one. In twenty years, in a city drowning in things that needed saying, our type had said nothing worth the lead it was cast from. We had printed the *Gazette*'s supplements and the tax notices and the drainage announcements and the tonic advertisement with its cure for excessive hopefulness, and every sheet of it had passed across the censor's table stamped and smiling, and every sheet of it was a small brick in the wall we had built around ourselves and called safety.

And underneath that thought, quieter, in the place where the crack lives: *and it worked.* That was the obscene part. The silence had kept us safe. Father's careful, rehearsed, forty-one-fold nothing had bought us the shop and the license and Maren's nursing post and my own unremarkable face, and every year the price went up a little and every year we paid it and called it prudence, and tonight, holding a worn letter in a dark shop while students sang treason somewhere past the rooftops, I understood for the first time that we were not paying the price.

We *were* the price. The silence wasn't the wall around us. It was the mortar, and we were the bricks, and somewhere out in the dark a city full of people who had run out of silences was deciding what to do about walls.

I set the *e* in its corner slot. I blew out the candle.

And that is when the knock came.

Three raps, soft, spaced wrong — not the knock of the censor, not the knock of a customer, not any knock in the honest catalogue. A wet-sounding knock, if a knock can sound wet, and past curfew, and I stood in the black shop with my heart going like a loose platen and thought, absurdly, of my father's voice: *mind the k case, the ligatures stick* — as if the danger tonight were typographical.

The knock came again. Softer. And then, beneath the door, the smallest sound of all: someone breathing, trying very hard not to be heard breathing, which is the loudest thing in the world.
