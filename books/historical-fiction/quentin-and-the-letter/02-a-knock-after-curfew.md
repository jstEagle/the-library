## 2. A Knock After Curfew

There is a catalogue of knocks, if you keep a print shop in Velloran long enough. I had inherited the catalogue along with the type cases and my father's habit of humming when he was worried, which meant he hummed through most of every day.

The censor's knock was four raps, evenly spaced, delivered by a man who had all night and wanted you to know it. The knock of a customer who had come for a wedding invitation or a bill of sale was two quick raps and then an apologetic pause, as though the door itself might be inconvenienced. Drunks knocked in threes with a long slide at the end, like their fists were arriving separately from the rest of them. My sister Maren, coming home late from the charity ward, knocked twice, waited, then once more — a rhythm she had invented at eleven years old and never abandoned, on the theory that no one else would bother copying it.

The knock at the door tonight fit none of these. It was small and damp-sounding, and it came again after a silence exactly long enough for me to convince myself I had imagined it.

I stood in the dark between the press and the composing stone, one hand still resting on the frame where I had set the *e* in its corner slot not a minute before. The candle was out. Through the shutters came the orange wash of the street lamp at the corner of Inkrow Lane, and under that, the sound of the city holding its breath — because it was past curfew, and past curfew Velloran did not so much sleep as lie very still with its eyes open.

*Don't answer it,* said the sensible voice in my head, the one that sounded like my father reading aloud the list of things the regime permits and nothing more.

But beneath the door came the smallest sound of all: breathing. Someone breathing carefully, trying very hard not to be heard breathing, which — I don't know if you've ever listened for it — is the loudest thing in the world. It makes the clock sound like a blacksmith. It makes your own pulse sound like someone knocking from inside your ribs.

I crossed the shop floor without lighting anything. There are eleven floorboards in this establishment that creak, and I have known them longer than I have known the alphabet; my father taught me both, though only the floorboards were presented as a survival skill. I put my eye to the shutter seam.

A child stood on our step.

He could not have been older than seven. He was soaked to the collar — it had been raining on and off since supper, the fine cold drizzle that Velloran specializes in, the kind that doesn't fall so much as seep — and streaked with mud from his knees to his chin, as though he had crawled the last part of whatever journey brought him here. In one fist he clutched something white against his chest, held the way children hold things they've been told matter more than themselves: too tight, knuckles bloodless, arms wrapped around it like a small frightened barrel around its staves.

I looked up and down Inkrow Lane. Empty. The lamplight made a little island around him and gave the rest of the street back to the dark. Somewhere eastward there was a muffled boom — distant, probably a cannon being tested, probably nothing, the sort of sound the city had been making all week and pretending it hadn't.

I unbolted the door.

He didn't run. That was the first strange thing. Most creatures caught in lamplight bolt; cats do, thieves do, even Maren startles. This boy simply looked up at me — a thin face, mud-caked, eyes enormous and red-rimmed, the eyes of someone who has been crying in a way that has gone past crying into something drier — and held out the thing in his fist. A letter. Sealed with wax that had suffered some rain but held.

"For the printer," he said. His voice was scraped down to almost nothing. "It says. For the printer."

"Which printer?" I asked, which was a stupid question, since we were standing in front of the only lit window for three streets and I was the one holding the door open.

He didn't answer. He swayed slightly, like a fence post in wind, and I saw that his legs had been holding him upright out of pure contract with himself, and that the contract was expiring.

I should say here what any sensible person would have said: *Boy, go home.* But home was a word with teeth in it that week. The Minister Corvel had been taken nine days ago — everyone knew, nobody said — and the city had developed a new weather system made entirely of things unspoken. And here was a child, alone, past curfew, in the rain, carrying a sealed letter addressed to *the printer*, which in Velloran in that season was less an address than an accusation.

"Come in," I heard myself say. "Quickly."

He came in. He left wet prints on our floorboards — small ones, half-size, the first documents that boy ever set upon our premises, though far from the last — and stopped just inside the door as if the threshold were the edge of a game whose rules he'd only partly been told.

I closed the door. I shot the bolt. The click of it sounded enormous.

"Do you know whose shop this is?" I asked.

He shook his head. Then, after consideration: "It smells like ink." He said it the way another child might say *it has a dragon in it*, with a mixture of awe and wariness.

"It does smell like ink," I agreed. Everything smelled like ink. Our bread tasted faintly of ink. When Maren came home from courting — she claimed she went courting; I had never seen evidence — she smelled of ink, and the young men of the Paper District had learned to find this romantic, or had learned to pretend to.

I got the candle lit. Its light came up yellow and small, and the boy blinked in it like something hatched. Up close he was worse than he'd looked through the shutter — chapped lips, a scrape along one jaw crusted with dried blood, shoes that had given up being shoes sometime in another parish. Mud had gotten into everything, including, inexplicably, his ears.

"You're hurt," I said.

"No," he said, in the tone children use to mean *yes, but I've decided against discussing it.*

"I'll get you something—"

"The letter," he said, and thrust it at me again, harder, as though I'd been slow about it. "First the letter. He said first the letter."

*He said.* I took it. The wax seal was unbroken, pressed with no signet I recognized — just a plain blob, anonymous, which in itself told you the sender had money, because anonymity is expensive. The paper was good stock. Better stock than we sold. I turned it over.

The address was written in a hand so steady it might have been printed:

**To the house of Fairweather, print-shop, Inkrow Lane. To be opened by the elder or the younger only. By no other hands.**

My name was on it. Both our names, actually, though "elder or younger" covered it neatly, and something about that phrasing — someone knowing there were exactly two of us who mattered — put a cold finger on the back of my neck.

"Wait there," I told the boy. "Don't touch anything. Especially not—" I gestured generally at the shop, at the forest of type cases and the great iron press squatting in the corner like a patient ox, "—anything."

He nodded gravely, as though this were reasonable instruction and not madness, and stood precisely where he was, dripping, while I went to find my father.

* *

Aldous Fairweather kept late hours the way other men kept dogs: out of habit, and because they barked at things worth hearing. He was in the kitchen behind the shop, where the stove still held heat, mending a pair of spectacles that did not need mending. He did that when he couldn't sleep — took them apart, cleaned each lens, reassembled them, and then complained for days that they sat wrong on his nose. It was, I realize now, the same impulse that governed everything about him: take apart the thing that frightens you, handle every piece, put it back together, and pretend it's the same object.

"Papa," I said. "There's a boy at the door."

He didn't look up. "Then charge him for the water he's dripping on the boards."

"He came past curfew. Alone. With a letter." I held it out. "Sealed. Addressed to us by name. He said — 'for the printer.'"

That got him. Not all at once — that's the thing I keep returning to, the thing I want to describe properly, because everyone afterward assumed my father understood immediately, and he did, but understanding arrived in him the way dawn arrives, sideways and unwelcome. First he set down the spectacles. Then he took the letter, and I watched his thumb move over the paper the way a man checks a coin for clipping. Then he read the direction, and I watched the steadiness of his own hand change.

"Aldous Fairweather," he read aloud, softly, tasting it. "Print-shop. Inkrow Lane." He turned the letter over once, twice. "Who carried it?"

"He did. He's standing in the shop."

"In the *shop?*" Something moved through his face then — not alarm yet, more the particular irritation of a man whose careful arrangements have been disturbed — and he rose and went past me into the passage, and I followed, and that is how we both came to stand in the doorway of the front room looking at the boy.

The boy looked back. He was still holding his arms around himself, still dripping, and the candlelight found the mud on him and made it shine like gilding.

My father went white.

I want to be precise about this, because I have thought about that moment more times than I have thought about almost any other moment in my life. White the way paper is white — total, flat, finished. His face didn't drain gradually. It was as if someone had pulled a sheet through him, and for one full second Aldous Fairweather, who had faced down censors and inspectors and the licensing bureau and Maren's cooking, stood in his own doorway looking like a proof pulled too early: all the ink run out of him, blank where a man should be.

And here is what unsettled me most, and what I did not understand until later: he knew the boy. Not *recognized* him, in the vague way one recognizes a neighbor's cousin — *knew* him. I saw it land. I saw the knowledge arrive complete, dread preceding understanding, horror getting to the door before explanation had even put its boots on. Whatever was written in that letter, my father had begun grieving it before he broke the seal.

"Papa?" I said.

He didn't answer me. He crouched down — slowly, the way you'd crouch near something skittish — to the boy's level, and said, in a voice I had never heard him use, a voice with all the workshop gruffness scoured out of it:

"What's your name?"

The boy looked at him. Looked at me. Looked at the letter in my father's hand, and something in his exhausted face suggested that this was the question the whole night had been building toward, the final gate, and that he had rehearsed his answer through every wet and terrible mile of it.

"Quentin," he said.

* *

Later — much later, years later, when the word had been carved into me — I would understand what that single syllable detonated in that room. But that night I only knew this: my father flinched. My father, who did not flinch. Who had taught me that a printer's hands do not shake, that fear is a luxury item we do not stock. Aldous Fairweather flinched at a seven-year-old's name as though the boy had struck him.

"Quentin," my father repeated. Not a question. A confirmation, the way a man confirms a debt.

"Yes sir," said the boy, and then, with the terrible directness of the very young and the very tired: "Are you the forgetting people?"

I stared at him. "The what?"

"He said forget my face." Quentin recited it with the flat diligence of a lesson learned by rote, syllables handled carefully so as not to drop any. "Feed him and forget his face. That's in the letter. You can check. I'm not supposed to tell you, but I checked and it *is* in the letter, so it isn't telling, it's just reading out loud." He paused, considering the theology of this. "Also I'm hungry. Feeding comes first. He was very firm about the order."

My father had not moved. He was still crouched, still pale, and he was looking at the boy with an expression I had no shelf in my mind to store — grief and guilt and something ferociously tender all crowded onto one face, fighting for the light.

"Orsen," he said quietly. Without turning around. "Bolt the front door again. All three bolts. Then bring me the letter, and then bring the boy bread, and then, Orsen—" and here his voice did something I had never heard it do in twenty-three years of knowing it, something that made the hair on my arms stand up under my sleeves, "—then come and help me think."

"Think about what?" I asked. Though even as I said it, I felt the answer moving toward me across the dark shop, slow and certain, the way the censor's knock moves down a row of houses.

My father finally looked up at me. And in the candlelight, with the rain starting again against the windows and a mud-streaked child shivering on our floorboards, Aldous Fairweather said:

"About how much trouble we are in, son. About how much trouble we have been in for a very long time."

Behind him, Quentin sneezed — a small, apologetic sound, utterly ordinary, the least ominous noise ever made in that room.

It did not help at all.
