## 3. What Father Knew

The boy was asleep in the back room, curled on Maren's spare blanket like a comma between sentences, and Aldous Fairweather had not moved from the doorway in eleven minutes.

Orsen counted. Counting was what he did instead of screaming, a habit learned young at the type case, where a dropped sort meant a ruined line and a raised voice and sometimes, if the day went badly enough, his father's hand closing over his wrist with a pressure that said *careful*. He counted the seconds of his father's stillness the way he counted em quads: one, two, three—until the number stopped meaning anything and became simply a shape of dread.

His father was not a still man. That was the thing no one understood about Aldous Fairweather, who had a reputation on Copper Lane for being grave and deliberate, a printer of the old school. At home he hummed off-key while he inked the press. He argued with the almanac. He had once spent an entire supper explaining to Maren why the government's new standard weights were half a grain light out of spite, gesturing with a bread knife until Orsen's mother—who had been alive then, who was not alive now—had taken it away from him with the weary tenderness of a woman disarming a much-loved idiot.

Aldous Fairweather fidgeted. Aldous Fairweather *hummed*.

He stood in the doorway now like a man looking into a well he had dug himself, and Orsen felt the floor tilt under the shop's familiar smells—ink and oil and lamp-black—and knew, with the terrible clarity that arrives just before bad news is spoken, that the world had already changed and was only waiting for them to notice.

"Father." His own voice came out strange. "You know him."

"I know his coat," Aldous said, which was not an answer, or was the worst possible answer, because it meant he had looked past the mud and the matted hair to some detail—a button, a hem, a monogram stitched by a minister's wife—that told him exactly whose son lay breathing on the spare blanket.

"Whose coat?"

"Sit down, Orsen."

"I'd rather stand."

"That's very noble at four in the morning." His father rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and for one unguarded moment he looked not grave but *old*, old in a way that had nothing to do with years. "Sit down anyway. If you're going to hate me, do it from a chair."

There was a joke buried in there somewhere, the kind of gentle gallows humor their family traded like currency, but Orsen could not find it, could not dig it out, so he pulled the stool from the composing bench—the one with the wobble he'd never fixed because fixing it would mean admitting he sat there more than anyone—and he sat, and his father lowered himself onto the case of twelve-point bodoni as though his legs had been quietly removed without his consent.

"The letter," Aldous said. "Read it again."

"You read it."

"I want to hear how it sounds to you."

So Orsen unfolded the paper again, though he had it by heart. It was brief. It was brutal in its brevity, which was its whole design: *Feed him. Keep him warm. Forget his face. Burn this.* No signature, no seal beyond the wax—which was black, unmarked, the wax of someone who had bought plain sticks rather than risk a crest. Just those four instructions in a firm, educated hand, and beneath them nothing at all, which was somehow louder than any signature.

"'Feed him,'" Orsen read. "'Keep him warm. Forget his face. Burn this.'"

"Burn this," his father repeated softly. "And yet here we sit, holding it."

"Who wrote it? The hand is—"

"I know whose hand it is." Aldous looked at the press in the corner as if asking it for help, and the press, being a press, gave none. "It belongs to a man named Havelin, who was second secretary in the Ministry of Records, who once paid us—privately, in cash, before your mother died—to print four hundred invitations to a birthday party that never happened. A party for a child who had been sent away. You don't ask why a man pays to print invitations to a party that won't occur, Orsen. You take the coin and you set the type and you say nothing, because that's the trade. That has always been the trade."

"Father—"

"His name is Quentin Corvel." His father said it the way a man pulls a splinter: fast, deep, all at once, wanting it done. "The boy asleep in our back room. Quentin Corvel. Son of Minister Edmun Corvel, who was arrested nine days ago, whose house on the Velloran Ring was sealed Tuesday, and whose name appeared—" Aldous stopped. Started again, quieter. "Appeared on a certain list."

"What list?"

"You know what list."

"I want to hear you say it."

And here was the hairline crack widening, here was the sound Orsen would remember for the rest of his life, which was no sound at all: his father opening his mouth and the silence that came out first, lasting three heartbeats, four, while the candle gutted and the boy slept on ten feet away, oblivious, trusting, seven years old.

"The proscription lists," Aldous said finally. "The ones posted on the quarter-day boards. The ones naming enemies of the state, whose property reverts, whose families are subject to—relocation." He pronounced the word like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "Minister Corvel's name is on the current list. Which means the boy's face is worth money to the wrong people and safety to no one."

Orsen found he was gripping the edge of the composing stone so hard his knuckles had gone white. "How do you know the list contents before posting? Even the censors don't see—"

"Aldous," his father said, to himself, in a small corrective voice, as if introducing a stranger. "Tell him about Aldous."

"Stop it."

"No. You asked. So." He folded his hands, and Orsen saw that they were shaking—not the fine tremor of age but the coarse shake of a man holding something down. "Fourteen months ago, an inspector came to the shop. Not Vasse; a lesser man, since promoted, which tells you everything about how the regime rewards diligence. He brought a document. Names, dates, charges—draft form. And he said, *Master Fairweather, the state requires a printer of discretion. The official works will be set elsewhere. But drafts must exist somewhere before they are final, and we require that somewhere to be quiet.*"

"He wanted you to print the drafts."

"He wanted me to *set* them. Proof them. Correct the spelling of men who were about to die, because even murder, Orsen, even murder has a clerk, and the clerk wants his commas right." His father laughed—one dry, awful note, immediately strangled. "And I said no. Of course I said no. I am Aldous Fairweather, I have principles, I once refused to print a wedding announcement because I disapproved of the groom. I said no, and I put my hand on my license, which hangs on that wall, and I said, *This shop prints only what is permitted, and permitted does not include this.*

"And he smiled—he had a smile like a drawer opening—and he said, *Permitted. Yes. Let's discuss what's permitted.* And he took down my license, and he held it, and he said the license required renewal in the spring, and renewals required recommendation, and recommendation required *cooperation*, and that if the cooperation was good, the shop would continue, and if it was not, the shop would become a bakery, because bakeries cannot print pamphlets." He looked up. "Your mother was ill that year. The doctor's bills. The rent on this building, which doubles every year because the landlord is the cousin of a man on the council. Maren's nursing school fees. Your apprenticeship tools. There was a great deal of arithmetic, Orsen. There is always a great deal of arithmetic, and it is always done in other people's blood, and the clerk wants his commas right."

"You set the list." Orsen's voice sounded far away to himself, as if someone else in the shop were speaking. "You set the death lists."

"I proofed them." As if the distinction mattered. As if the distinction were the last plank of a drowning man. "I corrected the spelling. I improved the kerning. I made the names legible, so that when they were posted, every citizen of Velloran could clearly read who was to be erased." His hands had stopped shaking now, which was worse; they had gone still the way water goes still before ice. "And each quarter they returned with a new draft, and each quarter I told myself: this is the price of the shop, this is the price of Maren's fees, this is the price of keeping you both fed and invisible, and invisibility, I told myself, is a virtue in times like these. The silences that keep us safe. I taught you that. God forgive me, I taught you that as if it were wisdom."

The candle guttered again. In the back room, the boy murmured in his sleep—a small sound, a child's sound, the sound of someone dreaming about something harmless—and Orsen watched his father flinch as though struck.

"Does he know?" Orsen asked. "About his father?"

"He knows his papa went on a journey. Hiding children from the purge—there's a network, there has to be, Havelin didn't invent himself. They move them at night along the east road and scatter them to farms and convents and whatever mercy can be bribed. Something went wrong. The safe house fell, or the courier was taken, and someone ran with the child through the rain to the one address in Velloran where a letter could be written in a trusted hand." Aldous closed his eyes. "Because Havelin knew. Havelin has seen the drafts pass across my stone. Havelin knew exactly what I was, and what I'd done, and he brought the minister's son *here*—to the man whose typeface printed Corvel's condemnation—because either I would protect the boy out of guilt, or I would turn him in out of habit, and either way Havelin would know what manner of creature I truly am. It's a test, Orsen. It's a confession delivered by courier. *Forget his face*—as if I ever could. I set that name in lead. I know every letter of it. C-O-R-V-E-L. I tightened the spacing myself so it would fit the column."

The silence afterward was long. Somewhere outside, far off toward the university district, a whistle blew twice and stopped—the curfew patrols changing watch—and neither of them moved.

"You told me," Orsen said at last, and his voice cracked in the middle like a bad cast, "that silence was strength. You told me the neighbors stopped greeting us and you called it *wisdom*. You told me we don't print the pamphlets, we don't sign the petitions, we keep our heads and our hands clean, and I believed you. I built my whole life inside that silence. I sorted type by candlelight and I catalogued everything we don't say, and I thought—I actually thought—you were the one man in Velloran who knew which silences mattered." He stood, and the stool wobbled behind him, unfixed, unfixed forever now. "Which silences mattered, Father. Tell me. Because you kept silent when the greengrocer was taken, and silent when the Lerois' son disappeared, and silent every quarter-day for fourteen months while your own hands fed the machine. And tonight a wet, starving child walked through our door carrying a letter that says *forget his face*, and you couldn't even manage that silence. You looked at him and went white as fresh stock. So which silences mattered?"

Aldous took it. Orsen would give him that, always: he took it standing, eventually, taking the full weight of his son's voice without raising his own, the way he'd taught Orsen to take a misaligned forme—steadily, without blame, because blame fixes nothing.

"I don't know anymore," he said simply. "That's the truth, and it's the only inheritance I have left you. I used to believe I knew. I believed the arithmetic balanced. Feed the family, feed the machine, keep everyone breathing until better days. But better days were always next quarter, and the lists got longer, and my hand got steadier, and tonight—" he gestured helplessly toward the back room, toward the small sleeping breaths, "—tonight the arithmetic walked up to my door and knocked, wearing a dead man's coat, and asked me for bread."

"So what do we do?" Orsen heard his own heartbeat. "The letter says forget his face. If anyone finds him here—if the neighbors, if the patrols—"

"If anyone finds him here, we are all dead. You, me, Maren, and him, and probably the widow downstairs for good measure." His father said it flatly, without drama, the way he quoted prices. "Yes. I've done the sums."

"Then we send him away. Tonight. Back into the rain, to a church, to—"

"And which church, Orsen? Name one that isn't watched. Name one street that isn't patrolled. Name one pair of hands in this city you'd trust him to, knowing what you now know about the hands you grew up trusting." Aldous picked up the letter from the composing stone, held it to the candlelight, and studied it with a craftsman's eye—an eye that measured paper weight and ink density and the particular pressure of a pen—and Orsen saw something move behind his face, quick and cold, a thought forming that his father visibly wished to unform and could not.

"What?" Orsen said. "What is it?"

"Hm? Nothing. The paper. Good stock. Expensive." He set it down too carefully, aligned its edges with the stone's edge, the way he aligned everything, and Orsen realized his father was doing it deliberately—giving his hands a task, any task, so they would not shake. "We keep him tonight. We decide at dawn. Wake your sister gently; she'll want to look at his feet, the wet ruins them at his age. And Orsen—"

"Yes?"

"Not a word to anyone. Not Maren yet, not the widow, not God if you can manage it." The ghost of the old dry humor flickered and died. "We have survived fourteen months on knowing which silences matter. Tonight we learn whether we know anything at all."

Orsen turned to go, to fetch the extra blanket, to do the small domestic tasks that suddenly seemed like load-bearing walls holding up the sky—but at the doorway of the back room he stopped, because the boy had rolled over in his sleep, and the candlelight had found his face, washed clean of mud now where he'd rubbed at it, and Orsen understood with a lurch why his father had known the coat.

The boy had his father's brow. Everyone in Velloran who read the ministry bulletins knew Minister Corvel's portrait from the gazettes—the heavy thoughtful brow, the deep-set eyes. The gazette portrait hung in their own shop's archive, in a bound volume of permitted printing, page after page of a smiling official whose name Orsen's father had later set in a column of the condemned.

The boy stirred, opened his eyes halfway, found Orsen standing over him, and smiled—small, tentative, trusting, the smile of a child who had been promised by his papa that if he counted the letters on the walls, someone kind would come.

"Is this the game?" the boy whispered. "Am I doing it right?"

Orsen could not answer. Behind him, in the dark of the shop, he heard his father begin, very softly and very badly, to hum.

* * *

Maren would be home within the hour. Orsen knew her footsteps on Copper Lane the way he knew the press rhythm—quick, uneven, nurse's stride, three miles on her feet and still faster than any man's. And he knew what she would do: come through the door, smell the fear in the air the way she smelled fever, and demand answers in that flat, unsentimental voice that made grown surgeons step back.

He stood in the back room doorway, torn between the sleeping child and the front room where his father sat motionless among the type cases like a man sitting in the wreckage of his own monument, and he understood that whatever Maren decided—whatever fierce, practical, terrifying mercy she proposed—they had perhaps one hour of darkness left in which this family still resembled the one he had believed in.

Down the lane, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of her boots.

Orsen went to wake his sister early.
