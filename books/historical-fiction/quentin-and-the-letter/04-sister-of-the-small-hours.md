## 4. Sister of the Small Hours

Maren Fairweather had a rule about dawn: she did not trust it.

Dawn was the hour the ward gave back its dead. Dawn was when the laudanum wore off and the fevered began their arguments with God, and dawn was when she walked home through streets still blue with night, her boots loud on cobbles that belonged to no one but her and the milk carts and whatever the curfew had failed to swallow. Six years at Saint Verene's charity ward had taught her that the world was at its most honest before the sun came up, which was why she preferred it to any hour that came after. People lied better in daylight. So did governments. So did brothers.

She was three steps from the shop door, counting the cracks in the step out of habit — two new ones since Tuesday; the frost was working on the lintel again — when she noticed the light.

Not candlelight exactly. Candlelight in the print shop had a particular color, a warm amber she'd grown up inside of, and this was that color, but it was wrong the way a familiar face is wrong when it's frightened. It flickered too fast. Someone behind it kept moving.

Maren stood on the step a moment longer than a sensible woman would have, because six years at Saint Verene's had also taught her that the moment before you open a door is often the last moment in which you are permitted not to know things.

Then she opened it.

The smell hit her first: tallow, ink, wet wool. Then Orsen's face, gray as unsized paper, hovering over the type cases like a man guarding them from thieves. And then — small, mud-colored, folded into the big chair by the stove as though someone had tried to make him disappear into it and only half succeeded — a boy.

He couldn't have been more than seven. His boots were ruined. There was river mud to his knees and something darker dried on his sleeve that Maren's professional eye catalogued before her heart could interfere: old blood, someone else's, smeared where he'd been held. He was asleep sitting up, chin dropped to his chest, one hand closed around a wooden letterform — a capital Q, she saw, from the display case — the way other children held soldiers or marbles.

"Well," said Maren.

"Don't wake him," Orsen whispered, which was absurd, because she hadn't moved, hadn't spoken above the volume of a closing door, and yet he said it the way men say *don't shoot*.

"Orsen." She set down her bag. She always set down her bag first; it was how she told herself a situation could be managed. "Why is there a child in our kitchen at half past five in the morning?"

"He was left here."

"People are left *cabbages*, Orsen. On doorsteps. With notes about who's to have them. Children are somewhat more—"

"There was a note."

That stopped her. Not the fact of it — notes were paper, and paper meant nothing in this city anymore, paper was what they printed all day for the Ministry, obedient paper, blank-faced paper — but the sound of his voice saying it. Orsen had a voice for jokes and a voice for cataloguing silences, and this was neither. This was the voice of a man standing in a room where the floor had quietly become water.

She looked at the boy again. At his hands, chapped raw. At the mud line on his trousers, which was river mud — she knew river mud, half the ward came in coated in it after the spring floods — which meant he'd come along the embankment, past the guard posts, alone, at night, at seven years old.

And then she looked at her father's door, upstairs, shut tight, and at the lamp burning low beside the composing stone, and she understood that whatever this was, it had been going on for hours without her, and that both the men in this house had agreed — silently, in the way of men — that the sister would simply be informed once it was safe to inform her.

Maren had been managing situations since she was nine years old, when Aldous Fairweather decided grief required silence and Orsen decided grief required questions and Maren decided somebody in this family had to keep everyone fed. She crossed the room, knelt by the chair, and put two fingers to the child's throat without asking anyone's permission.

Pulse quick but steady. Skin hot — properly hot, not stove-hot. She peeled back one eyelid with a practiced thumb.

"He's fevered," she said. "Has been for a day, I'd say. Maybe two. He's dehydrated and he's walked miles and there's dried blood on his sleeve that isn't his." She stood. "Who is he?"

Orsen opened his mouth.

"And before you decide," Maren continued, untying her apron, "consider that I have spent the last twelve hours holding down a cooper with a broken leg while a surgeon dug splinters out of him, so my patience this morning is a coin I've already spent. Who is he, Orsen?"

"The Corvel boy," said a voice from the doorway, and there was Father, dressed, shaved, composed, looking every inch the licensed printer of approved materials, except that his hands were clasped behind his back like a man hiding something from an inspector, which in Aldous Fairweather's case was more or less a resting position.

The name went through Maren like cold water through cloth.

Minister Corvel. Purged in the autumn arrests. His name had come off the public rolls the same week the shop's license had been renewed with unusual smoothness, and Maren had noted the timing the way nurses note a symptom — filed, unspoken, waiting for the disease to declare itself. Well. Here it was, declaring itself, seven years old and burning with fever in her father's chair.

"Quentin," Father said, as if the name needed introducing. "He was left with a letter. The instructions were—" His mouth did something complicated. "Brief."

"I'll read it later," Maren said. "Right now the instructions are mine."

* * *

What followed took forty minutes and Maren performed it the way she performed everything: quickly, without ceremony, and with a running commentary designed to make it sound smaller than it was.

She woke the boy gently — he came up swinging, actually caught her wrist hard enough to leave marks, and then went rigid with apology, which told her a great deal about the last weeks of his life — and got willow bark tea into him, weak, sweetened with the good honey Maren hoarded against winter and now surrendered without a flicker of regret. She stripped his ruined clothes and found bruises on his ribs, old, yellow-green, in the pattern of gripping fingers. She washed the mud off in water Orsen heated, and while she worked she talked to him in the flat, unhurried voice she used on the dying and the frightened, about nothing — the weather, the smell of ink, whether he liked honey — until his answers stopped being flinches and started being words.

His fever was high enough to worry her and low enough to manage. That was the arithmetic she gave Orsen, who hovered with his sleeves rolled like a surgeon's useless nephew.

"He needs to stay in bed, drink this every quarter hour, and be seen by no one," she said. "Can your type arrange that?"

"Maren—"

"No. Listen." She wrung out the cloth, laid it across the boy's forehead. He'd stopped flinching at touch somewhere around the second cup of tea, which was either trust or exhaustion, and she suspected exhaustion wearing trust's coat. "You don't understand what you've brought into this house. I do. The ward has been full of it for a fortnight. Full of *questions*."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind orderlies ask while changing dressings, very casually, to men too sick to lie well. Whether anyone's seen strangers on the east road. Whether families on our street have taken in lodgers. A woman came in Tuesday with a broken arm she swears she fell getting out of a cart, and while the bone was being set she asked *me* — me, in my apron, with her arm hanging wrong — whether the print shops were still keeping curfew registers." Maren folded the bloody washcloth with unnecessary precision. "Someone is walking the city collecting answers, Orsen, and they're doing it through charity wards because sick people talk and nurses are assumed to be too busy to listen. Which is flattering, in its way."

Father had gone very still by the stove. "The inspectorate wouldn't use the wards. It's crude."

"It's efficient," Maren said. "Which is worse. Crude men kick doors. Efficient men ask about your mother's cough." She turned back to the boy — Quentin, she made herself say internally, Quentin, son of Corvel, say it plainly or it'll own you — and found him watching her with an expression she recognized from the ward: the careful face of a child deciding how much of the adults' trouble is his fault.

"You," she said to him, "are going to sleep in the garret, and when you wake up you're going to be my nephew."

His eyes widened slightly. Orsen made a sound like a man swallowing his own tongue.

"Pip," Maren went on, briskly, because hesitation was the one thing she never allowed near a lie — a lie, like a dressing, had to be applied fast and smooth or it wouldn't take. "My name is Maren, yours is Pip Fairweather, you're eleven—" he looked briefly outraged at this arithmetic, which pleased her enormously, a child who wanted credit for his full seven years would behave honestly in every other respect "—no, eight, you've been sent from your aunt in Marrow Lane because of the sickness, scarlatina, in the house, and you've been quarantined in the garret, which is why nobody sees you. Scarlatina frightens people wonderfully. Nobody visits a house with scarlatina. They cross the street."

"That's a fever chart," Orsen said slowly.

"That is a *fever chart*, yes." She pulled her bag onto the table and drew out a proper ward ledger page — she had a dozen, purloined over the years, because Saint Verene's threw away paper like a rich man and Maren did not — and began writing in her neat clinical hand. *Patient P. Fairweather, age 8. Fever onset approx. 3 days prior. Willow bark, qtr-hourly. Quarantine observed.* She dated it four days back, smudged the ink with her thumb, blotted it unevenly, and aged the corner over the stove flame until it browned like something that had lived in a drawer through an illness. "There. If anyone comes asking, we have documentation. Nurses' records carry weight; nobody thinks to doubt a woman with a ledger."

"You forged a medical record in under a minute," Orsen said.

"Two minutes. And it isn't forging, it's *charting*. Charting is what happens before events, if the chartist is diligent." She blew on the ink. "I've been keeping fever charts for this household since I was fourteen, Orsen. You simply weren't ill enough to notice."

Father laughed — one dry syllable, quickly suppressed, the laugh of a man who remembered too late what was funny and what was fatal — and Maren shot him a look she had been perfecting since childhood, the look that said *we will discuss you later, at length, and you will not enjoy it.*

Because she had seen it, of course. In the space of ten minutes she'd seen everything: Father's white face last night, preserved in Orsen's retelling; the way Aldous stood apart from the boy as if proximity itself were incriminating; and underneath it, threaded through the whole room, the particular silence of a man who knows precisely how much trouble he is in and has known for some time. Maren had spent six years reading bodies for a living. Her father's body was confessing in every joint.

But that was later. First: the child.

Quentin — Pip — had gone heavy-eyed, the fever and the honey pulling him under. As she lifted him, he murmured something into her shoulder. She bent closer.

"...counted to nine hundred," he whispered. "On the walls. All the letters."

"Yes," Maren said, into his hair, because it was clearly the correct answer to something. "Very good. Sleep now."

She carried him up to the garret herself, refusing Orsen's help with a jerk of her head, and settled him into the cot among the stored paper stock, wrapped in two blankets and her own shawl. He slept the way exhausted children sleep: completely, instantly, like something falling.

When she came back down, the men were waiting in the positions they'd apparently held for an hour — Orsen at the table, Father by the window, the letter lying between them on the composing stone like a specimen.

"Now," said Maren, sitting, folding her hands. "Read me the letter. All of it. And then, Father, you are going to tell me why you recognized that child on sight, because I watched your face when you came downstairs and I have seen that expression on men in the ward who've just been told the surgery didn't take."

"Maren," Aldous began, in the reasonable voice, the licensed-printer voice, the voice that had talked inspectors off the doorstep for twenty years—

"Don't." She didn't raise it. Raising it was for amateurs. "It is six o'clock in the morning. I have been awake for thirty hours. There is a purged minister's son sleeping over our heads and a forged chart in my bag explaining why, and I have just committed us all to a fiction I will now have to maintain with my hands and my face for however long this lasts. You do not get the reasonable voice. You get the truth, and then you get to watch me decide what we do next."

The clock ticked. Outside, the milk cart rattled past, and somewhere a shutter opened on the ordinary morning of a city that did not yet know what was coming for it.

Orsen pushed the letter across the stone toward her. And Maren Fairweather, who had read death in faces all night, sat down to read what the dead minister had written, and felt — beneath the fear, beneath the fury, in the place where she kept the thing that had made her a nurse instead of a ghost — something fierce and unsentimental settle in her chest, taking up its post.

Whatever her father had done, whoever came asking — the boy upstairs was hers now. That part, at least, required no deliberation at all.
