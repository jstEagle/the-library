## 5. The Letter's Second Life

The letter sat on the workbench between the ink stone and the burnishing bone, and it looked, for all the world, like any other piece of paper that had passed through the Fairweather shop in the last twenty years.

That was the trouble.

Orsen had been staring at it for the better part of an hour, long enough for his candle to gutter twice and require trimming, and he had not touched it since he'd first smoothed it flat under the paperweight—a chipped slab of marble that had once, according to family legend, held down a royal proclamation nobody in Velloran had been permitted to read. The letter was small, folded in thirds, sealed with plain wax rather than any signet. Its message ran to four lines:

*Feed him. Keep him warm. Forget his face.*

And below that, smaller: *Burn this.*

Orsen had not burned it. This was either wisdom or folly, and he suspected that by morning he would know which.

He had spent his whole life learning to read type the way other people read faces. His father said a compositor could be blindfolded and handed a page and tell you within a paragraph whether it had been set by a careful man or a hurried one—the spacing told you, the kerning, the little hesitations where a hand had wobbled. Orsen had thought this was one of Father's exaggerations, the sort of thing Aldous Fairweather said to make the trade seem like a priesthood rather than a business of ink-stained cuffs and lead poisoning. But now, with the letter under the candlelight, Orsen understood that Father had not exaggerated at all. He had perhaps even understated it.

Because Orsen knew this typeface.

Not merely recognized it—knew it. Knew it the way you know your own handwriting, or the creak of the third stair, or the particular way your father clears his throat when he is about to say something unwelcome. The capital *F* in *Feed him* had a small notch cut into its lower arm, a flaw so minor that no censor, no inspector, no reader in the entire city would ever notice it. But Orsen had noticed it every working day of his life, because the notch had been there since before Orsen could read, because the letter had come off his father's graver when Aldous Fairweather was still a young man cutting matrices in a shop half this size, and because Aldous had once told him, laughing over supper, that the notch was a mistake he'd decided to keep. "A flaw in the punch," he'd said, "is like a mole on a pretty face. It proves somebody made it."

Somebody had made it. Somebody had made it at the bench three feet from where Orsen stood, with tools hanging on the wall behind him, in the shop that the state licensed precisely because the Fairweathers printed exactly what they were permitted to print and nothing more.

The letter left with Quentin had been set in their own type.

* * *

He did the sensible thing first, which was to check whether he might be wrong. He crossed to the type cases—the great banks of shallow wooden drawers, each compartment a pigeonhole of leaden letters—and slid open the case marked for the body size used on official correspondence. He found the capital F. He tilted it toward the candle. There was the notch, worn smooth by years of use but unmistakable, like a scar on a familiar knuckle.

He set the letter down beside the case and compared them again, line by line. The *K* in *Keep* had its distinctive high crossbar. The *B* in *Burn* sat slightly proud on its base, a quirk of casting that Father compensated for with a sliver of paper under the wrong side of every forme. Even the spacing betrayed them: whoever had composed this letter had worked quickly, in poor light, by someone who knew where everything was without looking.

Someone who worked at this bench.

Orsen sat down heavily on the composing stool and let the paranoia arrive, because it was going to arrive anyway and it seemed better to receive it properly than to have it ambush him later.

Here were the possibilities, laid out as neatly as sorts in a case.

Possibility the first: someone outside the family had stolen access to the shop—borrowed the type, set the letter, returned it undetected. This required a thief with a compositor's training, knowledge of which case held which face, and the nerve to stand at this bench past curfew while the whole household slept above. Possible. Not likely. The shop locked like a strongbox; Father was obsessive about the bolts; and anyone who could pick those locks could surely have bought type anywhere in Velloran rather than risk ours.

Possibility the second: someone inside the family had set it. And there was really only one member of the family whose hands moved across those cases with the confidence of long habit, whose graver had cut the very letters in question, and who had gone white as unbleached stock when the boy appeared at the door and had needed to sit down on the stairs like a man who'd taken a blow.

Father.

But why? Why would Aldous Fairweather—who had built twenty years of safety out of silence, who refused pamphlets, who lowered his eyes to neighbors, who had taught Orsen that invisibility was the only wealth worth keeping—why would such a man compose a letter ordering himself to shelter the son of a purged minister?

Unless he hadn't composed it. Unless someone else had used the shop's type to write the letter, knowing—or guessing—that the trail would bend toward the Fairweathers. Knowing that if the authorities ever traced the typeface, they would come knocking at our door with questions we could not survive.

Whoever wrote it wanted a trail that led home. Or wanted a trail that could be erased—burned, as instructed, leaving nothing behind but a boy with mud on his boots and a face everyone was ordered to forget.

Either way, the shop itself was evidence now. Every drawer, every matrix, every stick of type was a confession waiting to be read aloud by the right pair of eyes.

Orsen became aware, distantly, that he was holding the letter so tightly he had creased it, and that his hands were shaking in a way they never shook at the bench. He put the letter down. He breathed the way Maren had taught him years ago, when he'd fainted over a dissection diagram she'd brought home from the charity ward: *in through the nose, out through the mouth, and think about something boring.* He thought about the smell of lampblack. It helped only marginally.

* * *

"You're going to wear a hole in the floorboards."

Maren stood in the doorway to the stairwell, still in her ward cloak, her hair pinned up crookedly from the night's shift. She looked tired in the specific way that nurses get tired—not sleepy, exactly, but emptied, as though she had poured herself out into other people and would refill by morning. Her eyes went from Orsen's face to the letter on the bench, and something sharpened in them.

"So," she said. "You've read it."

"Quentin's asleep?"

"Dead asleep, curled around the warming pan like it owes him money." She came into the room, pulled the door shut behind her, and stood with her back against it—an instinct, Orsen noted, that he was beginning to share. "I checked on him twice. He sleeps like a child who has learned that sleeping is a job you do well or else." She nodded at the letter. "Well? Say it."

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on for the last hour loud enough to hear through the floor. You pace when you're frightened, Ors. You've always paced. You paced before Mother's funeral until you wore a groove in the landing."

"I don't pace."

"You are pacing now."

He stopped, discovered that she was right, and felt the peculiar indignation of a man caught in his own habits. Then he picked up the letter and held it out to her. "Look at the F. In *Feed*. Tell me what you see."

Maren took it, squinted, turned it in the light. She was not a compositor, but she had grown up in this shop too; she had inked her first proofs at six years old, mostly by way of being somewhere out from underfoot. "It's a letter," she said. "An F. It says feed."

"The notch. On the lower arm."

She looked closer. Her brow furrowed. And then Orsen watched the same realization move through his sister that had moved through him—watched it land, watched her go very still in the particular way she went still when a patient's fever spiked unexpectedly. Nurses, he thought, and compositors, and probably spies: all professions of reading small signs.

"That's Father's F," she said quietly.

"That's Father's F."

"This letter was set here."

"In this shop. Probably at this bench." He heard how thin his own voice sounded. "Maren, whoever wrote this used our type. Our letters. Which means either Father wrote it—which makes no sense—or somebody wanted a letter that pointed straight at this house and then told us to destroy it."

"Burn this," Maren read aloud. She said it flatly, without inflection, the way she read symptoms off a chart. "So the tidy version is: someone does us a monstrous favor and asks us to leave no fingerprints. The untidy version?"

"The untidy version is that we're meant to be caught with him eventually. That the letter is bait, or insurance, or the first step of a longer game, and Quentin is the hook." Orsen rubbed his eyes. The candle had burned low enough that shadows pooled in the type cases like standing water. "Think about it. Who leaves a purged minister's son on a printer's doorstep? Of all the doors in Velloran? A butcher's, a cooper's, any house without a state license nailed above it—those families can hide a child. We can't hide a sneeze. Everything we print goes past the censor's table. Our name is in the registry. Father signs the license renewal every quarter in front of witnesses."

"Unless," Maren said slowly, "the person who chose this door knew something about us that we don't know."

"What could anyone know about us that we don't know?"

"I don't know, Ors. I patch people and count linens. You arrange little metal letters. Father—" She stopped.

"Father what?"

"Nothing." But she said it in the voice she used when it wasn't nothing, and she folded the letter along its original creases with great care, as though it were something living that might startle. "Where is Father, by the way?"

"Asleep. Or lying awake pretending to sleep, which with him amounts to the same posture." Orsen hesitated. "Maren, when he saw the boy—he knew him. I mean *knew* him. He said the name before he'd read anything. Quentin. He said it like a man spitting out a stone."

Her eyes came up sharply. "You're sure?"

"He said 'that's Corvel's boy' before the boy said two words. How does he know that? How does Father look at a mud-streaked child and see a minister's son in him?"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Down in the street, far off toward the river, a dog barked itself hoarse and gave up. Somewhere nearer, the curfew bell had long since finished its rounds, and the city lay in that held-breath quiet it managed on bad nights, like a patient trying not to cough.

"There's something he isn't telling us," she said at last.

"There's always something he isn't telling us. Silence is his trade."

"No." Maren shook her head. "This is different. I've watched him lie to inspectors—I've watched him smile at men I'd cross the street to avoid—and he's good at it, better than either of us will ever be. Tonight he wasn't lying. Tonight he was *afraid*, and worse than afraid, he was—" she searched for the word, "—*guilty*. Like the boy was a bill come due."

Orsen looked at the letter again, at the little notch in the F, at the instruction to forget a face that was currently asleep upstairs with a warming pan. The paranoia bloomed further, putting out roots. If Father knew the boy on sight—if Father had some history with Corvel, with the ministry, with whatever purge had orphaned a seven-year-old—then the letter might not be bait aimed at the Fairweathers from outside at all. It might be a thread in a web Father had been spinning for years, one strand among many, and Orsen and Maren simply flies who had never noticed the silk.

No. That was the fear talking. Father was cautious, dull, devout in his smallness—but he was not a spider. Was he?

Twenty years of printing exactly what was permitted. Twenty years of surviving a regime that devoured printers the way winter devoured late crops. What did survival cost, exactly? What had it cost, all these years, and who had paid it?

"Tomorrow," Maren said, breaking into his spiral, "you ask him. Straight out. No dancing."

"And if he lies?"

"Then we'll know the shape of the lie, which is more than we know now." She tucked the letter into the pocket of her cloak, and Orsen started to object, and she fixed him with the look she used on interns who questioned her dosage arithmetic. "It'll be safer on me than on you. You'd stare at it all night and wear yourself to ribbons. Besides—" a ghost of her usual dry humor surfaced, "if anyone searches the house, they won't search a nurse's cloak. Everyone assumes we're only carrying laudanum and other people's secrets."

"That's exactly what you're carrying."

"Among other things." She paused at the door, hand on the latch, and glanced back at him. "Ors. Whatever Father's hiding—it doesn't change what's upstairs. A child slept in a press crate tonight because his father told him letters on walls would keep him company. Whatever game the adults are playing, he thinks it's the kind with rules that protect him." Her jaw tightened. "We should try to be worthy of being mistaken for the good guys."

Then she was gone, up the stairs, and Orsen was alone with the type cases and the guttering candle and the terrible new knowledge sitting in his chest like swallowed lead.

* * *

He should have gone to bed. Instead he did what compositors do when the world stops making sense: he went to work.

He lit a second candle and opened the case drawers one by one, checking. It was ritual as much as investigation—the same motion his hands made a hundred times a day, sorting, verifying, restoring order—and it calmed him even as it confirmed his fears. The capital F was there. So was the K, the B, the whole alphabet that had written the letter, every piece accounted for in its proper cell. Nothing had been taken away. Whoever had set that letter had used the shop's type and put it back, neat as a prayer, leaving the crime scene tidier than they'd found it.

Which narrowed things further. A stranger wouldn't bother. A stranger wouldn't know the system. Only someone who belonged here—

His hand stopped over a drawer near the bottom of the case. The private stock. Father's own cabinet, kept locked, holding the special matrices—the commemorative faces, the license seal, the ornamental initials used on documents fine enough to require them. The lock hung open.

Orsen stared at it. Father never left that cabinet unlocked. He'd seen the man check it twice before locking up on ordinary days, days when nothing in the world was wrong.

Had Father opened it tonight, after carrying Quentin inside? Had he set the letter himself after all, at some hour Orsen had slept through, and failed to lock up behind him in his agitation? Or—

Or had someone else been in the shop tonight, someone with the run of the place, someone whose hands knew every drawer in the dark?

From upstairs came a faint sound: footsteps crossing the floor above, slow, deliberate, pausing—Orsen could chart the path by the creaks—pausing at the top of the stairwell. Standing there. Listening down into the dark shop where his son sat among the evidence of a crime neither of them had yet confessed to.

The footsteps retreated. A door closed, softly, with the exaggerated care of a man who wanted very much to seem asleep.

Orsen blew out the candles and sat in the black, listening to his own heartbeat, understanding suddenly and completely that the letter was not the beginning of anything. It was the middle. Whatever story it belonged to had been running underneath their lives for years, silent as water under ice, and tonight it had finally cracked the surface—and everything under the ice was coming up.

In the morning, he would ask his father. Straight out. No dancing.

Above him, faintly, the third stair creaked anyway, though no one had stepped on it.
