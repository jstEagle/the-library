## 6. Ink on the Censor's Table

The knock came at half past nine, which was wrong in three ways.

First, customers did not come to Fairweather & Son before noon; the shop's trade was in official notices, tax schedules, and almanacs, and the men who ordered such things liked their errands conducted in daylight, with lunch somewhere in the middle of them. Second, Aldous had gone out at dawn to the Guildhall to collect a proofing job and had not returned, which meant Orsen was alone upstairs with a seven-year-old boy who had been instructed, in the sternest whisper Maren could manage, to be a fever. And third—this was the way that mattered most—the knock came in threes. Even. Measured. The knock of a man who had never once in his life had to wait for anything.

Orsen knew that knock the way he knew the smell of lampblack.

"Quentin," he said, without turning from the type case he'd been sorting. "You remember what your fever is?"

"Hot," said Quentin, from behind the stack of unsold hymnals where Maren had installed him at dawn. He sounded delighted about it. The boy had taken to the game of being ill with the enthusiasm other children reserved for fairs. "And I cough when the lady says."

"There's no lady today."

"I cough anyway," Quentin said generously.

Orsen wiped his hands on his apron, considered wiping them again for no reason at all, and went down the stairs. At the bottom he paused, pressed his palms flat against his thighs until they stopped trembling—they mostly stopped—and opened the door.

Inspector Vasse stood on the step with his hands clasped behind his back, gray-coated, gray-faced, gray-eyed, a man assembled entirely out of overcast weather. Behind him waited two soldiers of the city watch, one of whom was examining the shop's shutters with the expression of a man who found all woodwork morally suspect.

"Master Fairweather." Vasse's voice was dry paper. "A routine inspection."

"Of course," Orsen said, because there was no other answer in Vellora that morning, or any morning, or in the whole history of mornings under this particular regime. "Come in. Mind the ink."

* * *

Vasse did not mind the ink. Vasse stepped precisely around it, as though ink were beneath his notice, which in fairness it was—it was beneath everyone's notice, that was rather the entire architecture of the state—and proceeded into the shop with the unhurried gait of a man who intended to look at everything and be looked at by nothing.

Orsen had met Vasse twice before. Both times had been inspections. Both times had been, officially, routine. Orsen had privately begun to suspect that Vasse's entire life was routine, that the man woke routinely, ate routinely, slept routinely, and would die routinely someday, and that his gravestone would read HE INSPECTED THINGS, AND THE THINGS WERE FOUND IN ORDER. There was something almost restful about it, if you didn't think too hard about what happened to the things Vasse sometimes found out of order.

"The elder Fairweather is not in?" Vasse asked, drifting toward the composing stone.

"He's at the Guildhall. Collecting proofs." This was true. It had been true since dawn. Orsen clung to its truth the way a drowning man clings to a plank that is, admittedly, also somewhat wet.

"Hm." Vasse picked up a composing stick from the stone, turned it over, set it down in exactly the place he'd found it—to the inch, Orsen noticed, to the actual inch—and moved on. His fingers trailed along the frame of the press like a man greeting an old acquaintance. "Business is well?"

"Well enough."

"And your father's hand is still the finest in the district. So I am told." A pause, delicate as a scalpel laid on silk. "By people who know such things."

"He works hard," Orsen said, and heard how thin it came out, and hated himself for it.

"Mm." Vasse arrived at the type cases. Of course he did. Orsen had known, from the first knock, that they would end here; the type cases were the heart of the shop, and inspectors were drawn to hearts the way carrion birds are drawn to—no. Warmth, he reminded himself. The guidance of his own better nature. Inspectors were drawn to hearts the way tax assessors were drawn to parlors. That was kinder, and kindness cost nothing, and he had a boy hiding behind hymnals upstairs.

"These," Vasse said, resting two fingers on the topmost case, "are old."

"Some of them. My grandfather cut some of the matrices."

"Your father cut most of them." Vasse's eyes came up, gray meeting whatever color Orsen's eyes were, which at the moment felt gray as well. "He has a gift. A graver in the hand of Aldous Fairweather is a thing to see, I'm told. I've never seen it done." Another pause, longer. "I should like to, sometime."

"I'll ask him," Orsen said, and the words came out steady, which surprised him, because inside he was doing arithmetic of a very ugly kind: the letter left with Quentin had been printed in this shop's typeface, cut by his father's graver, and the man standing in front of him knew typefaces the way priests knew scripture.

* * *

It was while Vasse lingered over the small-pica case that he mentioned the east road.

"A child was reported," he said, conversationally, the way another man might mention the price of coal. "On the east road. Three nights ago. A woman at the tollhouse saw a cart stop, and someone carry a boy into the fog. Toward the river quarter." He lifted a sort from its compartment, held it to the light from the window, examined the face of the letter as though checking it for sins. "She thought perhaps the family was fleeing the sickness in the wards. Though of course the wards are where sick people belong, so it seemed odd to flee them."

Orsen's mouth said, "People do odd things in fog," which was either very brave or very stupid, and his heart voted stupid.

"They do," Vasse agreed mildly. He returned the sort to its compartment. Click. "The tollhouse woman described the boy. Small. Dark-haired. About seven years of age." Click. Click. His finger walked along the compartments as he spoke, touching each one lightly, a blind man reading a page. "One imagines a frightened child would be hungry. Cold. Possibly ill." Click. "Ill children require nurses."

"My sister is a nurse," Orsen said, and could have bitten his own tongue off.

"So she is." Vasse smiled. It was not a smile; it was a drawing of a smile, executed in gray pencil by a competent but uninspired artist. "At the charity ward. Where the questions have been coming thick lately, my colleagues tell me. Everyone asking after everyone else's business." He closed the case drawer with a soft wooden sound that made Orsen flinch. "I find that interesting. Don't you?"

"I find it tiring," Orsen said. "All that asking."

"Yes," said Vasse, watching him with those flat gray eyes. "I imagine you would."

* * *

What saved Orsen—or nearly saved him, or possibly doomed him, the verdict was still out—was the bell above the door, which jangled at eleven o'clock with a customer: Master Hobb, the corn-factor, come to collect his quarterly grain notices. Hobb was a large man with a voice like a landslide and no curiosity whatsoever about anything beyond wheat, and he filled the shop with complaints about tariffs until the air itself felt safer.

But before the bell rang, there was a moment—a single, terrible moment—when Orsen heard footsteps overhead. Small ones. Pattering across the garret floor with the unmistakable rhythm of a seven-year-old boy who had been told to stay still and had interpreted this instruction creatively.

Vasse heard it too. His head tilted, minutely, like a hound's.

"Cats," Orsen said.

"Cats," Vasse repeated.

"We have several. They get into everything." Orsen's voice performed calm with more conviction than he felt, which was the trick of performance, he supposed, and he was suddenly, achingly proud of his father, who had done this every day of Orsen's life without ever once letting the seams show.

"Ah," said Vasse. "Cats."

Above them, mercifully, Quentin trod on something soft, and the pattering ceased, or at least became the pattering of a cat, which is to say it continued at a slightly guiltier pace.

Hobb's arrival broke the moment. Vasse watched the corn-factor take his bundle of notices, watched Orsen count coin, watched everything, always watching everything, and then straightened his coat and announced that his inspection was concluded and that he found the premises in order.

"Though," he added, at the threshold, turning back with his drawn-on smile, "do give my regards to your father. And tell him—" he paused, savoring it, a man tasting a phrase he had clearly rehearsed on the walk over, "—tell him the censor's table misses his ink. There aren't many hands left in Vellora that can set a clean proof. One does hate to see talent go... quiet."

He tipped his hat. The soldiers followed. The door closed.

Orsen stood in the middle of his own shop, among his own letters, in the smell of his own ink, and discovered that his hands were shaking so badly he had to grip the edge of the composing stone just to stand still.

* * *

He found Quentin upstairs, sitting cross-legged amid the hymnals, wearing a blanket as a cape and looking extremely pleased with himself.

"I coughed," Quentin announced. "Twice. When the boots went past the stairs. You didn't hear because I was quiet about it."

"You were meant to be a fever, not a cat."

"I was both." The boy shrugged, a gesture of magnificent sophistication he had certainly learned from Maren. "Fever-cats are the worst kind. Papa told me about them once. Everything in Vellora is the worst kind, if you listen long enough." Then, tilting his head, studying Orsen with the unnerving directness of children and drunkards: "Why are your hands shaking? Are you a fever-cat too?"

Orsen sat down heavily on a crate of unsold psalters—the same crate, he realized, and his stomach dropped through the floorboards, the same crate that had carried this boy's father's son across half the city—and put his face in his trembling hands.

"No," he said. "I'm the other kind."

"What kind is that?"

"The kind that's going to have to learn to lie better."

Quentin considered this with great seriousness. "Papa says lying is a tool," he offered. "Like a graver. It depends what you're cutting."

Orsen looked up. The boy sat in the candle-smoked light of the garret, small and mud-stained and alive, the exact age at which children begin remembering things permanently, and Orsen understood with sudden clarity why Inspector Vasse wanted him found, and why Aldous had gone white at the sight of him, and why the letter in the drawer downstairs had been printed in their own typeface—because whoever wrote it had wanted the trail to lead here, or had wanted the trail to be erasable, and either way the shop itself had become evidence, and evidence, in Vellora, was a fire that burned outward from the center.

Downstairs, the shop bell jangled again.

Orsen went cold. Hobb had already collected his notices. Vasse had already inspected his order. Nobody else knocked at Fairweather & Son before noon, and nobody knocked twice.

He rose, pressed his palms flat against his thighs until they stopped trembling—they mostly stopped—and started down the stairs, counting the steps, twelve of them, each one colder than the last.

Through the stairwell window he could see the street below, and the figure waiting at his door: not gray, not Vasse, but a woman in a nurse's shawl with her collar up and her jaw set in the particular way that meant his sister had run the whole way from the ward, and that whatever she had learned there could not wait for the courtesy of a locked door.
