## 7. Quentin Speaks

The counting game is the best game.

Papa said: *Count the letters, Quentin. Count them until someone comes.* And I have been counting them for ever and ever and ever, which is maybe three days or maybe one hundred days, because the letters are everywhere in this house, on every wall, in little boxes with little drawers, and some of them are backwards and Papa says that is a secret only printers know but he isn't here to say it so I say it to myself instead, quietly, under the blanket where nobody can hear me being clever.

*A is for Apple. B is for Bear. C is for Cat.*

The backwards ones are the best ones. There is an R that lives in the third drawer down and it looks at you wrongways like it's been standing on its head all day waiting for somebody to notice. I noticed it first thing this morning. Or maybe it was yesterday morning. The man with the tired face — Orsen, Mama would say his name is Orsen, be polite, Quentin — came up the stairs and blew out my candle and said *back to sleep* even though it was morning-ish, probably, and I said nothing because of the rule.

The rule is: *don't tell anyone your name.*

That's the second-best game actually. It's called Don't Tell Anyone Your Name and I am extremely good at it. I have not told anyone my name for the whole entire time I have been here, not even when the lady with the nurse shoes looked right at me and said *what do we call you, little one*, which was a trap, obviously, because she wanted me to say my name, and if I had said my name I would have lost the game and then Papa would have made the sad face, the one where his mouth goes flat like a shelf.

So I said nothing. I won.

I am winning so much lately.

* * *

Here is how the game started, in case nobody knows except me:

Papa woke me up in the night — not the nice kind of waking where there's bread, the fast kind, his hand over my mouth like a warm wooden spoon — and he said *Quentin. Listen. We're going to play the counting game now.* And I said *the one from the garden?* because we used to play the counting game in the garden where I'd shut my eyes against the pear tree and count to a hundred while he hid, except he never hid properly, he always stood behind the same fig tree smoking, thinking I couldn't see the smoke going up over the leaves like a little grey ribbon that said PAPA IS HERE, silly Papa.

But this time he said *no, the new one. The big one.* He was putting shirts into a bag very fast, shirts and also my other shirt and also a piece of dried apple wrapped in paper, and his hands were doing the thing they do when he's late for the Council, moving quick-quick without looking at what they're picking up.

He said: *You'll go to a print shop. You know what a print shop is?*

And I said yes, because I did know, because Papa's study smells like ink and once he let me set my own initials in type, Q and C, and printed them onto a scrap and I still have the scrap, except I don't have the scrap anymore, it's gone, everything's gone, but I remember it. Q and C. My letters. They were small and black and perfect and Papa said *there, now you exist twice,* which I didn't understand then and I understand less now but it felt important, like a coin you keep in your pocket even when it doesn't buy anything.

He said: *You'll hide in the crate. The big one by the wall. Can you be quiet in a crate?*

I said I could be quiet anywhere. Once I was quiet through an entire sermon. Two sermons. Mama laughed about it after, that special laugh she does with her nose.

Then Papa crouched down and held my shoulders and his face was wet even though it hadn't rained inside, and he said the rules. Papa is good at rules. He has rules for eating soup (spoon away from you), rules for meeting ministers (hand first, eyes up), rules for everything, and these were the most important rules of all the rules ever:

One. Stay in the crate until the shop is quiet, then find the stairs, then stay near the stairs.

Two. Do not tell anyone your name. Not your real one. If someone asks, you are nobody. You are a boy who belongs to no one yet.

Three. Count the letters. Every day. All of them. A to Z and then backwards Z to A, and the backwards ones too, the mirror ones, and don't stop until someone comes.

And I said *who comes?* and he said *someone will come, someone always comes for letters,* and then he kissed the top of my head so hard it almost hurt and put me in the crate and it was dark and smelled like oil and metal and him, and I heard him talking low to someone outside, and there was paper sounds, a letter being folded, and then the door, and then the quietest quiet in the whole world.

So that's why I'm here. It's a game. A very long game. Papa arranged the whole thing, Papa arranges everything, Papa knows everybody, Papa is a minister which is like a king but with more papers. He planned it all: the crate, the walking-through-the-dark part which I mostly don't remember except mud and running and a man carrying me whose heart was beating very loud right against my ear like a drum in a coat, and then the knocking at this door, knock-knock-knock, three knocks, which is the secret knock number, obviously, because everything Papa does has a reason even when it doesn't look like it.

This whole shop is the game board. That's what I figured out. The man and the lady here aren't strangers, they're *players*. Papa sent me to them on purpose because they have the most letters of anybody in the city. It makes perfect sense. If you want to hide a boy, hide him in the letters. Even I know that and I'm seven.

* * *

Today I decided to show the man-with-the-tired-face that I am good at the game. Because he keeps looking at me funny, like I'm a sum that won't come out right, and I thought if I showed him my counting he would stop looking like that and start looking pleased instead.

He was downstairs sorting the little drawers — the type case, that's what it's called, I learned it from listening, I learn everything from listening, it's my best thing after quiet — and I came and stood next to him and pointed at the drawer he had open and said:

"That one's the E. There's lots of E. Papa says E is the greediest letter, it takes more than everybody else."

His hands stopped.

"And that's the backwards R," I said, pointing up at the frame where the mirror letters live. "It's my favorite. It looks like it fell down."

He kept stopping his hands. Grown-ups are always doing that around me lately, stopping their hands, like their hands forgot something. Then he said, very gently, the way you talk to a bird:

"Your papa taught you the cases?"

"He taught me everything," I said. "Except the press, he wouldn't let me touch the press, he said the press waits until you're eight." I held up my fingers. "Eight is soon."

Orsen sat down on the little stool so he was smaller than me, which is polite, and he looked at me for a long time and I looked back because staring contests are also a game and I am unbeatable at those, ask anyone, ask the cat we used to have.

"Quentin—" he started, and then he stopped himself and made a strange face like the word had jumped out of his pocket without permission, and I felt very smug and also a little bit cold somewhere under my ribs, and I don't know why both of those happened at the same time.

"You said the name," I told him. "You're not supposed to say the name. That's a rule."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry. I broke the rule."

"That's all right," I said generously. "Everybody breaks one rule sometimes. But only one."

* * *

Later — later is my word for when things happen that I don't have a time for, because nobody tells me anything in this house, they just do faces at each other over my head like I'm furniture that breathes — later, the lady with the nurse shoes sat next to me on the cot and gave me bread with honey on it, actual honey, which means either it's a holiday or I'm dying, and she watched me eat it the way Mama watches me eat it, which is to say like watching bread is the most interesting thing that has ever happened in the history of the world.

"When your papa comes back," she said, "what will you tell him first?"

I chewed. This was a trick question, I could feel it, questions about Papa usually were, everyone asked about Papa, Papa Papa Papa, where is your papa, what is your papa's name, what did your papa do — as if I would fall for that. As if I didn't know the difference between a question and a trap. Papa taught me that too: *a question with your name in it is a net, Quentin. Swim around it.*

So I said: "I'll tell him I got to forty-one letters before breakfast. Forty-one! The old record was thirty-eight. I beat it by three."

She went still, the lady did, stiller than still, and her eyes got shiny like the ink pots, and I worried maybe I had broken her with my counting, the way sometimes you break a grown-up just by saying a true thing. Grown-ups break easy. Nobody warns you about that. Everybody warns you about stoves and rivers and strangers with sweets, and nobody ever once warns you that you can break a whole adult just by mentioning how many letters are on a wall.

"Forty-one," she repeated.

"Forty-two if you count the one on the ceiling," I said. "But the one on the ceiling is cheating, because it's upside down and also I can't reach it, so Papa says ceiling letters go in a separate pile." I licked honey off my thumb. "When I get to a hundred days I get a prize. That's the rule. A hundred days of counting and then somebody comes and the game ends and you win. Papa said somebody always comes for letters."

The lady looked at me for a long, long time. Out the window the sky was doing its evening orange thing, and somewhere far off there were bells, but the wrong bells, the fast angry ones, not the dinner ones. She opened her mouth and closed it and opened it again, and finally she said:

"What happens on the hundredth day?"

"You win," I said, like it was obvious, because it was. "Somebody comes. Somebody always comes. And then Papa takes me home and Mama makes the fish with the lemon and I get the prize and I tell them about the backwards R and how I won the name game every single day, and Papa laughs his big laugh, and—" 

I stopped, because the next part of the sentence had a hole in it, a hole shaped like I-don't-know-what-comes-after-winning, and I stood in front of the hole for a moment, seven years old, holding my honey bread.

The lady reached out and tucked the blanket over my legs even though the blanket was already fine, and she said, "You're a very brave counter, Quentin," and then she caught herself saying the name too, and I said "that's two," and she said "two what," and I said "two broken rules today, this house is terrible at the game," and she laughed, but it was a wet laugh, a leaky one, and she hugged me quick and hard like she was checking I was real, and left the candle burning even though Orsen always says candles out.

* * *

But here is the thing. Here is the thing I haven't told anybody, not even the blanket, not even the backwards R.

Last night I heard them talking. Downstairs, through the floor, in the low voices grown-ups use when they think children are asleep, which is the thickest floor in the world but I have ears like a fox, Papa says so, ears like a fox.

The man said a word. The lady said it back. They said it several times, quiet-quiet, like passing something hot from hand to hand: *Corvel. Corvel. Corvel.*

And that is Papa's name.

Not the whole one — the whole one is longer, it has a *Minister* in front of it like a hat — but it's Papa's name, the middle of it anyway, the part that goes in the important papers, the part that was on the scrap next to my Q and my C.

They said it scared. Not happy-scared like a birthday surprise. The other scared. The kind of scared that lives in cellars. And then the man said something else, lower than low, so low it almost wasn't words, and the lady said *he's a child, Orsen, whatever you think you know, he's a child,* and then there was a silence so big I could hear the candle eating itself.

I lay in the dark and did the math, the way Papa does math at dinner, tapping the table.

If this is Papa's game — and it is, it must be, Papa planned it, Papa always plans — then why do the players say Papa's name like it's a bruise?

Why did nobody come on day ten? Day twenty? Day thirty?

Why does the man look at me like I'm a sum that won't come out right, and why, when he thinks I'm sleeping, does he stand in the doorway and watch me with a face like the backwards R — like something familiar turned all the way around?

I counted the letters today. All of them. Forty-one before breakfast, sixty-three by supper. I counted them the regular way and then the mirror way, A to Z and Z to A, and the walls were full and the drawers were full and the whole shop was humming with little lead people standing shoulder to shoulder waiting to be made into words.

And on day — I think it's day thirty-four, or thirty-five, the days slide around like type in a loose case — on day thirty-four-or-thirty-five, somebody knocked at the door below, hard, three times, but not the secret knock, a different knock, a knock with boots in it, and Orsen went white like fresh paper and the lady grabbed my arm and said *upstairs, now, and Quentin —*

and her voice did the thing Papa's voice did on the last night,

the fast thing,

the not-a-game thing —

*— not one sound.*
