## 8. The Barge at Salt Quay

Maren Fairweather had learned long ago that the charity ward kept two kinds of secrets: the kind you buried with the patient, and the kind that walked out the door on someone else's tongue. She preferred the first. The second had a way of following people home.

It was Sister Ansel who brought this one, in her usual fashion—which was to say sideways, while pretending to talk about linen.

"We're short on sheets again," Ansel said, folding a bandage with the precision of a woman who believed God noticed creases. "The whole east ward's overflowing. Fever cases. And you know what fever means."

"Laundry," Maren said.

"Laundry," Ansel agreed solemnly, "and questions. Half the ward's asking after the coal barge. Quarter-day, out of Salt Quay. You'd think it was a pilgrimage."

"A coal barge."

"Mm." Ansel smoothed the bandage flat. "Three berths for passengers. Downriver, past the checkpoints, all the way to Merrow's Landing where nobody asks anybody anything. But they don't take coin."

Maren's hands did not stop moving, which was the first lesson of nursing and, as it happened, the first lesson of being a Fairweather. "What do they take?"

"Paper," Ansel said. "Printed safe-conducts. Proper ones, with seals and signatures and the little flourishes the inspectors like to see. The bargemaster can't read worth a damn, but he knows what an official document looks like when it's held up to a lantern." She paused, delicately, the way one pauses before setting down something fragile. "And your family being in the printing trade—"

"Our family prints almanacs," Maren said. "Almanac-makers don't forge documents. Almanac-makers predict weather badly and apologize for it annually."

"Of course," said Ansel, who had once watched Maren talk a gendarme out of searching the ward's medicine chest, and who therefore believed approximately nothing Maren said about her family's limits. "I only mention it because Widow Corvel's cousin asked me, and I told her I'd ask around."

"Widow Corvel's cousin."

"There are a great many cousins since the minister was purged," Ansel said mildly. "Grief makes people genealogists." She gathered her linens, stood, and added at the door, without turning around: "Quarter-day is six days off. In case anyone needs to know how much time things take."

Then she was gone, leaving behind the smell of carbolic and the distinct impression of having handed Maren a live coal wrapped in clean cloth.

* * *

Maren walked home through the gray end of the afternoon, taking the long way past the canal so she could think, which was where she always took the long way when she needed to think, and which Orsen teased her about because he thought she went to look at the water. She did not look at the water. She looked at nothing and arranged facts.

Fact: there was a barge. Fact: three berths. Fact: payment in forged papers, which meant the Fairweathers could pay better than almost anyone in Velloran, if they were willing to become the sort of people who paid that way.

Fact—the fact she made herself hold up to the light last, the way you turn a wound to check it—there were four of them now, counting the boy asleep in her room with his thumb hooked in the collar of a shirt that had belonged to Orsen at age nine.

She counted them again anyway. Four. Three berths. The arithmetic sat in her chest like a stone swallowed wrong.

Their father would say, *We were never four.* Aldous would say it gently, reasonably, with the terrible reasonableness he used for everything from pricing almanacs to deciding which neighbors not to greet. He would say Quentin had never been theirs, that the letter had ordered them to forget the boy's face, that sentiment was a luxury the shop had never stocked. And every word would be true the way a ledger is true—accurate, balanced, and utterly without mercy.

Maren had spent her life among the dying, and she had learned this: ledgers balance. People don't.

She came home by the alley door, as she always did now, and found the shop dim and smelling of ink and lamp oil, and found Orsen at the composing stone sorting type by candlelight with the concentration of a monk, and found Quentin sitting cross-legged under the worktable watching him with the wide-eyed devotion children reserve for anyone performing manual labor.

"He's been helping," Orsen said.

"I have *not* helped," Quentin corrected with dignity. "I'm learning. There's a difference."

"There is indeed," Maren agreed. "Helpers get paid. Learners get supper."

Quentin brightened at the word supper the way only a child who had once hidden in a press crate could brighten, and Maren felt the stone in her chest shift and settle somewhere deeper. That was the trouble with feeding people. It attached them to you. It attached *you* to *them*, which was worse.

"Where's Father?" she asked.

"Supper with the guildmaster. Negotiating the winter almanac rates. He'll be back late and insufferable." Orsen glanced up, and whatever he saw in her face made him set down his type stick. "What is it?"

"Send him to bed first," Maren said, nodding at Quentin. "I'll mind the lamp."

Orsen looked at her a moment longer. Her brother was many things—careful, dutiful, prone to alphabetizing his feelings—but he was not slow. "Bed, Quentin. All of you goes to bed, arms and legs and everything."

"All of me," Quentin confirmed gravely, and padded off toward the stairs, pausing at the bottom to inform them, "The letters upstairs smell different than the letters down here. Sweeter. I checked."

He disappeared. Maren waited until the small footsteps faded, then told Orsen everything: the barge, the quarter-day, the three berths, the price in paper. Orsen listened the way he sorted type—one piece at a time, each turned over before it was placed.

"Forged safe-conducts," he said at last. "That's not printing. That's—" He stopped himself, but Maren heard the word he'd swallowed. Treason. It hung in the lamplight between them, unclaimed.

"It's arithmetic," Maren said. "That's all it is tonight. We can pay. That's more than most people in this city can say."

"And there are four of us," Orsen said quietly.

So he'd gotten there too. Of course he had. They had been raised by the same silences; of course they arrived at the same sums.

"Father will say we were never four," Maren said.

"Yes."

"You won't say it."

Orsen looked down at the type case, at the hundreds of small lead letters in their rows, each one identical to its brothers, interchangeable, replaceable. When he spoke, his voice was careful. "No. I won't say it."

But—and Maren marked this, filed it in the same place she filed symptoms she didn't yet understand—he didn't say the other thing either. He didn't say *all four of us go.* Some part of her brother had already begun doing sums he wasn't sharing, and she recognized it because she was doing them too, and because shame looks the same on everyone: very still, very quiet, very busy with its hands.

* * *

Aldous came home at half past ten, smelling of pipe smoke and other men's arguments, and Maren told him at the kitchen table while he was still unwinding his scarf.

She gave it to him plainly, because Aldous respected plainness the way he respected type—only when it was properly set. The barge. Quarter-day. Three berths. Payment in printed papers.

Aldous listened with his hands folded, and when she finished he was silent for exactly as long as it takes a man to decide which truth is safest to offer.

"The bargemaster is a smuggler," he said finally. "Half the rivermen are. Forged conduct-papers are common enough on the coal run—the inspectors mostly look the other way for the right fee. It's feasible." A pause. "Expensive. Risky. But feasible."

"Good," said Maren. "Because we're doing it."

"*We*," Aldous repeated, in the tone of a man examining a word for flaws.

"The four of us, Father."

"Maren." He removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on—all the little rituals he performed instead of raising his voice. "The boy has been here nine days. He is fed, mended, and alive, which is more than his father managed to arrange for himself. Whatever debt we owed the dead Minister Corvel, it is paid. Paid twice over, given what keeping him costs us nightly in risk. The letter said to forget his face. It was good advice."

"It was advice from a frightened man in a hurry," Maren said. "Frightened men give bad directions. Ask any nurse."

"This is not the ward, Maren. This is our license, our name, our necks—"

"Our necks," she said, "are four necks. I've counted."

Something crossed Aldous's face then, quickly, like a shadow of a bird over a window—and Maren, who had spent years reading faces across sickbeds, read it correctly and wished she hadn't. It wasn't anger. It wasn't fear for the family.

It was *calculation.*

"The barge leaves quarter-day," Aldous said, in a voice gone smooth and administrative. "There will be inspections before then, and after. Inspector Vasse is already asking questions about a child on the east road. These are not abstractions, daughter. Every day the boy is under this roof is a day we spend against ourselves."

"So we spend faster," Maren said. "We make the papers. We go. All of us."

"All of us," Aldous echoed, softly, testing the phrase the way he tested a proof sheet—for errors, for weight, for whether it would bear inspection. He rose, folded his scarf with geometric precision, and said, "It's late. We'll speak of it tomorrow, when everyone's head is clear."

Which was Aldous-speak for *when I've had time to arrange the outcome,* and they both knew it, and neither said so.

Maren climbed the stairs to her room, where Quentin slept in a nest of blankets with one arm flung out and his mouth open, breathing the deep unbothered breaths of a child who believed, still, in the goodness of arrangements. Nine days, and he'd already started calling the print shop *the letter house.* Already left his little hoard—a button, a blue feather, a bent nail—on the shelf above her bed, the way children do, claiming territory by treasure.

Three berths, she thought. Four names.

She lay awake a long time doing the arithmetic she hated, turning each sum over like a coin, checking for the flaw. Send Father alone and keep the children together? No—Aldous alone in Velloran was a man holding a lit fuse and calling it a candle. Take Father and Quentin, leave Orsen? She imagined explaining that to her brother and got no further than imagining his face. Take the boys, send herself away to safety like a parcel? She laughed aloud in the dark, briefly, bitterly. Orsen would sooner forge the archbishop's death certificate.

Every arrangement failed. That was the finding of her research. Four names, three berths, and no honest way to divide a family.

Unless the papers themselves lied bigger.

She was drifting toward sleep on that thought when she heard it: the faint, particular sound of the shop below—a type case sliding open, then closed. Then open again. At midnight. Their father, alone downstairs, going through the letters.

Maren lay still and listened to the small metallic whisper of a man counting, and understood that she and her brother were not the only ones in the house doing sums.

* * *

In the morning she found Orsen in the yard beating a rug that did not need beating, which was how Orsen prayed.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"You were up thinking too. I heard the stairs creak at some indecent hour, and you only climb them indecently when your conscience has fleas."

Orsen beat the rug twice more, then leaned on the beater like a farmer leaning on a fence. "I've been thinking about the papers," he admitted. "If we do this—if Father agrees, if he ever agrees—it's not one document. It's three. Same seal, same signature, same hand. Three documents that have to match perfectly or the bargemaster's lantern catches the difference and we're all standing on a quay looking stupid while soldiers look clever." He paused. "Four documents."

"Four," Maren repeated, carefully.

"In case," Orsen said, not meeting her eye. "In case we find a fourth berth somehow. Spare paper costs nothing."

They looked at each other, and the whole unsayable thing passed between them—the arithmetic, the triage, the terrible tenderness of two siblings each secretly planning to be the one who stayed behind and neither willing to let the other do it. Neither named it. Naming it would make it real, and real things demanded answers, and neither of them had one yet.

"Spare paper costs nothing," Maren agreed. "Except everything, if we're caught."

"Everything," Orsen said cheerfully, "is our family's traditional rate."

Despite herself—despite the stone in her chest, despite the night of bad sums—Maren laughed. That was the other thing she'd learned in the wards: laughter isn't the opposite of dread. It's what dread does to keep moving.

She was still smiling when the bell over the shop door rang, and a neighbor's boy skidded into the yard, breathless, mud to the knees, with news spilling out of him before he'd finished arriving: students massing by the university, torches, shouting, the state print works surrounded, someone had torn down the censor's notices on the Corn Exchange and—

The words ran together, and underneath them Maren felt the ground of the city shift, the way a floor shifts just before it gives.

Orsen had gone very still beside her, the rug beater hanging forgotten in his hand.

"Six days," Maren said quietly, to him, to herself, to the arithmetic. "Quarter-day is six days off. Let's hope the city can wait that long to burn."

Down the street, thin and rising, came the first far-off sound of many voices shouting together—and it did not sound like a city willing to wait.
