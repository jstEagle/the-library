## 9. Barricades of Borrowed Paper

The bells started wrong.

Orsen knew every bell in Velloran the way a printer knows his fonts—by weight, by voice, by what each one was good for. The cathedral bells spoke in long bronze sentences. The chapel of Saint Ferreol had a cracked note like a dropped type case. But this was none of them. This was the alarm bell at the Salt Gate, rung out of turn, rung ragged, rung by someone who did not care who heard.

He was sorting type when it began—fourteen-point Garamond, the morning's job, a funeral notice for a man who had died of nothing in particular and would be buried with state approval. His hands stopped on their own, the way a dog stops when it hears a door it doesn't recognize.

Below him, through the floorboards, he could hear his father's press fall silent mid-pull. Aldous Fairweather never stopped a pull once begun. It was a point of professional pride, almost religious: *the press speaks, we listen.* Now the press was silent, and in the silence Orsen heard his father climb the stairs slowly, each step placed like a man walking on ice over deep water.

"Listen," Aldous said, though there was no need. The bell was still going.

"I hear it."

"Do you know what that is?"

Orsen considered lying, decided it would be an insult to them both. "It's the Salt Gate alarm. Rung by hand." He set down the composing stick. "Someone's taken the gatehouse."

His father stood in the doorway of the type room, and for a moment—the first moment since Quentin had arrived mud-streaked at their door, since the letter, since everything—Aldous looked less afraid than simply *old*, which was somehow worse. "Then it's begun," he said. "God help the paper district. We're made of exactly the wrong material."

* * *

By noon, the students had come.

They came down Copperlane in a loose river of coats and borrowed sashes, maybe forty of them, maybe sixty, carrying crowbars and lantern oil and, incongruously, an enormous wooden sign from a shuttered bakery that someone had repainted overnight: THE TRUTH HAS A PRESS OF ITS OWN NOW. Orsen watched them from the shop window while pretending to sweep, which fooled no one, least of all Maren, who stood beside him with her arms crossed and her nurse's apron still dusted with the ward's chalk.

"They're children," she said.

"They're armed children."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive categories, Ors. I've bandaged enough of both."

The students stopped at the corner of Quillmonger Street, where the regime's official print works squatted behind its iron shutters like a toad in a rain barrel. There was a speech—there was always a speech—and then there was the sound of iron giving way, and then a cheer that went up so loud it startled pigeons off three roofs at once, and then smoke, sweet and resinous, as they burned the censor's ledgers in the street.

Maren exhaled slowly. "Well," she said. "That's that."

"That's not *that*. That's the beginning of something much longer."

"Don't be dramatic before lunch."

But even as she said it, Orsen felt the thing he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe: a warmth opening in his chest, dangerous and lovely, like standing too close to a forge. For eleven years the Fairweather shop had printed exactly what the regime permitted and nothing more—burial notices, tax schedules, almanacs with the weather carefully pre-approved. Eleven years of silences catalogued like inventory. And now, four streets away, other people were printing *truth* with stolen presses and burning the lists that told everyone what they were permitted to know.

It would have been a lie to say he didn't want it. God, he wanted it, the way a man dying of thirst wants rain, without any thought for flooding.

Upstairs, faintly, Quentin's voice came through the ceiling, counting letters. He counted everything now; his father had taught him that the walls of print shops were full of words and that finding them was a game. *A... B... C... D...* The small voice moved along the garret wall where the old specimen posters hung. Orsen closed his eyes briefly.

There was a child upstairs whose existence was a hanging offense, and a revolution outside that would either save him or bury him faster, and no way yet to know which.

* * *

The neighbors arrived within the hour.

This was the part Orsen had not predicted, though perhaps he should have. The Fairweathers had spent a decade being pleasantly invisible—nodded to but not greeted, greeted but not known, known but not trusted. Old Widow Sarelle hadn't spoken more than six words to Aldous since the year of the bread riots. Cobbler Duntz crossed the street to avoid them. The whole quarter had agreed, wordlessly and without any meeting anyone would admit to, that the print family was best left alone, the way one leaves alone a house where something sad happened long ago.

Now they were at the door in numbers.

Widow Sarelle first, astonishingly, holding a basket of eggs like a peace offering from a nation that had recently discovered diplomacy. Behind her Duntz, and the tallow-chandler's boy, and three women Orsen knew only by their washing lines, and a young man in a student's coat with ink already on his fingers—he'd been at the seizure, clearly, and had come straight here, glowing.

"We need pamphlets," the student said, breathless, as though this were a request one made daily. "Five thousand by Friday. The Committee of Public Letters has authorized—"

"The *what* has authorized—"

"Committee of Public Letters. We formed it this morning. In the print works." He said it the way one says *in the palace*, with the specific pride of the newly powerful. "We seized the state type. It's ours now. All of it."

Orsen looked past him at the crowd of faces, and what struck him—not funny, but nearly—was how many of those faces had spent years perfecting the art of looking through him. Widow Sarelle had once reported a neighbor for humming a banned song. Duntz had denounced a journeyman for reading. These were not revolutionaries. These were people who had smelled which way the wind had turned and were already adjusting their sails with impressive speed.

And yet. And *yet*. They were also his neighbors, and some of them—the women with the eggs, the chandler's boy who used to fetch coal—were people he had silently liked for a decade across the barrier of mutual carefulness.

"Pamphlets about what?" Orsen asked, because someone sensible had to.

"The lists," the student said, and the word went through the little crowd like a current through wire. Everyone had heard about the lists by now—the death lists, the purges, the names read aloud in the seized print works that morning from documents found in the censor's own drawers. "Everyone's name. Everyone who vanished. Printed openly, so no one can ever pretend they didn't know."

Orsen's mouth went dry. Somewhere upstairs a seven-year-old was counting letters on a wall, the son of a man whose name was on just such a list—a list partly drafted, in this very building, in this very type, by the man currently pretending to examine a proof sheet in the back room.

"I'll need to speak with my father," Orsen said. "It's—it's a family decision. Our license, you understand."

"Forget your license!" The student laughed, bright and terrible. "The licenses burned this morning! That's rather the entire point!"

* * *

His father said no.

Not loudly. Aldous Fairweather never did anything loudly anymore; loudness was a luxury the shop had surrendered along with its conscience years ago. He said no the way he set type—with small, precise movements, in the back room, with the door shut, while Maren listened at the stairwell with the shamelessness of a younger sibling and no intention whatsoever of pretending otherwise.

"Father. They're printing the truth. Finally. After everything—"

"After everything," Aldous repeated softly. "Yes. Do you know what 'everything' includes, Orsen? It includes our name. Not on their side of the ledger. On *neither* side—that was the whole strategy, eleven years of it. We survive revolutions by being furniture." He turned a proof sheet over, studied its blank back as if it might contain advice. "Furniture is not asked questions. Furniture is not dragged to tribunals. Furniture does not have sons."

"And when the revolution wins? What are we then? Furniture that helped?"

"Furniture that *survived*, and then it will be someone else's problem entirely."

"It won't be someone else's problem. It'll be *Quentin's* problem. If the old regime comes back, he's dead. If the new one comes and finds we printed nothing, stood for nothing—" Orsen stopped, because the next part was unsayable: *if the new regime ever reads the old lists closely enough, Father, it will find your fingerprints in the lead.*

His father's eyes flicked up, quick as a compositor catching a wrong sort. He had heard the unsayable part anyway. They stood in the smell of ink and lamp oil, two men shaped by the same trade, silent in the particular way of a family that has learned silence as a survival skill.

"You want to print," Aldous said at last. "You've wanted it since you were twelve. I know. I've watched you look at the forbidden cases the way other boys look at—"

"At girls, yes, Father, thank you."

"—and I am telling you that wanting a thing and surviving a thing are different trades." He set down the proof sheet. "But go on, then. Print your pamphlets. Print five thousand. Only understand what you're doing." He paused at the door, and for one unguarded moment he looked not angry but frightened in a new way, frightened *for* rather than frightened *of*. "You're not choosing a side, Orsen. You're choosing to be *seen*. No one who has ever been seen by a government has thanked the experience."

* * *

They printed through the night.

Orsen composed the first pamphlet himself—his first free composition in eleven years, his first text that no censor had touched, and his hands shook so badly setting the opening line that he had to start it twice. *TO THE CITIZENS OF VELLORAN: YOU HAVE BEEN TOLD WHAT HAPPENED TO YOUR NEIGHBORS. HERE IS WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED.* Simple. Enormous. The words sat in the composing stick like live coals.

Maren worked the press with him, sleeves rolled, hair tied back, pulling the lever with a nurse's efficient strength. She had opinions about layout ("no one reads a wall of text, Ors, break it up") and about margins ("wasteful") and absolutely none about whether they should be doing it at all, which was its own kind of answer.

"Father will come around," Orsen said, somewhere past midnight.

"Father will do what fathers do." Maren pulled the lever. The press spoke; the sheet emerged, wet and true. "Adapt. Quietly. Watch which way it goes, and by Friday he'll claim he suggested it."

"You don't think he means it? What he said about being seen?"

Maren was quiet for a moment, feeding the next sheet. "I think Father has been seen before," she said, "and paid a great deal to become unseen again. And I think men who've paid that much don't easily let themselves get billed twice." She glanced at him. "Print faster. Thinking slows my arms."

* * *

The trouble came on the third day, and it came wearing a neighbor's face.

Duntz the cobbler stood in the shop doorway at closing time, hat in hand, wearing the expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech and forgotten most of it. Behind him, at a tactful distance, stood two young men Orsen had never seen—clean-shaven, soberly dressed, with the patient stillness of people who wait for a living.

"Maren Fairweather," Duntz said. "And you. Your pamphlets. Very fine work. Very fine." He swallowed. "Only—the Committee asks—since you have the skill, you understand, and the type, and it's really a matter of civic duty at this point—"

"Duntz." Orsen kept his voice pleasant. "What does the Committee want?"

"A list," Duntz said, miserable. "Of names. Who bought what from you, these eleven years. Who ordered the funeral notices, the announcements, the—everything. They want to know who was *working with* the regime, see. Through the print trade." He couldn't meet Orsen's eye. "They asked me to ask. Because I'm known to you. Sort of."

Behind Duntz, one of the waiting men smiled. It was a kind smile. It was the worst smile Orsen had ever seen.

Because here was the arithmetic, arriving on schedule: a list of the shop's clients was a map of everyone who had needed the regime's permission to mourn, marry, announce, exist. And among those clients, if anyone pulled the thread far enough, were orders that led back to the inspectorate itself—to safe, quiet, licensed jobs commissioned by men who preferred their paperwork done by a shop with reasons to keep its mouth shut. A list of names, freely given to the new authorities, would burn the Fairweathers' bridges behind them forever. Refuse, and two patient men would report the refusal, and the Committee of Public Letters would begin to wonder why a print shop that printed truth for the revolution balked at a simple act of transparency.

And beneath all of it, quiet as a buried matrix, the older fear: any serious audit of this shop's history would eventually reach the death-list type. The graver's marks. The master mold hidden somewhere in his father's tools. The trail that led home.

"Tell the Committee," Orsen heard himself say, "that the shop's records are at their disposal. We keep honest books. We have nothing to hide from the city." Even as he said it, he thought: *except one child, one letter, and one mold.* "My father will prepare the ledgers personally."

Duntz's relief was enormous and pitiful. "Good lad. Good. I told them—Fairweathers, I said, solid folk, always were—" He retreated, hat bobbing, and the two patient men drifted after him without hurrying, the way things drift that intend to arrive regardless.

Orsen stood in the doorway a long time after, watching the lamplighters work Copperlane. The barricades had gone up that afternoon at the far end of the street—students and carters and one enterprising upholsterer, stacking paving stones and furniture and, in a gesture Orsen found he could not stop thinking about, great armloads of confiscated censorship files, soaked in pitch, ready to burn. Barricades of borrowed paper. The whole quarter was lit orange and restless, beautiful and doomed-looking, and everywhere people were choosing sides out loud for the first time in their lives, loudly, joyfully, with no idea of the bill.

He went inside and bolted the door, and found his father sitting alone in the dark of the back room, not working, not reading, just sitting with his hands flat on the type cabinet like a man taking a pulse.

"They want the client ledgers," Orsen said.

"I heard."

"Then you know what's coming after the ledgers."

Aldous was silent long enough that the clock downstairs finished striking nine into the hush. When he finally spoke, his voice was very level, the levelness of a man who has already been somewhere worse in his head.

"Yes," he said. "Which is why, first thing tomorrow, I have an errand to run. Alone."
