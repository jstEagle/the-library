## 10. Aldous's Bargain

Aldous Fairweather had spent forty years learning that the most dangerous words in Vellora were not shouted from barricades. They were whispered across a compositor's stone: *just this once*, and *no one will know*, and — worst of all, the words he was whispering to himself now, in the small hours, with his coat already buttoned to the throat — *it is for the family*.

He stood in the workshop with a single candle lit, though he had no intention of setting type. Setting type was what honest men did, and whatever else tonight required of him, honesty was not among its materials.

The boy slept upstairs. He could hear him through the floorboards if he held his breath — a small, even breathing, the sound of a child who had been told by his father, in the last words that father would ever speak to him, to count the letters on the walls until someone came. Aldous had heard Orsen reading to him earlier, some nonsense about a merchant's donkey, and had stood outside the door like a man listening to water rise around his ankles.

*Quentin Corvel.* Seven years old. The son of Minister Edouard Corvel, whose name Aldous had set in the eleventh column of the fourth list, in the very type his own graver had cut, and whose head — so the coffee-house talk said, so the ward gossip said, so Maren said with a flatness she had learned from her patients — had come off in the Place des Typographes on a Tuesday, in front of a crowd too frightened to be quiet and too quiet to be forgiven.

Aldous had set that name himself. Had checked it twice against the master copy, because sloppy kerning in a death list was the kind of error the Inspectorate noticed, and the Fairweather license — the shop, the house above the shop, the lives of everyone under this roof — hung from that license the way a gutted fish hangs from a hook.

And now the boy was sleeping one floor above the very type case that had killed his father.

"There is a symmetry there," Aldous told the candle. "It is not a comforting one."

The candle, being a candle, declined to comment.

* * *

He had drafted the letter three times before he allowed himself to admit he was drafting anything at all. That was the trick of it — the whole rotten architecture of a man like Aldous Fairweather. You did not decide to betray anyone. You decided to *write out your thoughts*. You decided to *clarify matters*. You sat down with pen and paper purely for the sake of orderliness, and somewhere between the salutation and the signature you looked down and discovered you had written an offer to sell a child.

*To Inspector Vasse, Inspectorate of the Fourth Quarter.*

He had chosen Vasse deliberately, which was to say he had chosen the only man in Vellora whose price he could calculate. Vasse was not cruel, exactly. Cruelty was a passion, and passions made men unpredictable, and unpredictability was the one quality Aldous could not afford. No — Vasse was something far more manageable: a careerist. A man who kept his ledgers balanced, who had risen through the Inspectorate by never once mistaking duty for enthusiasm, who had inspected their shop four times a year for nine years and had developed, Aldous believed, something adjacent to respect for the quality of the Fairweather proofs. State-approved. Immaculate margins. Not one unauthorized character in nearly a decade.

Vasse would not torture the boy. Aldous clung to this the way drowning men cling to driftwood that is visibly shorter than they are. Vasse would deliver Quentin to the proper authorities, the proper authorities would process him according to the proper protocols, and the protocols — this was the part Aldous did not permit himself to think about while awake — the protocols ended in the same place all the Corvel protocols had ended.

In exchange: immunity. Written, sealed, countersigned. The Fairweather family exempted from reprisal, from inquiry, from the general settling of accounts that everyone with sense could see coming down the street like weather. When the revolution came — and it was coming; even Aldous, who had spent nine years perfecting the art of not seeing things, could see it — when the students seized the print shops and the neighbors remembered old grievances, the Fairweathers would stand behind a wall of official paper.

One child's life, weighed against three. Perhaps four, counting the boy himself, whom Aldous included in the ledger with a scrupulousness that made him want to be sick.

"That is the arithmetic," he informed the candle. "That is simply the arithmetic."

The candle flickered, which could have meant anything.

* * *

The problem — and here Aldous paused, quill hovering, because the problem deserved to be stated precisely, and precision was the last respectable thing left to him — the problem was that the arithmetic only worked if you agreed not to look directly at any of its figures.

Look at the boy: a seven-year-old who had hidden in a press crate while men searched the house for his father, who had walked out of the city along a ditch in the rain, who had been carried to this door by someone who trusted a printer's shop the way people used to trust churches. Look at Orsen: who had taken the boy in without hesitation and had since been looking at his father with a new expression, an expression Aldous recognized from funerals, the look of a mourner arriving early. Look at Maren: who had moved the boy's pallet without being asked and who watched her father across the dinner table now with the patient attention she gave to fevers that hadn't yet declared themselves.

They knew. Not everything — he was almost sure they didn't know everything — but enough. Enough that every kindness they showed him now had a question folded inside it like poison in a pastry.

And still. Still. He was their father. He had fed them, clothed them, kept them alive through two purges and a bread riot by the simple expedient of printing exactly what was permitted and nothing more, and if the cost of continuing to keep them alive was one more entry in a ledger that already stank to heaven, then —

Then he was the man who had set the lists, wasn't he. One more name. It was practically a tradition.

"Except," said Aldous to the candle, with the air of a lawyer who has just lost his own case, "except that I know precisely how those entries are processed."

* * *

He wrote the final draft at the kitchen table, because the workshop felt too much like a confessional and the kitchen was where a man did practical things. He wrote it in the careful, upright hand he used for invoices, because if the thing was ever traced to him he wanted it to look like what it was — commerce, cold and clean — rather than what it actually was.

*Inspector —*

*I write regarding the child reported on the east road, of whom your office has made inquiry. The child is presently sheltered at premises known to you. I will not commit the address to paper; you know my shop, and you know me, and you know that in nine years of inspections you have found nothing in my house that was not approved.*

*My terms are these: written guarantee of immunity for myself and my two children, Orsen and Maren Fairweather, effective immediately and irrevocable, countersigned above your office. In return, upon receipt of such guarantee, the child will be surrendered to your custody within two days' time.*

*I make no argument for my character. You have seen my work. Judge the hand, not the man.*

*A. Fairweather*

He read it over four times. It was good work. It was, he thought with a distant, professional pride, some of the best prose he had ever produced, and he had once typeset a wedding ode for the Governor's daughter that had made the Governor weep into his cravat. Every clause load-bearing. No sentiment anywhere near the load-bearing sections. The single line of feeling — *judge the hand, not the man* — placed exactly where it would do the most work and cost the least.

Aldous Fairweather, who had taught both his children to read on state-approved primers, who had taught Orsen to cut a letter clean on the first pass, who had taught Maren that the world took what it took and you bandaged what remained — Aldous sat back from the finished letter and understood, finally and completely, what sort of father he was.

Not the sort who died for his children. Plenty of men managed that; it was practically fashionable this season. He was the other sort. The sort who found the one commodity in the house that wasn't his, that had been entrusted to him by a dead friend in desperation, and priced it.

Edouard Corvel had come to him eleven years ago, when Quentin was still a red-faced infant, and had drunk two glasses of Aldous's terrible plum brandy and laughed at his jokes and said, "If it ever goes wrong for me, Aldous — and it may, I am too honest for this ministry and not honest enough to resign — if it ever goes wrong, yours is the trade that knows how to make things disappear. Ink can erase. You've always said so." And Aldous had laughed too and said ink could indeed erase, given the right solvent, and had thought no more about it, the way a man thinks no more about a promise made to a friend across a table, because promises to friends are not real until they are needed, and by then it is too late to have kept them properly.

Corvel had trusted him. Corvel had sent his son into the dark and the rain carrying a letter that said *forget his face*, and had chosen this shop — of all the shops in Vellora — because of a conversation over bad brandy and a belief that printers were decent men.

"He was half right," Aldous said. "I am a printer."

* * *

Sealing the letter took longer than writing it. His hands, which had set ten thousand pages without a tremor, shook now over the wax, and he dripped it twice and swore — quietly, because the boy was asleep overhead, and even his blasphemies had learned to tiptoe — and pressed his seal into the third attempt with more force than the wax required.

He would deliver it at dawn. That was the arrangement with himself: dawn, when the inspectorate opened, when the streets were empty of everyone except bakers and the newly arrested. Dawn, before Orsen woke, before Maren returned from the ward, before either of them could look at him with those funeral expressions and ask him questions he would answer honestly, God help him, because lying to censors was a craft and lying to his children was a wound.

There was just the matter of the drafts.

Three false starts lay on the table beside the fair copy, plus the fair copy itself, plus — he counted twice, because accuracy mattered even in damnation — four sheets of practice in which he had worked out the phrasing of the terms. Eight pieces of paper that together constituted the fullest confession ever penned by a Fairweather.

He should burn them all. Obviously. Any sensible criminal burned his drafts; the manuals said so; common sense said so.

But Aldous Fairweather had kept the matrix. That was the thing his children didn't know yet, the thing sleeping in the bottom of the graver cabinet beneath the spare quoins: the master mold of the list-type, cut in secret, preserved against the day the Inspectorate might decide the Fairweathers were expendable after all. Nine years he had kept it. Insurance, he'd called it. Leverage. Proof of his own guilt, hoarded like a miser hoards the coin that damns him, because a man who owns the evidence of his crime owns the men who'd hang him for it.

So he understood — better than any man in Vellora — why a person keeps the draft.

Because the draft is the truth. The fair copy is a performance; the draft is where your hand shook. Somewhere in those eight sheets was the moment, he was almost certain, in the second attempt, where he had written *the child is blameless* and then crossed it out. Crossed it out and continued. That crossed-out line was the most honest sentence he had produced in a decade, and he hated it, and he could not burn it, because a man who burned his own honesty was —

Was what? A monster?

"A monster is a word for other people," Aldous said to the candle, which had burned down to a stub and was giving up on the conversation entirely. "I have read the lists. I know exactly what the word covers, and I know which side of it I fall on, and I have fallen there with excellent margins."

* * *

In the end he compromised, because compromise was his native language.

He burned the five practice sheets and the two abandoned drafts. He fed them to the stove piece by piece, watching each one curl and blacken and go to nothing, and it was easier than it should have been, which frightened him more than the letter did. Paper forgives quickly. That was the whole trouble with paper.

The fair copy he did not burn. He wrapped it in oilcloth, tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, where a man carries the documents that define him, and told himself it was prudence — deliver it fresh, uncreased, presentable; a creased offer insulted the buyer.

He told himself several other things as well, standing there in the grey hour with ash on his cuffs. He told himself the boy would be processed humanely. He told himself that two days was plenty of time to grow accustomed to the decision, or failing that, to stop thinking about it, which amounted to the same skill. He told himself that Orsen and Maren need never know, that he would carry this alone the way he had carried the lists alone, that a father's final duty was to be the one who got dirty so his children stayed clean.

He was aware, distantly, that this last claim was self-serving nonsense. He was aware that his children were not clean and never had been; they had eaten off his earnings, slept under his roof, lived inside the license his lists had bought. If the family was stained, they had all been drinking from the same cup for years. But it was a comfortable nonsense, and comfort, at his age, at this hour, with dawn coming up over the Paper District like a slow accusation — comfort was not a thing a man refused lightly.

He banked the stove. He straightened the chairs. He performed, in short, the entire liturgy of a household pretending nothing has happened, a liturgy at which the Fairweathers had long ago achieved something like mastery.

Then he climbed the stairs past the room where the boy slept, quietly, on the side where the stair didn't creak, the way he had climbed past his own children's rooms for years, and at the landing he stopped.

Through the door, in the light of the guttering night-lamp Maren insisted on, he could see Quentin's face on the pillow — turned toward the door, mouth open slightly, one fist curled under his chin. Asleep, he looked like his father. That was the cruelty of it; the resemblance was precise and unavoidable, the same stubborn jaw, the same heavy brows, and Aldous stood there looking at Edouard Corvel's face in miniature and hearing, absurdly, the clink of plum brandy glasses, and the laugh, and the words: *yours is the trade that knows how to make things disappear.*

"Yes," Aldous whispered. "You chose well."

He went down the stairs, out the back, into the street where the bakers were beginning to move, and turned toward the inspectorate with the letter over his heart and his footsteps timed to sound like a man running an ordinary errand.

Behind him, at the corner of Lantern Row, a figure in a nurse's cloak paused in a doorway and watched him go — and Aldous, head bowed against the wind, never saw her at all.
