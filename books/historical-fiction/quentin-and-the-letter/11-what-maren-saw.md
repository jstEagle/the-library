## 11. What Maren Saw

Maren Fairweather had learned long ago that the truth about people arrived not in their words but in their feet.

Her father's feet, for instance, had a particular sound on the stairs of the print shop—she'd known it since she was six years old. A confident tread when the day had gone well. A dragging heel when a client haggled too hard over a wedding announcement. And lately, since the boy Quentin had come to them like a parcel nobody wanted to sign for, a strange new rhythm: quick going out, slow coming back, as though each errand were costing him more than the last.

She was thinking about this at half past four in the morning, kneeling in the charity ward with her fingers inside a cooper's wife, counting the seconds between contractions, when the thought sharpened into certainty: *Father went out yesterday evening and he did not say where.*

He had said where. That was the thing. He had said it too smoothly—"the type foundry on Coppersmith Row, they've finally cast the small caps I ordered"—and he had put on his good coat, the one he wore for inspectors and bishops, and he had kissed Maren's cheek, which he did perhaps four times a year and never without a reason.

Nurses learned to read reasons the way sailors learned weather.

"Push now," Maren told the cooper's wife, who pushed with the magnificent fury of a woman who had been lied to about everything her whole life and was done tolerating it. The baby arrived at quarter to five, red and furious and perfect, and Maren held him a moment before giving him to his mother, feeling that familiar ache in her chest that she had decided, years ago, was none of anyone's business.

She walked home through streets gone soft and grey with the hour before dawn. The Velloran curfew still held in theory; in practice the watchmen had stopped bothering after midnight, because the city itself had begun breaking its own rules. There was paper everywhere—pasted on walls, stuffed in gutters, blowing down the Rue des Fontaines like enormous grey leaves. Most of it was official proclamations. Some of it was not. Maren stepped around a sheet that promised THE PEOPLE THEIR TRUTH in letters an inch high and thought, not for the first time, that whoever printed that had beautiful nerve and probably three days to live.

The print shop stood dark and patient on its corner, the way it had stood for forty years, saying nothing to anybody. Maren let herself in through the kitchen door, hung up her cloak, and stopped.

Because there was a light moving downstairs.

Not candlelight—candlelight sat still. This was the slow sweep of a lantern being carried from room to room by someone who knew the house but was checking it anyway, the way you check your pockets twice before leaving. Her father checked rooms like that. She had watched him do it before inspections, before tax assessors, before the one terrible winter when the censor's men had come asking about a pamphlet.

But her father had come home already. Or—he hadn't. The stairs creaked in the front hall, and Maren moved to the top of them and looked down into the dimness below.

Aldous Fairweather stood in the doorway of the shop, letting himself in with his own key, and the lantern light caught his face for exactly one second before he turned it away.

Maren had seen tired men. She worked in a charity ward; tiredness was her native country. But this was something else. In that single second of lantern light she had seen a man whose face had been *wiped*—not pale, not frightened, but smoothed over, the way you smooth a bedsheet before laying a body on it. And she saw, clutched in his left hand, folded small enough to hide in a fist, a piece of paper that he transferred to his coat pocket with the care of a man handling a live coal.

Then he saw her.

"Maren." His voice came out level. Too level. "You're late home."

"You're early out," she said. "Or late in. I can never keep the two straight."

A pause. Down the hall, faintly, came the small wet snuffle of Quentin turning over in his sleep—the boy slept in the little room off the kitchen now, behind the paper stock, where the walls were thick and the street couldn't be seen from the door. Aldous's eyes flicked toward the sound and then away, quickly, the way a man looks at a debt collector.

"I couldn't sleep," he said. "Went to check the press. The forme for the customs schedules has been slipping."

"At five in the morning."

"The press doesn't keep hours." He smiled. It was his shop smile, the one for customers who wanted a discount on funerals. "You look half dead. Go to bed."

Maren didn't move. On the stair above her own head, dust sifted down through a crack in the plaster, and in the silence she could hear her father breathing, and she could hear herself breathing, and underneath both, steady as a metronome, the boy's sleeping breath through the wall.

*Feed him,* the letter had said. *Forget his face.* As if a face could be forgotten like a chore. As if a seven-year-old who asked what the letters on the wall spelled could be filed away between the mourning cards and the customs schedules.

"Father," she said quietly, "where is your good coat?"

He blinked. "I'm wearing—"

"No." She came down three steps. "You changed coats. You went out in the good coat and you came back in the old one. The good coat smells of pipe smoke and you haven't lit a pipe since Mother died. So either you've taken up smoking again at the inspectorate on the Rue Vasse—which would be interesting, given the hour—or you've been somewhere else entirely, and you changed clothes in some doorway or alley like a man who didn't want to bring the smell home."

The lantern trembled. Just slightly. Just enough.

"You're overtired," Aldous said. "Imagining things. Nurses do that—too much death, not enough sleep—"

"Father." She reached the bottom step. She was not a tall woman and he had always seemed enormous to her, the way mountains seem enormous until you have to climb them and discover they are only rock. "I spend my life watching faces for the lie underneath. It is the entire profession. A woman says *I'm fine* and I count her pulse. A man says *it's nothing* and I look at his hands. Your hands are shaking, and you've hidden something in your right coat pocket, and you came home smelling of tallow candles and cheap ink—not our ink, ours is walnut-oil—and I am asking you once, plainly, because I love you: what did you do?"

For a long moment Aldous Fairweather looked at his daughter, and something moved across his face like weather—a storm deciding whether to make landfall. Maren braced for it. Whatever it was—the whole ugly truth, the lists, the license, all of it laid bare at last—she found she was almost ready for it. Almost relieved.

Then the storm turned aside.

"It's shop business," he said. "Type contracts. Nothing you'd want to hear about at the end of a night shift." And he lifted the lantern, kissed the top of her head as though she were still nine years old, and climbed the stairs to his room, and shut the door, and did not lock it, which somehow was worse than locking it would have been.

Maren stood in the hall a while, listening to the house settle.

* * *

She should have woken Orsen. That was the arithmetic of it—two against one, siblings against sire, the alliance that had held since childhood against every tyrant from their grandmother to the parish priest. But Orsen slept like the dead and woke like the resurrected, confused and gentle and useless for a full quarter hour, and besides—she wasn't certain yet. Not of anything. Only of a smell, a coat, a tremble. Nurses did not act on symptoms alone. They watched.

So she watched.

She made tea in the kitchen at six, and when her father came down dressed for the day, crisp and composed, she poured him a cup and chatted about the cooper's wife's baby and watched his hands. Steady now. Steadier than hers. That steadiness frightened her more than the shaking had. Shaking meant a man fighting himself. Steadiness meant a man who had won.

Quentin appeared at seven, hair like a thornbush, dragging the blanket he refused to surrender even in summer. He climbed onto the bench beside Maren without ceremony, which was how he did everything now—he had been with them eleven days and he had already arranged himself into the household like type into a case, each piece in its proper box, waiting to be set.

"Is it bread day?" he asked.

"It's always bread day if you ask nicely."

"Please," said Quentin, who had clearly been coached in manners by someone desperate, and then, watching her spread the butter: "Maren. Do letters get cold?"

She paused. "Cold?"

"Like porridge. If nobody uses them." He said it very seriously, buttering with total concentration. "Papa said letters are alive if you use them and asleep if you don't, and the ones in the crates were all asleep, and I counted them until somebody came. Four hundred and twelve." He looked up. "I'm going to wake them all up someday. Papa said I could."

Maren's throat did something complicated. She got it under control. "I expect you will," she said. "Eat your bread."

Across the table, her father was reading the state gazette with the rigid attention of a man memorizing it rather than reading it, and at the mention of *Papa* his page-hand had tightened, just once, just visibly, before smoothing itself flat.

There it was again. The flinch. Every time the boy said his father's name, Aldous Fairweather flinched like a man hearing a bell he'd been trying not to hear.

Maren took the boy's plate away, kissed his thornbush head—his face, deliberately, thoroughly, memorizing it the way her father had apparently been ordered not to—and went upstairs and began, without letting herself think about it too closely, because thinking would give her time to find reasons not to, to move furniture.

* * *

The little room off the kitchen had a cot in it, and on the cot lay a boy's blanket, and the blanket lay on a pallet that smelled faintly of paper dust and coal smoke and, underneath, of child. Maren stripped the cot, bundled the straw tick into her arms—it weighed almost nothing, he weighed almost nothing, they had given this child nothing but floor and blanket and he had thanked them for it—and carried it up two flights to her own room under the eaves.

Her room was cold up there and the ceiling sloped so low you had to stoop near the window, and the window itself overlooked the street, which meant watchmen, which meant risk. All true. None of it mattered. What mattered was this: her room was at the top of the house, and the stairs to it passed directly beneath her father's door, and no man carrying a lantern at dawn could reach the boy without passing her first.

She made the pallet up fresh. She tucked the thornbush-blanket at the foot. Then she went down and told Quentin, matter-of-factly, in the voice she used for patients being moved to a new ward:

"You'll sleep upstairs with me from tonight. The kitchen's damp and it's making your cough worse."

"I don't have a cough."

"You do now. I'm a nurse. I'm never wrong." Which was a lie, nurses being wrong hourly, but he grinned—the first real grin she'd seen on him, gap-toothed and sudden as sunrise—and that settled it.

Her father watched the whole operation from the shop doorway, arms folded, saying nothing. When she carried the last armload down, he spoke to the floor rather than to her.

"She sleeps deeply, does she? Your patient?"

"Never," said Maren pleasantly. "Occupational hazard. We wake when a spoon drops three wards away." She met his eyes and held them, and let him understand exactly what she was telling him, and exactly what she was not saying, and exactly how much of it she had seen. "Sleep well, Father."

* * *

Orsen found her at noon sorting herbs in the kitchen, and he had that look he got when he'd noticed something and was assembling the courage to say it, like a compositor hunting for a ligature that wasn't in the case.

"Maren. Last night—did you hear Father go out? Before dawn?"

"I heard him come in," she said, not looking up from the feverfew. "In the wrong coat. Smelling of someone else's candles."

Orsen set down the tray of type he'd been carrying, very carefully, the way he handled anything breakable. "The inspectorate is on the Rue Delorme. The tallow-and-ink smell—that's the district around it. And he told us Coppersmith Row."

"Coppersmith Row closes at dusk. Everyone knows it. Even Father." She looked up then, and brother and sister regarded each other across the kitchen table with eleven days of unspoken arithmetic hanging between them. "He flinches when the boy says *Papa*. He came home wiped clean as a slate. And Orsen—he *won*. Whatever it was, whatever he did out there in the dark with his good coat on, he came home having won, and I have nursed enough dying men to know that a man at peace at five in the morning, after a night like this city is having, is a man who has sold something."

Orsen was quiet a long moment. Through the wall came the small sounds of Quentin in the shop below, talking to the type cases in his whisper-count, waking the sleeping letters one by one.

"What do we do?" Orsen asked. It was not rhetorical. He genuinely did not know, and neither did she, and there was a strange comfort in that.

"We watch," said Maren. "And we don't leave that boy alone with him. Not at night, not for an hour, not for the space of a kettle boiling." She tied off the herb bundle with more force than the herbs required. "Whatever Father has traded—and he has traded, I'd stake my license on it—we are not part of the bargain. Do you hear me? Whatever he signed, whatever he sold, he sold it alone."

Orsen nodded slowly. Then he said, in the small careful voice he used for the most dangerous observations: "Maren. If it comes to it—if it comes to choosing—"

"Don't." She said it fast, and harder than she meant to, and saw him flinch. She softened. "Don't ask me that yet. Ask me on the barge, if there's a barge. Ask me at the quay. But don't ask me in a kitchen with bread on the table and the boy counting letters ten feet away." She exhaled. "Ask me when we know the price. Then I'll answer you in a heartbeat, and you won't like how fast."

Down in the shop, faintly, Quentin's voice rose through the floorboards, patient and sweet: *"…four hundred thirteen, four hundred fourteen…"*

Brother and sister listened together, not speaking, not needing to.

Above them, behind a closed door, a pen scratched steadily across paper—the sound of a man writing something he intended, sooner or later, to burn.
