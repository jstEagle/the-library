## 12. The Type Case Confession

My father kept his graver tools the way other men kept their sins—close, oiled, and out of sight.

I had known this drawer existed my whole life without ever once being invited into it. It sat at the bottom of his workbench, shallower than the others, fitted with a lock so old it had stopped being a lock sometime during my grandfather's tenure and become merely a suggestion of privacy. As an apprentice I had been told, in the tone Father reserved for matters of theology and fire safety, that the cutter's tools were not for small hands.

I was twenty-six now, and my hands were larger than his. And after the night Inspector Vasse had stood in our shop asking after a child on the east road—after the way Father had performed calm like a man performing a role he'd rehearsed too many times—I found I could no longer treat that drawer as furniture. It was a door. And I was tired of walking past doors in my own house.

The shop was empty on the morning I opened it. Maren had taken Quentin with her to the charity ward, bundled in a nurse's spare shawl, her story about a fever convalescent already worn smooth from use. Father had gone to deliver proofs to the Censor's Office—the weekly humiliation he performed with the cheerfulness of a man paying rent to a wolf. For two hours, perhaps three, the shop belonged to me alone.

I lit no candle. Daylight came gray through the front window, the color of dishwater, enough to sort type by if one wasn't proud. I knelt at the bench, took out my knife, and considered the old lock for approximately four seconds before deciding that breaking my father's furniture felt more honest than picking it.

The wood gave with a crack that sounded, in the silent shop, like a gunshot. I froze, heart hammering, half expecting the door to burst open and Vasse himself to step through with his notebook and his terrible patience. Nothing. Only the press ticking softly as it cooled, the way presses do, like some enormous patient animal digesting.

The drawer slid out.

Inside lay the tools themselves: the gravers in their graduated sizes, angle-cut and mirror-bright; the scorper with its worn handle shaped by forty years of my father's grip; the burnisher, the punches, the little brass gauge he used for measuring letter heights, dented where he'd once dropped it and sworn in language I hadn't heard again until the night the boy arrived. Everything arranged with a precision that would have looked loving to anyone who didn't know him. Orderliness was how Aldous Fairweather loved things. It was also how he hid them.

Under the tools, a sheet of oilcloth. Under the oilcloth—

I expected blanks. Every type founder keeps blanks, unfinished matrices, experimental cuts. A man in Father's trade accumulated metal the way a river accumulates silt. So when my fingers found the wrapped bundle at the back of the drawer, I unwrapped it with the boredom of routine.

Then I stopped being bored.

It was a matrix—a master mold—but not for any letter. Not for any letter I knew. The characters cut into its face were smaller than body text, condensed, each one carrying a peculiar double-strike weight, as though the punchcutter had wanted every glyph to look slightly heavier than honest. I knew that heaviness. I had set columns of it. Every soul in Velloran knew it, though most had never held it close enough to feel the burrs.

It was the face of the Lists.

The death lists. Published quarterly, posted at the quarter-house, read aloud in the squares by criers with voices trained to be neither sad nor glad. The names of the purged, set in this exact condensed type with its dishonest weight, so that ten thousand names could fit where eight thousand should. Someone had decided that efficiency required a special alphabet, and someone had cut it, and the cutting had been done at this bench, by these hands, with these very gravers that were now lying six inches above the proof of what they'd made.

I sat down on the floor. It seemed wise.

* * *

Here is what I had told myself for years, in the way one tells oneself stories at night to keep the darker ones from getting in: that Father had set the Lists because he was compelled to. That the regime had come to the shop with soldiers or threats or something visible and monstrous, and that Aldous Fairweather, a printer with a family and a license, had done what any printer would do. That his guilt, if he carried any—and I had never once seen him carry anything visibly—was the ordinary guilt of a small man under a large boot.

It was a comfortable story. It had a victim in it, and the victim was my father.

But a matrix is not compulsion. A matrix is insurance.

You do not keep the master mold for the death-list type unless you mean to prove, someday, to somebody, that you made it—or that you can unmake it, or that you alone possess the thing that ties your name to every name on every list since the first quarter-day they were printed. The regime thought it owned that typeface. It didn't. It owned copies. My father owned the origin.

He hadn't been only the regime's instrument. He had been its creditor.

I turned the matrix over in my hands. On the reverse, scratched small with a needle point, was a date. I did the arithmetic the way you do arithmetic you dread: the year of Minister Corvel's appointment. Before the Lists began. Before Corvel had done anything but rise. My father had cut this face while the man whose son slept in our garret was still alive, still powerful, still signing documents with the confident hand of a minister who believed the state he served would serve him back.

And then, presumably, someone had come to the shop—I could see it now, see it the way you see things you've spent years refusing to look at—and said, *Aldous, we need a new face for a new publication,* and my father, who survived by printing exactly what was permitted and nothing more, had picked up his finest graver and made the permitted thing beautiful. Efficient. Heavy with a false weight, so the dead could be listed faster.

No wonder he had gone white at the door. No wonder he had recognized Quentin instantly—not because the boy resembled his father, though he did, around the eyes, but because my father had been reading that man's name in his own typeface for years. You come to know a name you have set a hundred times. It gets into your fingers. Corvel. Even now I could feel how the letters would fall in the case, where the sorts would sit, the narrow e's running short the way they always do.

* * *

I heard the street door long before anyone entered—Maren had a walk like weather coming, and she announced herself, as always, by kicking the snow off her boots with unnecessary violence.

"In the shop," I called, and put the matrix down, and then, hearing myself think, picked it up again. Hiding it felt wrong. Showing it felt dangerous. I settled for holding it behind my back like a schoolboy with a broken window, which fooled nobody, least of all Maren.

She came in smelling of carbolic and cold air, cheeks slapped red by the wind. "The ward's full," she said, unwinding her scarf. "Half of them don't need nursing, they need pamphlets. Old Tomas asked me today—in front of everyone—whether the Fairweathers were still printing *only* what we're told to print. He smiled when he said it. There are smiles like that going around the whole city now." She hung the scarf on its nail. "Where's Father?"

"Censor's Office."

"Ah." She looked at me kneeling on the floor amid the wreckage of a drawer that had been locked since before I could read. She looked at the object in my hand. I watched her decide, with visible effort, not to ask yet. Maren's restraint has always been less a virtue than an ambush conducted slowly.

"Quentin?" I said instead.

"Asleep in the linen room. I told him if he rested quietly he could help fold papers tomorrow. He believes folding papers is part of the game." Her voice did something complicated on the word *game*—tenderness folded over grief folded over fury, pressed flat, like type in a chase. Then she crossed the room, held out her hand, and said simply: "Show me."

So much for ambush. I gave it to her.

She turned it over. Read the scratched date. Her face went through the same stages mine had gone through an hour earlier, except faster, because Maren has never wasted time being shocked when she could be angry instead.

"He kept it," she said. "All these years. In a drawer ten feet from where we sleep."

"It's why he's alive," I said. "Don't you see? It's not sentiment. It's not even guilt, or not only. If the regime ever decided the Fairweathers were worth hanging, he could—" I heard myself say it and hated how clean it sounded. "He could prove he was useful. Or he could threaten to prove who else's hands touched it. Whoever ordered the typeface cut didn't order it personally. There's a chain, Maren, from the Censor's table up to whoever signs the orders, and every link in that chain is printed in this face, and my father holds the mold."

Maren handed it back. "Then we're not a family," she said. "We're a strongbox."

"Maren—"

"No. Think about what you're holding. He watched us learn to walk in a house built on that. He let you believe—" She stopped herself. That was the thing about Maren; even furious, she rationed. "The boy upstairs thinks this shop is a game his father arranged for him. And I used to think the worst secret in this house was the pamphlets we refused to print."

She went up the stairs to check on Quentin, and her footsteps on the boards sounded like a verdict being delivered in installments.

* * *

I stayed on the floor a while longer. The light thickened toward afternoon. Somewhere outside, a patrol passed, boots keeping time, and I counted the steps out of habit—our habit, the family habit, the counting we do instead of screaming.

Here is the confession I arrived at, sorting through it there among the gravers, and I record it now because a chapter called a confession ought to contain one:

I was not surprised.

That was the worst of it. Some buried part of me had always known that my father's silences weren't fear. Fear makes men clumsy. Father was never clumsy. His silences were load-bearing. He had built this family's survival the way he built a page—measuring margins, weighing every word, leaving nothing to chance and nothing to love either. And I had mistaken the craftsmanship for virtue, because he was my father, and because the alternative was to admit that the safest man in Velloran was safe for reasons that would curdle milk.

He owned the proof of his own guilt, and he had raised us inside it, and we had been warm, and fed, and invisible, and none of it—not one candle, not one meal—had ever been free.

I cleaned up the broken lock as best I could, wedging the drawer shut with a shim, and put the matrix back exactly where I'd found it. Exactly. I measured the position against the dust shadow. If my father noticed the drawer had been forced, he would know; he notices everything; noticing is the trade he actually practices, printing being only its cover.

But here is what I could not make myself do: I could not put it back and forget it. Because the matrix was not just proof of the past. Sitting there in the gray light, it looked suddenly like leverage pointed the other way—a thing that might buy berths, or silence, or time. Quarter-day was coming. The barge was coming. And somewhere between those two facts, Inspector Vasse was circling our shop like a man reading a page he intended, eventually, to censor.

I heard the street door again near dusk. Slower this time. Heavier. My father's tread has a particular drag on the left side, a legacy of the press accident years ago, and I knew it the way Quentin knows his father's knock—without looking, without wanting to.

Aldous came into the shop stamping snow off his boots. His face had the flattened look it wore after the Censor's Office, the look of a man who has spent an hour being polite to people who own him. He saw me kneeling at the bench. He saw the shim in the drawer. I watched his eyes go to it, flick away, and return, and in that half-second I saw him understand everything—what I'd found, what it meant, what it cost him—and choose, with magnificent craftsmanship, to say nothing.

"Supper's on," he said. "Your sister made the barley."

"Aldous." I never called him that. He flinched at the sound of it. "We need to talk about the type case."

For a moment—one long moment, long enough for the press to tick twice—he stood in the doorway with the snow melting off his shoulders, and I swear he looked older than the shop, older than the city, older than the typeface he'd cut for the dead. Then he set his hat on its peg, straightened it twice, and said, in the voice of a man opening a ledger he has dreaded for thirty years:

"Yes," said my father. "I imagine we do."
