## 13. Fire in the Paper District

Quentin counted one hundred and nine letters on the wall of the garret before he got bored of counting and started making words out of them instead.

That was the game Papa had given him. *Count the letters on the walls until someone comes,* Papa had said, crouching down in the dark of the print shop with his coat smelling of smoke and river water, his hands squeezing Quentin's shoulders so hard it almost hurt, which meant it was important, because important things always hurt a little. *Letters everywhere in that place, Quentin. Every wall is made of letters if you look hard enough. Count them for me.*

So Quentin looked.

The garret room was at the top of the print shop, up stairs that creaked like an old man telling jokes he'd already told. The sloped ceiling had a window, small as a bread loaf, that looked out over the roofs toward the Paper District, where the big state print works sat with its tall chimney like a giant's finger pointed at the sky to say *I am here, I am very important, everyone look at me.* Quentin had decided the chimney was showing off.

On the garret walls there were letters everywhere. Somebody long ago had pasted proof sheets right onto the plaster—pages of practice printing with crooked lines and smudged words—and where the paper had peeled away, the plaster underneath had cracks, and the cracks were also letters if you squinted, which Quentin was extremely good at doing.

The crack above the bed was a K. The crack by the window was either an M or two small mountains holding hands. There was an H in the corner that Quentin suspected of being an H only when it felt like it.

He had names for all of them now. The K was called Konrad. He hadn't told anyone this. It seemed like the kind of thing that would get you a look from Orsen, who gave looks the way other people gave change—quickly, and often without meaning to.

* * *

The Fairweathers were strange people. Quentin had been working on understanding them for what felt like a hundred days, though Maren said it had been eleven and Maren didn't lie about numbers, only about how many biscuits remained in the tin, which she always said was none even when it was three.

Orsen was the tallest and the quietest and he smelled like ink, all of him, even his hair, even, once, when he'd let Quentin hold his hand crossing the lane, his glove. He sorted little metal letters into boxes all day and sometimes he let Quentin sort them too, which was the best game in the whole house, better than counting wall-letters, because sorting type was a game where you could win forever. There was always another letter. There was always a wrong one hiding among the rights, and finding it made your chest feel like a struck bell.

Maren came and went like weather. She smelled of carbolic and cold air and once, memorably, of sick, which Quentin did not hold against her because she'd also brought him a boiled sweet from the ward and taught him to fold it into his cheek so it lasted four songs' worth of time. When Maren laughed, which was rare, it was short and sharp like a door closing, and Quentin had learned that if you said something funny right before she left for the ward, you could hear the door close twice.

And then there was the father, Aldous, who lived mostly downstairs behind the press, and whose hands Quentin watched whenever he thought no one was watching him watch. Aldous's hands were stained black to the second knuckle, always, like they'd been dipped in night. He had helped make something bad happen. Quentin didn't know what. He knew it the way you know a dog doesn't like you—not from being told, but from the angle of its ears.

Nobody in the house ever said Papa's name. Not once. Quentin had listened with his whole self, lying flat under the table like a rug, and nobody said *Minister Corvel*, nobody said *your father*, nothing. It was like Papa had been erased. Like somebody had taken a great wet cloth and wiped him off the world.

Quentin knew about erasing. In the ward—no. Not the ward. Before. At home, there had been a slate in the kitchen, and Mama wrote the day's list on it, and at night she wiped it clean, and everything she'd written was gone except in her head, and Quentin used to think: what if her head gets wiped? Then the list would be nowhere at all.

He thought about that a lot lately, lying in the garret bed, looking at Konrad the K.

* * *

It started, the day of the fire, with a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

Quentin was on the floor with the type case Orsen had given him—a real case, with real worn-out letters, the ones too battered to print straight anymore—and he was setting his own name, Q-U-E-N-T-I-N, which took forever because there was only one Q and it was bent and kept wanting to lie down.

The sound came again. A deep *whump*, far away but not far enough, and the little window buzzed in its frame like a trapped fly.

Quentin went still. He had a rule with sounds: freeze first, decide after. He'd learned it from the nights back home when boots went by in the street below and Mama would put her hand flat on his chest without saying anything, and the hand meant *be a stone.*

But then came a second sound, and the second sound was worse, or maybe better, Quentin couldn't decide—it was cheering. Far-off, thin, like the sea in a shell, but definitely cheering. Hundreds of somebodies, glad about something.

He climbed onto the bed and knelt at the loaf-window and looked out.

Smoke was standing up over the Paper District. Not cooking smoke, which lay down lazily on rooftops and smelled of bread. This smoke stood straight up like it had somewhere to be, thick and black and boiling, and underneath it, orange light flickered against the underside of the clouds, pulsing, like the sky had a heart and the heart was beating too fast.

Quentin pressed his nose to the glass. Below in the street, people were running—but running *toward* the smoke, which was the wrong way, everyone knew the correct way to go regarding smoke was away, Mama had drilled it into him along with *don't lick the frost* and *never follow strangers even nice ones especially nice ones.* Yet these people ran toward it, and they were shouting, and some of them had sticks with cloth on the ends, red cloth, and one man had climbed a lamppost and was waving both arms like a windmill having the best day of its life.

Then the state print works caught properly alight.

Quentin saw it happen—the whole face of the great building bloomed orange at once, windows filling with light like lanterns, and the crowd roared so loud the window buzzed again, and sparks went up the chimney-black column of smoke like bees.

And Quentin's stomach did a strange flip-flop, because he knew that building. He knew it from the carriage, from going past it with Papa, many mornings, and Papa would point at it with his chin—he never pointed with fingers in public, Papa said fingers were for private—and say:

*See that ugly place, my Quent? One day we'll burn every lie inside it. That'll be our game. You and me. We'll watch it burn and roast chestnuts on the embers of their lies.*

He'd said it quietly, in the joking voice, the secret-voice, the one he used for games that weren't really games. And Quentin had laughed, because chestnuts.

Now the ugly place was burning. Just like Papa promised.

Which meant—

Quentin's heart went *bang bang bang* against his ribs like someone knocking. Papa's game was happening. Papa's promise was keeping itself. Which meant Papa was coming. Games only worked when everybody came. That was the whole entire rule of games. You couldn't have hide-and-seek with one person; Orsen had explained that solemnly when Quentin asked, and then looked sad about it for some reason, which was another strange-Fairweather thing.

So Papa would come up the stairs, creak creak creak, any minute now. He'd find Quentin in the garret, exactly where the letter said, and he'd scoop him up and smell like smoke like the night he arrived, and they'd watch the fire together and eat the chestnuts Papa had definitely brought, because you couldn't have a fire game without chestnuts, that was just science.

Quentin scrambled down and dragged the blanket off the bed and spread it flat, because Papa liked to sit. He found the tin where Maren hid the biscuits (three) and set it on the blanket, then felt guilty and put two back, then felt guilty again and ate neither, which was the worst outcome for everyone including the biscuits.

Then he sat on the blanket with his knees hugged to his chest, facing the door, waiting for the creak of the stairs.

The stairs stayed quiet.

Downstairs, faintly, he could hear Orsen and Aldous talking low and fast, the way grown-ups talked when the words were heavy and they didn't want them waking anyone. He couldn't make out the words. Grown-up heavy words never traveled well through floors; they arrived as murmurs, like the river under ice.

The window went orange-brighter. Quentin looked back at it. The chimney of the print works was glowing at the top now, actually glowing, molten, and pieces of the roof were coming off and sailing up in the fire-wind, burning papers, hundreds of thousands of burning papers, whirling up into the dark like enormous terrible fireflies.

Papers. Burning papers. All those letters going up at once.

Something cold walked up Quentin's spine on little feet.

All those letters, and every single one was being un-counted. Nobody could count them now. They were flying apart into sparks, and sparks aren't letters, sparks are nothing, sparks are the opposite of a name—

His name was Q-U-E-N-T-I-N. Seven letters. He'd set it that very morning in the battered old case. If letters could burn, could names burn? Could a whole boy's name be wiped off the world the way Papa had been wiped off the world, off the walls and the lists and the slates?

"No," Quentin told the window firmly, in the voice Maren used for fever charts. "No, thank you."

But the fire didn't listen. Fire never listens; fire is the rudest thing there is. A burning page came down spinning and slapped against the outside of the glass right in front of Quentin's face, and for one heartbeat he could read it—great fat official letters, a word that might have been ORDER or might have been DEATH, he couldn't tell, the flames were eating the middle of it—and then it curled up black and blew away over the rooftops, gone, un-countable, ash.

Quentin screamed.

He didn't mean to. It came out of him sideways, high and cracked, the scream of a person watching their own name fly away, and he clapped both hands over his mouth but it was already loose in the room, bouncing off the sloped ceiling, and downstairs the murmuring stopped dead.

Boots on the stairs. Creak, creak, creak—fast ones, Maren-fast, and the door banged open and there she was in her ward apron with her hands still smelling of somebody else's sickness, and behind her Orsen, and behind him, slower, Aldous, black-handed, white-faced.

"Quentin." Maren crossed the room in three strides and knelt and took his wrists, gently, the way she took fevers. "What is it? What happened? Are you hurt?"

"The letters," Quentin managed, "the letters are all burning, they're going up, nobody can count them now, Papa said—" His voice wobbled and betrayed him completely. "Papa said we'd watch it together. It's the game. It's happening and he's not here and the game doesn't work without everybody."

Maren's face did several things quickly and settled on careful. Over her shoulder, Orsen stood in the doorway with the orange light moving on his face, and Quentin saw him swallow like the words he wanted were too big to fit.

Aldous came forward slowly and crouched at the edge of the blanket, at the exact distance you'd keep from a strange dog, and his black hands hung between his knees.

"It's not your father's game, lad," Aldous said. His voice was rough, like a file. "Those are students and dockmen burning the state works. Your father isn't—that isn't him."

"But he *promised.*"

"I know." And Aldous looked at Quentin then, full-on, and his eyes were wet and terrible, and he said, quieter: "I know what he promised. Men promise children things they can't deliver. It's the kindest lie there is, and it's still a lie."

"He's *coming* though." Quentin heard his own voice go small and stubborn, a fist of a voice. "This is the place. This is the letter place. He sent me where the letters are, he said count them, so he knows where I am, so he's *coming.*"

Nobody answered. The firelight pulsed on all their faces, orange, orange, orange, like a slow heart beating too fast, like the sky's own chest.

Maren sat down fully on the blanket beside him and pulled the biscuit tin over and opened it and took out a biscuit and handed it to him like this was an ordinary evening, like the world wasn't burning next door, and that was so absurd that Quentin almost laughed and almost cried and did a bit of both into the biscuit.

"Eat it," Maren said. "Fire's no reason to skip supper. Fire's no reason for anything except standing further away than that window, come to think of it." She glanced up at Orsen. "We should douse the shutters. Sparks travel."

"They do," Orsen said softly. He was still looking at Quentin. "Sparks travel."

They moved around the room doing practical things—shutters, water bucket, the lamp trimmed low—and Quentin sat on his blanket with the biscuit getting soft in his grip, and watched through the gap as the great chimney of the state print works finally, groaningly, leaned and folded and went down behind the roofs in a geyser of sparks, and the crowd cheered again, and the cheering sounded different from up close than it had from far away. From far away it had sounded like joy. Up close, through glass, with ash starting to sift down on the rooftops like grey snow, it sounded like something else. Bigger and hungrier. A door closing on purpose.

Konrad the K crackled slightly in the heat, or perhaps Quentin imagined it.

Later—much later, when the light had died down to a sullen red lump on the horizon and Maren had pretended to leave and then not actually left but sat in the stairwell where she thought he couldn't see her—Quentin lay in the dark and held his own name in his mouth like a boiled sweet, letter by letter, so it wouldn't melt. Q-U-E-N-T-I-N. Seven. Still seven. Still his.

And that was when he heard it, drifting up through the floorboards: Orsen and Maren, downstairs, talking in the heavy murmur, and one word surfacing clear through the wood because Maren said it twice, sharp as a slammed drawer:

"*Berths.*"

Then, Orsen: "There are only—"

And the floor swallowed the rest.

Quentin didn't know what a berth was. But he knew the sound of arithmetic being done about you. He'd heard it before, in another house, in another life, the night Papa carried him down to the press crate and told him to be brave and count letters.

He pulled the blanket up to his chin and counted Konrad until sleep took him.
