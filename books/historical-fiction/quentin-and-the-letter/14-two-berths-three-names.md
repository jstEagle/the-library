## 14. Two Berths, Three Names

Quarter-day came down on Velloran like a lid coming down on a box.

You could feel it in the streets. The market stalls that usually sprawled halfway into the road had been pulled back behind their chalk lines by order of the wardens, and the tax clerks moved among them with their ledgers and their little brass seals, collecting what was owed before anyone could decide whether the city would still exist to owe anything to. The bakers had doubled their prices and halved their loaves and somehow everyone pretended this was normal. A man sold roasted chestnuts near the fountain and no one bought any, because chestnuts were a pleasure, and pleasures had become suspicious.

In the shop, we counted.

Not money — there wasn't enough of it for counting to be interesting. We counted days.

"Three," Maren said, setting down her cup of chicory with the particular click she reserved for facts she didn't like. "The barge leaves quarter-day morning, high tide, Salt Quay. That's three mornings from this one."

"And Vasse wants the boy in two," Aldous said from the type cases, not turning around. He had been sorting sorts all evening, which is what my father did when his mind was eating itself: he put the alphabet back into order, letter by letter, as though tidiness in one small corner of the world might hold off the disorder everywhere else.

I sat at the worktable with Quentin asleep against my shoulder — he'd fallen asleep mid-sentence again, something about a dog made entirely of letters, a Q-shaped dog, I think — and I did not move, because moving him meant waking him, and waking him meant he would ask questions, and questions were the one commodity our house could no longer afford.

Two days and three days. Two berths and three names.

Nobody said it. That was the remarkable thing. Four people in one room — well, three people and one sleeping child who weighed about as much as a sack of flour and was considerably more trouble — and the arithmetic hung over us like the smell of ink, present in everything, mentioned by nothing.

* * *

It started, as these things did, with Maren being practical, which is Maren's way of being kind.

She cornered me in the kitchen past midnight while Father sorted letters in the front room and Quentin slept in the pallet nook behind the flour sacks. She had come off a double shift at the charity ward and still smelled faintly of carbolic and other people's misfortunes, and she stood at the stove warming milk she had no intention of drinking, which was her way of thinking out loud without appearing to think out loud.

"The barge master's name is Colvin," she said. "Denny Colvin. He owes me."

"Denny Colvin owes half the city."

"He owes me specifically because I pulled a fishhook out of his brother's palm last spring without taking payment, and because I didn't report that the fishhook incident occurred during an altercation involving a customs officer's hat." She stirred the milk. "He'll take coin or he'll take paper. Paper being printed safe-conducts, properly done, with the right seals. He says he can stretch to two berths at the price we can pay. Three if the price doubles, and the price cannot double, because the price is everything we have."

"So it's two," I said.

"So it's two," she agreed, "unless someone invents money between now and quarter-day, or unless someone invents courage, which is cheaper but rarer."

"Maren—"

"Don't." The spoon stopped. She looked at me across the stove's small light, and my sister — who had spent ten years watching people die politely in a charity ward and had developed the bedside manner of a boot — looked, for one moment, exactly like the girl who used to hold my hand under the table during Father's long silences at supper. "Don't say it aloud. The moment someone says it aloud, it becomes real, and then it becomes a plan, and then someone volunteers, and then the others have to live with it. Say nothing. Let everyone lie awake doing their own sums privately, like civilized people."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes," she said, handing me the milk for Quentin's morning porridge. "It's also Tuesday. Go to bed."

* * *

But of course nobody could say nothing forever, because my father was constitutionally incapable of silence when the silence concerned himself, and by Wednesday morning he had begun talking around the thing in wide, careful circles, the way a man walks around a hole in the floor he doesn't want his children to notice he's noticed.

"There are," he said, arranging proofs on the stone with unnecessary precision, "alternatives to the barge."

"Name one," Maren said.

"The eastern passes. The shepherd roads. In '61 your grandfather moved an entire consignment of banned hymnals through the passes, and the wardens never—"

"Were you planning to carry Quentin up a mountain?" Maren asked. "On your knees? Because I've seen you climb the stairs after a night of sorting type, Father, and I've heard the sounds you make. The mountain would win."

"I am in perfectly good health."

"You make a noise like a hinge when you sit down."

"That noise," said Aldous with great dignity, "is wisdom settling."

I laughed — actually laughed, a short ugly bark of a thing — and then felt sick about it, because laughing was not the point, and the hole in the floor was still there, and we were all three of us standing around it describing the wallpaper.

Quentin, at the window with his chin on the sill, watching the warden patrols go by below, turned around and asked the question children ask, which is always the wrong question at exactly the right time:

"When do we get on the boat?"

The room went quiet the way a struck bell goes quiet — not silent, but ringing somewhere just past hearing.

"Soon," I said.

"Is Papa going to be on the boat?"

And there it was. The hole in the floor, walked straight into, by a seven-year-old, barefoot.

Aldous set down his proof very carefully. "Your papa," he began, in the voice men use when they're buying time with syllables, "wanted you to be safe. That was his whole wish. Everything else is—" He stopped. Even my father, who had drafted death lists with a steady graver-hand and negotiated with censors over brandy, could not finish that sentence with the boy looking at him. "Everything else is arrangements," he finished. "Adults spend most of their lives making arrangements."

"Oh," said Quentin, satisfied, and went back to counting the warden patrol — four men, one dog; he reported the count to us each time as though we'd placed a wager — and the three of us stood in the wreckage of the conversation, none of us looking at the others.

* * *

That afternoon Inspector Vasse came, though he hadn't been expected until Friday, and tightness has a way of announcing itself through small cruelties.

He didn't bring soldiers this time. He brought a clerk, a thin young man with ink-stained fingers who stood by the door holding a ledger like a shield, and Vasse himself strolled the length of the shop with his hands clasped behind his back, reading our spines the way other men read books.

"Quarter-day soon," he observed.

"It is," Aldous said. "We've the census supplements to print. Tax schedules. A proclamation about grain reserves that will surprise no one."

"Grain is never surprising," Vasse agreed. "That's its virtue." He drifted along the type cases, trailing one finger above the compartments without touching — he knew better than to touch; touching was for lesser inspectors — and stopped at the case where Father kept the display faces. "The ministerial office has inquired after certain printing. Certain documents that may have circulated. You'll understand they're being thorough."

"We print what's licensed," Aldous said. "Nothing more."

"No," Vasse said pleasantly. "You don't. That's rather the point of you, Fairweather. You are the most obedient printer in this district, and obedience is only remarkable when everyone knows what you'd print if you dared." He smiled at me. "How is the boy?"

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the bedrock of Velloran itself.

"Boy," I managed.

"The child reported on the east road. Mud-streaked, alone. The description was vague. Children generally are." He examined his fingernails. "There's a reward, you know. For information leading to recovery. Substantial. Quarter-day is expensive, I'm told. Taxes, tides, barges." His eyes came up, mild as tea. "A man with a family might find the timing providential."

"We've seen no child," Aldous said, and his voice was iron, iron poured smooth, and I marveled even as I wanted to strike him. "If we do, the inspectorate will hear within the hour."

"Within the hour," Vasse repeated, tasting it. "See that it's less. I'll call again day after tomorrow. Consider that a courtesy." At the door he paused, turned half-back. "Oh — and Fairweather. The deadline is firm. After quarter-day, the inquiry changes character. Recoveries become arrests. Do keep that in mind when you're doing your accounts."

The bell over the door jingled him out, cheerful as a Sunday.

Maren emerged from the stairwell where she'd been standing with Quentin's coat over his head and her hand clamped gently over his mouth, telling him through gritted teeth that this was a game, the quietest game, the winning-est game ever invented. Quentin, for his part, thought the game was excellent. He gave it four stars.

* * *

Supper that night was bread, hard cheese, and the unspoken.

We ate in the back room because the shop front faced the street and the street now faced back. Quentin ate with the methodical ferocity of a child who had known hunger professionally and did not intend to renew the acquaintance, and Maren watched him eat the way she watched fevers — charting, measuring, drawing conclusions she shared with no one.

It was Aldous who broke first. My father, who had survived thirty years of the regime by never breaking first, broke over a heel of cheese.

"There is a solution," he said, "that no one is saying."

"Father," I said.

"Listen to me. If the boy were found — delivered to the inspectorate, by me, with a full account of how he came to my door, framed correctly, positioned as entrapment I initiated — the family's immunity is already promised. Vasse said so. And the barge—"

"The barge would have two berths," Maren said quietly, "for two names. And the third name would sleep soundly knowing he'd purchased his passage with a child."

"With the son of Corvel," Aldous said, and his hands were flat on the table as if steadying it, as if the table might tip otherwise. "With a child whose father signed the same lists I set in type, Maren. Whose father is dead because men like me obeyed. There is no clean arithmetic here. There is only arithmetic."

"Then let's be exact," Maren said, and her voice didn't rise at all, which was how I knew we had passed beyond anger into something colder and more permanent. "Exact numbers. One barge. Two berths. Four of us in this house, and one of those four is seven years old and weighs forty pounds and has already hidden in a crate once because adults told him it was a game." She laid her knife down parallel to her bread, precisely, the way she laid out instruments. "So the question isn't whether the arithmetic works. It always works. Arithmetic always works. The question is which of us intends to look at that child tomorrow morning and explain our sum to him."

Silence. The good clock in the hall ticked like a metronome for an execution.

"I built this shop," Aldous said at last, very low. "Letter by letter. I bought your mother's medicines with its earnings. I kept you both fed through two shortages and a plague year by knowing exactly how far obedience could bend before it snapped, and I am telling you that it is snapping now, and that when it snaps it will not snap cleanly, it will snap the way things snap in revolutions — messily, and with lists."

"Then don't be on the list," Maren said.

"Someone is always on the list. I should know. I've written them."

The words fell into the room and lay there, and nobody picked them up. Quentin, who had gone to the pallet nook an hour before and was supposed to be asleep, appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt with the solemn expression of a child who has understood perhaps one word in ten but has understood the tone completely.

"I can share a berth," he offered. "I'm small. Boats are big. I counted the river once and it's very wide."

Something in my chest turned over and died quietly.

"Back to bed, sprout," Maren said, and carried him off, and through the wall we heard her murmuring to him — the fever-chart voice, the professional voice — and then, briefly, something softer, something that was only hers.

* * *

Later — very late, past the hour when even the wardens' dogs had given up — I found Father at the stone with a proof in his hands, not reading it, just holding it, the way a man holds a rope he isn't sure is tied to anything.

"Orsen," he said, without turning. "Whatever happens on quarter-day. Whatever arrangement is finally made." A long pause. The lamp guttered and steadied. "Know that every choice I have ever made in that censor's office, at that table, with those men — every single one — I made believing it was the choice that kept this family alive. Not the best choice. Not the right one. The alive one. When a man spends long enough choosing survival, he forgets there was ever another category, and then one day his children look at him across a table and he understands, too late, that he taught them nothing except how to survive, and survival—" his thumb moved across the proof, blurring a letter, "—survival is not a thing you can hand down. It dies with you. Every generation buys its own."

"Father—"

"Go to bed, Orsen."

I went. But I didn't sleep. I lay in the loft above the shop listening to the house settle, running the sum I'd sworn not to run: two berths, three names, four bodies, one child who believed boats were wide and games were games. And beneath the sum, quieter, a different number forming — the number of hours left before quarter-day, and inside that, the shape of a thing I could do with them.

Because there was a fourth option nobody had said aloud. Not a person to sacrifice. A document.

We were printers. We owned a press, and type, and my father's gravers, and thirty years of knowing exactly how authority liked its paper to look. Denny Colvin took paper instead of coin. And somewhere in that shop — in the cases, in the drawers, in the muscle memory of my own hands — was everything needed to make a safe-conduct so true that even the truth couldn't tell it apart.

It was forgery. It was treason. It was, if Vasse caught the trail, a rope for all our necks.

I lay in the dark and listened to Father's hinge-noise stairs creak one final time, and told myself I was only considering it.

I was lying.

Tomorrow night, when the house slept, I would open the graver drawer.
