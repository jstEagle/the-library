## 15. The Graver's Choice

The house slept in the way houses do when they are pretending. Maren's breathing came through the wall in slow, even tides—too even, I thought, for a woman who had spent the day counting coins she didn't have. Aldous's lamp had gone dark two hours past, which meant either that he was asleep or that he wanted us to believe he was. And Quentin—Quentin slept the way children sleep in dangerous times, absolutely and without apology, as though sleep were a country with open borders and he held permanent papers.

I sat at the composing stone with a single candle and did not light a second one.

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives in a print shop after midnight. People imagine presses clattering, ink smelling up the air, apprentices shouting over the noise of their own industry. They don't imagine this: the hush of ten thousand pieces of type sitting in their cases like sleeping birds, each one waiting to be told what to say. My whole childhood was that hush. My father taught me to walk softly between the cases before he taught me my letters. *The shop talks enough by day*, he'd say. *Let it rest.*

Tonight the shop was going to talk whether it rested or not.

The safe-conduct form lay flat on the stone where I'd hidden it inside a folio of hymn proofs. It was genuine—the paper itself, the watermark, the embossed seal at the head that some bored clerk in the Interior Ministry pressed without looking, the way Maren pressed bandages onto wounds without wondering whose blood it was. A clerk's carelessness had given it to us, along with eleven others, in a batch of scrap the ministry sold back to printers during the paper shortage. The regime was so confident of its own eternity that it sold its blanks to the people it would someday need to stop.

That confidence was about to cost them three berths on the coal barge *Merciful Ann*.

But a blank is only paper. What makes a safe-conduct real—what makes any document real—is not the seal. It's the hand. Every inspector in Velloran knew this, and Inspector Vasse knew it better than most, because Vasse had spent nine years learning to read documents the way my father read proof sheets: not for meaning but for habit. The tilt of an ascender. The weight a hand puts on a downstroke when it's tired, or frightened, or lying. A forged signature could be copied perfectly and still hang you, because copying gets the letter right and the man wrong.

Which is why I wasn't going to copy anything.

I opened the case marked with the little brass star—my father's case, the one he kept locked, though he'd stopped locking it weeks ago because there was nothing left in our house worth protecting except the things we were ashamed of. Inside sat the matrices. Not the ones he used for the shop's ordinary work; those were honest lead, bought from the foundry like anyone's. These were his own. Cut by his own graver, in his own hand, over thirty years: the private alphabet of Aldous Fairweather, the letterforms he made for documents too important to trust to bought type. Safe-conducts. Licenses. And once—I knew this now the way you know a scar's history only after someone tells you how you got it—a set of forms for lists of names.

I lifted out the capital F and turned it in the candlelight. There was a nick in the serif, top left, from the night in '41 when my mother died and my father cut letters until dawn because his hands needed to be doing something worse than shaking. I'd been six. He'd let me sit under the bench with a piece of scrap lead and pretend. Even then, even grieving, he'd taught me the first rule: *a matrix remembers everything you feel while cutting it.* Cut angry and the letter comes out angry forever. Cut frightened and it comes out thin.

*So don't be frightened*, I told myself, and immediately heard Maren's voice in my head saying, *Well, that's settled, then,* with the dry inflection she used on patients who swore they weren't in pain.

Here is what nobody tells you about forgery, and what I understood only as I sat there with the candle guttering and the whole city holding its breath outside: it is not primarily a crime of skill. Skill matters—God knows it mattered—but underneath the skill, forgery is an act of attention. To forge a thing truly you must love it. You must know it the way you know your sister's cough, your father's silences, the exact weight of the pause before Aldous Fairweather says something true. For weeks I had been studying my father's hand the way scholars study dead languages, and every hour of study had felt like betrayal.

Tonight it would feel like something else. Tonight I would find out what.

* * *

The first problem was the body of the text, and here the gentle humor of God—or whatever runs print shops—intervened. Because the wording of a safe-conduct is fixed. It has been fixed since before I was born. *Be it known to all officers of the Crown and persons acting under color of its authority, that the bearer(s) named herein travel upon lawful business...* I had set that sentence a hundred times for inspection copies, and my fingers could find every character blind. There was no creativity required, no authorship. Forgery, it turns out, begins with obedience. You must reproduce the state's own dull liturgy flawlessly before you may sin against it.

I composed. *Bearer(s) named herein*: and here was the second problem, the arithmetic problem, the one that had been sitting at our supper table for a week wearing different faces and refusing to introduce itself. Three berths. Four Fairweathers, if you counted Aldous—and I did count him, God help me, even after the lists, even after the dawn visits to the inspectorate, even knowing what Maren knew without either of us saying it. Three berths. One blank.

Unless the blank said otherwise.

My hand hovered over the case. The rule my father never wrote down but lived by: *the document must survive the second look.* Any fool could write *three bearers*. But a clerk checking a manifest against a conduct would expect the number to match the names, the names to match the spaces, the spaces to fit the form's printed lines exactly. So: three names, written in the spaces, in my father's hand.

And then a fourth name, in a space that didn't exist—until I made it exist.

I took up the graver.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because later—in the years after, when Quentin asked, and he always asked—I tried to make it sound noble, and nobility is just fear wearing its good coat. The truth is that my hands shook for the first twenty minutes and I ruined two pieces of type-cast lead before I remembered to breathe the way he'd taught me: *in through the nose, out slow, like the press bed descending.* The truth is that I thought about Vasse's face, that gray patient face, and my stomach turned to cold fat. The truth is that at half past two I nearly gave up, blew out the candle, and went upstairs to become again the boy who sorted type and kept silent.

What stopped me was the nick in the F.

Not the sentiment of it—the memory of my mother, grief, all that. No. What stopped me was technical, and that is somehow the more damning confession. I saw the nick and I saw, with terrible clarity, that I could *reproduce* it. That I knew my father's hand better than he knew it himself, better than he ever would again, because his hand had been declining for years—heavier on the downstrokes, lazier at the terminals, the fatigue of a guilty man leaking into his letterforms—and mine still remembered what his hand was supposed to be. I could cut Aldous Fairweather's alphabet truer than Aldous Fairweather. His best work existed, now, in me.

A son is a matrix. We are cut by our parents whether we consent or not, and everything they feel while cutting us stays in us forever. He had cut me careful. He had cut me observant. He had cut me, all unknowing, into a tool capable of undoing him.

I breathed in through the nose. Out slow, like the press bed descending.

* * *

Cutting type at night, alone, by one candle, is the closest thing to prayer I have ever managed. The world contracts to a quarter inch of lead. The graver speaks a small language—push, lift, turn—and your whole soul leans into listening. Around midnight (or what I judged was midnight; time in the shop goes strange) I stopped being afraid. Not brave—there's a difference, and I've had occasion since to learn it thoroughly. Fear didn't leave. It just got smaller than the work. When the cuts demanded all your attention, there wasn't room left over for terror, the way a full page leaves no room for marginalia.

The capitals went first. Then the lowercase. Then the numerals, which were the hardest, because my father cut his 3s with an old-fashioned flat crown that the foundries abandoned twenty years ago, and getting that flatness right meant relearning my wrist entirely. Twice I caught myself cutting *my* 3 instead of *his*, and twice I melted the piece down and started again. This is the discipline of the compositor's life, the one good thing my father gave me besides the trade itself: you do not argue with the letter. The letter is already perfect somewhere in the mind of whoever first drew it. Your job is not to improve it. Your job is to disappear.

Somewhere in the third hour, melting lead at the little stove, pouring molds by feel, I realized I was humming. Our mother's lullaby, actually—the one about the barge on the river, which under the circumstances struck me as either providence or poor taste. I hummed it anyway. The shop had been silent for so long. Let it have a tune.

By four o'clock the new sorts sat in a wooden dip I'd lined with felt: forty-odd characters of Aldous Fairweather, cut fresh, cut clean, cut by the other hand he'd made. I locked them into a little chase no bigger than a playing card and stood it on the stone, and looked at it, and felt—

I want to say pride. But it was stranger than pride. It was the feeling I'd gotten once as a boy, lifting a finished sheet off the press and finding it good—not *correct*, good—and realizing for one dizzy moment that a thing existed in the world that had not existed before, because of me, and that it would go on existing whether I was proud of it or not. Creation, my father called it, when he was being generous with himself. Tonight, alone, I finally understood the word didn't belong to him. It belonged to whoever was holding the graver.

Then I printed the test pull, and the feeling curdled beautifully.

Because there it was. *BE IT KNOWN TO ALL OFFICERS OF THE CROWN.* In his hand. Perfectly. And beneath the space for the official countersignature, the line I had added myself, in his letterforms, with a forged flourish I'd practiced forty times on scrap:

*Berths granted: four (4). Bearers: FAIRWEATHER, ALDOUS. FAIRWEATHER, ORSEN. FAIRWEATHER, MAREN. [name entered separately].*

Four. I had done it. I had solved the arithmetic the way a typesetter solves it—not by choosing among the names but by adding space. The barge master would see a conduct good for four, issued on proper stock, signed in a hand every inspector in the district knew, and he would take four passengers and ask no questions, because questions were for people whose paperwork invited them.

Three of us could go. Perhaps all four. Perhaps—

No. I made myself finish the thought, because a forger who flinches at his own document is already caught. The fourth name. Whose? If we ran together, all four, then no one stayed behind to answer Vasse, and Vasse did not forgive absences; Vasse collected them. Someone had to remain as the shop's living proof that nothing was amiss, or the soldiers would come down on the *Ann* at her moorings like cats on a pigeon. Someone had to stay.

I looked at the empty space after Maren's name, and my hand—the steady one, the one that had just cut an entire alphabet without trembling—shook.

It would be me. I knew it the way you know weather coming. Father couldn't stay; Vasse watched him too closely, and if the lists surfaced, he'd need to run eventually anyway, or hang. Maren couldn't stay; a woman alone in a burning quarter with no shop license to shield her, and besides, someone had to hold Quentin's hand on that barge, and I trusted exactly two people in the world to do it, and one of them was me. Which left—

Which left me. And the strange thing, the thing I keep returning to even now, is that the knowledge didn't arrive as sacrifice. It arrived as relief. All week the family had circled the arithmetic without speaking it, three berths orbiting four names like a debt no one would name aloud, and everyone pretending not to do sums at the table. I had done the sums. The sums came out with my name in the remainder. And some deep, unheroic part of me—call it the part my father built, call it the part Maren mocked, call it mine—felt the beautiful simplicity of a solved equation. One stays. Three go. The child lives.

I dipped the pen. I wrote QUENTIN CORVEL in the fourth space, small and clear, in a plain clerk's hand—no sense forging that; the boy would need his own name someday, and it might as well begin on paper.

Then I sat back and looked at what I'd made, all of it: the false authority, the true names, the lie built out of my father's face and my own hands. Forgery, treason, and filial rebellion, all in one sheet of recycled ministry stock. Somewhere across town, students were seizing print shops and printing their truth in whatever type they could steal. I envied them for about four seconds. Then I remembered that their truth would get them shot by Thursday, and mine—at least tonight—was going to get a seven-year-old onto a boat, and I decided I could live with the comparison.

There remained one last task, and it was the one that made me laugh, quietly, alone, at four in the morning, in a way that probably qualified as hysteria. The countersignature. The document required a countersigning authority, and the only name available—the only hand in Velloran that would pass Vasse's second look without a flicker—belonged to Aldous Fairweather, Master Printer, holder of License 114, cutter of death lists, traitor to two regimes and counting.

I signed his name.

I signed it well. I signed it better than he could currently sign it himself. And as I sanded the ink and held the sheet to the candlelight, checking my work the way he'd taught me—tilt it, let the light rake the surface, look for the shine of wet spots and the shadows of uneven pressure—I understood that somewhere above me, in the dark, my father was going to wake tomorrow, or perhaps had woken already, and come down these stairs, and see his own handwriting on a document he never wrote, committing treason he hadn't authorized, spending a berth he didn't know existed.

He would know instantly whose work it was. Not because of flaws—there were none, or none he'd find. He'd know because of the 3s. Flat-crowned, old-fashioned, correct. The alphabet he'd cut in grief and passed to me in silence, come home without him.

I banked the stove. I buried the chase in the dummy forme of Sunday hymns, where a search party would have to be very devout indeed to find it. I pinched out the candle and stood a moment in the dark of the shop, in the hush of ten thousand sleeping letters, and I noticed—for the first time in my life—that the silence felt less like a wall around me and more like a held breath.

Above me, a floorboard creaked.

Someone was awake. Someone had been awake for some time, by the sound of it—moving slowly, the way Aldous moved when he was thinking, pausing at the top of the stairs, where the seventh board always spoke.

And then, soft as a compositor setting a line he doesn't want the foreman to hear, I heard him start down.
