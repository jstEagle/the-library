## 16. Aldous Learns the Arithmetic

Aldous Fairweather had spent forty years learning to read type the way other men read faces.

He could walk past a broadside nailed to a wall and know within three strides whether it had been set by a drunkard or a master, by a man in a hurry or a man in fear. He could tell the age of a font by the wear on its shoulders. He could look at the letter *e* and tell you which graver had cut it, and in what year, and whether the cutter had been happy that day — for gravers, like pens, recorded the hand's weather.

So when he came down the shop stairs at half past four in the morning, because his bladder had opinions and his conscience worse ones, and saw the press standing open with fresh ink still tacky on its platen — he did not need to pick up the sheet to know whose hands had worked it.

He picked it up anyway.

The candle stub on the composing stone was burned to a coin. Someone had been here since midnight. The galley had been returned to its shelf crooked, which Orsen never did when he was thinking clearly, and the *q* tray sat a finger's width out of true, which Maren always did, because she composed the way she nursed: quickly, competently, and without any patience whatsoever for tidiness between crises.

But it was the letter itself that stopped Aldous's breath.

A safe-conduct. Quarter-day berth passage on the coal barge *Merry Alice*, downriver to Ferren's Landing. Three names written into the body of the document in careful two-line justification:

*Maren Fairweather. Orsen Fairweather. Quentin Corvel.*

And beneath them, the seal of the Inspectorate of Papers, rendered so faithfully that for one full second — one shameful, vertiginous second — Aldous believed it. Because he knew that seal. He had studied it across a censor's table a hundred times. And he knew the hand that had forged it, because it was his own.

Orsen had not merely imitated the state's documents. He had imitated *Aldous*. The spacing was Aldous's spacing — the slight generosity he allowed between words, a habit from his apprentice years that no master had ever beaten out of him. The terminals on the serifs were cut with Aldous's particular restraint, that economy of stroke that Vasse himself had once complimented, God rot him, over a cup of the inspectorate's terrible coffee. His son had sat down at his father's type case and become his father.

It was the finest piece of forgery Aldous had ever seen.

He had taught him everything except this, and the boy had learned it anyway, the way children learn the things you most wish they wouldn't — by watching, by breathing the same air as the knowledge until it settled in their lungs.

*Three names,* Aldous thought. *Three berths.*

And then, because he was an honest man in the only room where honesty had ever been permitted him, he did the arithmetic.

The barge carried berths for those who could pay in printed paper. Coin was worthless now; the quarter had taught everyone that. Three names on the sheet. Four people in the house above the shop — if you counted the boy, which Orsen plainly did, and Maren plainly did, and which Aldous, God forgive him, had very nearly stopped doing.

Which meant one name too few.

Which meant his children had already done the thing he could not make himself do even in the privacy of his own skull: they had counted, and they had chosen, and the choice had a shape, and the shape was a man of fifty-three years who knew how death lists were made because he had made them.

Aldous stood in his own shop, in his own ink-smell, holding his own face in someone else's hands, and waited to feel rage.

It did not come.

What came instead was something quieter and far more terrible: pride. A great swelling, absurd, unfitting pride, the kind a man feels watching his child take a first step off a high place — terror and triumph in the same instant, indistinguishable, indissoluble. *Look at him,* some ancient animal part of Aldous crowed. *Look what he made. Look how clean the justification is.*

And beneath the pride, cold as river water in March: *One of them will stay behind for me, and I will not allow it, and there is nothing in this world I can do about either fact.*

* * *

He did not sleep again. Sleep and Aldous had been estranged for years now; they met occasionally in a chair by the banked stove, nodded civilly, and parted before dawn.

Instead he climbed to the garret, where the old mold room kept its dust and its dark, and lit the lamp he was not supposed to burn after curfew because lamplight in windows drew eyes, and eyes drew Vasse, and Vasse was due at the door within two days demanding a seven-year-old boy as casually as another man might demand a loan repaid.

Two days. The barge left in three. Even arithmetic this simple felt like drowning.

In the corner of the mold room, under sacking, lay the casting bench. On it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the matrix — the master mold for the death-list type, the letters that had spelled out the names of Minister Corvel and forty-one others, the letters Aldous had cut with his own graver because the regime wanted a hand too fine to be traced to any official foundry, which was to say they wanted a scapegoat with excellent penmanship, and Aldous, wanting his license and his children fed and his silences to keep mattering, had obliged.

He kept the matrix for one reason only: insurance. A man who owns the proof of his own crime can sometimes negotiate the sentence. It was the vilest wisdom he possessed, and he had kept it oiled and dry the way other men keep wedding rings.

He unwrapped it now and held it up to the lamp.

And here is where Aldous Fairweather, who had lied to censors and ministers and his own children, finally told himself the truth, standing alone in a dusty room at five in the morning with a dead minister's alphabet in his hands:

*I am not going to be on that barge.*

Not because the sheet said three names. Because the sheet said *Quentin Corvel*, and his son had cut that name into a government document with steady hands, knowing what it would cost if Vasse ever saw it, and had gone to bed — or had not gone to bed — having decided, at twenty-two, to spend himself for a child who wasn't his. And Maren had moved the boy's pallet into her own room weeks ago without one word of explanation, which was her way of signing things.

His children had chosen. Beautifully. Stupidly. In the manner of their mother, God rest her, who had once waded into a flooded cellar to rescue the neighbor's cat and come up with two cats and a case of spoiled brandy and a smile like sunrise.

They had chosen Quentin. They would each privately believe themselves the one who'd stay behind — Orsen would try to give his berth to Maren; Maren would forge a fever chart proving Orsen medically unfit to travel if it came to it; he knew them, he knew exactly how the next forty-eight hours would go, a tournament of quiet sacrifice, each sibling losing gracefully to the other until the boat left with neither.

Unless someone older and more practiced in cowardice intervened.

*Well,* Aldous told the matrix. *You've come to the right shop.*

* * *

There was a fourth berth to be had. There was, in fact, a way to make the sheet read four names, and Aldous Fairweather knew better than any living soul how to do it, because he had built the machine that would forbid it.

Here was the trouble with the safe-conducts: they were cast from matrices held at the inspectorate, and every legitimate document bore the inspectorate's private mark — a tiny fleuron embedded in the seal, invisible unless you knew to look, placed there precisely to catch forgers. Vasse had shown it to Aldous once, smug as a bishop, certain that no printer outside the walls could duplicate it. The fleuron was a small lily, cut at an angle no commercial graver used, and it was why the quarter-day papers were trusted at every quay from Salt Quay to the sea.

Orsen's forgery lacked it. It was otherwise perfect. It was perfect the way a portrait is perfect when the sitter has no nose. Any clerk who looked twice — and clerks would be looking twice this quarter, with the city burning — would feel the wrongness before he could name it, and would fetch a man with soldiers.

So Aldous did the only penance available to a man of his particular sins. He sat down at the casting bench, took up his graver, and began to cut the inspectorate's own secret mark into steel.

For the regime. One last time. Freely given, which somehow made it the opposite of everything the mark had ever meant.

His eyes were not what they had been. He worked by feel more than sight, letting his fingers remember what the lamp couldn't show him — the angle of the lily's stem, that odd oblique cut, seventeen degrees off vertical, which he had measured once with a protractor while Vasse drank his terrible coffee and praised his letterforms. Seventeen degrees. He cut it sixteen and corrected, cut it eighteen and cursed, and somewhere around the third attempt he caught himself laughing — a dry, rusty sound, startling in the little room — because this was, by any honest measure, the most difficult job of his career, and he was doing it unpaid, uninsured, at dawn, for free, for love, and it was the first work in twenty years that he did not feel filthy doing.

Lead and antimony. That was the alloy — lead for softness, antimony for hardness at the face, the old printer's marriage of yielding and stubbornness, and if that wasn't a fair portrait of fatherhood Aldous didn't know what was. He melted the type-metal in the little crucible, watching it go from dull to mirror, and thought: *I have spent my life making letters that killed people, and now I will make one letter that might let a child live, and the metal does not care, and perhaps that is the mercy of metal, that it takes whatever shape you bring it and bears no witness.*

Except that it did bear witness. That was the whole horror of print, the thing he had tried to teach Orsen in the years when teaching still went smoothly: ink does not forget. Every impression ever pulled still exists somewhere, in a proof drawer, in an archive, in a memory. You cannot unprint a thing. He had built his life on that truth and betrayed it daily, and now, cutting the lily at last to the correct seventeen degrees, he understood that the record was coming for him — that somewhere a sheet was being pasted up or a testimony being taken, and that his name would stand in it in letters just like these, and that he deserved no better and possibly no worse.

The mold came off the bench near eight. He cast the fleuron six times, chose the two cleanest, and carried them downstairs like a man carrying water — carefully, levelly, spilling nothing.

* * *

He did not fix the sheet.

That was the decision, and it cost him more than the cutting had. He could have completed the forgery himself, perfectly, in an hour, and saved his son the exposure. But a completed forgery was Orsen's confession; an unfinished one was a lesson Aldous could still deliver. So he left the sheet where Orsen had abandoned it on the composing stone, tucked the two cast fleurons into a twist of paper beside it, and weighted the whole with the brass rule, the way he'd left corrections on Orsen's apprentice proofs ten years ago: *here is your error, here is the tool, the rest is yours.*

Then he put the kettle on, because whatever else happened in the next two days, his family would face it caffeinated. It was the one tradition of his mother's he had kept, and she had survived two purges and a famine, so there was presumably something in it.

Maren came down first, hair escaping its braid, smelling of the ward's carbolic under the kitchen smells. She looked at the kettle. She looked at her father. She had the nurse's gift for reading a room's temperature before she'd crossed its threshold, and Aldous watched her clock the open press, the burnt-down candle, the twist of paper on the stone — watched her decide, deliberately, to say nothing, and loved her for it and feared for her because of it.

"Your lilies," she said instead, nodding at the twist, "are upside down in the paper. If that matters."

"It doesn't," Aldous said. "Thank you."

"Father." She poured two cups without asking. Her voice dropped to the register she used for telling families hard news. "Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm planning eggs," Aldous said. "We are a household in crisis. Eggs are indicated."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't. "The ward heard the barge list is posted. Quarter-day, three paying berths. Everyone knows everyone pays in paper now." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit of hers when she was choosing words like instruments. "Everyone also knows the inspectorate has been asking after a child on the east road. Father, the whole quarter is doing your arithmetic. The whole quarter is counting us."

"I know," Aldous said.

"Do you?" And there it was, the look she'd been carrying since the dawn she'd seen him coming home from the inspectorate — the look that asked a question she refused to ask aloud, because asking it aloud would make it real, and some things a daughter will not make real in her own kitchen. "Because I count four."

"Then you count better than the forger," Aldous said mildly, and cracked the eggs, and the moment passed the way such moments passed in the Fairweather house: acknowledged in full, discussed in nothing.

Orsen came down last, gray-faced, having clearly slept not at all, and stopped dead when he saw the twist of paper on the composing stone through the doorway — Aldous watched from the kitchen as his son went to it, opened it, stared at the two small cast lilies in his palm, and then stared at the unfinished sheet, and then stared at nothing at all, doing his own arithmetic, arriving — Aldous prayed — at the same sum.

Their eyes met across the shop. Neither spoke. There are conversations a family has entirely in doorways, and this was one: *I know. I know you know. I am not angry. I am not going to let you.* And Orsen's answer, jaw setting in that stubborn line he'd inherited from a woman thirty years dead: *Neither am I.*

Aldous turned back to the eggs, because hands that are busy cannot shake visibly, and because breakfast, like type, is set one piece at a time, and because there was still one more thing to cast before quarter-day, and this one could not be done in lead.

Vasse would come within two days. Vasse always came. And when he came, Aldous Fairweather intended to be standing in his own doorway with a bargain prepared — the only currency he had left, the only trade a man of his record could still make — and he intended, this final time, to set the terms himself.

He just hadn't yet told himself, honestly, what the terms would be.

The kettle began to sing behind him, and upstairs, faintly, a child's voice was counting — *a, b, c* — the way his father had taught him, the way Aldous had taught the walls of this shop, letter by letter, and Aldous stood in the steam and listened to the alphabet arrive in the house like spring, late, imperfect, and unbearably dear.
