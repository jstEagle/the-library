## 17. Vasse at the Door

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, which was wrong on two counts. Inspectors did not call at half past seven, and they did not knock—they arrived, the way weather arrives, with no courtesy of warning. So when Orsen heard three measured raps against the shop door, he knew before he opened it that this was not an inspection.

It was a reckoning.

He had slept, if it could be called that, on the cot behind the composing stone, his boots on, his jacket buttoned. Maren had taken Quentin up to the garret two nights ago without a word exchanged between them about why, and since then the house had moved with the careful hush of a sickroom where everyone knows the patient is dying but no one will say its name.

Aldous was already at the door. Of course he was. His father had a genius for being where the worst thing was about to happen, as though he could absorb some of it by proximity.

Through the frosted glass, Orsen counted shadows. Four. Maybe five. One wore the broad hat of a soldier; the narrow one beside him could only be Vasse.

Aldous opened the door.

"Inspector," he said, in the voice he used for state-approved proofs—pleasant, unremarkable, a voice designed to be forgotten the moment it stopped speaking. "You're early. The coffee isn't."

"I don't drink coffee before ten," said Inspector Vasse. "It makes me optimistic." He stepped inside without being asked, and his soldiers followed, and Orsen watched their boots track street-mud across a floor his father had scrubbed on his knees for twenty years to keep spotless for exactly this kind of visit.

Vasse was a thin man the way a file is thin—all of him narrowed down to a single working edge. He had small eyes that never seemed to blink so much as take inventory, and he carried no notebook, because men who intended to remember everything found notebooks vulgar.

He looked around the shop slowly. At the press. At the type cases ranged along the wall like coffins for very small people. At Orsen, standing by the composing stone with ink still on his fingers from a morning's honest work.

"You've been printing," Vasse said.

"Always printing," said Aldous. "It's what we do."

"Mm." Vasse lifted a printed sheet from the drying rack—a handbill for Herrin's Boot Oil, harmless as bread—and held it to the light as though checking for hidden scripture between the letters. "There was a child on the east road," he said conversationally. "Nine days ago. A woman walking behind him saw soldiers turn him away at the toll bridge and thought it strange, a boy traveling alone. She reported it. People report things now. It's become fashionable."

Orsen felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it did after too little sleep, and steadied himself against the stone.

"We don't know anything about a child," Aldous said.

"No," Vasse agreed pleasantly. "Most people don't. That's what makes the ones who do so interesting." He set the boot-oil handbill down, aligning its edge precisely with the stack beneath it, and turned. His small eyes found Orsen. "And you're the son."

"Orsen."

"Orsen. A printer's name if I ever heard one." Vasse smiled, and the smile had all the warmth of a ledger. "Your father is famous in certain rooms, you know. The finest graver in the quarter. Letters cut so clean the censors weep. Tell me, Orsen—when you look at type, do you see words? Or do you see the man who cut them?"

* * *

They put the soldiers by the door and Vasse wandered, and wandering, Orsen understood, was the inspection. The man touched nothing and read everything: the dates chalked on the slate above the press, the number of chairs (three), the coat pegs (four—one empty, and Orsen saw Vasse notice the emptiness the way a hunter notices a broken twig).

"My daughter-in-law tells me—" Vasse began.

"You have no daughter-in-law," said Aldous.

"I'm speaking hypothetically. Hypothetically, a man in my position hears things. He hears that a certain minister's son may have been passed along the river towns like a parcel. He hears that the parcels always travel by night, and always toward the water. And he hears—" Vasse paused at the type cases, running one finger along their edges without opening them, a restraint that frightened Orsen more than rummaging would have—"that the person best placed to know where such a parcel rests is the man who cut the type for the lists that made the parcel necessary."

The silence that followed had weight. Orsen could feel it pressing on his eardrums.

"Inspector," Aldous said quietly, "you should sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"You'll want to sit down for what I'm going to tell you."

Something in his father's tone made even Vasse pause. It was not fear. Orsen had spent nineteen years learning to read his father's silences—the pamphlets refused, the neighbors ungreeted, the whole architecture of survival built from things unsaid—and he had never heard this particular silence before. It sounded, absurdly, like relief.

Aldous crossed to the workbench and took down the lamp, though the morning was light enough, and lit it anyway, because a man needs something to do with his hands when he is about to burn the last bridge between himself and safety.

"Eleven months ago," Aldous said, "I was summoned to the Ministry and asked to cut a special face of type. Small, dense, economical—a face for documents requiring many names. I was told the purpose. I was paid well. And I was told that if I refused, my license would be reviewed, and my family would be reviewed with it." He looked at Orsen as he said it—not asking forgiveness, Orsen realized, but making sure he heard it plain, once, in front of a witness who mattered. "So I cut the type. And the type was used. And every name it printed has been sitting in my chest ever since like a bullet too close to the heart to remove."

Vasse had gone very still. Stillness, on him, looked like appetite.

"Go on," said the inspector.

"The boy on the east road," said Aldous, "is Minister Corvel's son. And he came to this shop because someone sent him here—to me—as leverage. Whoever holds that child holds proof of what I did. They meant it as blackmail, Inspector. Feed him, forget his face—that was the instruction. Keep him alive and quiet, and my silence stays bought." Aldous's hands were steady on the bench. Only Orsen, who had grown up watching those hands set ten thousand letters without a tremor, saw the fine shaking that lived underneath, like water under ice. "But blackmail requires two parties who can keep secrets. And my children—" here his voice did something Orsen had never heard it do, cracked slightly, like type dropped on stone—"my children appear to have consciences. They've been feeding him. Hiding him. Asking questions I cannot answer. My scheme, if it ever was one, has come apart in my own house."

"You're confessing," Vasse said slowly, "to treason."

"I'm confessing to forgery of state documents, conspiracy in the purges, and harboring a fugitive child as material for extortion." Aldous recited it flatly, like a bill of lading. "I'll give you a written confession in my own hand. You won't need torture or testimony from anyone else. The case closes with me."

"And the child?"

"Gone. Passed upriver weeks ago—I was to receive payment at quarter-day and none came, so he was moved. You'll find nothing here but a family that prints boot oil and minds its license." Aldous met the inspector's eyes. "That is the whole of it. Take me and leave the rest."

Orsen stood by the composing stone and could not move.

This was the arithmetic, then. This was the sum his father had been carrying since the night of the wet knock at the door—the same terrible bookkeeping that had bought the shop's license with other men's lives, performed now in reverse. His father was selling himself to buy the boy. Not the family—Orsen understood that with a cold, clarifying shock—*the boy*. Maren and Orsen were incidental, covered under the general amnesty of an innocent household. But the confession, the surrender, the whole monstrous magnificent trade was for Quentin, the child Aldous had helped condemn and could not stop trying to save.

*I am saved by this*, Orsen thought, and the thought was horror. *We are all saved by this, and it costs exactly one father.*

Vasse walked to the window. For a long moment he looked out at the street, where the barricades of the last week had left scars—scorched cobbles, a door off its hinges leaning against a wall like a drunk against a fence. Somewhere eastward a bell rang wrong, fast and arrhythmic, the alarm-bell rhythm Orsen had been hearing for days in his dreams.

"Quarter-day is tomorrow," Vasse said at last, not turning around.

"Yes."

"The barge at Salt Quay leaves tomorrow."

"So I'm told."

"And your son and daughter will be on it." Vasse turned. His small eyes went from Aldous to Orsen and back again, and Orsen felt them like fingers checking the weight of coins. "Because a household whose master is arrested for treason does not simply continue printing boot oil, Fairweather. Someone will come. Within the day. The shop will be seized, the family questioned, the neighbors interviewed. Your sacrifice buys your children a cell, not a berth."

The words landed in the room and lay there.

"That's not—" Orsen started.

"It's exactly how it is," said Vasse. He straightened his cuffs, a small precise gesture, a man closing an account. Then he did something extraordinary. He looked at Aldous for a long moment—looked at him the way one craftsman looks at another's work—and said, quietly, almost to himself: "You cut the list-type knowing what it was. You kept the boy alive when selling him would have saved you. And now you trade yourself for him a second time." He shook his head slowly. "You are the most complicated criminal I have ever failed to understand."

"Inspector—"

"Take him," Vasse said to the soldiers.

They crossed the room. Orsen lunged forward without deciding to—his body moving on some older loyalty than thought—and one of the soldiers caught him by the shoulder, not roughly, almost apologetically, the way one restrains a mourner at a graveside rather than a criminal at an arrest.

"Papa—" he said, and hated the childishness of it.

Aldous paused at the door. He looked back at his son, and there was no drama in his face, no speech prepared. Just the tired, ordinary face of a man who had spent his whole life keeping silent, finally allowed to say one true thing out loud.

"The fourth case," he said. "Bottom drawer. Don't open it until you're on the water."

Then he was gone, into the gray morning, between the soldiers, and the door closed behind him with the soft, final click of a well-cut letter dropping into its bed.

* * *

For a while nobody moved. The soldiers' boots faded down the street. The lamp Aldous had lit burned on in the daylight, pointless and steady.

Maren came down the garret stairs with Quentin half-asleep against her shoulder, drawn by the sound of voices, and read the empty doorway, and the mud on the floor, and Orsen's face, in the order a nurse reads symptoms.

"They took him," she said. Not a question.

"For the lists," Orsen said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "He confessed to everything. To all of it. So they'd stop looking for—" He couldn't finish. He looked at Quentin, who was blinking awake, confused, clutching Maren's collar with one small fist. "So they'd stop looking for any of us."

Quentin looked at Orser with the grave, searching gaze of a child who has learned that adult faces contain weather reports. "Is the game over?" he asked. "My papa said when the counting was done, someone would come and—"

"It's not over," Maren said quickly, smoothing his hair. "It's just moving to a new page. Go on back up, love. I'll bring bread."

She waited until the stairs creaked under his small feet, and then she turned to Orsen, and her face had gone hard and bright, the way it went on the ward when a patient turned critical and there was work to be done and no time to weep.

"Barge leaves tomorrow," she said. "Papers by tonight. And Orsen—" She nodded at the type cases along the wall, at the whole silent arsenal of their father's life. "Whatever he left you in that fourth case, whatever trick he's got waiting—we'd better hope it's enough. Because he didn't trade himself for a dead boy. He traded himself for a live one. Let's not waste the purchase."

Orsen stood a moment longer looking at the door. Then he went to the fourth case, knelt, and put his hand on the bottom drawer—where, he now remembered with a jolt, his father's graver tools had hung missing these past two nights.

His father had known. His father had known what Orsen would do, had perhaps known it before Orsen himself had, and had left him the tools to do it.

Orsen opened the drawer.
