## 18. The Last Proof

Maren Fairweather had spent six years learning to write lies that looked like truth. Fever charts, mostly. Little rows of figures in a careful hand—temperature rising, temperature falling, pulse noted at intervals so regular you could set a clock by them—that existed for no purpose except to make the charity ward's ledgers balance against a world that preferred its orphans healthy and its paperwork tidy.

She was very good at it. That was the shame of it, and she knew it was a shame, because there were nights she lay awake admiring her own forgeries the way other women admired embroidery.

Tonight, for the first time, she was going to use the skill in the other direction.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes in the Fairweather household did, with Orsen standing too long in front of the type cases.

"You've been staring at that drawer for a quarter hour," Maren said. She was at the worktable, rolling lint into bandages for the barge—because even if they escaped, someone on a coal barge would inevitably split a knuckle, and Maren Fairweather did not travel unprepared. "Either the type has said something to you or it hasn't."

"It's not the type." Orsen didn't turn around. He had his arms folded, which he did when he was carrying something heavy and didn't know where to put it down. "It's what we're not printing."

"Orsen."

"Three sheets, Mare. Three sheets of paper and an hour of composition and every pressman in Velloran would know Aldous Fairweather's name for what it is. What it *was*." He finally turned. In the candlelight his face looked younger than his twenty-two years and older than his father's. "They're going to take him tomorrow. Vasse will see him tried as a forger, a collaborator, whatever story is convenient. And the only account of the lists—the true one, with his name in it—dies in this room with us."

"So we print it and we hang beside him. Is that the arithmetic? Because I've done the arithmetic, Orsen, and I don't care for it."

"I'm not asking you to like it."

"No," Maren said. "You're asking me to agree with it, which is worse, because then it becomes *my* idea too, and I'll have to live with it in whatever country accepts coal-barge refugees with forged papers."

Quentin was asleep in Maren's room, where she'd moved him four nights ago without a word to anyone. Their father had noticed. Their father said nothing. There was a great deal of saying nothing in the Fairweather house; it was practically the family trade, second only to printing.

She looked at her brother for a long moment. Then she put down the bandage roll.

"What did you have in mind?"

* * *

The composition took until well past midnight.

They worked by two candles and a screened lamp, the shutters barred, the press covered with sacking in case anyone glanced through a window—which no one could, because the window was shuttered, but Maren had learned from their father that you prepared for the inspection that couldn't happen, because the day it happened anyway was the day you wished you had.

Orsen set the type. Maren wrote.

That division of labor had been her condition, and she'd made it flatly: *I draft, you compose.* If they were going to commit treason, she wanted it to be legible treason, honest treason, treason with its facts straight. She had spent years watching men die of fevers the regime insisted didn't exist, in wards whose supply requisitions vanished into the same ministries that had drawn up Corvel's death warrant. She knew how the lists worked. She knew the names. Some of them she knew personally—not the ministers, God forbid, but the clerks, the wardens, the quiet functionaries who signed things, the way her father had signed things, the way anyone signed things when the alternative was written in fire.

The sheet was one page. It had to be—one page could be pasted up before dawn; two pages invited questions. She drafted it seven times.

The first draft was too angry. The second was too careful. The third she burned in the candle flame out of pure spite, which improved her mood considerably and accomplished nothing else. On the fourth try she found the voice—or rather, she stole it. The plain, unhurried voice of a shipping notice. *The following goods left the warehouse of record on the following dates.* Nothing dramatic. Just names, dates, and one sentence near the bottom that she rewrote eleven times:

*The undersigned printer, Aldous Fairweather, set this list with his own hand in exchange for his shop's license, and confesses it freely.*

"There," Orsen said quietly, reading over her shoulder. "That's the line that gets him hanged."

"That's the line that keeps him from being a liar forever," Maren said. "There's a difference. It's a small difference. It's about the width of a grave. But it's a difference."

Orsen flinched. Gentle humor had never been Maren's strongest instrument; she kept it in the back of the drawer with the broken sort.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, mostly. "But if we do this, we do all of it. His name goes in. Not because he deserves it. Because leaving it out would make the whole sheet just another lie, and we've printed enough of those in this shop to furnish a cathedral."

"He'd say the same thing," Orsen said after a while. "That's the worst part. He'd stand right there and say, 'Set my name, boy, or don't bother setting anything.'"

"I know," Maren said. "I've been dreading that he'd say exactly that since about half past nine."

* * *

At some point past two o'clock, Quentin appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt, dragging his blanket, hair sticking up like a startled hedgehog.

"I heard the clicky noises," he announced.

"The clicky noises," Maren said gravely, "are state secrets."

"Oh." He considered this. "Can I watch them?"

"You can watch them," Orsen said, "if you sit on that stool and touch absolutely nothing, and if you tell no one—not the soldiers, not the neighbors, not God himself if He comes asking—what you saw tonight."

Quentin climbed onto the stool with the dignity of a magistrate taking his seat. He watched Orsen lift letters from the case with the little steel hand, watched them slot into the composing stick upside down and backward, and after a while he said, in the hushed voice children reserve for genuine revelation:

"It's backwards."

"It is backwards," Orsen agreed.

"Why?"

"So that when the ink meets the paper, it comes out forwards." Orsen held up the stick. "Everything worth reading starts out backwards, Quentin. You just have to trust the pressing."

Maren caught her brother's eye over the boy's head. Neither of them said anything. Neither of them needed to. Somewhere between the fourth draft and the seventh, between the bandages and the blanket and the small insomniac witness on the stool, the thing they were doing had stopped being a debate and become a fact, and facts, once set in type, are remarkably difficult to un-set.

* * *

The proof came off the press at half past three.

Maren pulled it herself—her first time at the big press, though she'd grown up hearing its heartbeat through the floorboards. The sheet was still warm when she laid it on the stone, and they stood over it together, three of them, the smallest one on his tiptoes, reading the thing they had made.

It was ugly work, honestly. Orsen's spacing wobbled in two places where his hands weren't as steady as he pretended. One of the ampersands was from the wrong case entirely, older and fatter than its neighbors, like a fat uncle in a family portrait. But the words stood up straight. Forty-one names. Nine dates. The confession at the bottom, in cold, level type.

Aldous Fairweather's name sat in the middle of it like a nail driven through the page.

Maren found she was crying, which annoyed her intensely, because she hadn't cried since the fever winter and had rather prided herself on the streak.

"Mare." Orsen's hand hovered near her shoulder, uncertain, the way their father's hands always were—God, even the tenderness was inherited. "We can pull it apart. Right now. Nobody's seen it but us."

"And the child," Maren said, wiping her face with her wrist. "Don't forget the witness."

"I won't tell," Quentin said promptly. "I already forgot everything twice."

She laughed—a wet, cracked laugh, but a real one—and that settled something. She looked down at the sheet again, at her father's name, and let herself feel the whole weight of it at once: that she loved him, that he had sold forty-one lives to keep a print shop, that both of these things were true in the same hour and neither canceled the other. Loyalty was a lovely thing. Loyalty had kept this family fed and licensed and invisible for twenty years. And loyalty, unchecked, would have kept this sheet in the type case forever, and Aldous Fairweather would have gone to his trial as a mere forger instead of a confessed man, and the forty-one would have stayed numbers instead of names.

"We paste it up," she said. "All of it. Every corner of the quarter we can reach before dawn."

* * *

They went out at four.

This was Maren's plan, naturally, because Orsen's plans tended to end with him heroically arrested. They went in twos: Orsen took the paper stock and the flour-paste bucket toward Tanner's Row, where the students had been chalking slogans since the barricades went up; Maren took Quentin—he could not be left alone, and could not be left with Aldous, and so would come along and be, officially, a nephew out very late with his aunt, which was the kind of lie the city was too busy burning to check.

The quarter was strange at that hour. Fires still smoldered toward the Paper District, staining the low clouds a bruised orange. Barricades of overturned carts and—Maren stopped briefly to appreciate the poetry of it—borrowed paper bales stood at the corners, manned by students who saluted her solemnly because she carried a medical bag, which in the new arithmetic of the streets apparently outranked a musket.

They pasted the first sheet on the wall of the charity ward itself. Maren chose the spot deliberately: eye level, beside the door, where every clerk and warden and functionary who ever came to sign anything would have to walk past it. She brushed the paste on in long strokes, smoothed the sheet down from the center outward the way she'd been taught to apply poultices, and stepped back.

In the gray pre-dawn light, the names looked almost gentle. That was the trick of print, she thought. The same trick her father had used for twenty years and her brother had learned at his knee and she, tonight, had finally turned inside out: ink makes terrible things quiet enough to read.

"They'll tear them down," Orsen said. He'd materialized beside her, flour to his elbows. "By noon, maybe sooner. Vasse's men, or whoever holds the quarter next week. It doesn't last, Mare. Ink on a wall never lasts."

"No," Maren agreed. "But being read once counts. Ask any fever chart." She tapped the sheet, right over the confession. "Somebody reads this today—somebody who knew one of these forty-one, somebody whose brother or daughter is a number on a ministry ledger somewhere—and they remember it. And remembering is the only press that can't be confiscated."

From the shadows by the ward steps, Quentin piped up: "There's a backwards letter in the third line."

Maren blinked. "What?"

"There." He pointed with the absolute confidence of a boy who had spent an evening on a stool touching nothing. "It's facing the wrong way. Papa—" he caught himself, and Maren's chest did something complicated— "*your* papa would fix it. If he wasn't..." He didn't finish. Seven-year-olds are excellent editors; they cut whatever hurts.

Orsen squinted at the sheet, found the offending sort—an inverted *e*, flipped in the dimness—and laughed, short and helpless. "Four hours of treason and I've committed a typo. Father will disown me twice."

"Leave it," Maren said.

He looked at her.

"Leave it," she said again. "Let it be imperfect. Let whoever reads it know that human hands made this, frightened ones, in a hurry, before dawn. Perfection is what the ministry prints." She pulled the second sheet from under her arm and handed it to him. "Come on. Six more walls and about ninety minutes, and I want to be home scrubbing paste off my hands before your safe-conducts need signing."

They worked east along Tanner's Row, then up past the corn exchange, pasting in silence broken only by Quentin's running commentary ("that one's crooked," "this wall is slimy," "can I do one?"—and once, on the exchange's great doors, Maren lifted him up and guided his small hands smoothing the paper flat, and felt him go very still and solemn, a printer's apprentice of four hours' standing performing his first installation).

On the last wall—the corner of the square where the crier used to read official notices, before the criers stopped coming—they ran out of paste with one sheet to spare and half a bucket's worth of hope missing. Orsen solved it with the remains of the flour sack and water from the horse trough, producing a paste of such questionable consistency that Maren, a professional maker of things that had to hold, gave it a look usually reserved for unsanitary wells.

"It'll hold till sunrise," Orsen said.

"Sunrise is in fifty minutes."

"Then it'll hold fifty minutes, and that's one more than it needs."

She pressed the final sheet home and stood back, hands sticky, skirts filthy, heart pounding with exhaustion and something that took her a moment to recognize as peace.

Across the square, a church bell rang the hour wrong—it had been ringing wrong for three days, knocked askew by the shelling—and somewhere beyond the roofs, toward the river, a barge whistle sounded, long and low, testing itself for morning.

Quarter-day. The barge. Two berths' worth of coin, three names' worth of family, and a document in her apron pocket that said otherwise.

"Orsen," she said quietly. "When the soldiers check papers at the quay—"

"I know."

"—and they look at that seal—"

"I *know*, Mare."

"And if it doesn't hold—"

"Then it doesn't hold," he said, "and at least the wall will still be telling the truth when they come for us."

Maren looked at the sheet one last time—forty-one names, nine dates, one confession, and a single backwards letter that a seven-year-old had caught and a sister had refused to fix—and found, to her surprise, that she had nothing left to add. No asterisk, no footnote, no careful lie in the margin. For the first time in six years, Maren Fairweather had written something that was exactly what it claimed to be, and hung it where the whole quarter could see.

"Now," she said, taking Quentin's hand, "we go home, we wake our father, and we get to the quay before the fog lifts."

And behind them, faint in the graying light, they could hear the first early risers stopping at the ward wall, and the sound of someone reading aloud.
