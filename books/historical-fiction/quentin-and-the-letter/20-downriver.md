## 20. Downriver

The barge smelled of coal and wet rope and something underneath both that Quentin decided was the smell of leaving.

He sat on a crate near the front where he could see the water, and Orsen sat beside him on another crate that said FRAGILE in stenciled letters, which made Quentin want to ask fragile what, but Maren had told him that on the river you didn't ask questions with big answers until after breakfast, and it wasn't breakfast yet. The sky was the color of the inside of an oyster shell. The engine thumped below them like a great patient heart, and every time it thumped, the whole barge shivered a little, as if it were cold too.

"Are we past the city?" Quentin asked.

"Past it." Orsen's hand found his and held it. Orsen's hands were always ink-stained somewhere — in the creases of his knuckles, under his nails, a blue-black shadow no scrubbing ever fully took away. Quentin had decided this meant Orsen's hands were honest. You couldn't lie with your hands full of letters.

"I can't hear the bells," Quentin said.

"You won't again for a while. Maybe never."

Quentin thought about that. He had thought bells were like weather, something every place simply had. But then, he had thought fathers were like that too, and here he was, holding the hand of a man who was not his father, going down a river toward whatever came after fathers.

"Counting helps," he said. "When I can't hear things. Papa taught me. You count the letters on the walls, or the signs, or the bricks if there aren't any letters. Bricks are harder because they don't say anything, but they're good for when you're scared of loud things instead of quiet ones."

Orsen was quiet for a moment. Quentin felt him looking down at him, the way people looked when he said Papa things. They did that a lot. It was one of the things grown-ups did that made no sense.

"What did your father teach you first?" Orsen asked. "Letters or counting?"

"Counting. Then letters were just counting with shapes." Quentin held up his free hand. "Papa said every letter is only a number wearing a costume. A is one. B is two. If you know that, nobody can hide anything from you, because everything written down is just sums pretending to be words."

"That sounds like something a minister would say."

"He said it in the crate." Quentin looked at the water sliding past, brown-green and folded over on itself, carrying bits of leaves and one whole unbothered duck. "In the press crate. Before he shut the lid and drilled the holes. He said, count until someone comes, and I did, and someone came." He squeezed Orsen's fingers. "You came. Well — you knocked. Technically I came to you. But it counts."

"It counts," Orsen agreed, and his voice did the wobble thing it sometimes did, the thing where it sounded fine at the start and not fine by the end, like a chair with one short leg.

* * *

The morning came up slowly out of the east, gold spilling along the water so the whole river looked like a page being read left to right. Quentin ate bread with butter that Maren spread thick because she said thin butter was for people with better futures than theirs. She sat behind them on the coal heap with her nurse's bag between her boots, mending a tear in Orsen's coat sleeve, and she hummed while she sewed, a ward-song Quentin recognized from the charity hospital, the one the fever children sang when they wanted to be brave in front of each other.

"Papa liked butter," Quentin said.

Maren's needle stopped. Then started again. "Did he?"

"He put it all on one corner of the bread so the last bite was the best bite. He said life should be structured that way. Save the butter corner for last." Quentin turned the idea over. "I'm doing it wrong. I ate my butter corner already."

Maren reached forward and ruffled his hair without stopping her sewing, which Quentin thought must be very difficult, like patting your head and rubbing your stomach, and it made him feel oddly proud of her. "Next quarter-day," she said, "we'll buy a whole pound of butter, and you'll eat the corners off four slices in a row, and no one will say a word against it. That's what rivers are for."

"For butter?"

"For starting over with the important things done properly."

The banks went by. There was a church with a leaning spire and letters carved over its door — Quentin counted them, eleven, S-AINT-M-AR-CUS-ES, which was nine actually once you took away the spaces, and he frowned at himself and started over. There was a ferry landing with a sign: VELLORAN FERRY — NO LIVESTOCK WITHOUT TICKET. There was a fence with someone's name scratched into it, T-O-B-I-A-S W-A-S H-E-R-E, twenty-one letters, which seemed like a strange kind of prayer but maybe prayers were just names said out loud at fences.

"Papa said the country was full of writing," Quentin told Orsen. "That even when the city burned its paper, the country couldn't help writing on itself. Fences and doors and the bottoms of bridges. He said if they burned every book in Velloran it would take a hundred years to burn the fences."

Orsen laughed. It startled him a little, Quentin could tell — the laugh came out before he'd decided about it. "Your father," Orsen said, "would have gotten along scandalously well with mine."

"Was yours a letter person too?"

"He cut letters. Out of metal. With a graver. Every letter in our shop, he shaped it himself, like — " Orsen paused, searching. "Like a man carving little doors and hoping someone would walk through them."

"Did they?"

"Some did. One did." Orsen squeezed his hand. "One walked through ours and sat down on a crate and started counting."

* * *

They stopped at noon at a lock, and the barge bumped against the lock wall while men in oilskins argued about water levels, and Quentin used the time to count the lock gate. It had iron bolts in rows, and the bolts were letters if you squinted — X shapes, mostly, X-X-X-X — and he got to forty-eight Xs before the gate groaned open and the barge slid through into deeper, slower water, and the arguing men became small behind them.

It was on the far side of the lock that Quentin saw the smoke.

It stood up over the treeline behind them, back the way they'd come, three columns of it, gray-black, leaning east in the wind. Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke was polite. This smoke leaned like it was running.

"Orsen," Quentin said. "What's burning?"

Orsen turned. So did Maren. Nobody answered for a long moment. The engine thumped. The smoke leaned.

"The print works," Maren said quietly. "The state print works. It's gone up."

"Everything's going up," Orsen said. "We knew it would. We just hoped we'd be farther away when it did."

Quentin looked at the smoke for a long time. He was trying to find the feeling for it, and the feeling kept changing costumes the way letters did. Part of him was scared, the part that remembered the lid of the crate and the drill-holes and the sound of shouting getting closer and then getting quieter forever. But another part of him — a newer part, a part that had been growing since a wet knock on a shop door — looked at that smoke and thought: that's the building where the lists were printed. That's where the paper came from that said Papa's name. And it's burning.

He didn't tell anyone about that part. He kept it in the place where he kept Papa's voice saying *count*, and the butter corner, and the exact number of holes in the crate lid (nine; he'd counted them more times than he'd ever admit, because nine was also the number of letters in QUENTIN, and he'd decided that was not an accident).

"Will Velloran still be there," he asked, "when I'm big? To go back to?"

Orsen took a while answering. Quentin had learned that grown-ups' silences were also letters — you just had to count them differently.

"Yes," Orsen said finally. "Cities are like that. They burn and they're still there. It's the people who have to be carried out carefully and set back up."

"Like type," Quentin said. "You told me. When type falls over, you don't throw it away. You sort it back into the case. Even the crooked ones."

"Even the crooked ones," Orsen said, and now his voice did the wobble all the way through, and Quentin pretended not to notice, because that was the polite thing, and because Maren had told him that some kinds of crying were allowed to happen privately, and that letting a man have his privately was a gift you gave for free.

* * *

The afternoon went long and gold. Quentin slept against Orsen's side for a while and dreamed of the crate, but in the dream the crate floated on the river like a little boat, and the nine holes let in sunlight in stripes, and Papa's voice came through the wood not frightened but laughing, saying *you've miscounted, try again*, and Quentin woke up with his face wet and Orsen's arm around him and did not remember crying, which was the best kind of crying there was.

Maren gave him supper — bread again, and hard cheese, and one apple split three ways, and she gave him the biggest share of the apple and lied about it, and he let her lie, because that was a gift too, given for free.

Then, when the light went blue and the first stars came out over the water, Quentin did the thing he had been saving.

He climbed up onto the coal heap beside Maren and looked back the way they'd come, to where the smoke had faded into evening, and he said, "Tell me about him. My papa. Tell me true things, not sad ones. I'll know if they're sad ones dressed up as true ones."

Maren looked at Orsen across the deck. Some silent sibling-letter passed between them that Quentin couldn't read yet.

"He was tall," Orsen said. "Taller than me. He wore his coat like it was a burden he'd agreed to carry for someone else. And when he signed documents — I saw him sign the license papers once, through the shop window — he wrote his name very fast, like he was paying for something and wanted it over quickly."

"He hid me in a crate," Quentin said. "And drilled nine holes."

"Nine holes," Orsen repeated softly. "For a boy named with nine letters. Yes. I believe he counted."

"He counted everything," Quentin said. "That's how I'll know things about him. Whenever I count something and it comes out right, that's him. That's his handwriting. Numbers are just his signature all over the world."

Maren put her arm around him, and her arm was strong the way ward-doors were strong, and she said nothing, because Maren understood that some sentences were finished and adding to them only smudged them.

The barge went on. The engine thumped. On the dark banks, houses showed single lit windows, and Quentin counted those too — one, two, five, eight — and made each one a letter, and the letters made a word, and the word was HOME, though he checked the arithmetic twice, because home was the kind of word you had to be sure about.

"Orsen," he said, when the lamps on the barge had been lit and the water had gone black velvet. "When we get wherever we're getting. Will you teach me the graver? Papa said somebody should teach me. He said the person who teaches you letters owns a piece of you forever, so choose them carefully."

Orsen was quiet long enough that Quentin counted seven heartbeats.

"I'll teach you," Orsen said. "Every letter. Starting with Q. It's the hardest one and it's yours."

"Q," Quentin said, trying it out. "Q. It looks like an O that ran away with its tail."

Orsen laughed again, easier this time, and pulled the blanket up over them both, and the barge carried them downriver through the dark, past burning things and saved things and fences full of other people's names, toward a tomorrow none of them had written yet.

Quentin fell asleep counting, and for the first time since the crate, he lost track of the number — and woke briefly, much later, to hear low voices above him, Orsen and Maren, and Maren saying something urgent about a letter arriving, a letter that had followed them even here, and Orsen saying, very quietly, "From whom?"

But Quentin was too deep in sleep to hear the answer.
