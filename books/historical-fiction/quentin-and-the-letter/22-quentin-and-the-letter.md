## 22. Quentin and the Letter

The letter had lived at the bottom of my toolbox for eleven years, beneath the graver handles and the worn emery cloth and a tin of type-metal scraps I kept meaning to sort and never did. It traveled from Velloran to Salt Quay, from Salt Quay to the coast, from the coast to this narrow house above the harbor in Merrow's Reach, where gulls argued on the roof every morning as if they were being paid by the argument. Through all of it, it stayed folded in its original creases, sealed with wax I had never broken.

Quentin asked about it the way he asked about most things now—sideways, over his shoulder, pretending not to ask at all.

"You still have it," he said one evening. He was nineteen, tall as a mast, with his father's dark hair and Corvel's stubborn jaw, though he'd have bristled to hear me say so. "The letter. The first one."

I was setting a job for the harbormaster—shipping manifests, thrilling stuff—and I let the composing stick rest in my lap. "What makes you think I have it?"

"Because you keep everything." He leaned against the doorframe of the workroom, arms crossed, wearing that expression Maren called *the auditor*. "You have Papa Aldous's first graver. You have the proof sheet from the night we ran away. You have—" he counted on his fingers, enjoying himself, "—the chipped piece of type from the case Vasse dropped. You probably have the coal dust from the barge in a jar."

"I do not have the coal dust in a jar," I said, with dignity.

He waited.

"It's in an envelope," I admitted. "Labeled."

He laughed—the easy, unguarded laugh that had taken years to come back to him, that I still counted among the few things I'd ever actually made—and then the laughter settled, and he looked at me with something older underneath it.

"Can I read it? Someday?"

And there it was. The question I had been answering without words for eleven years: *not yet, not yet, when you're ready.* Except he was ready. He had been ready for some time, and we both knew it, and the only person who hadn't caught up was me.

"Someday came a while ago, didn't it," I said.

"It's been standing outside your door for years, Uncle Orsen. Knocking politely." He smiled. "It's very patient. It learned that from you."

* * *

I went down to the workroom after supper and took the letter out of the toolbox. Upstairs, Quentin was reading by the stove—he devoured books the way Maren devoured gossip, hungrily and with commentary—and I stood in the doorway holding the folded paper like a man holding something that might be asleep.

Maren was darning socks across the room. She looked up, looked at the letter, looked at Quentin, and understood everything at once, because she always had. She set down her mending.

"Well," she said. "About time."

"Maren—"

"Don't you *Maren* me, Orsen Fairweather. I've been waiting for this since he turned fifteen and started hovering near your toolbox like a cat near a larder." She stood, kissed the top of Quentin's head as she passed—a gesture she performed with exactly the same brisk efficiency she brought to bandaging—and paused beside me. Her voice dropped low. "Read it with him. Not to him. With him. There's a difference."

She took her mending to the other room and shut the door with the softness of a woman giving them privacy and the firmness of a woman who would absolutely be listening through it.

I sat down across from Quentin. The stove ticked. Outside, the gulls had finally gone to bed, or at least stopped arguing long enough for the tide to be heard, a long slow breathing against the harbor wall.

"The rules were simple," I said. "Feed him. Forget his face. That's the whole document. Fourteen words and a seal."

"Whose seal?"

"That's the part you should see for yourself." I held it out. "Go on. Break it. It's been waiting longer than you have."

His hands were steady—I'd taught him type-setting, and anyone who can set type has hands that know how to wait—but I saw him take a breath before he cracked the wax.

The paper opened along its old creases with a sound like a small sigh. He read it once fast, the way he read everything, and then again slowly, and I watched his face do what faces do when a thing they've imagined their whole life turns out to be smaller than they thought. Fourteen words. A mud-stained fold. A seal stamped with a signet I had spent one terrible night confirming belonged to our own shop's commission work.

"*Feed him,*" Quentin read aloud. "*Forget his face.*" He looked up. "That's it? Someone wrote fourteen words and it made a whole family lie for eleven years?"

"Somebody wrote fourteen words," I said, "and it wasn't even the important part. Turn it over."

He turned it over. On the reverse, faint, almost invisible unless you knew where to look, was the impression of the typeface used on the seal's accompanying card—the typeface my father had cut. The typeface that had also set the death lists. The typeface that had, in the end, been re-cut into forged safe-conducts by a terrified apprentice who couldn't sleep.

"This is your father's hand," Quentin said quietly. "Aldous's cut. I know it now. You showed me the matrices."

"Yes."

"So whoever left me here wanted the trail to lead home. To this shop. Either they wanted you caught with me, or—" He frowned, working it. "Or they wanted a trail that could be erased. Because a shop that prints everything can print nothing too. If you destroyed me, no one would ever trace it."

"And instead," I said, "we destroyed ourselves keeping you. Which I suspect nobody priced in."

He was quiet for a while. The fire shifted. Then he said, in a voice carefully stripped of anything, the voice he used when he most needed steadying:

"Do you remember my face? From that night? They told you to forget it."

"Quentin."

"No, I want to know. Truly. Everyone always talks about what was decided *for* you—Papa Aldous's bargains, Aunt Maren's fever chart, your forgery. Nobody asks what it was like to be handed a child and ordered to erase him." His jaw was very much his father's just then. "Did you forget me?"

I thought about lying to make it easier, and rejected it, because he'd have known, because he always knew.

"I remember the mud," I said. "Up to his knees—you were soaked to the waist, and shivering so hard your teeth clicked like a type case in a cart. I remember you wouldn't let go of the letter until Father pried it out of your fist, and then you cried, but silently, which broke my heart worse than loud crying would have. And I remember thinking: *this is the exact opposite of what I'm supposed to do.*" I shrugged, feeling foolish and not caring. "So no. Forgetting was the order. Remembering was the only act of rebellion available to a printer's son that night. I've been rebelling ever since. It's become a habit."

He crossed the space between us and hugged me—quick and hard, the way he did everything, before pulling back embarrassed and pretending to examine the letter again.

"You kept it folded all these years," he said. "Why never break the seal before tonight?"

"Because as long as it was sealed, it was still somebody else's letter." I took it from him gently, refolded it along its tired creases. "The moment we broke it, it became ours. I wanted it to be yours to open. Some things shouldn't be handed to you already opened. Even freedom comes better that way—it means more if you crack the wax yourself."

* * *

The next morning I cleared the bench.

Not the whole bench—just the far end, the good light under the window where the harbor showed through glass gone wavy with age. I laid out the tools on a square of felt: the burnisher, the file, the punches, and last, unwrapped from its oilcloth with more ceremony than strictly necessary, my father's graver.

Quentin came down when he smelled coffee and stopped in the doorway.

"What's all this?"

"Your education," I said. "You can set type faster than I can, you can read a galley upside down at arm's length, and last month you caught a kerning error in the harbormaster's manifests before I did. But you've never cut a letter. That's like knowing how to read and not how to write. Come here."

He sat. I put the graver in his hand and watched him hold it wrong, the way everyone holds it wrong the first time, and adjusted his grip without comment.

"Punchcutting starts with steel," I said, tapping the blank punch on the bench. "Hard steel, shaped to the letter in relief. You strike it into copper to get the matrix—the mold. Cast lead against the matrix, and you get type. Every letter you've ever set began as a man hunched over a bench cursing at a piece of steel. Your grandfather cursed beautifully. He had a gift for it."

"He had a gift for several terrible things," Quentin said. There was no bitterness in it anymore—there hadn't been for years, since the trial, since the pardon, since the day we'd learned that Aldous Fairweather would die in a bed and not on a scaffold, and Quentin had said, unexpectedly, *"Good. I didn't want to hate him forever. It's heavy."*

"He did," I agreed. "But he also cut the letters that printed the true sheet. The one we pasted across the quarter. Both things are true, and the type doesn't care which. Type is honest that way. It will print whatever you give it. The sin and the confession use the same alphabet." I tapped the steel again. "Which brings us to the real question. What letter do you want to cut first?"

He considered this with the gravity he gave to everything that mattered, which was nearly everything.

"My initial," he said at last. "Q. So I exist in a case somewhere. Officially."

"There's a Q in every standard case already."

"Not one I made." He met my eyes, and I saw he'd understood the assignment better than I'd assigned it. "Not one that says I was here. That someone bothered."

So we worked. I'll not pretend the first punch was anything but a disaster—he carved a Q that resembled a potato with ambitions—but the second was better, and by the third afternoon he had found the rhythm I remembered finding myself at thirteen: the tiny cuts, the patience, the way a letter emerges not from carving but from deciding what isn't part of it. Silence, again, but chosen this time. Shaped. Useful.

On the fifth evening he struck the finished punch into the copper matrix, and it bit clean, and he held it up to the window light and laughed out loud.

"It's crooked," he said, delighted.

"It's *yours*,* I corrected. "Crooked is a feature of ownership."

We cast it in lead, cooled it, dressed it, and then—because some rituals insist on themselves—I set it in a composing stick alongside the letters of my own cutting and Maren's single contribution (an asterisk, cut badly during a visit, defended fiercely ever since) and locked the line into a little chase, and printed it on a scrap of good rag paper.

One line. Three hands.

*Q.*

Quentin held the proof a long time.

"They told you to forget my face," he said finally. "Fourteen words. And look what happened instead." He touched the wet ink lightly, checking it, a printer's gesture he didn't know he'd learned. "There's a Q in your case now. In this shop. In every book this shop prints, technically, if I'm in the case."

"That's how inheritance works in a print shop," I said. "Not land. Not money. Just letters, passed hand to hand, each one carrying whoever cut it. Your father hid you in a press crate and told you to count the letters on the walls. I used to think that was a strange thing for a minister to teach a frightened boy." I shook my head. "It wasn't strange at all. He was leaving you instructions. Count the letters, Quentin. Letters are how we survive. Letters are how we remember. Letters are the only thing the fire never quite gets."

He was quiet. Outside, the tide turned, and the gulls resumed their parliament.

"Uncle Orsen," he said at last. "When I have children someday—if I do—I want to teach them this bench. This whole bench. The graver, the matrix, the counting. All of it." He looked up. "Including the letter. Can I tell them about the letter?"

I looked at him—at the mud-streaked ghost and the tall young printer occupying the same body, both entirely real—and found that the answer cost nothing at all anymore.

"Tell them everything," I said. "Start with the knock after curfew. Start with fourteen words." I picked up the proof, the little line of type with its crooked, stubborn, magnificent Q, and hung it on the nail above the bench where the light would find it every morning. "And then teach them to cut their own."

That night, long after the lamp was out, I heard him downstairs in the workroom, alone, the small rhythmic whisper of steel on copper. Cutting another letter. Then another. Building, perhaps, a word—or maybe just counting, the way his father taught him, the way I taught him, the way someone after him would count too.

I lay in the dark and listened to the letters being made, and slept better than I had in eleven years.
