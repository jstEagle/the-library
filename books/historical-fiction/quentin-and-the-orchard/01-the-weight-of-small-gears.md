## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The summons came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before the town had fully woken, delivered by a boy in the livery of the Vellmarsh Observatory who did not wait for an answer. He pressed the card into Kestrel Thackeray's flour-dusted hand — no, not flour; brass filings, though at that hour they felt much the same — touched his cap, and was gone down Cutler's Lane with the quick, unburdened stride of someone whose day belonged entirely to himself.

Kestrel stood in the doorway of Thackeray & Pell, Clockmakers, and read the card twice. The stock stillness of the street seemed to lean in around him, listening. *The Observatory requests the attendance of Mr. K. Thackeray, apprentice, regarding the master clock. Promptitude is essential.*

*Promptitude*, from an institution that had once refused his master's application to wind its regulators, on the grounds that guild men were tradesmen and tradesmen smelled of oil. Ambrose Pell had framed that rejection letter and hung it above the workbench, where it had watched over eleven years of Kestrel's apprenticeship like a saint's icon of grievance.

He turned the card over. Nothing on the back. No explanation of why the Observatory — which employed three horologists of its own, men with university vowels and waistcoats the color of good sherry — should summon an apprentice from a shop barely wide enough for two benches and a stove.

The obvious answer was that the other three were unavailable, or unwilling, or both. A failing master clock was not glory; it was a confession. Somewhere in that great gilt case, something had begun to lie about the time, and every clock in Vellmarsh — the church bells, the railway office, the shipping clerk's pocket watch, the alarm that woke the milk carts — took its truth secondhand from the observatory hill. When the master faltered, the whole town drifted out of agreement with itself, like a family quarreling over a will.

Kestrel went inside, put on his better coat, and told the empty shop he would be back by noon.

* * *

The walk up Observatory Hill took twenty minutes if you hurried and forty if you let yourself feel it, and Kestrel had learned long ago to hurry past the point where the orchards began.

Not that there was anything to see in January. The Marchand orchard lay bare as a ledger awaiting entries, rows of apple trees pruned into shapes like raised hands, the ground between them frosted hard and printed with the tracks of birds. In summer it had been — no. He corrected himself the way he corrected an escapement: firmly, without sentiment. In another life it had been green. In this one it was a field owned by people the newspapers had lately taken to calling *enemy aliens*, as though the trees themselves might be passing intelligence to the French consul through their roots.

He walked faster.

But the memory came anyway; it always did, at this exact bend in the road, because memory was the one mechanism he could never get apart and regulate. He was nine years old, and Odile Marchand was ten, and she had dared him to climb the wall between her father's property and the lane — a low wall, more suggestion than defense, built by some ancestor who believed orchards should be shared. She sat atop it with her legs swinging, eating an apple she had absolutely stolen, and when he reached the top she said, "If anyone asks, you're my cousin from the coast."

"I don't look like you."

"You look exactly like someone who needs an apple." She handed him one. It was warm from her pocket. "There. Now you're family."

He had eaten the apple down to nothing, core and all, sitting on that wall, and somewhere in the swallowing he had made a promise he never quite put into words — that whatever happened, he would keep the Marchands safe. Keep the name safe. Children make such promises constantly and mean them completely and forget them within a season, and perhaps he would have forgotten his too, except that Odile kept handing him apples for six more summers, and each one renewed the vow without either of them saying so.

Then the diplomatic rupture came — the ambassadors recalled, the headlines like hammer blows — and the Marchands, French-born in a country suddenly counting bloodlines the way bankers counted debts, became a problem to be administered. Their name went onto lists. Watchmen appeared at the orchard gate with the bored posture of men paid by the hour to guard fruit.

And Kestrel, who trusted gears, found he did not know what to do with a promise that had no moving parts.

* * *

The observatory at the top of the hill looked less built than deposited — a great pale dome set on a drum of dressed stone, flanked by the low wings that housed the chronograph room and the offices of the Time Board. Brass finials caught the morning light and threw it back in small insolent glints. Kestrel had seen it from below all his life, the way one sees a mountain or a cathedral: as scenery, as fact, as something that had nothing to do with him.

Now a porter in a grey uniform examined his card, then his face, as if comparing the two for discrepancies, and waved him through a side door into a corridor that smelled of floor wax and cold stone.

"Second door on the left," the porter said. "Mr. Quill will meet you. You'll want to wash your hands first. There's a basin."

Kestrel washed his hands. He did it slowly and thoroughly, partly out of courtesy and partly because the water was very cold and made thinking easier. Eleven years of apprenticeship had taught him that most problems surrendered to clean hands and patience. Oil the pivot. Ease the pallets. Find the place where the resistance lives and speak to it gently.

Mr. Quill turned out to be a narrow man with spectacles that magnified his eyes into two anxious moons. He shook Kestrel's hand as though handling borrowed property.

"Thackeray. Yes. Pell's boy." He said it the way one identifies a horse. "You'll understand we'd have preferred — well. It doesn't matter. Come and see it."

They went up a spiral stair and through a door into the clock room, and Kestrel stopped despite himself.

The master clock filled the far wall like an organ. Its case rose fifteen feet, glazed along its length so that the great pendulum — a rod of invar with a lens-shaped bob the size of a dinner plate — swung in slow golden view, left and right, left and right, keeping the seconds for a town it had never seen. Gears the diameter of cart wheels moved behind the glass with a hush like distant surf. Dials faced the room from three sides, each four feet across, their hands painted so finely they seemed drawn rather than made.

Kestrel had spent his working life among movements small enough to lose under a thumbnail. This was the same craft, magnified until it became architecture. He felt the way he imagined a village organist feels walking into a cathedral: reverence first, and underneath it, unwelcome but undeniable, the thought *I could fix this.*

"It's losing," Quill said. "Four seconds a day, and gaining on us. Started three weeks ago. Our own people have been over it twice." He said *our own people* with a bitterness that suggested the phrase covered a wound. "They adjusted the rating nut. They cleaned the pivots. It loses anyway. Then last week the striking train began to hesitate — a catch, a pause, like something's *in* the works. And Thursday night it stopped altogether for eleven minutes. Eleven minutes, Thackeray. Do you know what happens when this clock stops?"

"The telegraph office logs it. The railway runs on its own reserve. The churches—" Kestrel stopped. He knew what happens. Everyone in Vellmarsh knew what happens. The town simply becomes uncertain, and uncertainty, in a port town, costs money and occasionally lives.

"Precisely. The Board wants it quiet. No announcements. If the papers learn the master is unreliable—" Quill's moon-eyes contracted. "You come recommended. Pell says you have a feel for fault. That you can hear a bad tooth in a wheel across a room."

That was true, and Kestrel said nothing, because modesty was cheaper than confirmation.

He approached the case. Up close, the glass showed him reflections layered over machinery — his own face ghosted across the going train, superimposed on wheels he had no business touching yet. He put his ear near the striking-train housing, where Quill had indicated the hesitation, and listened.

The sound was wrong. Not broken-wrong — worn-wrong, or *occupied*-wrong. Beneath the expected click and fall of the strike mechanism there was a faint irregularity, a tiny drag in the rhythm, like a heartbeat with a murmur. Something was catching. Something that should not be there.

"When can you begin?" Quill asked.

"Today," Kestrel said. "I'll need to open the case. Fully. The fault isn't in the escapement you can reach through the front — it's deeper. In the striking train."

Quill hesitated. Kestrel watched the hesitation move through him like weather. "Our people opened it," he said at last. "After the stoppage."

"And?"

"And they found nothing." A pause too long to be innocent. "They found nothing worth reporting."

Something in the phrasing snagged on Kestrel like a burr on wool. *Nothing worth reporting* was not the same as *nothing*. He filed the distinction away, the way he filed every anomaly, in the drawer of things to be examined later under good light.

"I'll start tomorrow," he said. "I'll need the room to myself for a few hours. Faults hide from audiences."

Quill agreed with visible relief, wrote him a pass on cream-colored card stock heavier than any paper Thackeray & Pell could afford, and saw him to the stair.

* * *

The walk home was downhill and faster, and Kestrel let it be fast, because slowing down meant looking at the orchard again, and looking meant remembering, and remembering was a luxury the day could not afford.

At the bottom of the hill he stopped once — just once, involuntarily, the way one's eye goes to a missing tooth — and saw, far off across the frozen fields, a figure walking the bare rows of the Marchand orchard. A woman, wrapped dark against the cold, moving slowly among the raised-hand trees as though taking attendance.

He could not see her face at that distance. He did not need to. There was only one person in Vellmarsh who walked those rows now, and she walked them the way people visit graves.

Odile. Who had made him family with a stolen apple and never released him from it. Who was, as of the autumn gazettes, a name on a list, an enemy alien in her own childhood home, forbidden to leave the district, watched by men paid by the hour.

*I will keep your name safe.* He had never said it aloud. He had never needed to. That was the trouble with promises made in silence — they could not be produced as evidence, defended before boards, or repaired with tools. They simply sat in a man's chest, unwinding.

He turned toward the shop, toward the smell of oil and the framed rejection letter and Ambrose Pell waiting with questions he would pretend were casual. Tomorrow he would open the master clock's case and find the fault in the works.

He did not yet know that the fault would find him first — that folded into the heart of the town's great honest clock, hidden behind the wheels that kept ten thousand watches true, something was waiting that had nothing to do with time at all.
