## 2. A Letter in the Works

The observatory at Vellmarsh sat on its hill like a man who had stopped listening to conversation — patient, aloof, certain of his own importance. Kestrel Thackeray had passed beneath it a thousand times on his walks along the river road, and a thousand times he had thought of it as a kind of cathedral to the one honest thing in the world: the keeping of time. Now, standing in the clock room with his tool roll unrolled across a scarlet baize cloth and the master clock looming over him like a grandfather whose disapproval had outlived all affection, he felt less like a craftsman entering a cathedral than a boy caught somewhere he ought not to be.

The master clock was a regulator of the finest make, its case of black walnut gone soft-edged with a century of polishing hands. It stood seven feet from floor to cornice, and behind its glazed door hung a pendulum of solid brass, weighted at the base with a disc that could have served as a shield. From this single heart, wires and rods ran down through conduits into the town below, to the church of St. Aldhelm, to the guildhall, to the customs house on the quay — every dial in Vellmarsh obeyed this room, and this room answered to nothing but the stars, which the astronomers above took turns reading through the great dome like priests taking auguries.

Kestrel had been sent because the escapement was failing.

He had diagnosed it by ear before he ever climbed the hill. For three mornings running, the guildhall bell had struck its hour a half-second late, then a full second, then — yesterday — nearly two. Most men would not have heard it. Ambrose Pell heard everything; that was the trouble with Ambrose Pell, and the trouble, Kestrel suspected, with himself as well. His master had listened to the nine o'clock strike over breakfast, set down his cup, and said, "The observatory is dying, boy, and you will go and save it. Take the good loupe. And take your time." Then, at the door: "Not too much of it."

So here he was, alone in a room that smelled of machine oil and cold stone, with the afternoon light coming amber through the east windows and dust turning slowly in it, and the clock's case open before him like a chest awaiting surgery.

The broken escapement was the anchor type, old and beautifully made, its pallets worn past the point where oil could flatter them. He worked an hour removing it — first the pendulum, lifted free with both hands and laid on the baize like something sleeping; then the crutch, the suspension spring, each wheel in its turn, every piece wrapped or labeled, every screw returned to its hole in the paper diagram he'd drawn that morning in the workshop. This was the discipline Pell had beaten into him for six years: *a mechanism taken apart carelessly is a mechanism you do not understand.* Order was not a virtue in their trade. It was the trade itself.

It was when he reached into the case's interior to loosen the backboard screws — the last step before the movement could be drawn whole from its housing — that his knuckles struck something that did not belong.

Paper.

He withdrew his hand and looked. Between the backboard and the case wall, wedged flat into a gap no wider than two fingers, was an envelope. Not fallen there, not lost — *wedged*. The wood around it had been pressed and fitted; someone had made a place for it, deliberately, in the dark interior of the most important clock in the county. Someone who understood that a thing hidden inside a mechanism is hidden better than any strongbox could hide it, because mechanisms are trusted absolutely and almost never opened.

Kestrel stood very still. In the silence left by the stopped pendulum he became aware of how loud the room actually was — the tick of the observatory's lesser clocks in the corridor beyond, the creak of the dome overhead as the wind leaned against it, his own breath.

He should fetch someone. That was the correct procedure. An object found in the works of the master clock was not his property, not his business, not his risk. He was nineteen years old, an apprentice with three years yet to serve, a Thackeray of the small house on Rope Walk with a father who repaired fishing reels and a mother who took in mending. Men like him were not meant to find things in the works of grand machines.

But whoever had put the letter there had chosen the moment of its hiding well, and the moment of its finding badly. The astronomers were at their luncheon. The corridor was empty. And the escapement lay dismantled on the baize, which meant the clock could not run again until he reassembled it, which meant that if he called anyone now, he would have to explain why the great regulator of Vellmarsh stood open and gutted while he fetched help over a scrap of paper.

His hands, which had never trembled over a train of wheels, trembled now.

He drew the envelope out.

It was heavy stock, cream-colored, sealed with wax that had once been red and had faded to the color of dried blood. No name on the face. No direction. Only the wax seal, stamped with an intaglio signet he recognized at once and wished he did not: a tree bearing fruit, encircled by a motto ribbon. The arms of Marchand Orchard. He had seen that seal pressed into the wax of a birthday letter when he was eleven years old, delivered to the orchard gatehouse where his mother's sister kept the accounts, and he had carried that letter up the long lime walk to a girl in a green coat who read it standing under the oldest apple tree in the county.

*Odile.*

His thumb rested on the seal without pressing. The room seemed to lean in.

He told himself he would only look at the superscription. Letters had superscriptions; if it was addressed to the Astronomer Royal, or to some long-dead warden of the clock, then it was nothing — an antiquity, a curiosity, a thing to be reported to Mr. Pell with a shrug and forgotten by supper. He turned the envelope over. The reverse was blank. No address at all, then. A letter written to no one, or written to whoever found it, or written to be found only by —

He cut the seal with his smallest knife. Later he would not remember deciding to do it. The blade went through the wax as though the wax had been waiting twenty years for exactly that violence.

Inside was a single sheet, folded twice. The handwriting was fine, upright, French-inflected even in English words — he knew that hand too, from labels on jam jars and invoices for cider, from the little notes Monsieur Marchand used to pin to crates of apples sent down to the town. Étienne Marchand wrote the way he pruned: nothing wasted.

Kestrel unfolded the page and read, and the amber light in the windows seemed to go thin and gray as he did.

*To whom it may concern, being the finder of this paper, I commit to your keeping what I dare not keep myself...*

The letter ran a page and a half. It was dated — he checked twice, disbelieving — the fourteenth of October, 1891. Twenty years ago. It named ships. It named tides. It described, in the careful language of a man dictating to himself rather than to any court, an arrangement whereby goods came ashore at Vellmarsh under bills of lading belonging to other men, and moved inland by night along routes that avoided the toll roads, and were paid for in coin that never touched a bank. It named the men who had built this arrangement and the men who protected it: a customs officer, a magistrate's clerk, two shipowners whose names still appeared on the subscription lists for the new pier. And there, in the middle of the third paragraph, in the same unhurried hand:

*...and of these, none more necessary than the house of Thackeray, whose skill with locks and timing-pieces makes possible the whole economy of the night. Old Thackeray made our keys and regulated our watches so that we might move as one body. His son carries the trade forward. Whatever comes of this enterprise, the Thackerays are in it to the marrow, and I set down their names here so that no one may afterward pretend they stood apart...*

Kestrel read it three times. The words did not change.

*Old Thackeray.* His grandfather, Josiah, dead before Kestrel was born, a locksmith and clockmaker of whom the family spoke rarely and briefly, the way one speaks of a man who drank. *His son carries the trade forward* — his father, Aldous, who mended fishing reels and had never, so far as Kestrel knew, done anything in his life more daring than dispute an invoice. Could it be another Thackeray? The name was not common in Vellmarsh, but it was not unknown either. There were Thackerays up the coast. There had been a Thackeray hanged at Ipswich before the war, his mother said, though she never said for what.

And there was the deeper question, the one that sat in his stomach like swallowed river water: *why was this letter signed by Odile's father?*

Étienne Marchand had been, in Kestrel's lifetime, the most respectable man on the eastern road — Justice of the Peace, churchwarden, the grower whose apples won ribbons at the county show. If he had been part of this — if he had *built* this, and grown frightened of it, and hidden his confession inside a clock rather than burn it — then the orchard's gentility was a surface glaze over something older and darker, and every kindness the Marchands had ever shown the town was a payment on an unseen debt.

Or the letter was a lie. Or a plant. Or a trap laid for whoever opened this case — and who would open it? A clockmaker. A Thackeray. The thought arrived coldly, fully formed, the way solutions sometimes arrive in the mind the way answers arrive in a well-regulated train of wheels: not gradually, but all at once, with a click.

Someone had known the clock would need repair. Someone had known *who* would be sent to repair it. And a sealed letter naming his own blood, tucked into the dark of the master clock like a slug in an apple — what was that but bait on a hook, or a fuse with a very slow match?

He looked down at the page in his hands and saw that his fingers had left damp prints on it.

Outside, a gull cried over the town, and far below, faint as a pulse, the church of St. Aldhelm began to ring three o'clock — obedient, as always, to the dead clock beside him, striking an hour governed by a mechanism that had just stopped being merely a mechanism. Everything in Vellmarsh ran on this room. Every bell, every workday whistle, every tide-table pinned up in every counting house. And inside it, all along, had been this: the town's true time, its secret ledger, ticking away behind the honest face of the dial.

Kestrel folded the letter along its creases. He slid it into the envelope. And then — hearing, distantly, a door somewhere below, voices on the stair — he did the thing his hands decided before his mind could argue: he reached into the gap between backboard and case wall, and he pushed the letter home again, deep into the dark where it had waited twenty years, and he fit the loose block of packing wood back over it so that the interior showed nothing but walnut and shadow.

Then he picked up the new escapement, took up his pliers, and began to install it, and his hands were steady, and he hated them for it.

The pallet stones seated true. The impulse faced the teeth correctly. When he hung the pendulum and set it swinging, the beat came even and clean, the sound every clockmaker knows better than his own heartbeat, and the master clock of Vellmarsh resumed governing the town below as though nothing whatsoever had been disturbed.

Kestrel closed the case. He polished the glass until it shone. He packed his tools, rolled the baize, and stood a moment in the amber light with the letter's words printed on the inside of his eyelids: *the Thackerays are in it to the marrow.*

His father. His grandfather. Perhaps his father still. He would have to know. He would have to ask, carefully, without asking — and if Aldous Thackeray's face gave him the answer he dreaded, then everything would change: the shop, the guild, the future Pell had mapped out for him, and Odile — Odile most of all, Odile whose father had written those words, Odile whom he had promised, under a summer sky eleven years ago with juice from a stolen apple on both their chins, that whatever happened, whatever anyone said about her family, he would keep their name safe.

*You'll keep us safe, Kes? Promise?*

*Promise. Cross my heart and wind my watch.*

He had laughed when he said it. He was not laughing now.

Kestrel lifted his tool roll and went out, locking the clock room behind him with the key the astronomers had lent him, and descended the tower stair into the ordinary evening world of lamplighters and fish carts, carrying nothing visible in his hands and something enormous in his chest. Behind him, above, the master clock ticked on into the dark, keeping the town's time faithfully — and keeping, in its wooden heart, a secret that had already begun, silently and inexorably, to count down.

* * *

That night he could not sleep. At two in the morning he lit the lamp at his workbench, took out a sheet of good paper, and began — the way his master had taught him, the way he did everything, one small precise motion at a time — to draft a letter he did not yet know how to finish, addressed to the orchard on the eastern road, in a hand that shook despite everything, to a girl who had no idea what her father had set ticking inside her name.

He wrote and crossed out, wrote and crossed out, until the candle guttered. And outside his window, far off across the sleeping roofs, the church bell struck three — half a second late.
