## 3. The Orchard in Winter

The frost had come early that year, and it came like a verdict.

Odile Marchand stood at the kitchen window with her hands wrapped around a cup of chicory that had gone cold an hour ago, watching the light climb over the eastern wall of the orchard and touch the bare branches one by one, as though the sun itself were taking inventory. Below the window, at the gate where the lane met their drive, a soldier in Vellmarsh grey leaned against the stone post and smoked. He had been there since before dawn. He was always there since before dawn.

They had posted the notice on the gate in October, nailed up by a man who would not meet her mother's eyes: *By order of the Provincial Authority, the premises and persons of the Marchand household are designated enemy alien property. Interference will be prosecuted.* The word *premises* came first, she remembered. Before *persons*. As if the land were the greater crime.

Her father had read the notice twice, standing very straight in his good coat, and then he had said, in French that the soldiers could not follow, *Ils ont mis le papier avant les gens* — they put the paper before the people — and Odile had thought, absurdly, of clocks. Of how her father loved order, how he kept the ledgers of the estate in a hand so fine it looked engraved. Paper before people. It seemed the whole world had decided to arrange itself that way.

She drank the cold chicory anyway, because waste was a sin her grandmother had died insisting upon, and because warmth, even remembered warmth, even the idea of it held in ceramic, was something.

"Maman is asking for you," said Colette from the doorway. Her younger sister's voice had developed a new habit that autumn: it arrived already lowered, as though the house itself had grown thin walls through which anything might be overheard. "She wants the shutters done on the south side before the wind comes."

"The wind can wait."

"Odile."

"I'm coming." But she stayed another moment at the window, looking out at the orchard, because the orchard was the one thing in her life that had not yet been taken inventory of — not truly, not yet — and she wanted to look at it while it still belonged to no one but the trees.

---

The trees stood in their long rows like a congregation that had been dismissed and told never to return. In summer they had been heavy with cider apples and pears, with the greengages that made her father boastful and generous in equal measure; now they were black-limbed and patient, their pruned wounds healed into pale knots, their roots gripping soil that was beginning to freeze around them. The grass between the rows was silvered with frost that crunched under her boots like sugar.

She walked the third row, which had been her row since she was six years old, when her father had lifted her onto his shoulders and told her that every tree in this row would be hers to know — when it wanted pruning, when it wanted feeding, when it was sick and pretending not to be. *Trees are honest,* he'd said. *A tree cannot lie about what it needs. That is more than can be said for people.*

He had been thinking, even then, about people who lied. She understood now that he had spent his whole life among men whose friendship was a kind of ledger, and that some of the entries had finally come due.

At the end of the row stood the old Russet, the grandmother tree, older than the house, older than the family's claim on any of this ground. Its bark was furrowed deep enough to lose a coin in — she knew, because she had lost one there at nine years old and her father had told her the tree would keep it safe until she needed it. She pressed her palm against the cold bark now. Somewhere beneath her hand, in the cambium, in the slow green dark under the frozen skin of the tree, spring was already being written. No authority had thought to post a notice on the inside of a tree.

"You shouldn't be out here alone."

Colette had followed her, of course. Fifteen years old and faithful as a shadow, wrapped in a coat two sizes too large because their mother refused, on principle, to buy new wool from merchants who might be reporting on them.

"I'm not alone," Odile said. "I'm with four hundred trees."

"You know what I mean." Colette glanced back toward the gate, where the soldier had shifted his weight, smoke curling from him into the white air. "Papa wouldn't—"

"Papa is asleep, and Papa dreams about the harvest of 1908, which was the finest in twenty years, and I am not going to be the one to wake him from it." Odile heard the sharpness in her own voice and regretted it before the sentence ended. She put her arm through her sister's. "I'm sorry. Come. You can hold the basket."

"There's nothing to pick."

"That has never once stopped us."

And it was true — they walked the rows with an empty basket between them because the walking itself was the point, because the rows were the shape of their childhoods laid out in geometry, because a Marchand did not abandon an orchard in winter merely because winter had opinions. They walked and Odile named the trees silently as she passed them, the way one names the dead at a graveside, except these weren't dead, only sleeping, and she refused, absolutely refused, to conduct the naming as though it were a funeral.

But grief found her anyway, in the fourth row, without warning. It came as it always came now — sideways, unannounced, wearing someone else's face. This time it wore Kestrel Thackeray's.

She had not seen him since September. She had seen the notice go up on the gate; she had seen the town's faces change toward them in the market street, the way water changes around a dropped stone; she had watched Mrs. Aldery, who had bought their greengages for eleven years, cross to the other side of the road rather than pass close enough to speak. And through all of it she had waited to learn whether Kestrel's face would change too — whether the boy who had once sat with her in this very orchard, among the fallen blossoms of May, his sleeves rolled and his hands stained green from helping with the grafting, would become another stranger crossing the road.

He had not come. That was the whole of her knowledge. He had not come, and he had not written, and every day the silence grew a little harder to interpret. Silence could mean danger. Silence could mean forgetfulness. Silence could mean a man deciding, sensibly and quietly, that the daughter of enemy aliens was a debt no longer worth servicing.

Or silence could mean what he had promised her, all those years ago, under this same sky: *Your name is safe with me. Whatever happens, whatever anyone says — your name is safe.*

She had been fourteen and had laughed at him, because what could possibly happen to the Marchands, who owned half the riverbank and knew every magistrate between here and the capital? He had not laughed. He had that quality even then, at twelve years old, apprentice in a smock too big for him, grease under his fingernails — he had the quality of meaning things. Most people said words the way a clock strikes hours: regularly, mechanically, signifying nothing but the passage of time. Kestrel said words the way he built things. Once set, they held.

*Your name is safe with me.*

She had thought of it every day since October.

---

That evening the house gathered early in the parlour, because lamplight drawn across curtains invited attention, and attention was the one crop they could no longer afford to grow. Her father sat in his chair by the cold fireplace with a blanket over his knees, and Odile looked at him and felt the fear she kept folded away unfold itself a little further.

He had lost weight the way autumn loses leaves — gradually, then all at once. The cough had settled into his chest in November and shown no intention of leaving. Dr. Verlaine still came, still charged nothing, still said *rest, rest* in the tone of a man prescribing against weather. But Odile had grown up reading orchards, and she knew blight when she saw it working under the bark of a thing. The doctor saw it too. Everyone saw it. Nobody said it.

"Maman," she said, after Colette had gone up to bed, "the lamp oil won't last the month. I'll walk in Thursday, to Ferrier's."

Her mother's needle paused above its work. "You will not walk in Thursday. The Ferriers have not spoken to us since the notice. You'd stand in that shop while they counted their change slowly and hoped you'd leave."

"Then I'll write to Kestrel Thackeray."

The needle resumed. Her mother's face did not move, but Odile had inherited her reading of silence from somewhere, and she read the long pause the way she read a late frost — knowing exactly what it threatened.

"The clockmaker's boy."

"He's nearly finished his apprenticeship. He's a man."

"He is a guild man, Odile. The guild has signed the loyalty declarations with everyone else." Her mother's voice stayed level, thread pulling through cloth, stitch after stitch. "And his master sits on the same bench as the men who drew up our notice."

"Ambrose Pell signs whatever keeps Ambrose Pell comfortable. That's not the same as Kestrel." She heard how certain she sounded, and marveled at it, because underneath the certainty there was only a girl pressing her palm against a frozen tree, willing spring upward through the wood. "Mama. When we were children — when everything was ordinary — he gave me his word about my name. People like Kestrel don't spend their words. I've seen how he works. A thing either holds or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, he takes it apart and finds out why. He won't let this not hold."

Her mother looked at her then, fully, for the first time all evening, and something passed across her face that was almost — almost — a smile. "You sound like your grandmother," she said. "She also believed love was a mechanism. It got her through two wars and one very bad marriage." The needle dipped, rose. "Write your letter. Write it carefully. Say nothing that could hang anyone, including yourself."

---

She wrote it at midnight, at the little desk in her room, with the candle shielded behind a book and her breath fogging in the air because the fire had been allowed to die.

Four drafts she burned. The first was too full of fear, and fear, she knew, traveled badly in envelopes. The second was too full of him, and longing was worse than fear — longing could get a letter opened. The third tried to be practical and came out sounding like a stranger asking a tradesman for goods, and she hated it, and fed it to the flame along with the others.

The fourth she wrote plainly, the way he himself did everything.

*Monsieur Thackeray,* it began, and she smiled bitterly even as she wrote the formality — Monsieur, as if they had ever addressed each other so, as if the whole history of them could be conducted across a shop counter. Then, below it, simply: *Kestrel.*

*I will not insult you by pretending this letter is about clockwork. My father's health is failing. The soldiers at our gate are polite, which frightens me more than cruelty would, because politeness means procedure, and procedure does not stop. I do not know if you have heard anything. I do not know what you have heard. I know only that in May, under the Russet tree, you told me my name would be safe with you, and I have decided to believe that you are a person who does not spend his words.*

*I am not asking you to do anything dangerous. I am asking you to tell me that the promise still stands. If it does not, burn this and say nothing, and I will understand, and I will think of you kindly for the rest of my life, which may not be long, but will certainly feel it.*

*The orchard is bare but the trees are alive. I check every row. Yours in the fourth row is the strongest thing on this hill, and I have told it about you.*

*O.M.*

She read it twice. Then she sealed it with wax from the stub of a red candle left over from some forgotten Christmas, and pressed her thumb into the soft wax before it cooled — not a crest, they had surrendered anything resembling a crest, but just her print, whorls and ridges, the one signature no authority could confiscate.

In the morning she would give it to Joss the carter, who still drove his milk cart past their gate and who had begun, wordlessly, in December, to take their letters into town tucked under the milk cans, where no soldier searching for treason would trouble to look. Joss had never said a word about it. Neither had she. Some loyalties, like grafts, took best when neither party spoke of the cutting.

She set the letter under her pillow and blew out the candle, and lay in the dark listening to her father cough two rooms away, and to the wind moving down the rows outside, through the branches of the sleeping trees, making a low sound like a crowd murmuring before something begins.

She did not know — could not, in any universe, have known — that forty miles of frozen road away, in a cold workshop above a shuttered shop in Vellmarsh, her father's handwriting was already sitting folded in a young clockmaker's breast pocket, closer to his heart than his own tools, burning there like a coal he could neither drop nor grip.

She knew only that she had written, and that tomorrow the letter would go out under the milk cans, and that hope, like sap, rises in winter whether or not anyone believes in it.

Two miles downhill, at the gate, the soldier stubbed out his cigarette and lit another, and watched the dark windows of the Marchand house, and waited, as he had been instructed to wait, for spring.
