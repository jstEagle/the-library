## 4. What the Guild Requires

The bench where Kestrel Thackeray had spent six years of his life smelled of brass dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the beeswax Ambrose Pell rubbed into the wood every Sunday evening when the shop was closed and no apprentice was meant to know his master performed such tender offices upon mere furniture. Kestrel had discovered it once as a boy of seventeen, returning for a forgotten file, and had watched through the frost-furred window as the old man moved down the row of benches with a cloth and a small tin, unhurried, thorough, a priest anointing altars. He had never mentioned it. Some knowledge you keep the way you keep a spare key — not because you intend to use it, but because it seems wrong to leave it lying in the open.

Now, three days after the observatory, he sat at that same bench and could not make his hands behave.

The work before him was simple — a carriage clock with a worn pallet stone, the sort of repair he could have done in his sleep at nineteen — and yet he had fitted and refitted the same pivot twice, and both times his fingers had paused at the moment of seating it, hovering, as though the movement itself might open along some hidden seam and produce another letter.

He had hidden it well. That much he told himself each morning, and each morning the telling grew thinner. The letter lay inside the false bottom of his toolbox, beneath a layer of cotton waste and two broken mainsprings, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. It was the hiding place of a man who expected to be searched by amateurs. Against Pell it would avail him nothing. There was no drawer, no wall, no floorboard in that shop that Ambrose Pell had not opened a hundred times across forty years of ownership; the building was less a premises than an extension of the man's own skull, and everything kept inside it was kept inside *him*.

Kestrel seated the pallet stone. It sat true. He did not feel the small satisfaction it should have given him.

Through the shop's front window, Vellmarsh went about its morning at the pace the master clock permitted. The fishwives crossing to the quay moved at the tempo of a town that trusted its time; the guild hall's bell had rung nine o'clock within four seconds of the observatory's signal, as it always did, as it had since before Kestrel was born. He found himself watching the passersby with a new and ugly attention — cataloguing their strides, their errands, asking of each one whether it was natural or rehearsed. A man in a grey coat stood at the corner by the chandler's for the better part of an hour. Kestrel told himself he was a man waiting for a chandler. He told himself this four times before it stopped working.

"You've re-cut that escape wheel twice," said Pell behind him.

Kestrel started so badly that the graver skipped and bit a crescent into the brass. "Master—"

"Twice," Pell repeated. He came around the bench without hurry. He was not a tall man; he seemed assembled mostly of forearms and opinions, with a face like a knuckle — blunt, creased, capable of surprising gentleness. His eyes went over the ruined wheel, then up to Kestrel's face, and stayed there with the patient weight of a plumb line finding true. "In thirty years I have seen apprentices ruin work from laziness, from drink, from love, and from fear. You are not lazy and you do not drink." A pause, precise as a click in a train of gears. "So."

"It's nothing. A bad night's sleep."

"Mm." Pell took the carriage clock off the bench and turned it over in his hands, examining the movement with an attention he plainly did not need and was performing anyway, the way a magistrate reads a document aloud to give the courtroom time to sweat. "You were three hours at the observatory on Tuesday. Wexford says you asked to be left alone with the case. Wexford also says the escapement you replaced was not the fault — the fault was in the motion work, which any second-year apprentice would have found first." He set the clock down, aligned its edge parallel to the bench's edge, and stepped back. "I taught you better than to misreport a fault, Kestrel. So either you have become careless, which I will not believe, or you found something else in there, and you have been carrying it about for three days like a coal in your pocket."

The shop was very quiet. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere down the lane the gulls were quarrelling over the day's first offal. Kestrel became aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud, absurdly mechanical — *tick, tick* — a mechanism he could not open and repair.

"The clock was dirty throughout," he said. "I cleaned what needed cleaning."

"Yes," said Pell. "That is exactly the kind of answer a guilty man gives: complete, tidy, and useless."

* * *

Pell dismissed him early — "Go home, get sleep, come back tomorrow with whatever it is you're not bringing" — and Kestrel walked out into the grey afternoon knowing, with the certainty of a man who has watched a fuse burn past the last visible inch, that the conversation was not over. Pell never accused without already having decided. That was the whole doctrine of the man: measure twice, conclude once, and never reopen a judgment for sentiment.

Kestrel did not go home. He walked the long way, along the river wall, where the mudflats lay exposed and steaming under a thin sun, and let himself think the thoughts he had been refusing for three days.

Who had written the letter? Odile's father — the hand was unmistakable if you knew it, that upright French schoolhand with its little flourishes, the hand that had signed the receipt for two barrels of cider bought off the Marchands every autumn of Kestrel's childhood. Henri Marchand had sealed a confession of smuggling — names, dates, sums, half the respectable mercantile spine of Vellmarsh laid bare in ink — and hidden it inside the observatory clock, the one object in the town whose interior no one would ever casually open. It was brilliant. It was insane. It was the act of a man who trusted mechanisms more than people, and Kestrel recognized the temperament the way one recognizes a family resemblance.

But why hide it rather than destroy it? Unless it was insurance. Unless Marchand, watching the diplomatic weather turn black all summer, had salted away the evidence of everyone's complicity so that no one dared move against *him*. And now the rupture had come, the estate was posted with notices, the Marchands were enemy aliens with their property under inventory — and the letter sat in Kestrel's toolbox like a lit match among dry fuses, evidence that could hang a dozen families or, handled rightly, perhaps save one.

Or it was a trap. That thought came back every night at the edge of sleep. Perhaps investigators had planted it, or perhaps someone had watched him find it, and the whole thing was a net drawn slowly closed. He turned the possibility over and over until it wore smooth, and still could not tell whether he believed it or merely feared it.

And threaded through all of it, quiet and insistent as a second heartbeat: Odile. Three miles upriver, behind a hedge of official suspicion, in an orchard whose trees the government now counted like prisoners. He had promised her, years ago, standing between the apple rows with blossom coming down like weather — *your name will be safe with me, always, whatever comes.* He had been fifteen and had not known what promises cost. Nobody tells you. That is the great fraud of childhood: they teach you the words and withhold the arithmetic.

* * *

Thursday morning the shop felt wrong before he was through the door. Nothing had moved — every tool on its shadow-board, every bench square to the floorboards — but the air had the quality of a room recently and thoroughly examined. Pell stood at the far bench, working, or performing work, and did not look up.

"Shut the door," he said. "There's a gentleman in the back room who wants a word with you."

Kestrel's blood went cold and then, strangely, calm, the way water goes still before it freezes. "What gentleman?"

"A civil gentleman. A patient one." Now Pell looked up, and what was in his face was worse than anger. It was apology — genuine, wretched, immovable apology. "Sit down, Kestrel. Please. I'd rather you heard it from me first."

The back room held a desk, two chairs, and Investigator Harrow, who rose politely and did not offer his hand. He was a tired-looking man of fifty with the eyes of someone who had spent decades reading other people's faces for errors, the way Pell read movements. Between them on the desk lay a single sheet of paper covered in Pell's cramped, exact script.

"I'll say my piece quickly," Pell began, "because you deserve speed if not comfort. On Wednesday evening I received a visit from Mr. Harrow's office. They asked whether anyone at this establishment had done work at the observatory lately, and whether that person had behaved unusually afterward. I told them the truth: yes, and yes." He drew a breath that cost him something; Kestrel watched it cost him. "They asked me further whether I believed my apprentice would report anything unusual he encountered in the course of his duties. I told them—" the old man's jaw worked— "I told them I could not answer for what fear does to a young man's discretion. And in exchange for that candor, they have agreed that the guild's business, this shop, my name, and yours as my registered apprentice will stand clear of whatever follows. Provided you cooperate."

"You informed on me," Kestrel said. The words came out flat, almost curious.

"I *protected* you," Pell said, and for a moment the knuckle-face cracked and showed the man underneath, sixty-eight years old and afraid. "Don't you see it yet? If they come for you and find nothing, fine. But if they come for you and find something you hid from *me*, in *my* shop — then it's concealment, boy, and concealment is the rope they build their cases from. I have spent forty years teaching hands to be honest. I could not spend one hour pretending yours were."

"And so you traded my silence for the guild's skin."

"I traded *nothing you weren't going to lose anyway*," Pell said, sharp now, the old authority flooding back. "Precision, Kestrel. I taught you precision. Look at your situation precisely. A letter exists — don't look at me like that, Harrow described its general character to me and your face has confirmed the rest — a letter exists naming families in this town, possibly including yours, in matters that at present carry the word *treason*. The state has men, paper, soldiers, and patience. You have a toolbox with a false bottom." He shook his head slowly. "You were never hiding it, lad. You were keeping it warm for them."

Harrow cleared his throat — gently, courteously, the sound of a man opening a ledger. "Mr. Thackeray. I'm not here to arrest you. I'm here because I believe you're the most reasonable person currently involved in this affair, and reasonable people end these things faster. Sit down. Tell me about the observatory."

Kestrel did not sit. Through the back-room wall he could hear the shop clock ticking — Pell's regulator, the good one, the one that had governed his entire apprenticeship — and he understood suddenly that he was listening to the sound of the life he'd had, counting down.

He had one card, and it was burning in his pocket, and every eye in the room was on the smoke.

"The escapement was faulty," he said. "I replaced it. The clock keeps time. Ask Wexford."

Harrow smiled — not unkindly, which was the most frightening thing about him. "I intend to," he said, "among other people. We can do this the slow way, Mr. Thackeray. I have the time. I am, professionally speaking, made of it." He slid a chair out with one foot, an invitation, a trap, a courtesy. "But before we begin — you should know we've also taken an interest in the Marchand property upriver. Inventory begins tomorrow. Soldiers, clerks, the usual apparatus. Estates change hands quickly in wartime." He watched Kestrel's face with the frank attention of a horologist examining a new movement. "Ah," he said softly. "*There* it is."

Outside, far upriver, beyond the roofs and the cranes and the grey line of the water, the orchard was waiting — and now it had a date.
