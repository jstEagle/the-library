## 6. The Investigator's Patience

Investigator Edmund Harrow had learned long ago that the truth behaved like water in a locked house. It did not announce itself. It found the low places, pooled in the cracks of a man's habits, seeped through the seams he believed were tight. You did not chase it. You waited, and you watched where the damp appeared on the walls.

He sat now in the customs office on Cordwainer Lane with a pot of cold tea going bitter beside him and a map of Vellmarsh spread across the desk before him — not the official survey, which showed streets and bridges and the fine stone face of the observatory, but a working map of his own construction, annotated in pencil so faint it might have been mistaken for shadow. Red thread marked the Marchand orchard estate on the eastern slope. Blue thread ran from there down through the tannery yards, across the weir, to the river warehouses owned by a company whose directors included two aldermen and a churchwarden. A third thread, grey, connected the observatory to all of it by way of the telegraph wire that had carried, over eleven years, a great many messages about weather and a very few about cargo that no weather could explain.

Eleven years of ledgers. That was what people failed to understand about his work. They imagined investigation as drama — lanterns, disguises, a hand clapped on a shoulder in a dark alley. In truth it was arithmetic. It was the patient accumulation of small discrepancies until they summed to something undeniable. The Marchand orchards shipped cider to ports that reported receiving apples. The Marchand barges rode high in the water when their manifests said they should ride low. And the master clock at the observatory — the clock every ship's chronometer in Vellmarsh was set against — had been adjusted four minutes slow for a period of nineteen days in the spring of 1908, according to maintenance logs that someone had since attempted to amend. Four minutes. Nineteen days. Enough time for a boat running dark to clear the headland twice.

Someone inside the town's machinery had bent its timekeeping, gently, the way a man bends a sapling, so that it would hardly be noticed and would never quite be proven.

Harrow drank the cold tea anyway. It matched his mood.

* * *

The Marchand estate wore its watch like a bruise wears color. Two constables at the gatehouse, one at the lane's foot, a third who walked the orchard wall at intervals Harrow had timed himself: forty-one minutes per circuit, with a pause of six at the north corner where the wall met the mill stream and a man might cross on the stepping stones if he were young and foolish and in love.

He walked the perimeter in the late afternoon, hands clasped behind him, breathing the cold smell of cut stubble and old fruit rotting sweetly into the frost-softened ground. The trees stood bare as scaffolding. He had read the file on Henri Marchand three times — naturalized citizen, thirty-one years resident, decorated for flood relief in '94, orchardist, widower, and now, by order of the Crown following the rupture, enemy alien — and the file told him nothing that mattered. Files never did. What mattered was that a man with thirty-one years in a country does not betray it casually, and a man who smuggles for eleven years does not do it alone, and a man who is dying — the physician's report said the lungs, months now — does not burn his papers, as Marchand had visibly begun doing, out of loyalty to anyone but his daughter.

Odile Marchand. Twenty-two. Educated at the convent school in Ashford. Fluent in three languages. Seen last Thursday purchasing lamp oil and a newspaper she did not read past the shipping notices, if the stationer's boy was to be believed, and Harrow had paid the boy enough to be believed.

He stopped at the north corner and looked at the stepping stones a while. Forty-one minutes minus six left a window of thirty-five. He made a note of it without writing anything down. He had learned to keep some things off paper; paper had a way of becoming evidence for other people.

A constable touched his cap. "Quiet day, sir."

"They're always quiet," Harrow said. "That's how you know something's moving underneath."

* * *

The Thackeray name surfaced the way names do — not all at once but edge first, like a blade coming up through sand.

It began with the clock itself. The guild's records showed the master clock maintained under contract by Pell & Sons, Clockmakers, and the maintenance logs — including those suspiciously amended pages from 1908 — carried the apprentice signature K. Thackeray alongside the elder Pell's countersignature. Then came the observatory keeper's testimony, delivered in the dry voice of a man who resented being interviewed at all: the young Thackeray had been sent up alone in November to repair the escapement, had spent an unusual length of time with the case open, and had requested — this detail Harrow underlined twice — a copy of the historical maintenance ledger, claiming he needed to verify the pendulum's compensation figures.

No apprentice verified compensation figures. Apprentices replaced broken parts and swept up brass filings. An apprentice who asked for eleven years of records was either unusually diligent or looking for something specific.

And then there was the older matter, the one that made Harrow sit back and regard the ceiling beams for a long moment. The Marchands and the Thackerays went back further than the smuggling did. Land registry showed the orchard's eastern boundary abutting a Thackeray holding sold off in the '80s; parish records showed baptisms, weddings, funerals braiding the two families together across three generations. There had been a friendship. There had perhaps been more than a friendship. The constable at the gatehouse mentioned, in the offhand way of men reporting things they thought beneath notice, that a young man had been seen standing at the orchard wall on two evenings in December, not attempting to enter, just standing, hat in hand, in the manner of a man keeping an appointment he could not keep.

Kestrel Thackeray. Apprentice, twenty-three, unmarried, described by his own guildmaster as "precise to the point of stubbornness." Harrow had met a hundred such young men. They were the most dangerous kind to pressure and the easiest kind to break, because precision was only another name for a conscience with nowhere to hide.

He opened a fresh folder. On its tab he wrote THACKERAY, K., and beneath it, after consideration, ASSET? — and then, because he was honest even in his private shorthand, he crossed out the question mark. Not because the answer was yes. Because he intended to make it yes.

* * *

He chose the shop on a Tuesday morning, midweek, when trade was thin and Ambrose Pell could be relied upon to be at the assay office arguing about hallmarks. The bell above the door gave its small bright note, absurdly cheerful, and Harrow stood a moment among the ticking, letting the sound settle over him. A hundred clocks, none of them agreeing exactly. He had always liked shops like this. They were honest about what everything else lied about — that time was a human arrangement, kept by fallible machines, and that any machine could be persuaded.

Kestrel Thackeray emerged from the back room wiping oil from his fingers with a rag, and Harrow watched the recognition arrive in stages: the stranger's coat, then the stranger's stillness, then the understanding that stillness of that quality meant government.

"Mr. Thackeray. Investigator Harrow." He produced no warrant, showed no papers. He never did at the first meeting; papers put a shape around a conversation, and shapes could be refused. "I wonder if I might trouble you for five minutes. It concerns the observatory clock."

"We finished that work in November," Kestrel said. His voice was level. His eyes went once, briefly, to the back room — the stairs behind it, Harrow noted, and the street door beyond — and returned. "If there's a fault, you'd want Mr. Pell."

"Mr. Pell is at the assay office. I checked." Harrow smiled, mildly, apologetically. He let the silence do its work. Around them the clocks ticked, dozens of small hearts beating out of step, filling the pauses the young man could not.

"What fault?" Kestrel said at last.

"That's rather my question." Harrow moved along the counter, reading maker's names, letting his gloved finger hover without touching. "You spent nine hours with that mechanism open, by the keeper's log. Nine hours for an escapement. Either you are the slowest craftsman in Vellmarsh — and your guildmaster's letters suggest otherwise; he calls you the finest eye he's trained in twenty years, though I doubt he knows I've read them — or you were looking for something other than a broken part." He turned. "Which was it?"

"You'd have to ask the clock."

"I intend to. But clocks only speak through the hands that open them, and yours have been inside it most recently." Harrow took out the folded maintenance ledger — not the amended copy, the original, retrieved from the observatory's strongbox with a warrant signed ten days ago — and laid it on the counter between them. He opened it to 1908. "Four minutes slow, nineteen days. Your initials here, and again here. Countersigned by your master, who tells me — and I quote him with some regret — that the adjustment was made at the request of parties he was not at liberty to name, and that you were merely the instrument. He has been very helpful, Mr. Thackeray. Helpful men sleep well. I recommend it."

He watched the color leave the young man's face and return, watched the jaw set. Good. Fear and pride, pulling opposite directions. Between them, a man became talkative.

"Here is what I can offer," Harrow continued, quietly now, the way one speaks in a sickroom. "Full immunity. Your name removed from every document in this inquiry. The guild's protection — and you will need it, because when this breaks, and it will break, half the respectable names in this town are going to be dragged through the mud, and they will look for smaller names to drown first. Yours is already in the ledger. It stays in the ledger unless I take it out, and I take it out for one price." He tapped the page, the faded initials. "Henri Marchand. The estate. Who carried, who received, who signed. Give me the ring and you walk away clean."

"And if I say I know nothing?"

"Then I'll believe you eventually. I'm patient." Harrow closed the ledger and slid it into his coat. "That's the whole of my method, Mr. Thackeray. Everyone believes me eventually, because I simply don't go away. Time is on my side in a way it isn't on yours. Your master has already chosen his side — did he mention that? He came to me before I ever came to you. Men who teach precision, it turns out, know precisely what they're worth." He put on his hat. "You have until Friday. After that, immunity expires, and we proceed the ordinary way. You know the ordinary way. It isn't cruel. It's just thorough."

At the door he paused, hand on the bell's pull, and glanced back at the young man standing rigid amid all that ticking.

"One more thing, and it's freely given, no charge. The constables at the orchard wall change rotation Friday evening. The new rota starts Saturday." He let that sit in the air between them, unexplained, a key offered without instructions. "Whatever you decide, decide knowing I'll know. Good day."

The bell rang him out into the cold.

* * *

From the window of the Cordwainer Lane office — he kept no office at the courthouse, having found that courthouses made men formal and formality made them stupid — Harrow watched the lamplighter work his way down the far street, flame to flame, and reviewed the day the way he reviewed all days, for discrepancies.

Thackeray had not said yes. That was expected; they never said yes at first. But he had also not said no with any conviction, and more telling than either: he had not defended Marchand. A loyal conspirator would have denied everything warmly, generously, with the whole chest. This one had gone cold and precise, retreating into technicality — ask the clock — which was the posture of a man guarding a single thing rather than a cause. One letter, Harrow guessed. One document, hidden somewhere in that orderly mind or that orderly shop, worth more to him than his own clean name. Sentimental attachment to a piece of paper. It was always paper, in the end, or it was a woman, and often it was both wearing each other's clothes.

He poured tea that was fresh this time, and hot, and drank it standing.

Friday, then. Immunity expiring, the rota changing, the net drawn one mesh finer. He felt no triumph in it — he never did; triumph implied surprise, and he had stopped being surprised by men decades ago. They broke the way mechanisms broke: predictably, at the stress points their makers had built into them. Love was a stress point. So was betrayal, and he had supplied Thackeray with a fresh load of it free of charge, Pell's name handed over like a cracked gear.

By Friday, the young man would understand that everyone he trusted had already sold him, and a man with nothing left to protect except one girl and one letter would do almost anything to keep them — and almost anything, in Harrow's experience, included finally talking.

Or it included burning the whole works down around them.

He considered that second possibility longer than he wanted to, set down the cup, and reached for his hat. There was time yet tonight to walk past the observatory, and past the shop, and past the dark windows of a clockmaker's apprentice who was, at this very hour, deciding what kind of man he would be by Friday.

Harrow intended to know the answer before Thackeray did.
