## 9. Testimony

They held the hearing in the guild hall, which was fitting, Kestrel thought, because the guild had built the trap and only wanted a room large enough to spring it.

The chamber had once been used for the annual exhibition of apprentices' work — he remembered standing in it at fourteen, his first escapement balanced on a square of velvet while masters in dark coats bent over it like priests over a relic. Now the velvet was gone and in its place stood a plain oak table, and behind the table sat three men: Investigator Harrow in his grey civilian coat, Magistrate Corben with his spectacles and his reputation for mercy that had never once survived contact with paperwork, and Alderman Voss, who owned half the warehouses along the quay and whose face wore the particular blankness of a man protecting investments.

Kestrel sat alone at a smaller table facing them. His hands were cuffed loosely in front of him, more ceremony than necessity; Harrow had ordered it, Kestrel suspected, less to restrain him than to remind the gallery — two rows of townsfolk, curious and cold-eyed — that this was what became of men who stopped the town's time.

It had been nine days since the master clock died. Nine days since he had fed Odile Marchand's letter into the works and let the great wheels choke on it, since he had stood before the crowd in the observatory yard and said, in a voice steadier than he had any right to expect, *the mechanism is faulty — I will testify to nothing but that.* Nine days of a cell, of bread and watered tea, of Harrow visiting twice with his patient questions and his notebook he never seemed to write in.

Nine days without a single clock striking anywhere in Vellmarsh. The town had not yet learned to live without its shared hour. He heard it in the way people moved through the streets outside his window — hurried, uncertain, each man carrying his own private time now, disagreeing with his neighbor by minutes, by quarter-hours. A town unraveling at its seams because one clockmaker had chosen a girl over a gear train.

He did not regret it. That was the strange thing. In the cell, in the dark, he had turned the choice over the way he'd turn a worn part, looking for the flaw, the place where it might have been made differently. There wasn't one. There was only the promise, made under an apple tree when they were both children — *I'll keep your name safe* — and promises, unlike mechanisms, could not be taken apart and rebuilt.

"Thackeray." Harrow's voice carried the flat mildness Kestral had come to dread more than shouting. "For the record, state your name and trade."

"Kestrel Thackeray. Apprentice clockmaker." The word *apprentice* tasted like ash. Pell had revoked his indenture the day after the arrest — formally, publicly, a document filed with the guild clerk before Kestrel had even finished being processed into the cells. His master had sold him twice over: once to save the guild, and once to make certain no one thought the guild had anything left to hide.

"And your master at the time of the observatory commission?"

"Ambrose Pell."

Harrow nodded slowly, as though each answer confirmed something he'd already written down somewhere else. "You were sent to repair the master clock on the fourth of the month. You worked alone in the case for the better part of two days. During that time, you removed from the mechanism certain papers. Do you deny it?"

"I don't confirm it either," Kestrel said. "I cleaned the movement. Movements collect debris. Dust, oil residue, corrosion. I disposed of it as any craftsman would."

"Corrosion," Harrow repeated.

"Clocks are machines, sir. Machines decay. It is the entire reason my trade exists."

A murmur ran through the gallery — someone laughed, briefly, and was silenced by a look from Voss. Harrow did not smile. He opened his notebook at last, and Kestrel saw that it was not empty after all; it was full of small dense handwriting, weeks of it, and the sight of it did what all the cuffs and questions had failed to do. It frightened him. Harrow had been writing the whole time.

"We have testimony," Harrow said, "from Ambrose Pell, that you behaved strangely during the repair. That you asked unusual questions about the observatory's records. That you returned to the observatory at night on at least one occasion in company we have not yet identified. That when the clock failed — catastrophically, deliberately, by your own hand before thirty witnesses — you destroyed materials that had been concealed within it." He looked up. "Pell has also given us the name Marchand."

The name landed in Kestrel's chest like a dropped weight. He kept his face still. Behind his hands, his fingers found each other and held on.

"Pell is mistaken," he said.

"He may be. Men under pressure mistake things." Harrow closed the notebook. "Here is what I am prepared to offer, Thackeray, and I offer it once. Full immunity. No charges for the destruction of the clock, none for obstruction, none for whatever else we find. In exchange, you give us the letter's contents, the names it contains, and the names of anyone who helped you destroy it or helped you keep it hidden. You walk out of here today. The Marchands answer for themselves."

"They are enemy aliens," Kestrel said quietly. "They'd be deported. Or worse. You know that better than I."

"I know that a conspiracy has been running contraband through this river town for eleven years under the protection of some of its most respected families," Harrow said. "And I know that a twenty-three-year-old apprentice is sitting in front of me willing to go to prison rather than say so, which tells me either that he is the most loyal fool in England, or that the people he is protecting matter to him more than his own life." He paused. "Neither explanation interests me. Only the names interest me."

"Then I can't help you."

"You can. Everyone can. Everyone talks eventually, Thackeray. I've been doing this work nineteen years, and I have never met a man whose silence outlasted my patience." Harrow folded his hands. "But I confess yours has lasted longer than most. So let me ask you plainly, before the magistrate, so the record shows I offered. Will you name the conspirators?"

Kestrel looked at the three men. At Voss, whose warehouses he could see in his mind's eye, whose ledgers were almost certainly in that notebook. At Corben, whose mercy was already composing itself around whichever outcome cost the town least. At Harrow, weary and exact, a man who was not cruel — who might even, in another life, have been a friend — and who was nonetheless going to grind forward the way a clock grinds forward, indifferent, precise, until every tooth of the thing had done its work.

"No," Kestrel said.

"Give the court your reasoning."

It was the opening he had been waiting nine days for. He had rehearsed it in the cell, spoken it aloud to the wall, pared it down until nothing soft remained. He rose, as far as the cuffs allowed, and spoke clearly enough to reach the back of the gallery.

"Investigator. A clock testifies to the truth. That is its whole purpose. Put a good clock in a tower and it will tell every soul in the town the same honest hour, whether they want to hear it or not. But take that same clock apart — break its escapement, seize its wheels, feed it something it was never built to hold — and it cannot tell you anything. It stops. It goes silent. And no amount of questioning, no threat, no offer of pardon will make a broken clock speak again." He drew breath. "You are asking a broken man to testify. I broke myself doing what I judged right, and what remains of me will not name the Marchands, and will not name anyone. A broken clock cannot testify. Neither should a broken man."

Silence held the room. Somewhere in the gallery a woman began quietly to cry, and Kestrel did not look to see who.

Corben cleared his throat and reached for his pen. "The court notes the witness refuses—"

"The witness," said a voice from the doors, "is not the person you should be questioning."

Every head turned.

Odile Marchand stood in the doorway of the guild hall, and Kestrel's heart stopped the way a clock stops — completely, and then, after one impossible interval, resumed. She was thinner than she'd been beneath the observatory, dressed in a grey coat that had seen too many winters, her dark hair pinned back severely, her chin lifted in the old way, the orchard way, the way she had faced down her father's anger and the bailiff's notices and the whole slow machinery of a country turning against her family. Two constables flanked her, but they flanked her like escorts, not captors. She walked between them as if she had requested the honor guard herself.

"Madam," Corben began, "this hearing is—"

"Concerns me." She came down the aisle without hurrying. "My name is Odile Marchand. My family's estate is under seizure, my father is dying, and my correspondence has evidently become a matter of state." She stopped at the rail separating the gallery from the floor, close enough that Kestrel could smell winter and woodsmoke on her. She did not look at him. She was magnificent at not looking at him. "Investigator Harrow believes a letter was found hidden in the observatory clock. He is correct. I wrote that letter."

The gallery erupted. Corben struck the table with his palm. Harrow had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching a mechanism move in a way it was never designed to move.

"It was my letter," Odile went on, over the noise, her voice rising clean and hard, "written in my hand, sealed with my seal, concerning my family's affairs — affairs which, I remind this court, are the private property of a family this country has already stripped of every legal protection it could strip. If a letter of mine was in that clock, then it was there because I placed it, or caused it to be placed, and its contents are mine to give or withhold." Now she looked at Kestrel, only at him, and everything unsaid passed between them in the space of a heartbeat: *you kept the name safe; now let me carry it beside you.* "And I withhold them. Under the rights that remain to me, few as they are. If this court wishes to charge someone with concealing that letter, it may charge me. I am, after all, the enemy alien. It saves everyone the trouble of pretending otherwise."

Harrow rose slowly. For the first time since Kestrel had known him, the investigator looked tired in a new way — not the weariness of patience, but of a case slipping sideways through his careful hands.

"Madam Marchand," he said, "you understand that by claiming this letter, you claim its contents. Whatever those contents implicate—"

"I claim my father," Odile said simply. "Whatever he wrote, whatever he did, whatever names he signed. I claimed him long before this court existed." Her voice did not break, but it bent, one degree, like a wheel under load. "That is what testimony is, isn't it? Saying what you know to be true even when it costs you."

Harrow looked at her for a long moment. Then at Kestrel. Then, briefly and strangely, at his own notebook, as if it had betrayed him too.

"Remove the prisoner Marchand," he said at last, "to be questioned separately. And adjourn this hearing until—" 

But he never finished the sentence, because the doors burst open a second time, and a young constable — mud to the knees, hat gone, breath ragged — stumbled into the guild hall and shouted words that emptied every mind in the room:

"The Marchand orchard's alight! The whole estate — soldiers say it started near the drying sheds — it's burning to the ground!"

Odile's hand found the rail. Kestrel was on his feet before the cuffs pulled taut, and across the room their eyes met over the heads of magistrates and investigators and a town that had lost its time — and in hers he read, clear as a struck bell, the only order that had ever mattered to either of them.

*Run.*
