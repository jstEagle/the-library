## 10. What Can Be Carried

*Kestrel*

They released him on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before the market opened, when the constable's ledger was still damp with the night's ink and no one wished to be the one who kept a clockmaker past his hour. The case had collapsed the way Harrow himself had predicted it would — not with a verdict but with a shrug. A letter that could not be produced was a letter that did not exist; a mechanism declared faulty by its own repairman was a mechanism beyond the reach of subpoena; and a town whose respectable families stood implicated in half a decade of quiet smuggling preferred, overwhelmingly, to forget.

Harrow had shaken his hand at the door of the hearing room. It was not a warm hand, but it was an honest one.

"You broke a fine piece of work," Harrow said. "The clock, I mean."

"Yes," Kestrel said.

"And you'll not tell me why."

"I told the court why. The escapement was worn. The whole train was unreliable. A clock that cannot be trusted to keep time cannot be trusted to testify."

Harrow had looked at him for a long moment, and something in the investigator's tired face had almost smiled. "You know what I think? I think you're the only man in Vellmarsh who ever told me a true thing." He had turned away then, gathering his papers, and added, without looking back: "There's a coach south at dawn. If I were a man who carried things he shouldn't, that's when I'd carry them."

Now it was dawn, and Kestrel stood at the edge of the Marchand orchard with a canvas roll of tools under one arm and his coat pockets full of parts that had once belonged to the observatory's dead master clock — not enough to rebuild it, only enough to remember it. Beside him, wrapped in sacking against the cold, stood a sapling: a cider apple, grafted from one of the old Marchand trees, dug up two nights ago in the dark by Odile's own hands while he kept watch at the wall.

Behind them, above the roofline of the estate, smoke was rising.

It was not the house that burned. The soldiers had been orderly about it; they had inventoried everything down to the cellar shelves and left the buildings standing for whatever officer would claim them next. What burned was the orchard prunings — twenty years of cut wood, dragged into heaps at the field edges and set alight because no one knew what else to do with the property of enemy aliens, and because fire is cheaper than care. The smoke went up straight and white in the still air, and the smell of it reached them where they stood, sweet and bitter together, like cider left too long in the sun.

Odile had not cried. She had watched the smoke the way her father had taught her to read weather, and when she finally turned away she said only, "He'd have hated the waste," and picked up her end of the sapling.

Her father had died on the Thursday, quietly, holding both their hands as though they were the last two gears of some small machine he meant to see finished. To Kestrel he had whispered, "Two promises. Keep them both. That's all a man is — the promises he keeps," and then he had closed his eyes with the satisfaction of a craftsman setting down a tool at day's end. Kestrel had understood, in that moment, that the old man had known everything — had perhaps always known — and had chosen, in dying, to make his daughter's safety and the letter's destruction the same single instruction.

The coach south left from the toll bridge at first light. They walked toward it through the waking streets of Vellmarsh, past shuttered stalls and the first lamplighters going home, and no one stopped them. A few faces looked out from windows. Old Mrs. Calloway, who sold apples in the square, raised a hand — not quite a wave, not quite a salute; something between recognition and farewell. Kestrel nodded back. Whatever the town believed or pretended to believe, it let them go, and he understood that this too was a kind of testimony: that half of Vellmarsh had decided the cheapest truth was the one he'd already paid for.

At the toll bridge, the river ran brown and quick beneath the frost. Odile paused at the rail and looked back once — at the observatory dome pale against the brightening sky, at the smoke still climbing from the orchard — and then she did not look again.

"Vellmarsh will keep its time without us," she said.

"It will keep someone's time," Kestrel said. "It won't be right. But it will keep."

* * *

*Odile*

The new town was called Ferrow's Bend, three days south by coach and another world entirely — a mill town where the river turned great wooden wheels and nobody had heard of the Marchands, which meant nobody had heard of enemies either. They took a room above a chandler's shop, two narrow beds and a window that looked out on the water, and for the first week Odile slept as though sleep were a country she had been exiled from and had finally been granted re-entry papers.

Grief came later, the way winter comes after the leaves are already down. She woke some mornings with her father's voice so clear in her ears that she answered it aloud before she remembered. She missed the orchard with a physical ache, a hollowness under the ribs, and she learned that grief for a place is stranger than grief for a person, because a person is gone but a place goes on existing without you — trees budding, fruit ripening, strangers walking the rows her grandfather planted. Somewhere behind her, in another life, the grafts she'd made every spring were being harvested by men in uniforms.

But she was her father's daughter, and her father had never once, in all the failing years, let a tree die for want of attention.

She found work first — that was practical, and practicality was her inheritance too. The chandler's widow needed hands for the wick-dipping, and Odile's fingers, trained on bud-grafting and fruit-thinning, proved deft at it. Then, in her second week, she heard through the chandler that there was a workshop to let on Tanner's Lane: small, dusty, with a north-facing window and a broken sign that read *CLOCKS & WATCHES REPAIRED* in faded gilt.

"A clockmaker needs a workshop," she told Kestrel that evening, setting down the bread. "And you are a clockmaker."

"I'm a man who destroyed the finest clock in the county."

"You repaired it for six years before that. Destruction was one afternoon's work. Don't let it define you more than the craft does."

He looked at her across the table — this man who trusted mechanisms more than people, who had dismantled his own reputation gear by gear to keep a promise made to a girl under an orchard tree when they were children. She saw him turning her words over the way he turned over a stubborn movement, looking for the flaw, finding none, and being almost disappointed by it.

"The sign's broken," he said at last.

"Then we'll mend it," said Odile. "It's what we do."

* * *

*Kestrel*

The workshop on Tanner's Lane smelled of dust and linseed oil and, faintly, of the tannery downstream — nothing like Pell's guild rooms with their polished mahogany and their rules posted in brass frames. Kestrel cleaned it alone the first day, sweeping out a decade of another man's neglect, and when Odile came at dusk with supper wrapped in a cloth she found him sitting on the floor amid the debris, holding the broken sign across his knees.

He had fixed the hinge. He had regilt the letters by lamplight, working from memory of how Pell had once shown him gold leaf applied to a client's case — *breath, boy, not pressure; the leaf goes where the breath tells it.* The sign now read true and bright, and hanging it on the hook outside felt, absurdly, like the most consequential act of his life. More consequential than the observatory. More than the hearing. Those acts had ended things. This one began.

He built the small clock in November.

It was a simple piece — a thirty-hour movement, walnut case, brass dial he engraved himself with no maker's name, because he was done signing other people's names to things. He cut the wheels by hand, depthing them by feel, and worked on it evenings while Odile read aloud from the secondhand books they'd bought at Ferrow's Bend's weekly market, her voice moving through the room like a pendulum of its own. The sapling stood in a barrel by the south window, bare sticks in sacking, waiting for spring and ground that wasn't frozen. Some mornings Odile talked to it. He had stopped finding this strange.

On the evening he assembled the escapement, he sat for a long while with the pallets in his palm, considering.

Every clock he had ever built or mended, he had built to run true. That was the creed of the guild: *the work must be honest; the work must keep faith with time itself.* Ambrose Pell had drilled it into him since he was eleven years old — that a clockmaker's whole moral life lived in the escapement, in that small patient transaction between power and restraint, the letting-go and the catching-hold, tick and tock, the heart of every faithful machine.

Kestrel took up his file, and with four careful strokes he altered the impulse face of the pallet stone — barely anything, a few thousandths of an inch, invisible to any eye but a clockmaker's.

He assembled the movement. He set the hands to the church bell across the river, listened to the strike, and adjusted until the little clock ran — he timed it through the evening, checking it against the mill whistle, against his own pulse — exactly ninety seconds slow in every day. Not erratically slow. Faithfully slow. A clock that could be trusted completely to be slightly wrong.

"What are you doing?" Odile asked from her chair, watching him listen to it with his head tilted like a bird's.

"Making it honest."

"It runs slow."

"It runs slow *on purpose*. There's a difference." He set the case upright on the mantel of the workshop's small stove and stepped back. "Pell used to say a clockmaker's morality lives in the escapement. Every wheel, every tooth — the whole machine exists to make one honest promise: *this is the time; believe me.*" He touched the walnut case once, lightly. "I can't make that promise anymore. Not cleanly. I've seen what happens when everyone agrees on the time — when the observatory speaks and the whole town obeys, and the ones who set the hands decide whose hour strikes. So this clock will keep its own time. A little behind. Deliberately. Anyone who asks, I'll tell them plainly: it loses ninety seconds a day, and I built it that way, and if you want a clock that flatters authority, buy it elsewhere."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then she laughed — really laughed, the first he'd heard from her since the orchard — and came to stand beside him, her shoulder against his arm.

"My father kept a watch that ran fast," she said. "All his life. He said he liked arriving early to a world that was late."

"He sounds like he'd have quarreled with my clock."

"He'd have bought it." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "Ninety seconds a day. By year's end it'll be half a day behind Vellmarsh."

"Good," Kestrel said. "Let it fall further behind. Let it lose a whole day eventually, and a week, and live in its own time entirely — a time where the orchard still stands, and the letter was never written, and we're only two people who met under a tree."

Outside, the first snow of the season was beginning to come down over Ferrow's Bend, settling on the barrel and the sleeping sapling, on the bright new sign, on the town that had taken them in without asking their names. Inside, the small clock ticked its slow, deliberate, faithful lie-and-truth, and neither of them moved for a long while, standing in the warmth, keeping time the only way that mattered now — together, and slightly behind.

In the spring they would plant the sapling. In the spring, Odile said, they would find out whether a tree transplanted from burning ground could take root in kinder soil.

Kestrel believed it would. He had, after all, seen stranger things survive.
