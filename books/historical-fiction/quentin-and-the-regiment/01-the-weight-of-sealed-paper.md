## 1. The Weight of Sealed Paper

The snow began before dawn and did not stop, and by the time Casper Voss reached the Ministry of Foreign Affairs the steps had gone white and the city had gone quiet in the particular way a capital goes quiet when it is pretending nothing is wrong.

She stood at the bottom of the steps for a moment, letting the snow gather on her shoulders. Her coat was grey — ministry grey, courier grey, the grey of things meant to be overlooked. It was the best garment she owned and the most useful: a woman in grey with a satchel could walk through any door in Veldenmark's capital if her posture said she belonged there. Posture was half the trade. The other half was knowing which doors were watched and which clerks took bribes and which corridors smelled, faintly, of fear.

She climbed the steps. At the top, a soldier in a wet greatcoat checked her papers without reading them — she made sure he didn't read them; you handed over documents at an angle, already moving, so that checking them felt like more trouble than waving you through. He waved her through. Everyone waved everyone through now. Vigilance cost warmth, and warmth was scarce.

Inside, the marble hall echoed differently than it had in autumn. Casper noticed these things the way other people noticed weather. In autumn the hall had sounded busy — boots, voices, the slap of folders on counters. Now the sound went up into the high ceiling and came back thin, as though the building itself were holding less inside it. Half the lamps weren't lit. A porter she knew by face — old Marn, who had carried dispatch cases since before the war — was stacking sandbags against a side entrance that had no business needing sandbags.

"Morning, missus," Marn said, which was wrong twice: she wasn't married and he'd called her *miss* for three years. She let it go. People's vocabularies were slipping all over the capital, like ice underfoot.

"Morning, Marn. Cold enough for you?"

"Cold enough," he said, and then, lower, not looking up from his sandbags: "They're clearing the west archive again. Third time this month."

Casper kept walking. Clearing archives meant burning or boxing, and boxing meant crates, and crates meant ships, and ships meant the thing nobody above the level of porters would say aloud: that the Republic of Velden was packing to leave itself.

---

Her first delivery was to the Fourth Section, third floor, Room 311 — a room she thought of privately as the Room of Small Men. Not because its occupants were small, though they were, but because everything they did was small: small seals, small signatures, small decisions about other people's lives executed in small handwriting. She signed the ledger. The clerk on duty, Ferencz, a young man with chapped hands and ambitious eyes, glanced at the satchel and then at her.

"Heavy day?" he asked.

"Always heavy. That's why they pay me."

He smiled the way junior men smile when they want to be remembered by senior women. "You hear anything? In your line."

"In my line I hear paper," Casper said. "Paper doesn't talk."

"That's not what I've heard." He lowered his voice, enjoying himself. "I heard the delegation crossed the frontier Tuesday. And I heard what they were authorized to offer."

Casper signed her name — *K. Voss*, the K being for Karoline, a name she hadn't used since she was nineteen, since before Tomas was born, since before everything — and closed the ledger. "Then you know more than I do, Mr. Ferencz. Good day."

She took the stairs down rather than wait for the lift, because the lift required conversation and the stairs required only breath. In her satchel were eleven sealed dispatches. Nine were ordinary — troop movements recoded for allied legations that no longer answered promptly, budget authorizations, a protest note to a neutral power that would be filed and forgotten. Two were different. She could tell without opening them, which was fortunate, because opening them would have been treason. Different by weight, different by the stiffness of the wax, different by the way the seal had been pressed with the Foreign Minister's own signet rather than a clerk's stamp. Those two had come to her at six in the morning from a man whose hands had been shaking, and shaking hands at six in the morning meant either drink or dread, and this man did not drink.

The Republic of Velden had been dying for two years. Everyone agreed on the diagnosis and disagreed only on the date. But dying governments still generate paperwork — indeed, they generate more of it, the way a drowning man generates bubbles — and someone had to carry the paperwork, and Casper Voss was very good at carrying paperwork.

She had been good at it for six years, ever since a cousin of her dead father's had gotten her the courier post with a forged reference she had never questioned and had later, out of curiosity, tracked down and read. The reference described a Karoline Voss of unimpeachable discretion. Unimpeachable discretion. She had laughed alone in her rented room, because the truth was simpler and darker: she was discreet because she had learned at seventeen that identity was a garment, and garments can be changed quickly if the fire is coming.

---

* * *

The second delivery took her across the river, through streets where the snow lay undisturbed except for cart ruts and the tracks of dogs. The shops were open but sparse. A baker sold bread from a window guarded by iron bars, counting loaves out one at a time. A queue had formed outside a coal merchant and was not moving. Two women argued quietly over a handcart of turnips with the exhausted courtesy of people too hungry to shout. Above them, on a wall, a poster of the President — stern, paternal, printed two years ago when sternness still seemed like a plan — had begun to peel at one corner, so that he appeared to be winking.

Casper walked past it all at the pace of a woman with somewhere to be. That pace was armor. Slow walkers got stopped, questioned, helped, detained. Fast-but-purposeful walkers were part of the scenery, like mail carts and snowplows.

The Naval Ministry received her at eleven. Here the fear was better dressed. The officers wore good wool and kept their collars straight, and their secretaries typed with the crisp rhythm of women who intended to keep typing regardless of whose flag flew over the harbor. Casper delivered a dispatch to a captain named Ostermann, who broke the seal while she waited — protocol, though increasingly honored in the breach — and read it standing, and then set it down on his desk very carefully, as if it might detonate.

"Courier," he said. "This is dated the ninth. Today is the fourteenth."

"The Ninth Section holds dispatches forty-eight hours for security review, sir. Then routing takes—"

"I know the routing times." He looked at her. He had a long face and tired eyes, and behind the tiredness something moved that she recognized: the look of a professional watching his profession become irrelevant. "Five days, courier. A naval order held five days in peacetime is sloppiness. Held five days now—" He stopped himself, which told her more than finishing would have.

"I'll note the delay in my report," she said, "if you wish it noted."

"No." He almost smiled. "No, don't note it. God knows what noting it would accomplish." He signed her receipt book. His signature was steady; his thumb, where it pressed the page, left a small damp mark. "How many deliveries today?"

"Eleven."

"And how many of those would you say matter?" 

Casper considered lying, decided it would insult them both, and said, "Two."

Ostermann nodded slowly. "When I was your age I thought paper moved the world." He handed back the receipt book. "It only records which way the world was already moving. Get home warm, courier."

* * *

By midday the rumors had shapes. Rumors in the capital always had shapes — you could map them like weather fronts. This morning's front had come off the eastern ministries: the delegation had offered the frontier provinces. Rejected. Had offered the frontier provinces plus indemnities. Under consideration. Had been instructed, by persons unnamed, to accept whatever terms preserved the fleet's right to sail — meaning, everyone understood without saying, preserve the officials' right to sail.

In the tea room behind the Postal Directorate, where couriers of every ministry overlapped and gossiped with the shameless intimacy of rival soldiers, the talk ran along these rails while the samovar hissed.

"They're printing new ration cards," said Brakel, an army courier with a boxer's flattened nose. "New cards. Who prints new cards in the twelfth month unless they expect to honor them?"

"The same people who sandbagged the west entrance," said Ilse, from the Telegraph Office. She stirred her tea without drinking it. "My cousin works the harbor master's office. There's a requisition for forty civilian berths. Forty. On a diplomatic vessel."

"For whom?"

Ilse looked at him over the rim of her cup. "Who do you think? For whom does anything get requisitioned in this republic? For the people who sign the requisitions."

Casper sat at the end of the table with her tea untouched and her hands around the cup for the warmth. She had learned years ago that the safest expression in a rumor exchange was mild boredom. Boredom said *I have heard all this before*, and hearing-all-this-before was the posture of someone with nothing to hide and nothing to hope for. Hope, in a collapsing state, was a tell.

But the number went into her anyway, the way numbers did. Forty civilian berths. She thought: our family is five. She thought it the way you think arithmetic you don't want to do, sliding past it, doing it anyway. Mother. Herself. Tomas. Aunt Rilla. Little Joss. Five. Forty minus five was thirty-five for everyone else in a capital of nine hundred thousand.

"You're quiet, Voss," Brakel said.

"I'm drinking tea."

"You're staring at your tea like it owes you money."

A laugh went around the table, brief and gray. Casper gave them the smile that cost nothing. "Everything owes me money. It's December."

The door opened and the cold came in with it, and with the cold came a supervisor from the Postal Directorate calling names — routes cancelled, riders recalled, the northern line suspended until further notice. *Until further notice*: the phrase of the age. Every institution in Veldenmark had begun speaking in conditionals, as though the future were a foreign country whose visa requirements kept changing.

Casper finished her tea. It had gone cold. She drank it anyway; waste was a sin her mother had beaten into her with a wooden spoon and a lifetime of example.

---

The afternoon delivery was the one she dreaded, and she dreaded it not because of danger but because of geography. The Chancellery occupied the old ducal palace, all gilt and gravitas, and the Chancellery's Second Bureau occupied the wing where the light never quite reached. The Second Bureau handled what the ministries called *sensitive correspondence*, meaning letters written by men who would deny writing them within the year.

She was admitted at three o'clock by a secretary named Lantz, who took the satchel from her personally — irregular, but the Second Bureau was all irregularity now — and counted the dispatches twice.

"Four," Lantz said. "Where are the others?"

"Distributed per manifest. You have the receipts."

Lantz had the receipts. Lantz always had the receipts. He was a narrow man who treated bureaucracy as a religion and Casper, a woman, as a liturgical object — necessary, present, beneath comment. He checked each receipt against the manifest with a fingernail tracing the lines, and Casper stood in the green-lit office and let herself be traced, and thought about nothing, which was a skill, and thought about everything underneath the nothing: the weight of the two special dispatches, now delivered, now out of her hands; the shaking clerk at dawn; the phrase *authorized to offer*; forty berths; five souls.

"Sign here," Lantz said.

She signed. As she took back her receipt book, Lantz paused — a fractional pause, the kind that lasts half a second and changes a career — and said, without looking up:

"There's talk of a general census in the new year. House-to-house. Registration review." He blotted the page. "The Director wants clean rolls. Clean rolls make for clean transitions."

"Transitions," Casper repeated.

"Of administration." Now he looked up, and there was nothing in his face at all, which was its own kind of message. "You'll want your household registration in order, courier. All names current. All persons accounted for."

"All persons accounted for," she said. "Yes. Of course."

She walked out through the palace corridors, past portraits of dukes who had ruled this country when it was a country, and her heart was doing something quiet and terrible, keeping time like a metronome in a locked room. *All persons accounted for.* Her registration listed six members of the Voss household. Six had lived there when the registration was drawn. Five lived there now. The sixth had been dead for four years, and the dead could not be struck from rolls that no official bothered to update — which had been, at the time, merely convenient, and was now, in a season of clean rolls and clean transitions, a loaded gun lying on the kitchen table of her life.

Outside, the snow had thickened. The lamplighters were making their rounds early, their flames swallowed to halos in the falling white. Casper turned toward home along the canal, where the ice was forming in from the banks in gray lace, and let the cold press her face until her thoughts arranged themselves.

Here is what she knew, assembled from fragments, the way she assembled everything: the government was negotiating surrender terms and hiding the fact. Certain ministers were preparing to leave aboard a ship with forty civilian berths purchased — Ilse's word, *purchased*, not allotted — by persons with means. Certain dispatches crossing her shoulder contained the architecture of that departure. And a census was coming, house to house, roll by roll, and a woman named Karoline Voss who was also partly a woman named after a dead brother's paperwork — no. No, that wasn't it, that was the fatigue talking. The registration listed her brother Marek. Marek, dead four years this winter, fevered and drowned in the spring floods, buried in the parish yard at Szent-Istvan with a wooden marker because the family couldn't afford stone. Marek, still on the rolls. Still, in the eyes of the Republic of Velden, a living man of twenty-six, resident at 14 Alder Lane, tenement C, third floor.

She had kept him alive on paper for reasons that had seemed sound: an extra ration card, traded for coal; a voting certificate, sold during the last election to a man who wanted a spare; small economies, the economies of the poor, each one reasonable, each one a thread, and now the threads were being gathered up by someone in the Second Bureau who wanted clean transitions.

Casper reached the bridge over the canal and stopped, once, in the middle, where the wind came off the water and no one lingered. Downstream, the masts of the harbor showed through the snow like the ribs of something large and patient. Somewhere among them, presumably, lay a ship called — she'd find out the name — a ship with forty berths and a passenger list already bought.

She pulled her collar up. Her shoulders ached under the satchel straps, though the satchel was empty now, and she understood that the ache was not from leather. It was the weight of sealed paper, which is heavier than lead, because lead weighs the body only, and sealed paper weighs everything you love.

At 14 Alder Lane, the stairwell smelled of cabbage and stove smoke, and on the third floor her mother was waiting with supper and a question, and her mother's eyes went first to the empty satchel and then to Casper's face, and whatever they found there made her turn back to the stove without asking.

Later — much later, with the household asleep and the frost ferning up the window glass — Casper took out the household registration booklet from its tin box under the floorboard, and opened it to the second page, and read the six names by candlelight. Her finger stopped on the fourth line.

*Marek Voss. Born 14 March. Occupation: dockhand.*

Alive on paper. Dead in the ground. And somewhere between those two conditions, she suspected, lay the crack through which the whole winter would enter.

She put the booklet away, and blew out the candle, and lay listening to the snow against the glass, and did not sleep — because sleep, like hope, was a tell, and tomorrow she would carry more paper through a city that was learning how to die politely.

What she could not know — what no one at 14 Alder Lane could know — was that the census was not coming in the new year at all.

It was coming Thursday.
