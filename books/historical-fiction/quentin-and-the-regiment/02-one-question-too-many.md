## 2. One Question Too Many

The knock came at eleven minutes past four on a Thursday afternoon, and Casper Voss knew before she opened the door that it would ruin her.

Not because of the knock itself — knocks were common enough in the Marrow District tenements, where landlords collected rent with fists and neighbors borrowed salt with both hands. It was the rhythm that told her. Three measured raps, evenly spaced, patient as a clerk waiting for ink to dry. Rent collectors pounded. Drunks scratched. The Ministry knocked like this: politely, precisely, and only when they already knew what they wanted.

She had been sitting by the window in her courier's grey coat because the stove had gone cold again and the coat was the warmest thing she owned. On her lap lay the satchel, empty now, its morning deliveries made — three sealed dispatches to the Ministry of Ways, one to the Admiralty annex, one small grey envelope to a house on Lantern Street where no ministry existed but where the lights burned late and the curtains never moved. Her feet ached from six miles of snow. She had been doing nothing so dangerous as resting.

The knock came again. Same rhythm.

Casper set the satchel under the floorboard beside the loose brick — two seconds' work, drilled into her hands over four years — and stood. Through the frosted pane she could see only a shape: a man's shape, tall, still, hatless. No escort. That was either good or very bad; regime men traveled in pairs the way wolves did, for confidence.

She opened the door on the chain first. An old habit from a woman who had died once already and preferred not to repeat the experience.

"Good afternoon." The man on the landing held up a leather folio stamped with the seal of the Census Bureau — the grey-and-white crosshatch that every citizen of Velden knew the way they knew the smell of coal smoke. "Quentin Hale. Assistant registrar, Fourth Ward. I'm completing door-to-door verification of the winter rolls." His voice was quiet, unhurried, pitched to carry exactly as far as the hallway and no farther. He had the kind of face that was easy to forget five minutes after seeing it — brown hair going grey at the temples, spectacles with plain wire frames, a small scar through one eyebrow that might have been a childhood fall or might have been something else entirely. "May I come in? It won't take ten minutes."

"It's cold out," Casper said, which was neither yes nor no.

"It is." He glanced down the stairwell behind him, then back. "I have eleven more doors on this floor."

She unhooked the chain. Later she would ask herself why — whether it was exhaustion, or the arithmetic of refusal (a closed door to a census officer was a flag planted in a file), or simply that he looked tired in the way honest men look tired, and she wanted, just once, to believe in an honest man. But in the moment there was no decision at all. There was only the door opening, and the cold coming in off his coat like a tide.

* * *

Her rooms were one room, strictly speaking, plus a closet she called a kitchen because the stove lived there. She cleared a chair of mending and gestured him to it. He sat the way clerks sit, folio squared on his knees, and produced a pencil sharpened to a needle point.

"Name?"

"You have it there," Casper said. "On your form."

"I do. I'd like to hear you say it."

"Casper Voss."

He wrote nothing. He was looking at the form, not at her, and his silence stretched just long enough to become a question. "Occupation?"

"Courier. Ministry of State Communications."

"Employed continuously since—"

"Four years this spring."

"Residence at this address since—"

"Three years."

He nodded slowly, as though each answer were a coin he was biting. Then he turned the folio around and laid it on his knee so she could see it, which census men were not required to do, and tapped the line with his pencil.

**VOSS, CASPER. b. 8th of Thawmonth, 1901. Siblings: VOSS, DANILO. b. 1896. Residence: same. Occupation: dockhand.**

"Your brother," Quentin Hale said. "Danilo. Is he at home? I should verify him separately if he is — bureau regulation, same household, separate interview."

The room went very quiet.

It was the kind of quiet that has weight, that presses on the ears. Casper heard the stove ticking as it cooled, heard someone two floors down dragging a chair, heard her own heart do something strange and slow — not racing, worse than racing, a heavy deliberate thud like a fist against a locked door. *Knock. Knock. Knock.*

Danilo had been dead for seven years. Fever, the winter the ice broke early and the river took half the dockworkers with it. She had been nineteen. She had held his hand in the charity ward while a priest who smelled of cheap wine read words over him, and afterward she had walked home through the slush carrying his boots because they were nearly new and grief makes practical people of us all.

And eight months later, hungry and undocumented and seventeen years old in a city that had begun demanding papers for everything — bread, coal, a bed, a grave — she had done the thing that desperate people do. Danilo was registered. Danilo was real on paper. And Danilo, being dead, would never contradict a single line written in his name. She had taken his identity the way you take a coat off a body: reverently, guiltily, because the cold demanded it. Casper Voss, age sixteen, ceased to exist in any ledger in Velden. Danilo Voss became a young woman with careful handwriting and a talent for running fast through crowded streets without looking hurried.

Four years of couriers' work built on a dead man's name. One word from this tired clerk with his needle-pointed pencil and it would all come down — not prison, not even that, just erasure, deletion, the loss of the ration card and the lodging permit and the courier's credential that was the only reason anyone in the Ministry spoke to her civilly. Erased people couldn't testify. Erased people couldn't be paid. Erased people could be *anything done to them*, and the paperwork would show nothing had happened at all.

She had rehearsed for this moment for seven years. She discovered, sitting across from Quentin Hale, that rehearsal was worthless.

"He works late shifts," she heard herself say. Her voice came out level. Some animal part of her had taken over the speaking and she was grateful for it. "Mostly nights, at the grain piers."

"Ah." Hale made a small note. "Then I'll try again tomorrow evening."

"There's no need—" Too fast. She caught herself, softened it. "He sleeps days. He doesn't like being woken. You know how dockhands are."

"I do," Hale said mildly. "My father was one." He turned the folio back toward himself and read the entry again, lips moving slightly, and Casper understood with perfect clarity that she was watching a man decide something. Every detail of her life hung in the balance of whatever moved behind those wire-rimmed spectacles. If he asked for the brother's birthmark, his regimental exemption, the name of his schoolmaster, the scar on his chin from the hook-chain accident of 1919 — she could answer some of it, she had built Danilo a whole biography over the years the way other people build model ships — but not all of it, never all of it, and a census man with a suspicious mind needed only one splinter to pull the whole plank loose.

Outside the window, snow was starting again. Big slow flakes, the kind that meant it would settle and stay. Somewhere across the rooftops the ministries were lighting their lamps, and somewhere in those ministries men whose names she carried sewn into envelopes were deciding how much of Velden could be sold before the buyers lost interest, and here in this room a tired clerk was going to write one line, and the line would either be a door or a wall.

Hale took off his spectacles, breathed on them, polished them with a handkerchief that had seen better years, and put them back on. All of it, Casper realized, was stalling — his own version of her rehearsed answers. He was nervous too. That fact arrived in her mind and refused to leave. Why would a census man be nervous?

"Miss Voss," he said. "Do you know what happens to households flagged for irregular registration?"

"No," she said truthfully.

"Neither does anyone else," he said. "That's rather the point. The Bureau sends the flag to the Bureau of Internal Consistency, and Internal Consistency sends it wherever flags go, and nobody who files the flag ever learns what happened to the family at the end of it. We're taught to think of it as forwarding mail." He looked at her directly then, and his eyes behind the spectacles were not the eyes of a routine inspector. They were the eyes of a man standing on a ledge and measuring the drop. "You understand me?"

"I don't," said Casper, who understood him completely.

"Mm." He bent over the folio.

The pencil hovered. Casper watched the shadow of the nib on the paper, a small dark blade trembling between two futures, and found that she could not breathe around the pressure in her chest. Seven years of care — the forged signature practiced until it matched Danilo's schoolboy scrawl, the story maintained even in her sleep, the way she signed forms left-handed on Tuesdays because Danilo had been left-handed and she had decided, absurdly, that consistency was safety — all of it balanced on a graphite point above a single printed line.

*Verified,* would end it. *Discrepancy,* would begin it.

The pencil touched paper.

Hale wrote one word. Then he blotted it with the heel of his hand, squared the folio, stood up, and buttoned his coat with the brisk economy of a man whose day was half done and entirely ordinary.

"All in order," he said. "Thank you for your time, Miss Voss. Mind the ice on the north stairs — a woman fell there Tuesday and broke her wrist. The Bureau takes no responsibility, naturally, but I'd hate to hear of it."

He paused at the door. For a moment he seemed about to say something more — his mouth opened, closed — and then he simply nodded, once, the way one traveler nods to another on a night road, and was gone. His footsteps descended evenly, unhurried, and the street door below opened and shut, and the cold draft he'd let in finally began to die in the corners of the room.

* * *

Casper sat down on the bed and discovered her hands were shaking.

She held them out and watched them — watched them as though they belonged to a stranger, which in a sense they did, since they belonged to Danilo Voss, dockhand, born 1896, alive and well and working late shifts at the grain piers. The shaking lasted a full minute and then stopped as abruptly as it had begun, leaving her hollow and ringing.

She crossed to the window. Below, on the trampled snow of the Marrow Street, a hatless figure in a dark coat was walking away toward the river, folio tucked under his arm, pausing once to help a child who had fallen — righting her, brushing off her knees, sending her on. Nothing about the gesture was remarkable. Everything about it was.

He had marked it verified. She hadn't seen the word, but she knew. A man planning to destroy you does not tell you to mind the ice.

Why?

That was the question she carried with her for the rest of the evening, through the cold supper of bread and dripping, through the hour she spent re-reading yesterday's dispatch log by candlelight looking for anything that might connect her to trouble — a misdirected envelope, a name repeated where it shouldn't be. Nothing. Her work was clean. So the visit had been what it claimed: a routine sweep of the winter rolls, door to door, ward by ward, the collapsing state counting its citizens the way a gambler counts the chips he has left.

Except that census sweeps didn't happen in the Marrow District in the last week of Thawmonth, when the roads were snowed under and half the registrars had already fled south with their salaries. Except that census men didn't show you the form. Didn't stall. Didn't polish their spectacles with shaking hands.

He had known. She kept circling back to it, turning it like a stone in her palm. Somewhere between the third knock and the word *verified*, Quentin Hale had looked at the dead man's name on his own government's form and chosen — deliberately, at risk to himself, for reasons she could not begin to guess — to lie to his own ledger.

People didn't do that. People reported, complied, kept their heads down. Casper had spent seven years betting her life on the certainty that everyone kept their heads down.

She blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the snow settle on the roof, and thought: *I owe a debt I can't account for, to a man I can't explain.* And beneath that thought, quieter, colder, the thought that had been growing in her all winter like frost on glass:

*If a census clerk can lie for a stranger, then maybe the republic isn't as finished as the men selling it believe.*

Tomorrow she would find the tea house where the Fourth Ward clerks took their midday meal. Tomorrow she would learn why Quentin Hale had spent a word on her that wasn't his to spend.

Tonight, for the first time in seven years, she slept as Casper Voss — and woke, an hour before dawn, with her heart hammering, certain she had heard three knocks at the door.
