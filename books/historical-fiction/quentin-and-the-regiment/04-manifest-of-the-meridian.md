## 4. Manifest of the Meridian

The rumor arrived before the paper did, as rumors always did in the ministries — carried on the same breath that carried tea down corridors, passed between clerks who had learned to speak in half-sentences because full ones could be quoted back at you.

*The Meridian is being fitted out.*

Casper heard it first from a junior attaché in the Foreign Office lobby, a boy named Pell who still believed the republic would survive and therefore said dangerous things without lowering his voice. "They're pulling her out of ordinary service," he said, watching her sign for a dispatch pouch she had no intention of opening. "Diplomatic registry. Coaling through the week. They say —" he glanced toward the porter's desk — "they say she sails within the month."

"For where?"

He gave her a look that was almost pity. "Away, Voss. Away is the destination."

She signed her borrowed name — *Corin Voss*, her dead brother's name, written so smoothly now that her hand no longer hesitated at the letters — and took the pouch upstairs to the cipher room, where a man with grey sideburns unlocked it with a key he wore on a ribbon like a medal, and where the word *Meridian* appeared on exactly none of the official traffic.

That was the tell. Casper had spent three years learning what the silence of a ministry meant. Paper announced itself when it wanted witnesses; it went quiet when it wanted privacy. The Meridian was being coaled, provisioned, and crewed in a silence you could have wrapped around a coffin, which meant she was not carrying cargo or orders.

She was carrying people. The right people.

* * *

She confirmed it three days later, in the way she confirmed most things: by being invisible in a grey coat while men who never saw couriers talked over her head.

The berth office of the Admiralty Annex occupied a narrow room on the second floor, and its door was propped open against the heat of the stoves, and inside it two officials — a undersecretary whose name she knew only as Brant, and a naval paymaster she didn't know at all — were conducting an argument they clearly believed was private, in the tone of men who had stopped believing anyone was listening anymore.

"Forty," the paymaster said. "Forty civilian places and not one more. The Ambassador's party takes eighteen cabins as it stands. The military attaches want twelve. You can do the sum yourself."

"I've done the sum," Brant said. "The sum is why I'm here. There are ministers with families—"

"There are ministers with *money*," the paymaster said, "and there is a manifest with forty lines on it, and I will tell you how those forty lines get filled, Undersecretary, because you seem to think it involves merit." He laughed, a short ugly sound. "It involves bank drafts. It involves gold sovereigns sewn into coat linings, if you'll forgive the image. The list is drawn up by Thursday and the price per berth starts where your conscience ends."

"And the rest of the city?"

"The rest of the city has legs," said the paymaster. "Let them walk."

Casper stood at the end of the corridor with a sealed pouch under her arm and her heart going like a fist on a locked door. She did not move. She did not look up. She had learned that the safest place to overhear a thing was directly beneath the ears of the men saying it, because their eyes slid past a courier the way they slid past furniture.

Forty berths. Forty lines on a manifest, filled by gold.

Her family was five.

* * *

She did the arithmetic that night at the kitchen table while her mother slept in the next room and the stove ticked itself cold, and the arithmetic refused, every time, to come out any way but one.

Five Vosses alive in the capital. Her mother, Marta, sixty-one years old, hands ruined by thirty years of needlework, who had buried a husband and a son and kept sewing anyway because sewing was the only prayer she trusted. Her younger brother Tomas, nineteen, who worked the grain warehouses and came home smelling of dust and river water, all elbows and appetite and jokes aimed at keeping the rest of them from thinking. Her sister Lise, twenty-three, who took in mending and read forbidden newspapers and asked questions Casper couldn't answer without lying. And Nell — no. Not Nell. Nell belonged to another ledger entirely; Casper had met her only once, briefly, a thin child trailing Quentin Hale through the market, and she put the thought of her aside with an effort, because this night the arithmetic was cruel enough without borrowing other people's.

Four, then. Mother, Tomas, Lise, and herself. Four lives, and forty seats on a ship sailing away from a city that was going to be given away like a bad debt.

One berth cost more than her mother would earn in eleven years at the needle. Casper knew the exchange rates of desperation the way she knew cipher keys; she'd watched the black market all winter, tracked the price of exit visas the way farmers track weather. A berth on the Meridian would not be priced in crowns. It would be priced in whatever a man had left to sell — his house, his silver, his daughter's dowry, his silence. The paymaster hadn't been joking about coat linings.

And even if the money existed — even if it fell from the sky in a sack marked *Voss* — there remained the problem of the list itself. Forty lines, drawn up by Thursday, reviewed by men whose names Casper carried every day in sealed dispatches she was not permitted to read. The names on that list would be names the government owed favors to. Names that had bought their way aboard. Not seamstresses. Not warehouse boys. Not mending-girls with subversive reading habits.

A courier's salary could buy bread, coal, and one-third of nothing.

She sat at the table until the candle drowned in its own wax, moving numbers around like pieces on a board where every move lost. Sell everything, raise perhaps enough for one berth, maybe one and a fraction if the price softened — and then choose. Choose which of them went and which stayed in a city that would fall, that was already falling, that the men in the Admiralty Annex discussed with the briskness of clerks closing an account.

Choose between her mother and her brother. Between her brother and her sister. Between Lise's fierce unreadable future and Tomas's loud hungry one.

There was no version of the sum that ended well. She checked. She checked four times, the way she double-checked a seal, the way she counted her steps on delivery routes. The answer held: love, measured in seats, came up short.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark and let herself, for exactly one minute by the kitchen clock, feel what she felt. Then she stood, banked the stove, and went to bed, because tomorrow she had dispatches to carry and a family to feed and a problem that was not going to solve itself with sitting.

* * *

The problem got worse on Tuesday, because problems in the Republic of Velden had learned to multiply.

She was delivering to the Harbor Commission — a low brick building that smelled of tar and rotting pilings — when she saw the notice posted on the public board beside the entrance. It was typed on good paper, which told her everything: the government only spent good paper on things it wanted believed.

*By order of the Provisional Committee for National Continuity:* it began, in language so bloodless it might have been about drainage. *All maritime departures from the capital are suspended except by diplomatic charter. Civilian applications for passage will be received at the Office of Emigration, Third Pier, between the hours of ten and two, and will be adjudicated according to national priority.*

*Adjudicated according to national priority.* Casper read the phrase twice and translated it without effort, because she had spent years translating the regime's dialect. It meant: we will decide who deserves to live elsewhere, and the criteria will be loyalty, usefulness, and money, in whatever order serves us that morning.

A queue had already formed at the Third Pier office, though the hours were long past. She counted them as she passed — a habit, counting exits, counting faces, counting threats — forty, fifty souls standing in the sleet with folders of documents clutched to their chests like children. An old man in a fur hat too fine for him. A woman with twins strapped to her back, swaying slightly, endlessly, the way mothers sway when they've forgotten they're doing it. A clerk-type with ink stains and the particular hunch of a man who had spent his life making other people's paperwork true and now needed some made true for himself.

None of them would get a berth. Casper knew it the way she knew the weight of a sealed pouch: absolutely, and without opening anything. The queue was theater. The Office of Emigration existed so that the government could say, afterward, in whatever neutral country hosted its exile, *there was a process*. The forty lines on the Meridian's manifest were already spoken for, most of them by men whose signatures she ferried daily in envelopes she'd have burned unread if burning them wouldn't hang her.

But the woman with the twins — Casper found herself looking back at her from across the street, watching her rock and rock in the sleet — the woman didn't know. She was standing in line because standing in line was the last lawful act available to her, and she was performing it with her whole body, with faith.

Casper walked on. The sleet found the gap between her collar and her cap and ran a cold finger down her spine, and she thought: *this is what it costs to know things.* Not the danger. The danger she'd made her peace with years ago; fear was a workmate, you gave it a corner of your mind and it kept the rest of you sharp. What cost was this — walking past queues of the doomed, knowing precisely how doomed, carrying the proof in sealed envelopes and being unable to say a word to a single one of them.

Secrecy, she thought, is just cruelty with good manners.

* * *

Thursday, the manifest was drawn up, and by Friday afternoon Casper had seen it.

Not officially. Officially it lived in a safe in the Harbor Commission, and the safe's combination lived in the paymaster's head. But a duplicate routing slip — the kind of slip that crossed her desk because nobody, ever, in the whole history of bureaucracies, had successfully kept a courier from seeing what crossed her desk — a duplicate slip came up to the cipher room for filing, and the cipher clerk was at his lunch, and the slip lay face-up on the tray for perhaps ninety seconds while Casper waited with a pouch she had already delivered.

Ninety seconds was a career. Ninety seconds was a novel.

She read it the way she read everything: once fast, once slow, memory pressing each line flat like flowers in a book.

*MV MERIDIAN — DIPLOMATIC CHARTER — PROVISIONAL MANIFEST.*

Eighteen cabins, Ambassador's party. Twelve, military attaches. Six, ministerial staff. And then the block that mattered, the block she'd known was coming and still felt in her teeth:

*CIVILIAN BERTHS (BY SPECIAL ALLOCATION): 40.*

Below it, forty names. She scanned them with the speed of a woman who had trained her eyes on ciphers, and the names were exactly what the paymaster had promised: directors, deputies, a judge, two editors, a bishop, a man whose family owned half the southern rail lines, and — her breath caught, held, released — three names she recognized from dispatch headers, men whose correspondence she had carried for months without knowing what it contained. Men whose correspondence, she now understood, had probably purchased their own berths and several of their friends'.

No Voss. No Marta the seamstress, no Tomas of the grain warehouses, no Lise with her forbidden newspapers. Of course not. The manifest was a mirror, and mirrors don't show what's behind them — only who paid to stand in front.

She filed the slip, took the next pouch, walked out into the grey afternoon with her face arranged into courier neutrality, and did not break stride until she reached the canal bridge, where she stopped and gripped the iron railing and looked down at the black water and let the truth arrive all the way.

The ship was real. The ship was leaving. And it was full.

Full of the men who had sold the defense contracts — she didn't know that yet, not as knowledge, but she knew it the way you know a shape through a curtain, from the weight and the outline of everything she carried — full of the men who had negotiated the surrender while signing the mobilization orders, full of judges who had ruled for the regime and editors who had praised it and a bishop who had blessed it. Full of the authors of the fall, sailing away from the consequences.

And her mother, who had sewn burial shrouds all winter because someone in this city had to prepare for what the government refused to admit — her mother was not on the list. Her brother, who joked at dinner so the rest of them could eat. Her sister, who read the truth in secret because the truth was banned and she loved it anyway.

Forty seats. Five of hers. Zero.

Casper stood on the bridge until the cold climbed through her bootsoles, and then something shifted in her — not warmth, nothing so kind, but a hardening, a settling, the way snowpack settles into something you can walk on. Grief was a luxury rationed by time. She had dispatches at six. She had a family at eight. And somewhere between the two, she was going to have to find a way to make forty seats into five, or five into one, or one into a miracle, and she was going to do it outside every law the men on that manifest had written, because their laws were the reason the seats were full.

She pushed off the railing. Then she stopped.

Because there was a second document in Friday's routing — a small one, trivial, the kind of thing that ordinarily wouldn't have snagged her attention at all — and only now, standing on the bridge with the manifest printed behind her eyes, did its meaning unfold. A circular from the Census Bureau, addressed to all district registrars. *In view of anticipated population movements,* it read, *all household rolls shall be subject to verification audit. Registrars are reminded that discrepancies between declared and actual residents constitute falsification under Statute 44 and will be prosecuted.*

Statute 44. Falsification. Her registration listed Corin Voss, age twenty-four, courier's brother, resident. Corin Voss had been seven years in the churchyard at Aldenmoor.

Some clerk with a pen was coming through the tenements, checking names against breathing bodies. And when he reached the third floor of her building, the arithmetic of her life — the arithmetic she had guarded for seven years — was going to be laid open on a table under a lamp.

She pulled her collar up against the sleet and started for home, faster now, her mind running ahead of her feet down the darkening streets.

The ship was full. The audit was coming. And Casper Voss — who did not exist, who had never existed, whose every legal trace belonged to a dead boy — had less time than she'd thought, and fewer options than she'd feared, and one new and terrible understanding settling over her like the snow:

Before she could fight for a seat on the Meridian, she was going to have to survive a stranger's question at her own door.
