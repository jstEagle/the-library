## 5. The Ministry of Last Things

The Ministry of Last Things had once been the Ministry of Vital Statistics. No one had ever announced the change. The sign above the door was simply replaced one morning in October, and by November the clerks had stopped saying *vital* altogether, because nothing about their work felt vital anymore. They registered the dead now, and little else. Births had fallen off a cliff — no one brought a child into Velden in its final winter unless they were cruel or careless — and marriages had slowed to a trickle of soldiers with forty-eight-hour leave and a superstition that paper could protect them.

Quentin Hale sat on the third floor in a room that smelled of cold ink and colder radiators, and he stamped death certificates for six hours a day.

He had been a census man for eleven years. He knew how to read a population the way a doctor reads a pulse: the districts where numbers swelled, the districts where they thinned, the quiet arithmetic of who was leaving and who was being left. Now the state asked him only to count what it lost. The forms came up from the district offices in bundles tied with twine, and each one required his initials beside the registrar's mark, confirming that the deceased was deceased, that the name matched the record, that the date of death was not, as happened more often than anyone said aloud, invented to free up a ration card or erase a deserter.

His stamp was brass and worn smooth on the handle. He pressed it into the corner of each certificate with a small, deliberate force — *thunk* — and moved the form to the finished pile, and took the next.

Thunk. A weaver from the Tannery Quarter, fifty-one, inflammation of the lungs.
Thunk. An infant, unnamed, seven days.
Thunk. A man listed as *cause withheld*, which meant shot, which meant the family would never say so, which meant Quentin should not ask.

By the second week of December the bundles grew thicker. The influenza had come early and hard to the refugee quarters south of the river, and the ministry had requisitioned every available clerk to keep pace. Even the marriage registry staff were pulled down to help. The room filled with temporary workers — retired schoolteachers, a customs officer with gout, two young women from the typing pool who had never seen a death certificate before and flinched at the first dozen. Quentin found himself training them without being asked, showing them how to align the stamp, how to spot a forged date, how to say nothing when a certificate arrived for someone whose file showed them alive and well and recently conscripted. You stamped those too. Everyone understood this now. The paperwork of the dying state had its own physics: certain things were true only if the paper agreed, and the paper agreed only if you did not look too closely.

It was the third week, on a grey Tuesday when the snow came down so thick the windows turned to felt, that the order arrived.

It came up from the Office of Transport Liaison, which occupied the floor below and dealt with — among other collapsing concerns — the movement of persons out of the capital. The bundle was misdelivered; Quentin saw that immediately. It was addressed to Deputy Registrar Ohlm, who had retired in November and whose desk now belonged to nobody, and someone in the dispatch room had sent it upstairs out of habit rather than read the routing slip. Quentin might have sent it back down. He had spent his whole career sending things back down, correcting, routing, maintaining the tidy circulatory system of bureaucracy. But the top sheet had slipped loose in transit, and it lay face-up on his blotter, and he read the first line before he understood what he was reading.

**MANIFEST SUPPLEMENT — S.S. MERIDIAN — CIVILIAN ALLOCATION — RESTRICTED.**

The Meridian. Everyone in the city knew the name by now, though most pretended not to. She was a diplomatic packet steamer, white-hulled, fast, registered under a neutral flag, and she was to be the last vessel of her kind permitted out of the harbor before the blockade closed entirely. Forty civilian berths. That number had circulated through the tea houses and ration lines like a fever: forty seats on the last ship, for a city of four hundred thousand. People spoke of the Meridian the way drowning men speak of a plank — with contempt, with longing, with elaborate indifference that fooled no one.

Quentin should have set the page down. He told himself, later, that he meant to. But his eyes went on, the way eyes do, and the loose sheet beneath it — a continuation page, columns ruled in a clerk's careful hand — resolved itself into names.

There were forty-one entries. The first column held surnames he recognized from the newspapers: deputy ministers, directors-general, a judge whose signature appeared on half the treason trials of the past three years. The second column gave berth numbers, cabins, deck assignments. And the third column —

The third column was headed **CONSIDERATION**, and against nearly every name stood a figure. Not a price, exactly. Prices were printed and public. These were handwritten amounts in crowns, in gold marks, in one case a notation Quentin had to read three times: *consideration settled — 400 ha. timber rights, Krajova district.* Against another: *settled — remission of son's conscription.* Against a third, in a different ink, added after the fact: *balance outstanding.*

He sat very still. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, a door slammed and a trolley rattled across the transport office floor.

The ledger of the dying, he thought, is written in bribes.

He looked at the names again, slower now, the way he looked at a census roll when something didn't add. He was good at this — it was the one talent his life had refined, the patience to read a list until it confessed. And the list confessed readily. Berth nine: Undersecretary Dravik, whose ministry had overseen the grain requisitions of the autumn, the ones that had emptied the southern provinces. Berth twelve: Colonel-Major Fenner, whose name Quentin knew from Nell's school — the colonel-major's daughter shared a bench with her, and the tuition had gone up twice since September. Berth seventeen: a name Quentin did not know, followed by the annotation *proxy — minor children (3)*, meaning some absent great man had bought seats for his offspring while he himself stayed behind to manage the surrender, or profit from it, or both.

Forty berths. He counted the officials and their kin: thirty-three. The remaining seven were marked **OPEN ALLOCATION**, with no consideration recorded, and beside these, in the margin of the master sheet, a single line in the transport liaison's hand:

*Open berths to be assigned at discretion of boarding officer. Recommend discretion be exercised generously toward parties useful to transition.*

Useful to the transition. Quentin turned the phrase over. It meant collaborators-in-advance. Men who had already begun negotiating their usefulness to whatever authority replaced the republic, buying passage the way one buys goodwill — and the boarding officer, some captain with a neutral flag on his lapel and a pistol under his coat, would exercise his discretion generously indeed, provided the discretion flowed uphill.

*

"More coffee, Hale?" One of the typing-pool women, Marta, stood beside his desk with the ministry's tin kettle. Her sleeves were rolled and her wrists were red from the cold water in the lavatory taps.

"No. Thank you." He drew the loose sheets together, squared them, and became aware that he was holding them the way a man holds a stolen thing — slightly too tightly, slightly away from his body.

Marta glanced at the heading and made a small sound, half laugh, half cough. "The Meridian. My brother-in-law queued at the transport office for nine hours Tuesday. Nine hours, in that." She nodded at the window, where the snow erased the street. "They told him the civilian allocation was fully subscribed. Fully subscribed! As if it were a concert." She poured herself a cup, blew on it. "Subscribed by whom, I wonder."

"I couldn't say," Quentin said.

"No," Marta agreed pleasantly. "Neither could he." She went back to her desk, and Quentin understood that she had seen the third column too, or guessed it, and that her lightness was the same armor everyone in the building wore now: irony over grief, grief over rage, rage filed neatly where it could do no harm.

He worked through the afternoon. Thunk. Thunk. A ferryman, drowned, accidental. A woman of seventy-four, heart failure, Tannery Quarter. His hands performed their office while the rest of him stayed downstairs on the transport floor, standing in front of that misdelivered bundle.

Here was the thing he could not get past, the stone in his shoe all the way home: he had spent his professional life believing that records were the republic's conscience. That was why he had become a census man instead of a soldier or a merchant — because a state that counted its people honestly was a state that owed them something, and a state that kept honest books could be called to account. Every entry he had ever verified was a small promise: *you exist, you are seen, you are owed.* He had believed it even in the bad years, even when the counting served men he despised, because the alternative — a state that lied in its ledgers — was not a state at all but a protection racket with flags.

And here, on a misrouted sheet of ministry paper, was the truth of the last ship out: the books were not merely lying. They were for sale. The dying republic was auctioning its own rescue, lot by lot, berth by berth, and the auction house kept immaculate records. Consideration settled. Balance outstanding. Timber rights in Krajova.

He thought of Casper Voss.

He had not stopped thinking of her since the tenement — since the moment he had looked at her registration, seen the dead brother wearing her household like an old coat, and written *verified* in his neat clerk's hand. He had done it without deciding to. That was the part that still unsettled him. Eleven years of accuracy, and in one breath he had committed a falsification, and it had felt less like corruption than like recognition. Two people fluent, as he'd put it to himself that night, in the forgery of the soul.

Now he wondered what she was forging her way toward. A woman who borrowed her dead brother's identity in the final winter of a dying republic was not doing it for rations. Rations were small. Whatever she carried was large, and the state's paper — the same paper he stamped, the same paper now listing timber rights against a cabin on the Meridian — was hunting her kind.

*

He took the bundle home.

He did not decide to take it home either; that seemed to be the season for decisions that arrived already made. At five o'clock he squared the misdelivered pages, slipped them inside a death-certificate folder — the one place no inspector looks, because no inspector wants to linger among the dead — and carried them out through the lobby, past the porter, who nodded, past the notice board with its new decree about hoarding, out into the snow.

Nell met him at the door of the flat with the particular gravity of a nine-year-old who has been managing the household's coal supply and wishes it acknowledged. She had laid the table, badly, and heated the remains of a barley soup, and she watched him take off his coat with the narrowed eyes of an auditor.

"You're late."

"The trams stopped running. Snow on the wires." He hung the coat, keeping the folder flat under his arm. "You ate?"

"Some. There's yours." She ladled it out, overshot the bowl, mopped the spill with her sleeve, and then, because she had inherited her mother's gift for reading a room and her uncle's for reading silence, she said: "Bad day at the dying place?"

"We don't call it that."

"That's what Papa called it. Before." Her father had worked in the same ministry, in births, of all things — the bright floor, the ground floor with the windows — until the fever took him in the spring, and Nell along with everything else had become Quentin's. "You have your face on. The one where your mouth says fine."

Quentin sat down. The soup was thin and good. Outside, the snow kept coming down with the patience of something that intended to bury the whole year.

"Nellie," he said, "if a thing is wrong, and it's written down — properly written down, signed, official — is it still wrong?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave to sums. "Yes. Obviously. Writing it down just means they know it's wrong."

"And if everyone knows it's wrong, and no one can do anything?"

"Then somebody wrote it down so it could be proved later." She shrugged, the shrug of a child stating physics. "Miss Aldova says records are how the future gets its evidence."

Miss Aldova, he reflected, deserved a ministry of her own. He finished his soup, kissed the top of his niece's head — she tolerated this with dignity — and waited for her to go to bed. Then he cleared the table, set a single candle in the window's draft-shadow, and opened the folder.

The columns looked worse by candlelight. Bribes always do; flame is honest that way. He copied nothing, told himself. He was only reading. A man may read. But his eye, the census man's eye, the eye trained over eleven years to find the irregularity hiding in the regular, snagged on the seventh open berth — berth thirty-nine — and on the annotation beside it, fresh ink, different hand:

*Reserved pending payment. Inquiry via courier channels.*

Courier channels. He read it four times. It meant nothing and it meant everything: somewhere between the transport office and the harbor, the last clean berth on the Meridian was being negotiated through intermediaries, off any book he would ever stamp, and the phrase *courier channels* sat in his mind next to a memory of a woman in a grey coat whose papers named a brother six years dead.

He was not a suspicious man by nature. He was a thorough man, which in a collapsing country amounted to the same affliction. And thoroughness now assembled its findings with terrible calm: the republic was selling its escape; the sale was documented; the documents passed through rooms where he sat all day with a brass stamp; and the woman he had covered for — the stranger whose secret he carried like a coin under his tongue — might be tangled in exactly this trade, on either side of it, buyer or broker or something he hadn't imagined.

The candle guttered. Downstairs, a neighbor's radio played the anthem at low volume, as it did every night at nine, a ritual of defiance or habit, impossible to tell anymore.

Quentin folded the sheets back into the death folder, and sat in the dark with the shape of a choice pressing against him like weather. He could return the bundle tomorrow, misdelivery noted, hands clean. He could report it — to whom? To the men whose names were in the third column? He could burn it, and spend the rest of his life wondering.

Or he could do what a census man does with an anomaly. Follow it. Verify. Find out whether Casper Voss was one more name in the consideration column — or the person who could tear the column out.

He thought of her face in the tenement doorway, the terror arriving a half-second late, the control arriving a half-second after that. Nobody buys a berth with a face like that, he thought. Somebody runs from one.

In the morning he would return the bundle. Tonight, he took out a clean sheet of paper, and in his precise registrar's hand, he wrote down the only thing worth carrying out of that office: the manifest's routing mark, the liaison's initials, and the words *reserved pending payment — inquiry via courier channels* — then folded it into the lining of his hat, where a man keeps what he cannot yet afford to know he knows.

Outside, the snow went on falling on the ministries, the harbor, the white hull waiting at anchor — patient as paper, and about as innocent.
