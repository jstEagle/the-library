## 6. Mother's Hands

The shroud-workshop occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been respectable, on a street that had once been called Prosperity Row, and now answered to nothing at all. Casper smelled it before she reached the door — hot starch, tallow, and under it something sweeter and worse, the smell of new linen being cut for purposes no one said aloud.

Inside, her mother sat at the long table with six other women, all of them bent over white cloth as though in prayer. The windows had been papered over with old newspaper since autumn; the light came down yellow through the paste and print, so that everyone worked inside a kind of dim weather. A radio in the corner murmured official bulletins to an audience that had stopped listening weeks ago. The needle-women's silence was total except for the sound of scissors and the hiss of thread pulled tight.

Her mother looked up. She was fifty-four and had been a seamstress for forty of those years, and her hands did not pause when she saw Casper — they finished the stitch first, because her mother had never in her life left a thing half-done, not a hem, not an argument, not a goodbye. Then she set the work down and stood.

"You're thin," Marta Voss said.

"Good morning to you too."

"That *is* a good morning. Sit."

There was a stool by the cutting table, kept clear for visitors, which meant kept clear for Casper, which meant no one else came. The other women glanced up, nodded at Casper — the courier daughter, the one who moved through the city like weather — and went back to their cloth. One of them, old Frau Behm, smiled with most of her teeth. Casper nodded back and felt the familiar small ache: these women had known her since she was tall as the table, and every one of them would lie to an inspector about her without blinking, and none of them could be told what she carried sewn into her coat lining, because grief made people careless and these women lived inside grief like fish in water.

The workshop made burial clothes. It had always made burial clothes — Voss & daughters, once, when there had been two daughters and the sign still hung outside — but before the war the orders came one at a time, from families, with names attached and tears expected. Now the orders came by the gross. The Ministry of Civic Provision had contracted Marta Voss to supply five hundred shrouds by the first of March, and everyone in the room understood what five hundred shrouds by the first of March meant about the first of March. The city would be besieged or surrendered by then, and either way the linen would be needed.

"How is Tomas?" her mother asked, resuming her stitching. "He didn't come Sunday."

"He was Sunday. You were at Frau Behm's."

"I was at Frau Behm's until noon. He knows my hours." A pull of thread, precise. "That boy has his father's talent for arriving just after the moment matters."

"He's working more shifts at the docks. There's extra pay."

"There's always extra pay right before a thing goes wrong. That's how you know it will go wrong. They pay men to be where the trouble starts." Her mother bit the thread and tied it off. "And you? Still walking?"

"Walking."

"Still grey?"

Casper looked down at her coat. Courier's grey, government issue, cut plain so that ministers' clerks wouldn't see her coming and ministers themselves would forget her leaving. "It's warm," she said.

"It's invisible. I raised you better than to be invisible."

"You raised me to be *employed*, Mama. Invisible is employed, this winter."

Her mother made a sound that was almost a laugh, and went back to the cloth. Casper watched her hands. She had been watching those hands all her life and had never once seen them idle — not at the funeral, not during the hunger year, not the night the news came about Anselm, when Marta Voss had sat down at this very table at midnight and sewn until dawn because her hands needed a task or they would have begun tearing things. Anselm. The name sat in Casper's chest the way it always did, heavy and smooth, a stone worn round from handling. On her registration papers, in some clerk's ledger across the city, Anselm Voss was alive, twenty-six years old, resident of a tenement on Wren Street. Casper wore him like a coat. Their mother believed both her children were living, and one of them wasn't, and Casper had spent three years unable to decide which lie hurt her mother less.

She had come today to say the thing about the ship.

It sat in her throat like a swallowed coin. *Mama. There is a ship. Forty berths for civilians, and I can buy one, and I need you to help me choose.* She had rehearsed it on the walk over, three versions, each one beginning differently and each one dying at the same place: the word *choose*. Because choosing meant telling her mother the arithmetic — five of us, one seat — and telling her the arithmetic meant watching her mother do what she did with every impossible bill: lay it flat, study it, and begin cutting.

"Marta," said Frau Behm, holding up a length of linen, "this tear — mend or recut?"

"Recut. We don't mend the shrouds, Ilse. Whatever else we skimp on, the dead get whole cloth." Her mother said it without drama, the way she said everything true, and Casper felt the words land somewhere below her ribs.

*The dead get whole cloth.*

"Mama. When did you last eat properly?"

"When did you?" The counter-question arrived without delay. Her mother's eyes went over Casper the way they'd gone over every coat Casper had ever brought her — finding the worn places, the thin seams, the spots where the world had rubbed through. "You're sleeping badly. Don't argue. I can tell by the eyes. Your father slept like that the winter the mill closed, right up until—"

"Right up until he fixed it. I remember."

"Right up until he *tried*," her mother said quietly. "Fixing came later, and not from him."

* * *

They took the midday meal together in the back room, among bolts of cloth stacked like sandbags. Her mother produced bread that was mostly barley and a jar of pickled cabbage she'd been hoarding since November, and watched with open satisfaction while Casper ate, contributing nothing to her own plate beyond two bites performed for appearances. This ritual was older than the war. Casper had stopped fighting it years ago.

"Tomas says there are soldiers on the east piers now," her mother said, between stitches — she sewed even while eating, mending the elbow of Casper's coat, turning the sleeve inside out on her lap without asking permission. "Not garrison soldiers. The other kind. The ones who don't salute anyone."

"I've seen them."

"And the ministries?"

Casper hesitated. "Busy."

"Busy." Her mother tested the word, found it wanting. "In this family, *busy* means frightened and *meetings* means running, and *the couriers are moving documents at night* means—" She stopped. Looked up. For a moment the seamstress fell away and Casper saw the woman underneath, the one who had identified her husband's body at the mill gate and signed the form without shaking. "Casper. Are you in danger?"

"No."

"Liar. But thank you for making it quick."

"I'm careful."

"You were careful at seven years old, crossing the ice on the canal when I told you the pond was shallow. You calculated it. I watched you calculate it. And then you crossed anyway, because your brother dared you, and you came back soaked and proud and I learned everything I needed to know about you in one afternoon." Her mother folded the sleeve, smoothed it, began a new seam. "Careful isn't a character, love. It's just a speed."

Casper looked at her hands. The coin in her throat shifted. *Tell her,* said the part of her that had walked here rehearsing. *Tell her about the Meridian, tell her about berth thirty-nine, tell her you'll find a way, tell her anything except nothing.*

Instead she heard herself say: "I need your sewing kit. Mine lost its smallest needle."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment — long enough that Casper understood her mother heard the evasion perfectly, catalogued it, and chose, for reasons of her own, to let it stand. This was also a ritual older than the war: the two of them lying to each other gently, each pretending not to hear the other's lie, love conducted entirely in subtext like letters passed through a censor who must be satisfied.

"Second drawer," her mother said. "Take the whole tin. And while you're in there — the grey coat. Off. Let me see the shoulders."

* * *

She worked on the coat in silence while Casper ate, and Casper watched her mother's fingers move near the lining where the dispatch lay hidden, and felt something cold walk up her spine. Her mother's hands were three inches from the truth of the republic's collapse and knew it not at all. If she pressed the wrong seam — if she turned the lining out to check the wear, as she had done ten thousand times —

But of course she didn't. She sewed around the stiffened panel without remarking on it, the way she'd sewn around every hard thing in her life, incorporating it, making it neat.

"Your brother," her mother said suddenly, not looking up. "Anselm's birthday is next month."

The room got very quiet. Or perhaps it had always been quiet and only now Casper heard it.

"Yes."

"Twenty-seven. I keep thinking about what he'd be doing. Twenty-seven and useless, probably. Beautiful and useless, like his father." A stitch. Another. Her voice stayed level, conversational, a voice discussing thread counts. "Ilse asked me last week why I never visit the cemetery anymore. I told her the ground's frozen. Which is even true."

"Mama—"

"Don't." Not sharp. Just final, like a knot pulled closed. "Whatever you were going to say — don't. I know what you carry in your face when his name comes up. You've carried it three years. I let you carry it because taking it from you wouldn't lighten you, it would only empty you." She finished the seam and held the coat up to the yellow light, inspecting. "A mother learns which weights her children need. Even the wrong ones. Especially the wrong ones."

Casper's eyes stung. She blamed the cabbage.

"The coat's done," her mother said. "Shoulders reinforced, cuffs turned, and I put a second layer in the lining because you refuse to wear wool underneath like a sensible person." She handed it back, and their hands touched on the fabric, and neither of them mentioned that the fabric between them contained the evidence that could hang six ministers, because some cargoes a ship cannot declare. "Wear it in health. Come Sunday. Both of you. Tell Tomas he comes early or he eats standing."

"I will."

At the door, her mother caught her arm. Up close her eyes were pale grey, nearly colorless, and terribly clear.

"Casper. Whatever it is you're not telling me — and there's a ship in it, girl, don't look shocked, the dockworkers' wives talk, talk is the only industry thriving in this city — whatever it is, when you're ready to say it, say it plainly. No arithmetic. No protecting me. I have buried a husband and I sew burial clothes by contract and there is nothing left in this world that can frighten me except being managed." She released the arm. Picked up her needle again, already turning back to the table, already whole cloth and whole cloth and whole cloth. "Now go. Before I start crying into the linen. Salt shrinks it."

* * *

Outside, the snow had thickened. Casper stood on Prosperity Row with the mended coat warm on her shoulders and the borrowed sewing tin heavy in her pocket, and understood that she had failed at the one errand she'd set herself. She hadn't said *ship*. She hadn't said *berth*. She hadn't said *five and one*. She had come to deliver surrender terms and had instead eaten cabbage and been loved in stitchwork.

Behind the papered window, the yellow light burned on, and the needles hissed, and five hundred shrouds grew toward March.

Ahead of her, down the street, a telegraph office glowed green through the snowfall — and beside it, a man in a civilian coat was watching the shroud-shop door with the patient attention of someone counting comings and goings. He made a note in a small book. Then he looked up, directly at Casper, and did not look away.
