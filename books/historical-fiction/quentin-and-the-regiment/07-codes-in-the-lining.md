## 7. Codes in the Lining

The summons came the way all of them came now: a slip of grey paper, unsigned, tucked into the courier drop behind the pharmacy on Vell Street. *Collection at seven. The old address.* Nothing else.

Casper read it twice by the light of a shuttered lamp, then fed it to the stove in her landlady's kitchen while the kettle muttered. The paper curled, blackened, and was gone, and she stood a moment longer than she needed to, hands out toward the heat, watching the place where her instructions had been.

*The old address.*

Nobody used the old address anymore. It had been decommissioned two winters ago, back when the Ministry of Posts still believed the war would end with borders redrawn rather than the republic itself erased from the map. Back when there had still been such a thing as a routine delivery. She hadn't been to the flat above the shuttered bookbinder's on Kessel Lane since the autumn of the blockade, and the fact that her handler wanted her there — wanted her off the circuit, away from the countersigned drops and the dead-letter boxes that kept them both alive — told her something before she'd walked a single step.

Handlers did not summon couriers to old addresses for ordinary dispatches.

She dressed carefully. Grey coat, grey scarf, the courier's badge sewn flat against the inside of her collar where it could be shown or hidden in three seconds. Underneath, in her boot, the folding knife Tomas had given her two birthdays ago, back when he still laughed easily. Her mother's mended gloves. And because she could not explain even to herself why, the photograph of her brother Aldric that lived in her breast pocket, his grin half-ruined by the camera's long exposure, looking like a boy who had never heard of ministries or wars.

The snow had stopped but the cold hadn't. Velden in deep winter was a city holding its breath — smoke rising straight from ten thousand chimneys into air too frozen to move it, tram wires sagging under ice, queues outside the ration offices so long and so silent they looked like processions. On the corner by the Grand Archive, someone had pasted a handbill over the official notice board. Casper read it in passing without slowing down, the way she read everything now: *THE MERIDIAN DEPARTS WHEN THE MINISTERS ARE ABOARD AND NOT BEFORE.* Someone had torn at the corners of it. Someone else had pinned it back up.

She walked the long way, doubling twice through the fish market, checking the reflections in darkened shop glass out of habit so ingrained it had become almost restful. Nobody followed her. Or nobody good enough for her to catch.

Kessel Lane smelled of wet ash and old glue. The bookbinder's below was boarded, its windows painted over years ago, but the side door still opened if you lifted it on its hinge the way the hinge had been taught to open, and the stairs still creaked on the fourth step and not the fifth, because some things in this city held their shape even as everything else came apart.

The door at the top stood unlocked.

"You're late," said Maren Ilic, without turning around.

"I'm early," Casper said. "You just have no clock."

Maren stood at the window with its view of the chimney pots, hands clasped behind her back in the posture she used when she was about to lie about something small so that the large thing would go unnoticed. She was sixty-one years old and had run couriers through three governments, and she wore her competence the way other women wore jewellery — visibly, and with the expectation that people would comment on it. Tonight, though, the jewellery seemed tarnished. There were new lines around her mouth, or perhaps they were only shadows from the single candle.

"Sit," Maren said. "No. Stand. You'll want your coat on."

That, too, was an answer to a question Casper hadn't asked yet.

Maren crossed the room to the table where a sewing kit lay open — her mother had owned a kit exactly like it, brass thimble gone dull with use, thread wound on cardboard slips gone soft at the edges — and lifted from it a folded rectangle of heavy cream-coloured paper, sealed twice, wax stamped with the double eagle of the Foreign Ministry.

"This goes to Ambassador Reval, at the Legation House, tomorrow at eleven. Not tonight. Tomorrow." She set it down between them like a card played face-up. "And it doesn't go in the dispatch case."

Casper looked at the envelope. Then at the sewing kit. "In the lining."

"In the lining." Maren drew a breath, and Casper watched her draw it, watched the effort of it, and understood that what came next had cost her something to decide. "Left breast panel, under the flap where the seam is doubled. You'll carry it against your body. If anyone takes the coat from you, they'll find the standard courier papers in the pockets and nothing else, unless they take every stitch apart, and by then you'll be somewhere else."

"Whose orders?"

"The Chancellor's office."

"Which chancellor?" Because everyone in Velden knew there were two now — the one who signed things in the morning and the faction that unsigned them by evening, the government negotiating its own surrender in three rooms while pretending in a fourth that no negotiation existed.

Maren's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "That's the point, girl. That's precisely the question it exists to answer."

Casper didn't touch the envelope. "Say it plainly. You know I hate it when you do the oracle act."

"The oracle act has kept us both breathing for six years." But Maren sat down then, which she almost never did in front of a courier, and poured two glasses of the plum brandy she kept in the bookcase disguised as a volume of trade statistics. "All right. Plainly. This is my last collection. Mine and yours both."

The room went very quiet. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog barked twice and gave up, as if even the dogs were conserving their strength for whatever was coming.

"After tomorrow you're done," Maren said. "Paid, cleared, name wiped from the rolls — and don't look at me like that, I know better than anyone what the rolls are worth these days, but wiped is wiped, and you'll walk out of this with clean hands. That was the arrangement when you took the oath. Six years of service, and then your life back."

"And the republic?"

"What about it?" Maren drank. Set the glass down with a click too careful to be casual. "Casper. Listen to me, because I'm going to say this once and then we will never speak of it again, which is how everything true gets said in this country. The dispatches you've carried this winter — the ones to Reval, the ones to the naval attaché, the ones to the man at the Trade Delegation whose face you've never seen — they haven't been diplomacy for weeks. They've been logistics. Schedules. Manifests. Names of men and the order in which they mean to leave."

"I know," Casper said quietly. "I've known since the Berl Dispatch. You don't code evacuation timetables in diplomatic cipher unless—"

"Unless the diplomats are the cargo. Yes." Maren turned the envelope over once, studying the wax seal as though it might tell her something it had already told her. "Forty civilian berths on the Meridian, and I promise you not one of them goes to a widow or an orphan. So. When this arrived tonight with the Chancellor's seal and instructions that it go by body-carry, sewn in, courier's discretion on route, no receipt required—" she tapped the cream paper with one blunt fingernail "—I did what any sensible traitor would do."

Casper felt the cold come up through the floorboards. "You read it."

"I tried." For the first time all evening, Maren looked genuinely, humanly tired. "It's not our cipher. It's not any cipher I've seen, and I've seen most of them, including two that are supposed to be extinct. Which means it comes from outside the ministry structure entirely, or from a level of it that doesn't officially exist. Either way, a woman in my position should have handed it to you unexamined and gone to bed." She met Casper's eyes. "I couldn't. I've carried this government's secrets for thirty years, and in the last month every single one of them has been either a bribe or a betrayal, and I could not put another one into a young woman's coat without knowing — or without telling you that I didn't know — whether it was going to hang her."

"And does it? Hang me?"

"It depends," Maren said, "on what you do with it."

They looked at each other across the table, across the envelope, across twenty-nine years of age and a gulf of experience that had somehow, tonight, narrowed to the width of a candle flame.

"Why tell me at all?" Casper asked. "Why not burn it and give me a quiet week and let me walk away?"

Maren got up and went back to the window. From up here the city was a spread of rooftops going blue-grey with dusk, chimneys smoking, the harbor cranes visible in the far distance like the skeletons of drowned giants. Somewhere beyond them lay the roads north, the neutral border, the world after Velden.

"Because of what the seal is made of," she said.

Casper frowned. She looked at the wax properly for the first time. Double eagle, Chancellor's office — but the wax itself was wrong. Official wax was issued centrally, a dull red with grit in it, cheap and uniform. This was a deep garnet colour, smooth, expensive.

"That's private stock," Casper said slowly. "Ministers buy their own for personal correspondence. I've seen it on—"

"On letters between Minister Corvay and certain foreign parties. Yes. In the summer, you carried three of them for me, before the cipher changed. You remarked on the wax." Maren still wasn't looking at her. "A seal can be forged, of course. Anything can be forged; you and I are living proof. But the impression itself — the depth of the relief, the pressure pattern — forgery shows in the wax if you know how to look. I know how to look. That seal was made with the actual matrix. The actual stamp, Casper, from the Chancellor's own desk, pressed into private ministerial wax."

The meaning of it landed in her stomach like swallowed ice. "So whoever sent this had access to the Chancellor's desk. Or is the Chancellor. Or—"

"—or took the desk," Maren finished, "along with everything else that isn't nailed down. Governments in their last winter start eating themselves, and the first thing they eat is their own correspondence." She finally turned around. "Here is what I believe, and belief is all I can offer you. I believe this envelope contains proof of something. Not orders — orders don't get seals like this, and orders don't need a courier's body heat to keep them safe. Proof. Of the contracts, or the berths, or the surrender terms, or all three braided together. I believe somebody very high up wants this document delivered to Reval, and I believe that if it reaches Reval, it disappears forever into a legation strongbox, and the men who built this catastrophe sail away on the Meridian with their names clean."

"And the alternative?"

"The alternative is that it doesn't reach him." Maren came back to the table and stood over the envelope with her hands flat on the wood, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and then some. "I am sixty-one years old. I have no children. My sister died at Marren Field with nine hundred others whose deaths were purchased in advance by the same men who are buying their boat tickets now. I have spent my whole life believing that carrying secrets faithfully was a form of honour." She pushed the envelope an inch toward Casper. "Tomorrow, at eleven, you were going to deliver this and be free. I'm giving you the night to decide whether freedom means delivering it — or meaning to, and choosing otherwise."

"Maren—"

"Don't thank me. Don't refuse me yet, either. Take it home. Sleep on it against your ribs, the way it was meant to travel. Feel its weight." Something moved behind her eyes that might have been grief or might have been envy. "A document like this changes temperature in your hands, once you suspect what it is. I want you to know that feeling before you choose. Most people live and die without ever once holding the truth in their coat."

*

The needle bit through the doubled seam and Casper worked by feel more than sight, the candle turned low, the curtains drawn. She had learned to sew from her mother at the same table where her mother now shrouded the dying for money, and her stitches came out small and even and nearly invisible — a different inheritance than she'd imagined, but an inheritance nonetheless.

The packet slid into the left breast panel and lay flat against the lining, and she buttoned the coat and hung it on the back of the chair and looked at it for a long time.

It weighed nothing. That was the obscene part. Four sheets of cream paper and a smear of garnet wax, and it weighed less than the photograph of Aldric in her pocket, less than a roll ticket, less than anything. And yet the room arranged itself around it differently now. The chair was no longer a chair; it was a guard post. The window was no longer a window; it was a liability. She caught herself cataloguing exits, counting steps to the stairwell, estimating how fast she could pull the lining open and feed four sheets into the stove — and stopped herself, appalled, because she had already begun thinking of the burning as the second-best option rather than the last resort, and she didn't know yet which option was which.

She sat down across from the hanging coat, the way you sit with a difficult relative.

*Deliver it,* said the part of her that had taken the oath six years ago, nineteen years old and flattered that anyone trusted her with anything. *A courier delivers. That is the whole of the covenant. You carry what you are given; you don't open it, judge it, redirect it. The day couriers start deciding, nothing is safe for anyone.*

*And look what your faithful carrying has bought,* said the newer voice, the one that had grown all winter, that had read evacuation manifests coded as friendship and recognized bribes by their grammar. *Reval's strongbox. Forty berths for forty guilty men. Your family walking to the harbor to watch the ship leave without them. Aldric's name rotting on a memorial wall for a war that was sold before it started.*

She thought of Quentin Hale, the census man with the tired kind eyes who had marked her dead brother *verified* and closed his ledger without a flicker. Two people fluent in forgery of the soul, he'd called them, or something close to it, over tea that neither of them drank. He falsified records to keep a child alive. She had falsified her whole identity for six years to serve a state that was, at this very moment, sewing its escape into envelopes and selling the lifeboats.

What would he say, the honest forger, if he could see her sitting here negotiating with a coat?

She slept badly and woke at four with the candle guttered and the frost ferning the inside of the glass, and the coat was where she'd left it, patient as a debt. She rose, dressed, and put it on. The packet pressed against her breastbone, faintly warm already from the room, and she stood very still and let herself feel it — really feel it, the way Maren had told her to.

It was warm. It would stay warm, against her skin, all day. Whatever was written on those pages — schedules, sums, signatures, the arithmetic of a betrayed country — would travel at the temperature of a living body, tucked behind the ribs like the heart it was about to replace.

*Something worth more than diplomacy.* Maren had hinted it, and hinted again, and finally said it outright, and still Casper hadn't asked the only question that mattered, perhaps because she already knew the answer and feared what it would obligate her to do.

But standing in the freezing dark of her rented room, wearing the truth next to her skin, she found she could not leave the question unasked any longer. Not of Maren. Of herself.

*If it's evidence,* she thought, *evidence of the men who sold the republic and bought the berths — then delivering it isn't duty. Delivering it is complicity. And destroying it is mercy for murderers.*

Which left a third thing. A thing with no protocol, no oath, no precedent in six years of faithful service. A thing that would require a telegraph clerk who owed her a favour, or a printer who owed the revolution one, or both.

She buttoned the coat to the throat and went out into the blue hour before dawn, and the packet rode against her heart like a second pulse, and she did not walk toward the Legation House.

She walked toward the Central Telegraph Office, where a clerk named Edda Voss — no relation, though they'd laughed about the shared name until they weren't laughing anymore — had once told her, drunk on two glasses of cherry liqueur, that she could read any cipher in the ministry's register if someone brought her the traffic.

The snow began again as Casper crossed the empty square, fine and dry and endless, erasing the streets behind her one soft footfall at a time. By the time she reached the telegraph office steps, the city behind her had already forgotten she'd passed through it.

By tonight, she thought, climbing toward the yellow light in the high windows, she might know exactly what she was carrying.

And by tomorrow at eleven, when a sealed envelope was due in Ambassador Reval's hands — she would have to decide what, if anything, arrived.
