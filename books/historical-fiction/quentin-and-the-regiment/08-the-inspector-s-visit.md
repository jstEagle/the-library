## 8. The Inspector's Visit

The notice arrived on a Tuesday, which Quentin Hale would later consider the last ordinary day of his life.

It was pinned to the census office door between the schedule of collections and a faded proclamation about grain quotas — a single sheet in the regime's grey stock, stamped twice, signed once:

*By order of the Office of Internal Verification, all census rolls for Districts Nine through Fourteen will be subject to audit. Records officers will remain at their posts until relieved.*

Below the signature, a name: *Aldous Krein, Inspector Second Class.*

Quentin read it three times while the morning crowd jostled past him on the stairs. The words were bland enough to be furniture. That was what made them terrible. The regime never announced its violence in advance; it announced its *procedures*. An audit was a procedure. An audit had forms.

"Bad news?" asked Maren Oduya, who kept the birth registries two desks down and had worked beside him for eleven years without ever asking about his private life — a courtesy he had repaid by never asking about hers.

"An inspection," he said. "They're auditing the rolls."

She snorted. "Auditing dead people's paperwork. As if the war will be won or lost over whether Widow Brandt's late husband was recorded as a cooper or a carpenter." She gathered her shawl. "Let them count their empire as it burns."

But Quentin did not laugh, because Maren's district was clean, and his was not.

He thought about it all the way home through the snow — thought about it on the tram, standing among soldiers too young to shave, thought about it crossing the market square where the fishmongers had stopped selling anything that could be preserved. Eleven years of entries passed before his mind's eye like cards being turned. Births, marriages, deaths, migrations. He had always been careful. He had been *proud* of being careful, in the way of a man whose only power in life was accuracy.

And threaded through those eleven years, invisible to anyone who didn't know where to look, were nine falsified entries.

Not Casper Voss's — hers he had marked verified weeks ago and never touched again, one small mercy folded into the machine. The others were older, quieter sins. A widow listed as married so her ration category wouldn't lapse. Two families recorded at an address they'd fled from, because the address they'd fled *to* was unregistered and would have meant fines they couldn't pay. And Nell.

Nell above all.

His sister Lysa had died in the fever winter four years ago, and her daughter had come to him with a suitcase and a cough and no legal existence worth the name, because Lysa had never registered the birth — had been on the run herself, from a husband, from a debt, from something she'd never explained and Quentin had never pressed. A child without papers was a child the state would eventually collect. So Quentin had done the only thing his particular skills allowed. He had written Nell Hale into the census as his ward, born in this district, registered in this district, mother deceased, father unknown — entries dated, cross-referenced, initialed, flawless.

Flawless, he had believed, right up until a man named Krein decided to look.

* * *

The inspector arrived on Thursday, unannounced, in weather too cold for ceremony.

Quentin knew him before anyone said his name. Not from the grey coat, though the coat was right. It was the way the man stood in the doorway of the records hall — still, hands clasped behind him, head tilted slightly as though listening to the building itself — that told you he was not here to be received but to receive. He was perhaps fifty, narrow as a filing cabinet, with a face that had been arranged for patience. His eyes moved along the rows of clerks the way a man reads a ledger: top to bottom, left to right, missing nothing, judging nothing, *yet*.

"Gentlemen. Madam." His voice was dry and mild. "I am Aldous Krein. I'll be working among you for some days. Please continue exactly as you are. I require nothing but quiet and access."

The director of the office, a soft man named Pellows whose authority had survived the republic's collapse mainly because nobody had bothered to revoke it, hurried forward with the obsequiousness of the recently frightened. "Inspector, we're honored — we've prepared a room —"

"A desk is sufficient. Near the shelves." Krein's gaze settled on the archive wall, floor to ceiling, forty years of district memory bound in grey volumes. "I prefer to work where the documents can hear me."

A few clerks laughed, uncertainly. Krein did not.

He chose a desk near the death registries, which Quentin noted and then made himself stop noting, because noticing things obsessively was its own kind of confession. Krein set out his tools with the unhurried economy of a surgeon: a magnifying glass, a straightedge, ink in two colors, and a notebook bound in black oilcloth. Then he began.

He began, Quentin saw within the hour, at the beginning. Volume One. The earliest year. He read every page.

That was the first thing that frightened Quentin properly — not the magnifying glass, not the two inks, but the sheer *patience* of the man. Auditors sent before him had sampled. They'd pulled random pages, checked signatures against the register of commissions, grown bored by the third day and written reports full of satisfactory averages. Krein sampled nothing. He read everything, and he wrote in his black notebook constantly, and when he looked up from the pages his eyes went not to the documents but to the clerks — watching who glanced at him, who avoided glancing at him, whose pen hesitated mid-word.

By Friday afternoon, Quentin had developed a theory of the man: Aldous Krein did not believe errors existed. He believed only concealment. Somewhere in his long career he had learned that every irregularity in a record was a sentence written by a human hand for a human reason, and his profession was reading sentences.

Saturday, the first summons came.

"Maren Oduya." Krein pronounced it gently, almost kindly. "If you'd sit with me a moment."

Maren crossed the hall with her chin up, sat, answered questions for twenty minutes while the whole office pretended not to listen. When she returned to her desk her face was puzzled rather than pale. "He wanted to know about the Hartwell entry," she whispered to Quentin. "Twins, back in '31. One died in infancy, and I recorded both births and one death — but there was a gap in my dates, a week, because the family buried the child themselves before reporting it. He asked why the interval. He asked *why*, Hale. Who audits the interval between grief and paperwork?"

"Did you tell him?"

"I told him the truth. What else was there?"

What else indeed, Quentin thought, and felt the floor tilt under the question. Because that was the trap Krein was building, brick by patient brick: a hall where truth was the only currency, and where everyone holding false paper would eventually be invited to spend it.

* * *

Sunday the office was closed, but Quentin went in anyway. He couldn't help it. He told himself it was to get ahead of his quarterly reconciliation, and for an hour he actually did that work, checking figures against the master roll, and it calmed him the way arithmetic always had.

Then he took down Volume Nine — the current year — and found his own initials, and read his own entries as Krein would read them.

This was the exercise he set himself: to interrogate each line as a stranger. *Ward, Nell Hale, age nine. Relation: uncle, guardian.* Fine. *Born in district.* Was there a baptismal record? There was not; Lysa had distrusted churches. Was there a midwife's certificate? There was not. In a city where every institution had decayed, gaps were common enough — but common was not safe. Common was merely *plausible*, and plausibility was a judgment, and judgments belonged now to a narrow man with two colors of ink.

He checked the widow's entry. He checked the two families at the old address. Each one held, each one was defensible, each one was a thread, and threads could be pulled.

It was while he stood there, volume open, that Krein spoke behind him.

"Volume Nine," the inspector said quietly.

Quentin turned. He hadn't heard the door; the man moved like dust settling. "Inspector. The office is closed, I — quarterly reconciliation. I can leave, if you need the space."

"No need." Krein came forward and set his black notebook on the table, but did not open it. Up close he smelled of cold air and lamp oil and nothing else, a man with no vices to scent. "You're Hale. Records officer, eleven years' service."

"Yes."

"They speak well of you here." Krein studied the open volume upside down, which should have been difficult and did not seem to be. "Maren Oduya says you taught her the reconciliation method. Director Pellows says you've never once required correction." A thin pause. "Eleven years, and never corrected. That is either excellence or invisibility, Mr. Hale. In my experience the two are often the same thing."

"I try to be accurate."

"We all say so." Krein smiled for the first time, and the smile changed nothing in his eyes, which continued their slow inventory of Quentin's face. "Accuracy is the religion of this office. I respect it. But let me tell you what I've found so far, since you're here and the tea is cold everywhere else." He opened the notebook at last and turned pages with a fingertip. "In six districts, I have identified four hundred and twelve discrepancies. Do you know how many of them involve fraud? Embezzlement? Enemies of the state?"

"How many?"

"Nine." He let the number sit. "Four hundred and three of them are widows and children and clerks who mislaid a date. The state loses nothing to them. But the state does not distinguish, Mr. Hale. That is why I am employed. My superiors cannot read a record and see a widow. They see only deviation. My job is to find the deviations before someone less careful does — and then to decide, alone, at this desk, which deviations are mercy and which are crimes." He closed the notebook. "You may imagine I enjoy that."

For a moment — one unguarded moment — something moved behind the inspector's face, some fatigue too old for his position, and Quentin understood suddenly that he was not speaking to a fanatic. He was speaking to a functionary who had thought about his work more than his masters had, which was in its own way more dangerous.

"Why tell me this?" Quentin asked.

"Because you're accurate, and accurate men make the best informants, and I dislike wasting time on poor ones." Krein collected his notebook and straightened his coat. "If you observe an irregularity — in any volume, any year — you will report it to me directly. That is not a request dressed as a courtesy. It is a duty dressed as one. Good evening, Mr. Hale."

* * *

Quentin did not sleep that night.

He lay in his bed above the empty shopfront he rented, listening to the wind worry the shutters, and performed the arithmetic of ruin. If Krein reached Nell's entries — and he would, the man read *everything* — what would he find? A ward with no baptismal record, no midwife, no school enrollment before age five. A guardian who reported her appearance in his household eleven days after registering her residence. Small things. Threads.

And pulling them led backward, inevitably, to Lysa — to whatever she had fled, to the name she had used in the years she was gone, to the question of why a woman would keep her own child off the rolls of a state that, for all its rot, had never harmed her. Quentin had built his careful fiction precisely so no one would ever ask that question. Krein asked questions for a living.

At three in the morning he got up and lit the lamp and did the coward's calculation, honestly, because he had promised himself he would never lie to himself even if he lied to everything else. If Krein confronted him, what could he do? Confess and lose Nell to the institutions — orphanages in this season were warehouses with mortality rates published nowhere. Deny and be caught in denial, which was worse than the crime. Report himself preemptively, frame it as administrative error, beg correction?

Or — and here his mind went where he did not want it to go — offer Krein something else. Other names. Other deviations. The city was full of forged lives; he knew where several of them lived. He could buy Nell's safety with strangers' exposure, and dress it as cooperation, and sleep again.

He sat with that thought until the lamp burned low, testing it, the way you test a bad tooth. It fit. That was the horror. It fit his mouth perfectly. Every instrument of the regime was designed so that betrayal was the path of least resistance, so that a man protecting one child would feed it a dozen adults without ever feeling the moment his soul changed hands.

He thought of Casper Voss then — unexpectedly, completely — the courier with the dead brother on her registration, the stranger he had covered for on a frozen afternoon for reasons he still couldn't fully account for. She had looked at him across her own terror and seen, somehow, that he was a man who could be trusted with a lie. Now an inspector had looked at him and seen a man who could be leveraged with one. Both of them right. Both reading the same page.

"No," he said aloud to the empty room, and the word steadied him the way arithmetic did. Whatever he did when Krein came — and the man would come, the summons was already implicit in the black notebook — he would not purchase his niece with other people's names. He would find another way. There was always another way, if you were willing to pay in something other than other people.

He just had no idea yet what the price would be.

Monday morning, snow falling straight down like sifted flour, Quentin walked to the office early and found the summons already waiting on his desk, typed on the regime's grey stock, signed once:

*M. Hale — please attend the Inspector at your convenience this week regarding entries in Volumes Seven and Nine.*

Volumes Seven and Nine. Seven held the widow and the two displaced families. Nine held Nell.

Krein, arriving behind him with snow melting on his narrow shoulders, paused at the desk long enough to say, pleasantly, "No hurry, Mr. Hale. I'm a thorough man. We'll take our time."

And beneath his coat, against Quentin's heart, the pocket watch Lysa had given him ticked on, marking out the days like a clerk counting down a debt.
