## 9. Nell's Map

The census office smelled of wet wool and cold iron that February, and Quentin Hale had learned to love it the way a man learns to love anything he has decided to survive inside. The stove in the corner hissed when the sleet found a crack in the flue. The lamps burned low because lamp oil had been requisitioned — everything was requisitioned now, coal, bread, paper, patience — and so forty clerks worked in a brown half-dark, copying out death registrations for a state that seemed to be registering its own.

Quentin was good at his work. That was the trouble with him, people said. He could find an error in a column of numbers the way other men found faces in crowds. He had spent eleven years among the rolls of Velden City, eleven years of births and deaths and changes of address, and he knew the way paper lied better than anyone in the building. Paper lied constantly. It lied about who was alive and who was dead, about who lived where, about who existed at all. He had learned this first as an enemy of lies and then, somewhere in the last two years without quite marking the moment, as a practitioner.

His sister Maren had died in the influenza winter. Her daughter Nell had come to him with two suitcases and a coat too thin for the season, and Quentin had done the only thing he knew how to do: he had gone to the registry and made her real. A new entry, a new parentage, a dead cousin struck through and rewritten. He had used his own steady clerk's hand, the hand that had never once been questioned in eleven years, and he had written his niece into existence under his own name. It had felt, at the time, like the smallest possible crime. Now, watching the inspector Aldous Krein move through the office twice a week with his grey gloves and his little book, it felt like a rope around his neck that someone else was holding.

But that was evening worry. This afternoon there was Nell, waiting for him outside the office at four o'clock, standing very straight on the frozen cobbles with her school satchel and her breath coming out in white plumes, the way she always stood when she had something to show him and wanted him to guess.

"You're late," she said.

"The trams aren't running."

"The trams are never running." She fell into step beside him, matching his stride with the fierce economy of a child who had learned not to waste motion. "I have something."

"Homework?"

"Better than homework."

They walked east toward the river, past the shuttered bakery where the queue formed at five each morning regardless of whether there was bread, past the poster hoarding where a new notice had been pasted over an old notice over an older notice still, until the layers were thick as tree bark and no one could read any of them. Quentin carried the satchel. He always carried the satchel. It was one of the small unspoken arrangements between them, along with the extra spoonful of jam he pretended not to notice her taking and the way she left her boots by the door at night positioned exactly as his were, heel to the wall, laces tucked in.

At home — two rooms above a cobbler's shop, a stove, a table, a window that looked out on nothing but another wall — she made him sit down before she would show him whatever it was. She cleared the table herself, wiping it with the cloth even though he'd already wiped it that morning. Then she went to the drawer where she kept her things: pencil stubs, a tin of buttons, a dried flower from Maren's funeral that Quentin was fairly sure neither of them had put there but which neither of them had ever thrown away.

She unrolled the paper flat on the table and weighted the corners with the button tin and two pieces of coal.

It was a map. She had drawn it in pencil, and then gone over the lines in ink taken, he suspected, from his own desk bottle, which meant she had climbed onto the chair and reached up to the shelf while he was at work, which meant she had planned this. The harbor of Velden City spread across the page in careful child's strokes: the crescent of the quay, the finger piers jutting into the black water, the customs house drawn with its actual columns because Nell did not do things approximately.

"What is this?" he asked, though he already knew, and his heart had begun to do something slow and heavy against his ribs.

"It's the harbor," she said. "From the ferry. I ride the ferry sometimes after school. The man doesn't check if you've got a penny and I have pennies." She tapped the westernmost pier. "This is Pier One. Soldiers there. Two sentries, day and night. They've got a brazier but they don't let anyone else stand near it. This one—" Pier Three, "—is where they load the grain barges, and there's a guard hut but only one man and he sleeps. I watched. He sits down at four and by half past his head goes back against the wall. Pier Five is open. Nobody guards Pier Five because there's nothing there anymore, just the old fish sheds, but you can see the whole channel from it, and—"

"Nell."

She looked up. Her eyes were Maren's eyes, dark and level, and behind them was the particular patience of a child waiting for an adult to catch up.

"Why," Quentin said, very carefully, "have you drawn me a map of the harbor?"

"Because of the ship."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below them the cobbler hammered a sole, three strikes and a pause, three strikes and a pause, the heartbeat of the building.

"What ship?" he asked, though he knew this too. Everyone in the capital knew about the Meridian now; it was in the queues and the tram shelters and the mouths of men who drank too much and spoke too loudly. The last diplomatic vessel out. Forty civilian berths. Forty, in a city of a million souls who had begun to understand what was coming.

"The ship everyone talks about." She smoothed the map's edge with her palm, a small proprietary gesture. "Auntie Ilse at the fish stall says her cousin knows the harbormaster. She says the officials get the berths first and then the rich people buy the rest, and ordinary people get nothing, and she spat when she said it." Nell's finger moved to the channel. "So I thought, if we can't get a berth, we should know the harbor anyway. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case we need to leave fast." She said it the way she said her times tables. "You always say a person should know the exits. You taught me that. When we go anywhere — the market, the registry, church — you always know where the doors are. So I've been learning the exits of the whole city."

Quentin sat very still. Above the stove, steam from the kettle made a small ghost against the low ceiling. He thought: *she is nine.* He thought: *Maren would either kill me or kiss me.* He thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water: *she is right, and I am ashamed that she had to be.*

"How long have you been riding that ferry?" he asked.

"Since November." She had the grace to look slightly guilty. "Only when it's not dark. And I count things. Guards, mostly. I'm good at counting. Madame Rusk says I have a gift for enumeration."

*Enumeration.* His nine-year-old niece, gifted at enumeration, spending her afternoons memorizing the garrison pattern of a dying port. He almost laughed. It came out of him instead as a long breath, and he put his hand flat on the table beside the map, beside her small precise ink lines, and said nothing for a moment because everything he might say was too large for the room.

"Nell," he said finally. "Listen to me. What you've done here is clever. It's more than clever — it's the sort of thing trained officers do, and they do it worse than you. But I need you to hear the next part properly." He waited until her chin came up, until she gave him the small nod that meant she was listening with her whole self. "If anyone ever sees that map — anyone — it isn't homework. Do you understand? A child drawing pictures of guarded piers is a curiosity. That same drawing in the wrong hands is evidence. It's evidence of reconnaissance. People have been shot for less than what's on this paper."

Her face didn't crumple. That was the frightening part. She considered it, gravely, the way she considered sums, and then she nodded once.

"So we hide it," she said.

"We burn it."

"No." Her hand covered the corner of the page, quick as a bird. "You always say never destroy information you might need. You said it about the registry. You said records are how the powerless fight back."

"I said that about lawful records."

"A map is a record." Her voice stayed level, but her fingers pressed whiter on the paper. "Uncle Quentin. If we have to run — if the soldiers come, or the food runs out, or the city falls — I don't want to be the person who doesn't know which pier is open. I was the person who didn't know anything when Mama died. Everybody kept telling me things after it was too late to matter. I'm never going to be that person again."

The kettle began to sing. Neither of them moved to take it off.

Quentin looked at his niece — at Maren's stubborn jaw set in a face still round with childhood, at the ink stains on her right middle finger that mirrored his own — and felt something shift in his chest with an almost audible click. It was the sound, he would think later, of protectiveness becoming resolve. Of a man deciding that fear was no longer an acceptable strategy.

Because here was the truth he had been circling for weeks: Krein was auditing the rolls. Every forged entry Quentin had ever made was a thread, and the inspector was pulling threads one by one, patiently, gloved finger by gloved finger. Casper Voss's name was on one of those threads — the woman from the tea house, the courier with the dead brother's name, the stranger whose lie he had covered because her terror had recognized his own. When Krein reached his thread, Quentin would have perhaps an hour's warning, or perhaps none. And Nell — Nell, who was registered as his daughter in a fiction eleven years deep — Nell would be swept up in whatever came for him, because the regime did not distinguish between the forger and the child the forgery protected. It distinguished even less kindly.

He had been telling himself that preparation was panic. That a sensible man kept his papers in order and waited for events. But a sensible man, he understood now, watching a nine-year-old defend a hand-drawn intelligence map with both hands, would already know every exit.

"Keep the map," he said.

Her shoulders dropped a fraction — relief, quickly mastered.

"But we're going to improve it," Quentin went on, pulling the lamp closer, and now his own voice had changed, and he heard the change and let it happen. "Guard rotations change. What matters isn't where the soldiers are today, it's when they change shifts, how many, from which direction relief comes. Dates. Times. You'll write them in the margin, small, in pencil, so they can be erased. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"And the fish sheds on Pier Five — draw me the doors. How many ways in and out of each shed."

"Three in the big one." She was already reaching for her pencil, bent over the page, her breath held the way it was when she worked. "Two are boarded but the boards are loose. I checked."

Of course she had checked. Quentin sat back, and the fear was still there — it would always be there now, a cold passenger riding behind his breastbone — but it had stopped being in charge. He thought of the ledger in the Ministry of Last Things, the order he had glimpsed listing who might board the Meridian and what they had paid; he thought of names next to sums, ministers' initials in the margin; he thought of Casper Voss's face when he'd marked her entry *verified*, and of his own hand lying smoothly, fluently, treasonously.

"All right," he said quietly, mostly to himself. "All right. Exits."

Outside, the sleet turned to snow, fat and silent, burying the city one soft inch at a time. Below them the cobbler's hammer kept its time. And on the table, weighted with buttons and coal, a child's map of the harbor lay open between them — piers and patrols and loose boards, the exits of a drowning city, drawn in a steady hand.

It was nearly seven when the knock came at the street door — two sharp raps, a pause, two more — and Quentin froze with the lamp still burning and the map still open, because nobody knocked on the cobbler's door at seven in the evening, and nobody, in the Velden he now lived in, knocked politely unless they wanted something he could not afford to give.
