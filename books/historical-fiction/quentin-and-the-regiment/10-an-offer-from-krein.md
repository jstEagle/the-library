## 10. An Offer from Krein

The summons came on a Thursday, which Quentin Hale would remember afterward because Thursdays were the day he reconciled the death registers, and he had been halfway through the letter M when the runner arrived.

"Inspector Krein will see you at four o'clock," the boy said, not meeting his eyes. "Ministry annex, third floor."

Quentin set down his pen with a care that surprised him. The ink was still wet on an entry — *Marrow, Edith, b. 1871, d. 12th of the Frost Month, cause: inflammation of the lungs* — and he watched it blur slightly in the damp air, a small soft ruin, like everything else in this city.

He had known this was coming for eleven days. Eleven days since Aldous Krein's audit notice appeared on the census office bulletin board, typed in that particular bureaucratic font that made menace look like housekeeping. Eleven days during which Quentin had done nothing clever, nothing evasive, nothing at all except keep working, because the alternative was to stop working, and stopping was confession.

*Verified*, he thought. The word he had written beside Casper Voss's brother's name in October, in a tenement hallway that smelled of coal smoke and boiled cabbage. He had told himself it was a small mercy, a clerk's kindness, the kind of lie that keeps the machinery oiled without feeding anyone into it. He had told himself the same about Nell's entry, and her dead mother's, and the half-dozen other quiet fictions threaded through his registers like silver thread through dark cloth.

Now the man who audited such things wanted to see him alone.

"I'll be there," Quentin said.

The boy left. Quentin finished the Marrow entry, blotted it, closed the register, and put on his coat.

* * *

The ministry annex had once been a hotel. You could still see it in the proportions — the wide stairwell built for ladies in gowns, the brass rails gone dull, the marble floors scuffed by boots instead of polished by servants. Now the rooms held desks instead of beds, and the grand ballroom on the second floor was stacked to the ceiling with file boxes, the republic's memory packed away like linens in a house about to be sold.

Krein's office was on the third floor, corner room, two windows. Quentin had expected something austere, monastic, the lair of a true believer. Instead he found comfort. A good rug. A lamp with a green glass shade. On the desk, a tin of candied ginger, open, offered with a gesture that was almost hospitable.

"Hale." Krein did not stand. He was smaller than Quentin had imagined — the audit notices carried weight by typography alone, and Quentin had built him tall and grey in his head, a figure cut from the same paper as his memos. In person he was compact, neat, perhaps fifty, with the ruddy complexion of a man who took his walks regardless of weather and his meals at fixed hours. His eyes were the only unsettling thing about him: pale, quick, and patient, like a man watching a kettle he knew would boil.

"Sir."

"Sit. Ginger?"

"No, thank you."

"Suit yourself. It helps with the cold. This building never heats properly anymore — the boiler was requisitioned for the eastern hospital, which I'm told is also unheated, so one wonders where exactly the boiler went." Krein said this mildly, almost companionably, and turned a page on his desk. "You've been with the census office nine years."

"Ten in March."

"Ten in March. Attendance record exemplary. Error rate negligible. Your supervisors describe you as 'meticulous' and 'unimaginative' — I mean no offense; those are compliments in my trade." He smiled briefly. It was a good smile, practiced, the kind that had opened many doors. "You understand records, Hale. You understand what they're *for*."

"They're for counting people," Quentin said. "So the state can feed them, house them, tax them, and bury them. In roughly that order, lately."

Something flickered across Krein's face — amusement, possibly, or its professional cousin. "Candid. Good. I prefer candid men; they save time." He slid a folder across the desk. "Do you know what this is?"

Quentin looked. He didn't touch it. "An audit summary."

"It's a cross-reference matrix. Every registration your office processed in the last fourteen months, checked against parish records, military rolls, hospital admissions, and burial permits. Do you know how many irregularities we've flagged?"

Quentin's mouth was dry. "I couldn't say."

"Thirty-one." Krein said the number the way another man might announce a course of dinner. "Thirty-one entries in which a living person is recorded as someone who died, or a dead person as someone living, or a residence is claimed that no witness can confirm. Thirty-one small mercies, if you like. I've read the literature on this — clerks always call them mercies. As though mercy were a thing you could do to a stranger's paperwork without asking her." He leaned back. "Thirty-one. And here is the interesting part, Hale. Here is why I asked for you specifically, out of forty-one registrars."

Quentin said nothing.

"Nineteen of those thirty-one irregularities trace to a single pen." Krein tapped the folder. "*Yours.* Not sloppiness — I want to be clear about that. Sloppiness scatters. Yours clusters. Same districts, same pattern of verification, same confident little checkmarks. A man doesn't drift into that. A man *chooses* it, entry by entry, over the course of a year." He folded his hands. "Which makes you either the most corrupt registrar in this city or the most careful liar I have ever audited. I confess I haven't decided which. I find I don't much care."

The radiator under the window ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a telephone rang three times and stopped.

"What happens to the nineteen?" Quentin asked. His voice came out level. He was grateful for that, at least. Ten years of delivering bad news in calm tones — *I'm sorry, madam, but the record shows your husband was registered deceased in Vellmar two winters past* — had given him a voice for moments exactly like this one.

"That depends entirely on you." Krein opened a drawer and removed a single sheet, laid it face-down beside the folder, and rested two fingers on it as though pinning a moth. "Here is what you should understand about this moment in our national life. Everyone believes the state is dying, so everyone believes the rules are dying with it. Clerks steal. Registrars forge. Soldiers desert. They tell themselves the old law is finished and the new law hasn't arrived, so morality is suspended — a holiday between governments." His pale eyes settled on Quentin and stayed there. "But I am the new law, Hale. Or rather, I am what the next government will use as its law. And when the surrender comes, when the ministries are dissolved and the ships leave — there will be a reckoning. Records will be needed. Accounts will be settled. The men who kept clean books will matter very much, and the men who didn't will matter too, but differently."

"You're describing a purge."

"I'm describing *continuity*. There's a difference, though I grant it's subtle." Krein turned the sheet over at last.

It was a printed form. Quentin read it twice before the words stopped swimming. An indemnity. Full immunity from prosecution for irregularities committed in the course of duty, countersigned at the ministry level, contingent upon full cooperation with the ongoing records audit. At the bottom, a line for his signature, already dated.

"You'd sign off my forgeries," Quentin said slowly, "if I name them."

"I'd sign off your *confession*, which is a different document with different uses." Krein's tone didn't change at all. "Immunity means the state forgives you. It does not mean the state forgets. You would remain a registrar — a useful one, a vetted one. There will be enormous work after the transition, and competent men will be scarce. Most of your colleagues are already looking for ships. You strike me as a man without a ship."

"I strike myself that way most mornings."

"So take the offer. Name all nineteen. Give me the names behind them — who requested the false entries, who benefited, who paid. Every falsified record has an author above it, Hale; clerks don't invent the dead on their own initiative. Someone comes to your window with a story and a look on her face, and you decide whether to be stone or water. I want to know about every time you chose water. And then—" he paused, delicately, "—there is the matter of the twentieth."

Quentin kept his face still. "Nineteen was your number."

"Nineteen is what the matrix shows. But my matrix has a gap." Krein drew the folder back and opened it to a page near the middle. "District Nine, tenement on Corvich Street, household registration for one Casper Voss, courier, Ministry of External Affairs. Listed dependents: mother, two brothers. One brother — Tomas, aged sixteen — verified in September. The other brother—" his finger came down on the page like a stamp, "—*Marek Voss, aged twenty-two at death, interred Northfield Cemetery, plot 411, seven years ago.* Yet your field visit in October notes the household 'verified complete.' Both brothers present and accounted for." He looked up. "A woman whose brother has been dead for seven years cannot verify him alive, Hale. Unless someone helped her do it. And the only verification mark on that page is yours."

The room was very quiet. Through the window, Quentin could see the harbor cranes, black against a sky the color of dishwater, and beyond them the flat grey line of the sea where a ship called Meridian waited with forty civilian berths and a passenger list written in bribes. He thought of Nell drawing her map of the piers at the kitchen table, tongue between her teeth, marking guard posts with little crosses. He thought of Casper Voss standing in a tea house doorway saying, *Why did you lie for me?* with the wariness of a stray animal being offered a hand.

"A transcription error," Quentin said.

"Don't." For the first time, a blade showed through the mildness — just the edge of it, sheathed again at once. "Please don't insult us both. I've spent four months inside this city's paperwork. I know what an error looks like. Errors are timid. What you do is bold." He closed the folder. "Here is what the immunity covers, so we're precise. Sign, name the nineteen, give me the authors — including whoever stands behind the Voss woman — and every irregularity under your pen becomes state-sanctioned history. You keep your post. You keep your pension. When the tribunals come, and they will come, your name appears in the column marked *cooperating witness*, which in the new order is a very safe column indeed." He nudged the indemnity form an inch closer. "Refuse, and the nineteen become evidence. Your trial would be a formality — the matrix is thorough — and the tribunal's mood by then will not be gentle. These things never are. First the guilty are punished, then anyone nearby."

"And her?" Quentin heard himself ask. "If I name her. What happens to the courier?"

"She becomes a case file. She'll be questioned about her access, her dispatches, her contacts." Krein shrugged, a small economical motion. "What happens after questioning isn't really my department. I count things, Hale. That's all I've ever done. I count, and I report the totals, and other people act on them." He said it with the serenity of a man who had made that sentence bear his whole moral weight and found it sufficient. "I don't act on anything. I only write down what's true."

"*True*," Quentin repeated.

"The dead brother is dead. The living sister is using his name. Those are facts. Everything else — the sympathy, the circumstances, whatever sorrow drove her to it — that's weather. I deal in geology." Krein picked up his ginger tin, selected a piece, and ate it thoughtfully. "You know what I think, Hale? I think you're a good man in a bad ledger. I think every one of those nineteen entries cost you something — sleep, probably, and the comfortable belief that you were an honest clerk. I think lying has worn you thin, and that part of you wants, quite desperately, to set it down. To be forgiven. To hand the weight to someone whose job it is to carry weights." He pushed the form the last inch. "That isn't corruption. That's relief. And there's no shame in relief."

Quentin looked at the signature line. His own name would go there — *Quentin Hale*, in his tidy registrar's hand, the same hand that had written *verified* in a freezing stairwell while a woman watched him with the eyes of someone deciding whether to run. The same hand that wrote *orphan, ward of uncle, guardian employed* for Nell, because the alternative was a workhouse, and he had looked at her small stubborn face across the kitchen table and chosen forgery over arithmetic.

They would question her. He knew what questioning meant now; everyone knew. And they would question the others too — the widow in District Four whose son had died at the front and whose lodger had taken his name to keep the ration card; the ferryman in District Eleven; the girl whose age he had advanced two years so she wouldn't be conscripted as a juvenile laborer. Nineteen small fires, and Krein offering him a bucket of water in exchange for pointing out every house.

And underneath the offer, coiled and reasonable, the real transaction: *your safety for hers*. Justice, weaponized into betrayal, dressed in the language of duty. Krein hadn't invented a temptation. He'd simply found the exact shape of Quentin's conscience and poured the offer into it, so that refusing felt like cowardice and accepting felt like honesty. That was the craft of the man. That was what made him dangerous — not cruelty, but precision.

"I'll need time," Quentin said.

Krein's eyebrows rose fractionally. "Time."

"To review my registers. If I'm to account for nineteen irregularities, I won't do it from memory. Names, dates, districts — you want accuracy, Inspector. Accuracy takes a day."

Something moved behind the pale eyes — recalculation, perhaps, or respect. A kettle-watcher noting that this particular pot was slower than most. "Two days," Krein said. "Thursday evening to Saturday evening. After that, the offer expires, and the file goes to prosecution as-is." He stood, extending his hand across the desk, and Quentin took it. Krein's grip was dry and firm, the grip of a man shaking hands with a future subordinate or a future defendant, equally prepared for both. "You're doing the right thing, Hale. Whatever you decide, you'll tell yourself that. I'd only ask you to decide it quickly. In my experience, conscience is like ice on the harbor — best crossed early in the season."

Quentin walked out through the ballroom of boxed files, down the grand staircase built for a world that had believed in hotels, and into streets where the snow had begun again, fine and dry, sifting over the census hall, the tea houses, the print shops he didn't yet know existed, and the harbor where the Meridian lay loading fuel for a crossing most of the city would never make.

Two days. He had until Saturday evening.

He bought a paper on the corner more for the folding than the news, and by the time he reached his street the light was failing, and there in the downstairs window sat Nell with her map spread before her, adding a new pier in careful pencil, unaware that somewhere above her the whole fragile architecture of borrowed names had begun, quietly, to come apart — and that the first crack had a date on it now, and a deadline, and his own name waiting at the bottom of the page to be signed or spared.
