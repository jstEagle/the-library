## 11. The Brother Who Died Twice

The Registry of Vital Statistics occupied the corner of Wexler Square like a tomb that had outlived its usefulness. Its granite face was streaked with soot from the November fires, and the bronze doors — once polished bright enough for officials to admire their reflections in — had gone dull and spotted with verdigris. Nobody came here anymore except the desperate and the dead.

Casper came as both.

She had dressed for it. The courier's grey coat was too conspicuous on a civilian errand; instead she wore her mother's mended brown wool, the collar turned up against the wind, a headscarf pulled low. A widow's clothes. She had discovered years ago that grief made people invisible in a different way than grey did — people looked away from grief, the way they looked away from beggars and open wounds. Grief was an embarrassment. Grief said something had failed somewhere, and no one wanted to be reminded of failure in the eleventh winter of a republic that had promised everything.

Inside, the registry hall smelled of cold stone and old glue. Rows of clerks' desks stood empty, abandoned by men who had better things to do than record deaths in a city where deaths were about to become uncountable. One clerk remained at the far end of the hall, a young man with chapped hands and a ledger open before him, copying entries by the light of a green-shaded lamp.

Casper approached him the way she'd learned to approach all officialdom: apologetically, as though she were the inconvenience.

"I'm sorry to trouble you," she said. "I'm trying to find a record. My brother. He died some years ago, and our family copy of the certificate was lost when we moved lodgings."

The clerk looked up. His eyes had the flat patience of someone who had processed ten thousand sorrows and would process ten thousand more before the state finished dying. "Name?"

"Emmerich Voss."

"Date of death?"

"That's the difficulty," Casper said, letting her voice waver just enough. "We never knew precisely. He went east for work — this would be 914, spring of 914 — and we received word he'd been killed in a warehouse collapse near the river district. But we never saw the certificate ourselves. The employer's office handled it, they said. We were told it was done."

The clerk made a small note. "Warehouse collapse, 914. There were several." He rose, stiff with sitting, and led her back into the stacks.

* * *

The stacks of the Registry of Vital Statistics were what Casper imagined the inside of a mountain would look like if a mountain could be organized alphabetically. Iron shelving climbed three stories into gloom, loaded with bound volumes, loose folios, boxes tied with government twine. The air was dry as paper, because it was paper — forty years of the republic's births, marriages, deaths, its whole biography pressed into cellulose.

The clerk found the 914 death registers and set them on a reading table under a caged bulb. "I'll leave you. Call if you need assistance." He hesitated. "Madam, I should say — if you're looking for closure, these pages are very thorough, but they are only ink. Whatever your brother was, he isn't here."

"I know," Casper said softly. "That's rather why I've come."

He gave her a puzzled glance and retreated.

She waited until his footsteps faded among the shelves. Then she opened the register to the spring of 914 and began to read the dead.

It took two hours. The warehouse collapses of that year — there had indeed been three, one of them fatal to four men whose names she copied onto a slip of paper for no reason she could name except that someone ought to remember them — produced no entry for Emmerich Voss. No entry at all. She checked the summer. The autumn. Nothing. Her brother, who had died — she had seen the body, she had closed his eyes, she had helped carry him home wrapped in sailcloth because the family could not afford a coffin — her brother did not exist in the registry of the republic he had been born into.

Which meant one of two things. Either the record had been destroyed. Or it had never been made — and a death that goes unrecorded is a door left standing open.

She sat back in the iron chair and let the cold of the stacks settle into her. Quentin Hale's question came back to her across the weeks: *Your brother Emmerich. Is he living with you?* And her own lie, smooth as oiled cloth, *Yes, he works nights.* And Quentin's pen moving across the census form, marking her household as containing a man who had been eleven years in the ground.

She had thought herself clever, using Emmerich. A second ration card. A second identity to borrow when the first grew hot. It was a courier's trick, a smuggler's arithmetic — every extra person on paper is a coat you can hang lies on. She had never asked herself whether the trick was safe, only whether it worked.

But now, staring at the gap in the ledger where her brother's death should have been recorded, she felt the first true cold of the season. Because an unrecorded death meant someone else might have found that open door before her. Someone else might have walked through it, taken up Emmerich's name, his birth date, his existence — and lived inside it, warm and fed and officially real, while Emmerich lay under the thin turf of the Paupers' Field with no line anywhere to say he had ever been.

Paper could be stolen alive. She knew that better than anyone.

The question was: who?

* * *

She spent the next three days answering it, and the answer required no genius — only the particular literacy of a woman who had spent six years reading the republic's paperwork the way other people read weather.

The trail began at the labor exchange on Corvin Street, where Emmerich had registered for dock work in the winter before his death. Casper presented herself as a sister settling an estate — technically true, if eleven years late — and requested a copy of his employment file. The clerk there, younger than the registry man and more frightened, fetched it quickly. Too quickly. Files that matter get misfiled on purpose; files nobody wants come fast.

Emmerich Voss, dockhand, age nineteen, registered January 914. Assigned to the Harrow & Sons warehouses. Discharged April 914 — *deceased*, in the margin, in a hand that was not the clerk's own but a supervisor's, hurried and slanted. Below it, a transfer notation: *Records consolidated to Ministry of Contracts, Office of Procurement Audit, per directive.*

Contracts. Procurement. Not the ministry of labor, which would have been natural, but the ministry of money.

Casper stood on Corvin Street in the falling dusk and felt the shape of it before she could prove it. A dead man's employment record doesn't go to procurement unless someone needs it there. Unless someone is using a dead man to sign for things. Ghost employees were an old corruption — she'd carried dispatches through enough ministries to know that every bureaucracy breeds them, phantom workers drawing wages, phantom suppliers billing for goods that never arrive. Usually they were invented names, names built from nothing, because invented names are safe. No grieving sister comes asking after a name you made up.

But an invented name takes time to establish. A real one — a real dead one, already registered at the labor exchange, already known to employers, with a face in someone's memory — a real one is ready-made.

Someone had used her brother. Had worn him like a coat, the way she wore him now on census forms. The symmetry of it was so bitter she almost laughed aloud on the street, breath steaming, while a bread queue shuffled past her without seeing.

* * *

Getting into the Ministry of Contracts took four days and cost her the last of the silver buttons off her good coat, melted down and passed across a records-room counter to a man named Pell who owed her handler a favor and hated owing it.

"You have thirty minutes," Pell muttered, not looking at her. "The night auditor leaves at nine. The day man doesn't come till seven. The corridor lamp on the third floor has been broken since summer, which means either negligence or providence." He slid a key across the counter beneath a blotter. "Third stack, row C. Employment verifications, 914 through 917. If anyone asks, you're restoring misfiled folios and you're deaf."

"What happened to your hatred of owing favors?"

"It matured into hatred of being in debt," Pell said. "Go."

The Ministry of Contracts at night was a cathedral of the transactional. Marble floors, brass fittings, portraits of ministers dead and disgraced and worse — the walls here still hung the portrait of Chancellor Merek Vane, which told Casper something about how long rot takes to admit itself. Her footsteps rang too loudly in the corridors. She counted doors to keep her breathing even.

Row C, third stack. The volumes were fat, indexed by surname. *Voskovic, Vossel, Voss.*

There it was. Emmerich Voss, employment verification, filed March 915 — nearly a year after his death — marked *verified active, assigned to Contract Division 7, Harbor Works Reconstruction.*

Active. Eleven months dead and *active.*

She turned the page with fingers that wanted to shake and didn't allow it. Attached to the verification was a signature card — the specimen signature against which all documents bearing Emmerich Voss's name would be checked. A signature in a firm, practiced hand. Not Emmerich's. She knew her brother's handwriting the way she knew her mother's stitching; he wrote his *E*s with a flourish like a little flag, a boy's vanity he'd never outgrown. This signature was compact, economical, bureaucratic. A stranger's hand wearing her brother's name.

Below the signature card, the authorization block: *Verified and countersigned — L. Brandt, Asst. Clerk, Contract Div. 7.*

L. Brandt.

She copied the name onto the same slip of paper as the four dead warehousemen, then sat in the dark of the stacks and let the name do its work on her. Brandt. She knew the name. Everyone who moved through the ministries knew it — it surfaced in dispatch routing, in committee minutes she'd ferried sealed and unread. Lukas Brandt had risen in the decade since. Assistant clerk no longer. Deputy Director of Procurement now. A name that appeared, lately, in a context much closer to her own life:

The manifest rumors. The Meridian. Forty civilian berths, and the names whispered among couriers and clerks like a lottery nobody had bought tickets for. Brandt's name was on that list. She'd heard it twice — once from a telegraph clerk, once overheard between two ministry drivers sharing cigarettes outside the courier depot. *Brandt bought two, they say. Him and his wife. Paid in gold, they say, though where a deputy director finds gold in a dying republic—*

Her brother had died twice. Once under a collapsed roof in the spring of 914, and once again, slowly, across eleven years, as a stranger spent his name in ledgers, drew wages through his ghost, signed contracts in his stolen hand — and then, when the ship of state began to sink, purchased passage to safety with the proceeds. Emmerich Voss had bought Lukas Brandt's escape and would never know it, because the dead don't audit.

Casper folded the slip of paper small and put it inside her glove, against her palm, where she could feel it.

* * *

She walked home the long way, down along the harbor road, because she needed to see the water. The Meridian's berth was visible from the eastern quay — or would be, when the ship came. Cranes stood idle against a sky the color of dishwater. A patrol passed her without a glance; a woman in widow's wool walking the cold shore was beneath suspicion, beneath notice.

Grief, she thought, was a strange currency. For eleven years she had paid it out quietly — at the grave each spring, in the way her mother's hands went still whenever anyone said the name Emmerich, in the private superstition of using his identity, keeping him circulating in the world's paperwork because she couldn't bear for him to vanish entirely. She had told herself it was practical. It was also a vigil. A candle kept burning in a registry no one visited.

And all that time, someone else had been burning the same candle — for profit. Had warmed himself at her brother's memory. Had eaten from his name.

The anger arrived not as heat but as clarity, which was worse and better. Clarity told her what heat never could: this was not an isolated theft. A clerk does not rise to deputy director on forged wage claims alone; the small corruptions are apprenticeships. Brandt had learned on Emmerich Voss the way a forger learns on practice sheets — and then moved on to larger canvases. Defense contracts. Procurement audits. The sums a deputy director could touch made ghost-wages look like pickpocketing. And when the reckoning came, Brandt had a berth on the last ship out, purchased with whatever the large canvas had earned him.

The republic wasn't merely surrendering. It was being sold — and men like Brandt were holding the receipt.

Casper stopped at the rail above the frozen tideline and looked out at the dark water. Somewhere north, the enemy armies waited in their winter quarters, patient as creditors. Somewhere south, the evacuation ships would begin loading within weeks. And somewhere in between, in her coat lining, sewn flat against her ribs by her handler's careful hands, lay a sealed dispatch she had promised to deliver without opening — a dispatch whose weight had begun to feel less like paper and more like a stone, less like orders and more like evidence.

She hadn't opened it. She was a courier; the seal was the covenant; the whole grey machinery of her trade ran on the sanctity of unopened things. Six years of discipline said deliver it and ask nothing.

But discipline had never met Lukas Brandt. Discipline had never stood in a cold registry and read the place in the book where her brother's death should have been and wasn't. Discipline had never understood, until tonight, that the seal on her chest might be protecting exactly the kind of man who had turned Emmerich into a wallet.

She flexed her hand inside the glove, feeling the slip of paper with the names on it — four warehousemen, one brother, one thief.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would find the sympathetic telegraph clerk. Tomorrow, she would start learning what the codes actually said.

Tonight she stood at the harbor rail until the cold drove her home, and for the first time in eleven years, she did not grieve her brother.

She armed herself with him.
