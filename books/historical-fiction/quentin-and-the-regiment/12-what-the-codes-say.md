## 12. What the Codes Say

The dispatch had been riding against my ribs for six days.

I'd stopped noticing it most of the time, the way you stop noticing a stone in your boot until you take a step wrong and there it is again — that small hard weight sewn into the lining of my coat, just under the left arm where a mending seam would be unremarkable. My handler, Veyra, had stitched it in herself, sitting too close to me on a bench in the Winter Gardens with a needle flashing in her gloved hand, chatting about her sister's wedding while she worked thread through wool. Anyone watching would have seen two women gossiping. Only I felt the shape of it: a strip of oilcloth, folded to the width of a pencil, heavy as a sin.

Six days of carrying it through a capital that was quietly eating itself.

* * *

The ministries still opened at nine. That was the strange thing — the clocks kept going. Clerks still shuffled their papers, ministers still signed things, the great brass doors of the Foreign Office still groaned open onto snow each morning as if the republic were a going concern rather than an estate being inventoried for sale. But the rumors moved faster than the dispatches now, and I heard them everywhere I went, in the queue at the cipher room, in the tram, in the bread line outside Grelka's bakery where the price had doubled twice since the solstice.

They're negotiating terms. The armistice commission met at the Hotel Aurel under a neutral flag. They've already divided the naval yards. They say the Minister of War wept at the table — they say he laughed — they say he wasn't even there, it was his deputy, and the deputy has already bought passage.

In the cipher room on the third floor of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, a man named Anselm Dekker sat behind a grille of frosted glass and decoded other people's secrets all day long. He was fifty-some years old, bald as an egg, with ink-stained cuffs and the patient manner of someone who has spent forty years being shouted at by superiors who understood nothing. I liked him. More than liked — I trusted him, which in that building was a form of madness, and I knew it, and I did it anyway.

He was the one who first said, without looking up from his work, "You know they're selling us."

It was the fourth day of my carrying the dispatch. Snow ticked against the frosted glass. I was waiting for a return acknowledgment from the Northern Command relay, stamping my feet, pretending to be only a tired courier.

"Selling what?" I said, careful.

"The war." Dekker set down his pen and rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger, leaving smudges at the corners. "Defense contracts. Munitions licenses. The artillery works at Kolvast — do you know what happened to the artillery works at Kolvast? Half its output has been going east for eight months. Not north, not west. East." He tapped the desk. "Someone signed papers making that legal. Someone is still signing them. And when the terms are announced, those someones will be on a ship with their money already moved, and the rest of us will be standing in the snow wondering what happened."

"You shouldn't say these things," I told him. It was reflex. It was also true.

"No," he agreed mildly. "But I'm old, Courier Voss, and my wife died in the spring, and I have discovered that fear requires a future, and mine has gotten very short." He picked up his pen again. "Your acknowledgment will take another hour. There's coffee if you want it. Real coffee — I keep it for emergencies, and I've decided this qualifies."

* * *

That was how it began: with real coffee in a chipped ministry cup, drunk across a desk covered in intercepted traffic, while an old man told me the shape of the crime before I knew I was holding its evidence.

I didn't tell him about the lining of my coat. Not then. But I asked questions I shouldn't have asked, and he answered them, because grief makes people reckless and so does December, and somewhere in the second cup I realized I had crossed a line and could not find it behind me anymore.

"The sealed diplomatic class," I said. "The red-wax pouches. Where do they go after the cipher clerks?"

"To the ministers' private registry. Which is to say" — a thin smile — "to a safe in Deputy Minister Corvus's office, which has no lock worth picking but does have a very loyal door guard." He looked at me over the rim of his cup. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I carry them," I said. "And lately I've been wondering what I'm carrying."

Dekker set the cup down slowly, the way a man sets down something that might go off.

"What's your route tonight?" he asked instead of answering.

"Nothing. I'm dark after seven."

"Come back at half past nine," he said. "The night shift is one boy named Pell who sleeps from ten to midnight like clockwork. The duty cipher book for the ministerial class stays in my drawer." A pause. "I'm not asking what's sewn into your coat. I don't want to know. But if you wanted to know — I mean, if a courier wanted to verify the integrity of her cargo, which any conscientious professional would —"

"— she'd need a cipher book," I finished.

"And forty minutes," said Dekker, "and the good sense to burn everything afterward."

* * *

I spent the hours between seven and nine walking.

The city at night in that last winter had a quality I have never found words adequate to, though God knows I tried later. The streetlamps were lit at half strength — fuel rationed — so the snow came down through a dim amber haze, softening every corner, muffling every footstep. The cafés closed early. Soldiers stood at intersections in twos, stamping, breath rising, rifles slung, young faces above grey collars looking not fierce but lost. On the corner by the Opera, someone had chalked on the wall, in letters a meter tall: *WHO SOLD US?* Someone else had chalked beneath it: *THE ONES WITH SHIP TICKETS.* A municipal worker had scrubbed at it with lime water and only made it ghostlier, so the question floated there half-erased, like a thought the city couldn't quite suppress.

My mother's face came to me unbidden — her hands, always her hands, cracked at the knuckles, pushing a needle through grey wool. She didn't know what I carried. She thought I delivered envelopes between offices, honest work, dry work. She'd mended this very coat three weeks ago and commented that the lining was wearing oddly under the left arm and I'd nearly fainted into the tea.

Tomas would be out. Tomas was always out now, seventeen and restless, haunting the docks where the ships were, coming home with stories about berths and bribes and the prices of passage climbing like floodwater. My little brother measuring himself against the exit and finding it priced beyond him.

And somewhere across the city, a census taker named Quentin Hale was presumably marking his rolls, keeping his careful lawful records, protecting his dead sister's daughter with his neat forgery of a checkmark. I found myself thinking of him more than I meant to. Two liars, fluent in the same language. I wondered whether he ever thought of me. I suspected he thought of Nell, and that was better company anyway.

At twenty past nine I climbed the stairs of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, and the frosted-glass grille was dark except for one lamp, and Dekker was waiting with the drawer already open.

* * *

The cipher book was a fat green volume with a canvas spine and the arms of the Republic stamped in gold gone dull. Dekker laid it flat, squared it to the desk edge with unnecessary precision — a lifetime of clerical habit — and said, "Ministerial class uses the rotating table. Key changes daily at midnight; before that, yesterday's key governs, which is convenient, because yesterday's key is the one I remember best." He glanced at the wall clock. "Forty minutes. Start talking when I nod. Don't talk when I don't."

I unpicked four stitches.

Veyra's needlework was beautiful even under pressure; the oilcloth came free through a gap barely wider than a finger, and the coat showed nothing worse than a mend. Inside the oilcloth: a single sheet, folded in eighths, covered in columns of figures in a fine clerk's hand. No heading. No signature. Numbers, groups of five, marching down the page like soldiers.

"Contract class?" I whispered.

Dekker looked at the page for perhaps four seconds. His face did something complicated and settled into granite. "Contract class," he confirmed. "Rotating table, variant B." He pulled a second book from the drawer — thinner, blue, unofficial, a personal transcription he must have built over decades the way old men build models of ships — and set both volumes side by side. "Read me the first group."

I read. He turned pages. His pen scratched. The radiator in the corner knocked and knocked like something asking to be let out, and Pell slept on his folded coat in the anteroom, and outside the window the snow fell on the capital of a country that was being sold, group by group, five figures at a time.

Ten minutes. Fifteen. Dekker worked with a speed that startled me, muttering cross-checks, occasionally striking through a figure and re-deriving it. At the twentieth minute he stopped, sat back, and looked at the page as if it were a corpse.

"Nod," he said, meaning talk.

"What is it?" 

"It's an inventory," he said quietly. "A schedule of sales. Dated — look — dated eleven months ago. Artillery shells, rifle contracts, the coastal battery emplacements at Vard." His pen hovered. "Here. 'Delivery deferred per agreement with purchasing party.' You understand what that means? The purchasing party is the enemy. We sold them our own shells, and then deferred delivery of our own defense so the shells wouldn't come back at us too quickly. Someone arranged for our forts to be empty at a scheduled time." He looked up at me, and his eyes behind the smudged lenses were terribly clear. "Who gave you this pouch?"

"I can't say."

"No," he agreed. "Of course. But whoever they are, they aren't the ones who wrote it. This is a copy of a copy of the private registry. This is someone's insurance." He almost smiled. "Ministers keep insurance. They think everyone does."

"Can you finish it?"

"I can finish enough." And he bent back over the page, and the pen scratched, and the radiator knocked, and I stood in a warm room full of secrets with my coat hanging open like a wound.

* * *

What emerged, over the next thirty minutes, was not orders. It was not strategy, or troop dispositions, or anything a courier could hand to a general and be thanked for. It was arithmetic. Cold, itemized, initialled arithmetic.

Four contracts. Four signatures. The Minister of War — whose name I had grown up seeing in schoolbooks, whose portrait had hung in my classroom beside the President's. The Deputy Minister Corvus, keeper of the safe. Two others whose names meant less to me then but would come to mean more. Each contract: quantities, prices, deferral clauses. Each clause: payment routed through a shipping concern registered in a neutral port — a shipping concern, I noted, whose name appeared again and again, because it owned freighters, and freighters needed passengers, and passengers fleeing a collapsing state paid anything.

The same shipping concern, I learned later, held the civilian manifest of the ship called Meridian. But that night I only saw the names. Names of men I had saluted in corridors. Men whose dispatches I had carried against my ribs for two years, believing — believing, the way you believe in weather — that the sealed paper was the country itself, that carrying it was service, that the wax and the grey coat and the cold routes were the shape of loyalty.

"They'll be on the evacuation lists," Dekker said. He had finished. He was squaring the pages together with those precise old hands. "First priority berths. They always are. The people who sold the country get the seats off it." He paused. "You know what you're holding."

"Yes."

"Do you?" He took off his glasses and polished them on his cuff, unhurried, and when they went back on he looked older and much more awake. "You're holding proof that could hang four ministers. You're also holding proof that will get a courier shot before sundown if anyone learns she opened a sealed diplomatic pouch. There is no office you can bring this to — the courts answer to the ministers. There is no newspaper that will print it — the presses are licensed by the ministers. There is no general — the army takes its orders from the ministers. Do you see the architecture? It's not a crime hidden inside the law. The law is the hiding place."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below us a telegraph key chattered, sending somebody's sanctioned version of the truth out into the dark.

"So what do I do with it?" I asked. And I heard my own voice shake on the word *it*, because it was not *it*, it was the deaths at Vard, it was the empty forts, it was Tomas in a press gang's path and my mother's hands and every soldier stamping at the intersections — all of it folded in eighths, weighing less than a pencil.

"That," said Dekker, "is the question I've been waiting my whole life for someone to ask me." He slid the green cipher book back into the drawer, then hesitated, and added, in a different voice entirely: "There was a printer. Years ago. Before licensing, before the crackdowns. Old radical type, the kind they never quite catch because he prints slow and small and hides his plates in flour. If a person needed something set in type that no press would touch —" He shrugged elaborately. "I'm an old man. My memory of names is unreliable. But I believe the surname was Sova. And I believe he drinks at the Anchor & Rope, badly, alone, at closing time."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you asked what to do with it," Dekker said, "and because my wife used to say that a secret dies of dignity if nobody tells it. And because—" he looked at the reconstructed sheet, the four names, the four signatures, the arithmetic of betrayal rendered in a fine clerk's hand, "—because I spent forty years moving other men's lies through the wires, and I would like, before the lights go out, to move one true thing."

He held out the page. I took it. My fingers were steady, which surprised me; everything else in me was falling.

"Burn your drafts," he said. "Restitch your coat. Walk home the long way, past the tram depot, because the short way has a checkpoint now. And Courier Voss —" I was already at the door, coat buttoned, the weight back under my left arm heavier than any paper had a right to be — "when you meet Sova, tell him the wire clerk remembers 1909 fondly. He'll know what it means."

* * *

I walked the long way, past the tram depot, exactly as instructed, and the checkpoint on the short route glowed ahead of me like a fever I'd dodged. The city breathed around me, dim and amber and muffled. On the Opera wall the half-erased chalk still floated: *WHO SOLD US.*

*I can tell you,* I thought. *I can tell you four names and the dates and the sums. I am the only person in this whole sleeping city who can walk up to that wall and write the answer underneath.*

And there was nowhere lawful to put it. No court, no press, no office, no superior — the law itself was the hiding place, Dekker had said, and I felt the vertigo of it fully then, standing in the snow with my hand pressed flat over my own left ribs: justice, actual justice, sewn into a courier's coat lining, and every official channel in the republic designed to bury it.

Unless the channels themselves were bypassed. Unless the truth went out the way contraband goes out — slowly, small, hidden in flour, printed by candlelight by an old man who drank badly at the Anchor & Rope.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I would find the Anchor & Rope.

Tonight I walked home through the falling snow, carrying the fall of the republic against my heart, learning its weight.
