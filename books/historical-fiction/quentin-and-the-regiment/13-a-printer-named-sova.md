## 13. A Printer Named Sova

The telegraph clerk's name was Ilsa, and she had told Casper one more thing before they parted: *If you mean to use it, you'll need Sova. Nobody else will touch it.*

Casper spent two days finding him.

She started the way anyone would start — with questions, careful ones, asked in the right corners. The tea house where she'd cornered Quentin was too public, too watched since Krein's men had begun appearing on street maps of the city like weather fronts. The market stalls were worse; everyone talked and no one remembered. What she needed was a place where people still believed in something enough to risk it, and those places had grown rare in Velden. Belief was expensive now. Most people sold theirs early.

It was her mother who gave her the thread, though she didn't know it. Casper had come by the flat on Wednesday with bread and stayed past dark, and while her mother stitched at the lamp — burial shrouds now, three orders that week, which meant three families in the district had given up expecting anything but death — an old woman from upstairs came down to settle a debt of thread and mentioned, in the way neighbors mention things, that her nephew's boy had been arrested for handing out leaflets near the tannery.

"Leaflets," Casper said.

"About the war contracts." The old woman shrugged, unbothered. "Someone prints them. Someone always does. In my day it was the anarchists off Ropewalk Lane."

Ropewalk Lane was gone — renamed, half demolished, its tenements swallowed by a ministry annex. But the anarchists, Casper suspected, had simply moved deeper. She went looking the next morning, following the logic of hiding places: not the poor districts, where the police knew every face, and not the respectable ones, where fear wore better clothes. Somewhere between. A street of shuttered workshops near the canal, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators and the water smelled of iron and old dye.

* * *

She found the door because it was the only one on the street without a lock worth picking.

That sounds wrong, and it took her an hour to understand it herself. Every other door on the lane had been reinforced, double-bolted, chained against the press gangs and the looters. This door — narrow, paintless, set into the flank of what had once been a bookbindery — had a single latch, worn smooth, that lifted at a touch. A locked door says *keep out*. An open one, in this city, in this winter, said something else entirely: *you already know why you're here.*

Inside was a stairwell going down, lit by nothing, smelling of ink and machine oil and cold stone. Casper stood at the top for a long moment, doing the arithmetic she'd gotten good at lately — the weight of what she carried sewn against her ribs, the weight of what it might cost her — and then went down.

At the bottom, a corridor. At the end of the corridor, candlelight under a door.

She knocked twice, waited, knocked again. From within, a voice like gravel settling: "If you're selling, I'm poorer than you."

"I'm looking for a printer."

A pause. "Everyone's looking for a printer. Bread, coal, a berth west — printers are the new priests. Everyone wants absolution in multiples." The voice came closer. "What do you want printed?"

"The truth," Casper said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it, and said it anyway. "Proof. Documents. Something that can't be denied."

The door opened on a chain, four inches of gap, and one eye — brown, rheumy, sharp as a ledger line — regarded her through it.

"Show me your hands."

Casper turned them over, palms out, in the candle spill from inside. The eye studied them: the ink stains a courier accumulates, the paper cuts, the callus where a pen rests against the middle finger.

"Clerk's hands," the voice said. "Or courier's. Close enough." The chain rattled. "In or out. It's cold and candles cost."

* * *

Sova was older than she expected and younger than his voice. Sixty, perhaps — hard to tell with radicals, who aged in jumps, decades at a time when their causes died and then not at all when they were reborn. He was thin in the way of men who forget meals rather than skip them, wearing a leather apron gone stiff with age, and he moved through his basement the way a priest moves through a chapel: knowing every object, trusting none of them to be permanent.

The room itself stopped Casper's breath for a moment.

It wasn't large. Two presses — one a hand press old enough to have printed pamphlets against the last emperor, one a platen jobber that looked salvaged from a burned office — a composing stone, type cases ranked along the wall like soldiers, reams of paper wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. And everywhere, everywhere, the evidence of work: broadsides pinned up, proofs hanging from wires, a shelf of finished pamphlets in stacks as neat as ammunition.

"Forty years," Sova said, following her gaze. He said it without pride, the way a mason says forty years about a wall. "I printed against the monarchy until the monarchy fell, thought we'd won, printed for the Republic after. Now I print against the Republic." He shrugged. "The truth doesn't change sides. Only the liars do."

"My name is—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "I don't want it. Names are inventory, and inventories get seized." He pulled a stool toward the composing stone for her, sat himself on a bench worn concave by his own weight. "Tell me what you have."

Casper reached into her coat. Her fingers found the seam, the lining, the flat packet riding against her side — and stopped.

Every instinct she had built over six years of carrying other people's secrets told her the same thing: never show the cargo. Never confirm it exists. Deliver it sealed or die with it sealed. That discipline had kept her alive through checkpoints and audits and the slow collapse of everything around her.

But the dispatch had no destination anymore. The ministry it was addressed to was negotiating surrender terms with the very men it named. Delivering it meant burying it. Keeping it meant dying with it. There was exactly one thing left it could do, and that thing required showing it to a stranger in a basement by candlelight.

She unpicked the lining stitch with her thumbnail. It took longer than it should have; her hands weren't quite steady.

The packet was oilskin-wrapped, cipher-keyed pages inside, and beneath them — this was the part Ilsa's partial decipherment had revealed, the part that had turned suspicion into certainty — a plain-text annex, three pages, written in a minister's own hand because apparently some men are too arrogant to encode their sins.

Sova read all three pages standing up. He read them the way he did everything, completely and without hurry, while the candle guttered and Casper sat with her heart going like a telegraph key.

Then he laid them on the composing stone, facedown, gently, as if they were sleeping.

"Defense contracts," he said. "Sold to the invasion power before the first shot. Steel quotas redirected. Coastal battery schedules handed over in exchange for — " his finger moved down the page — "guaranteed positions in the occupation administration. For themselves." He looked up. "These are real? Not a forgery planted to discredit?"

"They're real. I carried them. I know the seal, the routing, the cipher key."

"And the man who wrote this annex — Minister Dravec?" Sova pronounced the name the way you'd spit. "He signed his own name. To his own treason."

"He assumed no one would ever see it. The whole point of a courier system is that the courier is furniture. No one reads the furniture."

"No," Sova agreed. "They don't." He began to walk the length of the room and back, hands clasped behind him, thinking aloud. "Two hundred copies. Minimum. Ministries, barracks, the harbor garrison, the queues at the ration offices — the queues matter most. Hungry men read well. Soldiers read best of all, if you reach them before their officers do." He stopped. "Paper I have. Type I have. Time—" he glanced at the candle, at the stone stairs leading up to a city full of informers — "time is the problem. Setting two hundred copies of eight pages takes four nights minimum. Four nights of light under a door, of smell, of movement."

"Can you do it faster?"

"Everything is faster if you're willing to be caught faster." He smiled, and it was not entirely a pleasant smile. "Yes. I can do it. One question first, courier, and think carefully, because it decides everything."

"What question?"

"Do you understand what happens when this is published? Not to Dravec — Dravec is finished either way, the invaders will eat their own collaborators soon enough. I mean to you. To whoever carried these papers." He tapped the oilskin packet. "This document proves treason. It also proves a courier carried treason through the ministries of Velden for weeks without reporting it. When the tribunals come — and they'll come, here or in exile, they always come — there will be a moment when someone asks: who moved this paper, and why didn't she speak sooner? You will be guilty of the crime of having known."

"I've been guilty of knowing for years," Casper said. "Everyone in this city is guilty of knowing. The only difference is what you do with it after."

Sova looked at her for a long time. The candle flame stood perfectly still between them, as if listening.

"That," he said finally, "is the correct answer. I've had patriots in this chair, idealists, martyrs-in-advance. They all wanted to be forgiven first and useful second." He pulled the oilskin packet toward him and weighed it in one hand. "You came to be useful first. Sit down, courier. I'll teach you to set type. If we're both hanged for this, you should at least hang knowing how it was made."

* * *

He taught her through the small hours.

The composing stick was heavier than she expected, and the type smaller — eight-point Caslon, Sova's favorite, "because it reads honestly," and the letters sat in their case in an order that seemed designed to frustrate: not alphabetical, but by frequency, the common letters nearest the hand. Lowercase left, capitals right. The metal was cold and slightly greasy and each letter had to be placed upside down and backward, so that reading your own composition meant holding it to a mirror or waiting for the proof.

"You're placing them too carefully," Sova said, watching her fumble. "Speed comes from trusting your fingers to find the letters. Like touch-typing a telegraph key. Don't look. Feel."

"I don't feel letters."

"You feel everything else, I'd wager. Streets, exits, moods of policemen. Same skill. Different alphabet." He set a line himself to show her — fast, fluid, the little steel voices clicking into the stick — and she saw that he was right, that it was a language, and that languages could be learned by the desperate faster than by anyone.

By the third hour she could set a sentence in under a minute. By the fourth, Sova had her working a headline while he locked up the first page in its chase, tightening the quoins with a key, testing the forme with a dabber of ink and a proof sheet pulled by hand.

The words came up black and crisp on the damp paper:

**TREASON AT THE MINISTRY OF DEFENSE — THE CONTRACTS THAT SOLD VELDEN**

Casper stared at it. Six years of carrying words she was forbidden to read, and here was a page of words she had chosen, set by her own hands, saying the thing she needed the world to hear. It looked small. It looked enormous. It looked like a weapon and a confession at once.

"It needs distribution before the Meridian sails," she said. "After that, the people who could act will be gone, and the people left behind will have nothing but the truth and no one left to tell it to."

"The ship." Sova nodded slowly. "You're trying to save someone."

"A child, maybe. It's not settled."

"Nothing ever is." He began breaking down the proof, cleaning the type. "Listen to me, courier, because I've done this before, in '31, in '38, and I've buried friends from both years. Printing is the easy part. Paper burns quietly and presses make no noise worth reporting. Distribution kills. Every hand that passes a pamphlet is a hand that can be traced. Every queue where one drops is a queue that gets searched. You will need runners — many, expendable-seeming, ordinary. Women with shopping bags. Boys selling matches. Old men who look harmless because they are nearly harmless."

"I know people."

"Know fewer than you think, trust fewer than you know, and pay no one. Payment creates witnesses; conviction creates volunteers." He hung the corrected proof back on its wire, where it swung gently among dozens of others — years of them, a whole museum of lost causes — and stood regarding it. "Four nights. Come tomorrow with the full text organized: annex first, contracts second, names largest. And courier—"

"Sova."

"When it's done, leave the city if you can. Truth is like a message in a bottle; once it's thrown, the thrower's part is finished. Don't drown holding the bottle."

"I'll decide what I hold," Casper said, "when I know what's worth keeping."

Sova laughed — a dry rustle, like paper turning. "Yes," he said. "You'll do."

* * *

She climbed out into the grey end of night, the oilskin packet resewn into her lining with Sova's thread — stronger than hers, he'd insisted — and stood a moment on the dead street breathing air that tasted of coal smoke and coming snow.

The sky over the harbor was just beginning to pale. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, the Meridian lay at her moorings with her forty berths and her cargo of bought salvation, and somewhere across the city Quentin Hale was waking to whatever fear an honest man sleeps with, and her mother was rising to shrouds, and Tomas to whatever odd jobs hadn't yet been taken by men more desperate than he was.

Four nights, Sova had said. Four nights of candlelight and cold type, and then two hundred small black-and-white detonations scattered through the city.

Casper pulled her coat tight and walked home through the emptying dark, and for the first time since Ilsa had put the decoded pages in her hand, the dread in her chest had changed its shape — less like drowning now, more like a fuse, burning slow and steady toward something.

She was three streets from the print shop's lane, still warm with the memory of it, when she saw the notice pasted on the customs house wall, fresh paste glistening under the new lamplight:

**BY ORDER OF THE INSPECTORATE: ALL PRINT ESTABLISHMENTS TO REGISTER PRESSES, STOCKS, AND OPERATORS WITHIN SEVEN DAYS. UNREGISTERED PRESSES WILL BE SEIZED AND OPERATORS DETAINED.**

Below it, the signature: *A. Krein.*

Seven days. Four nights of printing, and a man with a ledger and a patient smile already closing the net around every candle in the city.

Casper stood in the falling snow and memorized the notice, the date, the signature — and turned back toward the canal, walking faster.
