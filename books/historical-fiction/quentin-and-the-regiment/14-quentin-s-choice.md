## 14. Quentin's Choice

The audit forms arrived on a Tuesday, in a folder the color of old teeth.

Quentin Hale had been a census man for eleven years. He knew paper the way sailors knew weather — he could read a coming storm in the grain of it. This folder was not routine. Routine came in buff envelopes with the Ministry of Records stamp smudged by some clerk's hurried thumb. This came in grey board, sealed twice, with a card tucked inside that read:

*By order of Inspector Aldous Krein, Office of Internal Verification: all district enumerators will submit complete audits of irregular entries within seven days. Failure to comply will be treated as complicity.*

Complicity. The word sat on his desk like a loaded pistol.

He unlocked the drawer where he kept his tea things — the little tin of black leaf, the chipped cup with the painted wren — and did not make any tea. Instead he pulled out the ledger for District Fourteen and laid it open under the lamp, and there it was, third column from the left, in his own careful hand:

**Hale, Q., enumerator. Entry 4-1187: Voss household, 9 Ropemaker's Yard. Male sibling listed as verified.**

Verified. He had written it nine weeks ago with a stranger's terrified eyes fixed on him from her own doorway, and he had written it again in his memory every night since, turning it over like a coin to see which face would land up.

Seven days. Krein wanted every falsified record named, dated, and signed.

Quentin closed the ledger and looked out the window at the census office yard, where a cartload of registration books was being hauled toward the furnace shed. Even now, even as the republic died, they were burning nothing — just moving records from one building to another, because moving records was what the state did when it could no longer do anything else.

Nell was waiting for him at home. Nell, whose mother was three years dead and whose existence lived or died by a line of ink in Quentin's own hand: *dependent minor, ward of enumerating officer.* A lie so small it fit inside one line. A lie so large it held a whole child inside it.

He understood now, better than he had at her doorway, why Casper Voss had kept her brother on the rolls. The dead made excellent shelter. The dead asked no questions, ate no rations, drew no attention — unless someone went looking. And someone was looking.

* * *

The office emptied slowly that evening. Quentin stayed past the lamplighter's rounds, past the moment when the typists' floor went dark and the radiators ticked themselves cold. He spread the audit form flat and read its instructions four times.

*Enumerator will list below every entry verified without full documentation, together with the name and current address of the individual concerned.*

There it was. Not *every irregularity.* Every entry *without full documentation,* together with the name and address of the person protected. Krein didn't want errors corrected; he wanted people delivered. An audit was a net dressed as a form.

Quentin took out a fresh sheet of paper and began, very deliberately, to think.

He was not, by nature, a scheming man. His whole life had been built on the opposite instinct — record what is true, file it where it belongs, let the archive be the one honest thing in a dishonest country. When he had marked Casper Voss's brother *verified,* it had felt less like rebellion than like mercy wearing a bureaucrat's coat. But mercy, he was learning, does not stay small. Once you have lied for one person, you begin to see the architecture of lies everywhere — who is protected by them, who is hunted by their absence, who builds whole careers out of other people's fear of the truth.

And Aldous Krein built his career out of exactly that. Quentin had seen him twice now: once in the corridor, gloved hands folded behind his back, reading the district ledgers upside down across a clerk's shoulder; once at the door of the records vault, asking the archivist — gently, terribly gently — how often enumerators were visited by members of the public seeking corrections.

Corrections. As if Casper Voss had come to correct something. As if she were not simply a woman trying to remain a family of five instead of a family of four.

So. He could write Casper's name. Entry 4-1187, Voss household, 9 Ropemaker's Yard. And within a week she would be a folder on Krein's desk, and within a month a number in a detention register, and within a year — if the war went as it seemed to be going — a line in someone else's death book, processed through the very office where Quentin worked, filed by some other tired enumerator doing his duty.

Or he could leave her out.

Leaving her out was not free. If Krein cross-checked the rolls himself — and men like Krein always cross-checked — the omission would hang off the audit like a torn sleeve. Quentin Hale, enumerator, District Fourteen, had submitted an incomplete audit. There was a word for that too.

Unless the audit was full. Unless it was so full that no one thought to look for what wasn't in it.

Quentin dipped his pen.

* * *

He worked until two in the morning, and what he produced was, he admitted to himself, a masterpiece of the only kind he was capable of: a truthful lie.

He listed eleven irregular entries. Eleven was enough to look thorough; more would have looked like performance. Each one was real — he checked each against the ledgers, because a fabricated error was a gift to an inspector, and he refused to hand Krein anything that wasn't true. A widow in Copper Lane who had kept her husband registered eighteen months after his burial because his pension fed three children. A lodger counted twice in two districts by a careless colleague, doubling a ration allocation neither recipient had requested. A boarding house keeper who listed seasonal workers as permanent residents so the house wouldn't be condemned as underoccupied. Small mercies, all of them, committed by ordinary people against a state that measured everything and understood nothing.

For each entry he gave a date, a district reference, and a note on cause: *clerical oversight,* *enumerator error,* *documentation unavailable due to relocation.* What he did not give — what the form technically required but what he rendered in handwriting just faintly less legible than the rest — was anything that pointed toward a living, findable human being. The widow in Copper Lane appeared as *party relocated, address unconfirmed.* The doubled lodger became a reference number and a shrug. He buried Casper Voss in the grammar of bureaucracy, that great soft snowfall under which the whole country was already disappearing.

Then, at the bottom of the eleventh page, he added three names of his own choosing.

The first was Deputy Director Emmerich Vaas, Ministry of Procurement. Quentin had processed the household registration of Vaas's mistress in the spring — a woman of twenty-six listed as a "research assistant" resident at an address Vaas had owned through a shell company since before the war. The entry had been flagged by a junior clerk and then unflagged from above, the correction withdrawn with a note Quentin had never been meant to see: *resolved administratively.* Resolved administratively. Three words that had bought a minister's comfort while the same ministry sold the city's defenses out from under it — though Quentin didn't know that part yet. He knew only that powerful men did not get their paperwork questioned, and that this asymmetry was the actual business of the census, beneath all its arithmetic.

The second was Colonel Brandt Reiner, quartered at the garrison annex, whose household roll claimed six dependents drawing civilian rations in a city where children queued for bread at five in the morning. Quentin had walked past that queue every day for a month before he'd found the courage to check the annex figures.

And the third — Quentin paused over this one, pen hovering, heart going hard and slow like a fist knocking on a door —

The third was the man above Krein. Director-General Oskar Meissner, Office of Internal Verification. It was Meissner's signature that appeared on requisition orders for "verification transport" — motorcars and fuel drawn against the census budget, logged to districts that had no vehicles. Quentin had found it by accident, reconciling fuel allocations, the way he found everything: patiently, without wanting to.

Naming Meissner was madness. Naming Meissner was also armor. Because an audit that accused the inspector's own superior could not possibly be the work of a man protecting a courier on Ropemaker's Yard. It was the work of a zealot, a crank, a dangerous little archivist with a grudge against the entire apparatus — and Krein would believe that, because Krein believed everyone served himself. That was the whole of Krein's philosophy, Quentin suspected. Men who sold everything assumed everyone else was selling too.

Let him believe it. Let him spend his suspicion where Quentin sent it.

He signed the last page. *Q. Hale, Enumerator, District Fourteen. Certified complete and true.*

Complete and true. He almost laughed. It was both. That was the miracle of it, the thing he would turn over for years afterward: he had not written one false word. He had simply chosen which truths to stand in a row, and the row told a story, and the story was a weapon aimed at the men who deserved it, and the woman it was built to hide stood outside the frame entirely.

He sealed the folder. Grey board, sealed twice, matching the original. Somewhere in the city, Sova the printer was setting type by candlelight, Casper Voss was carrying evidence sewn into her coat lining, and Quentin Hale had just committed his first act of war.

It felt, strangely, like filing.

* * *

He slept badly and woke early and was at the office before the stoves were lit, which is how he happened to be standing at the window when Aldous Krein arrived.

Krein crossed the yard unhurried, a lean man in a good coat that managed to suggest either wealth or mourning depending on the light. He carried no folder. Men like Krein didn't need folders; folders were for subordinates. He stopped halfway across the yard, looked up — directly, precisely — at Quentin's window, and inclined his head perhaps half a centimeter.

Then he continued inside.

Quentin stepped back from the glass, his pulse loud in his ears. Coincidence. It had to be coincidence. Windows faced yards; men glanced at buildings; the mind stitched meaning onto nothing because fear was a loom that never stopped working.

But he spent the morning anyway re-checking his audit, page by page, hunting for the thread that could unravel him. Entry by entry. Date by date. The handwriting consistent. The omissions invisible. Vaas, Reiner, Meissner — named with dates and document references, dry as dust, damning as dynamite.

At noon, a runner brought the internal post. Among the circulars was a single card, no signature:

*Audits under review. Certain enumerators may be invited to discuss their findings in person.*

Certain enumerators. Quentin set the card down and looked at it for a long time. Invited. In person. The language of courtesy stretched over the language of the cell.

He thought about warning Casper. One note, left unsigned, slipped under the door of the print shop or passed through whatever channel she used these days — *he's auditing, be careful, burn your papers, go.* But a warning was a thread too, and threads led back. Everything led back now. That was what he had learned in one night at his desk: there was no longer any clean position in the country, no neutral ground, no honest clerk watching honestly from the sidelines. You either filed the truth they wanted or the truth there was, and either way you signed your name to something.

He had signed his name to his choice. Whatever came through that door next, he had chosen it on purpose, in ink, certified complete and true.

That evening he walked home the long way, past the harbor, and stopped at the rail to watch the cranes loading the ships that would carry out the people who could pay. Somewhere among those vessels rode forty civilian berths, bought and bribed and forged into legality, and somewhere on Ropemaker's Yard a woman was doing cold arithmetic with her family's lives. He couldn't help her with berths. He had no money, no connections, no power at all except this: he knew where every body in the district officially was, and therefore where every hole in the record lay, and holes were doors.

A patrol passed along the quay, boots ringing on frost. Quentin turned up his collar and walked on.

At home, Nell had left her map of the harbor pinned above the stove, weighted at the corners with buttons. She'd added something new since yesterday — a small red X near the eastern piers, and beneath it, in her careful child's hand: *gap. two men only. change shift at dusk.*

He studied it a long time. Then he took it down, copied it exactly into his notebook — the notebook where he recorded fuel discrepancies and phantom vehicles and the quiet crimes of important men — and put the original back exactly where it had been.

Because if Krein came for him, and someday soon a runner's card said he would, then Quentin Hale intended to have already decided what mattered most: not the audit, not the office, not even his own clean record, but the gap at the eastern pier, and the ship, and whoever could still be gotten through that gap before the ice closed the harbor for good.

Two nights later, the summons came. Not a card this time but a formal letter, heavy paper, embossed seal: *Inspector A. Krein requests the attendance of Enumerator Q. Hale, Thursday, ten o'clock, Office of Internal Verification, fourth floor. Bring your ledgers.*

Fourth floor. The floor with the good windows and no return corridor.

Quentin read it twice, folded it into his breast pocket, and reached for his hat. Thursday was two days away. He intended to spend them well.
