## 15. Berth Thirty-Nine

The money was in a leather satchel under the third floorboard of a dead man's office, and Casper Voss had been standing in that office for eleven minutes trying to decide whether taking it made her a thief or an accountant.

Ministry of Contracts, fourth floor, Room 412. The nameplate on the desk read *A. Verel, Senior Clerk*, though Verel had not occupied it in three years — not since he'd been reassigned to the Harbor Commission, where men with his particular talents were put to use deciding who left and who stayed. Casper had followed him there the way she'd learned to follow anyone: by paperwork. A man who could steal a dead boy's identity and wear it for a decade without chafing was a man whose ledgers would repay close reading.

She had read them for six nights, sitting in Sova's basement with a candle guttering and her coat still smelling of coal smoke from the delivery runs. And what she had found, folded into the columns of harbor requisitions like a hair in bread, was this: forty civilian berths aboard the *Meridian*, and beside each number, in Verel's tidy hand, a price.

Not all of them had been paid in money. Some had been paid in silence, in signatures withheld, in a blind eye turned at the right moment. But berth after berth ran down the page like a litany, and at the bottom — unassigned, marked only with a dash — sat number thirty-nine.

One berth left. One man alive who knew it, and one woman crouched over his floorboard with a pry bar she'd taken from a building site two streets away.

The satchel held four hundred and sixty crowns in mixed notes and coin. It was, by Casper's arithmetic, exactly what Verel had extracted from a widow named Harsany to move her son from the waiting list onto the manifest — before the waiting list itself had been quietly dissolved and the boy's berth resold to a shipping magnate's cousin. The widow Harsany had died in November. The money had never been returned. It sat under the floorboard the way stolen things sit anywhere: comfortably, in the dark, belonging to no one.

*You're not stealing,* Casper told herself, working the board loose with the flat of the bar. *You're completing a transaction the seller failed to finish.*

It was a good line. She'd been telling herself good lines all winter. The courier's trick, she had learned long ago, was not a steady hand but a steady story — you carried the paper, so you carried the reason, and as long as the reason held, your hands didn't shake. Her hands were shaking now. She noted it the way she noted weather, and kept working.

The floorboard came up. The satchel came out. She replaced the wood, pressed it flush with her palm, stood, and brushed the dust from her grey coat, and for three full seconds she allowed herself nothing at all — no triumph, no guilt, just the cold clean fact of the weight in her hand.

Then she let herself feel it, briefly, on the stairs down through the dark building: *I have done something irreversible.*

Outside, snow was falling again, fat and slow, erasing the street in soft white increments. Somewhere east of the river the guns had gone quiet for the second night running, which everyone in the capital understood was worse than noise. A quiet enemy is an enemy counting.

Casper pulled her collar up and walked into the white, a courier carrying nothing, which felt more dangerous than any dispatch she'd ever run.

* * *

Verel received bribes on Thursdays, between four and five o'clock, when the Harbor Commission offices emptied early and the porter went round the back to drink.

Casper knew this because she had spent two days being invisible outside the Commission, which was easy work; a woman in a courier's grey coat with a document case under her arm could stand in a doorway for hours and be seen by no one, because everyone assumed she belonged to someone else's business. Invisibility, she had come to understand, was not a cloak. It was other people's certainty that you were somebody else's errand.

Thursday, four-twenty. She climbed to the second floor and knocked on a door marked *Manifestations and Clearances*, and Verel called "Enter" in a voice already bored, and she went in and closed the door behind her and set the satchel on his desk without a word.

Verel was smaller than his reputation. Middle-aged, soft-jawed, with the pale indoor complexion of a man who had spent twenty years turning other people's desperation into ledger entries. He looked at the satchel. He looked at her. His eyes did the quick professional flick — face, hands, coat, case — cataloguing threat the way she catalogued exits.

"I don't know you," he said.

"No," Casper agreed. "You know my brother."

Something moved behind his eyes — small, fast, quickly killed — but she caught it. Three years of wearing Tomas Voss's registration papers, of answering to a dead boy's name in queues and ration lines while her own identity gathered dust in a drawer, and here was the man who had built that arrangement and sold it onward, and he had thought himself safe because the family was poor and the dead stay dead.

"Sit down, Mr. Verel," she said, and took the chair opposite his desk before he could decide whether to offer it.

He sat. Slowly. His hand drifted toward the drawer where, she happened to know, he kept a service revolver with an expired permit.

"The pistol's permitted through March," she said pleasantly. "After that you'll want to renew, and renewals require a records check, and I've spent some time in your records." She opened the document case — slowly, letting him watch — and drew out three sheets. Not copies. Originals. Verel's own requisition notes in his own cramped hand, the ones she'd transcribed at Sova's and returned to their files with a forger's tenderness. What lay on his desk now were different pages entirely: the widow Harsany's payment record, annotated; the transfer of berth twelve to the magnate's cousin; and a single line, initialed, confirming receipt of funds "for consideration of passage, vessel Meridian, berth 39."

"I'm not here to expose you," Casper said. "Exposure is cheap. Anyone can burn a house down. I'm here to buy."

Verel wet his lips. "Berth thirty-nine isn't—"

"It's unassigned. Your own note says so. You were holding it, I imagine, for whoever made the largest final offer, and the ship sails in nine days." She slid the first sheet toward him, then the second, then the third, arranging them like cards. "Here is what happens if you sell it to me. You take the money. You enter the sale in your ledger in whatever fiction pleases you — charitable donation, clerical error corrected, act of spontaneous mercy. The berth is mine. Nothing changes for you except that your last unsold asset stops gathering dust."

"And if I refuse?"

"If you refuse," Casper said, "I walk out of here with these three sheets and I deliver them to the office of Inspector Aldous Krein, who is currently auditing this commission's manifests with what I understand is considerable appetite. Krein doesn't want money, Mr. Verel. He wants names. And you are such a well-organized name."

The silence stretched. Snow ticked against the window glass. Down in the street a cart ground past through slush, and neither of them looked at the window, both of them watching each other across the desk like duelists who had agreed on pistols and were now regretting the distance.

"You're a courier," Verel said at last. Not a question. "I've seen the grey coats. You people carry sealed paper for a government that's already—" He stopped himself, recalculated. "Why does a courier need a berth?"

"That," Casper said, "is above your clearance."

Which was, strictly speaking, a lie — there was no clearance above it, no authority left in the republic higher than the water rising around its ankles — but lies delivered in the tone of truth had carried her through every checkpoint of the winter, and they carried this one too.

Verel opened the ledger. His pen scratched. He wrote for a long time, inventing, she supposed, some small plausible kindness for the historical record, and Casper sat with her hands folded and watched the snow and thought about nothing, which was the hardest work she'd done all week.

"There," he said finally, and turned the ledger so she could see the entry. *Passage, vessel Meridian, civilian accommodation, berth 39. One soul. Paid.*

"One soul," Casper repeated.

"That's how they're counted now." Verel said it without cruelty, which was somehow crueler. "Souls. The naval term. Each berth is one soul, no dependents, no exceptions. The manifest closes when the souls equal the berths." He blotted the entry and pushed a carbon slip across the desk. "Boarding authorization. Name goes in at the pier. Whoever presents this is the soul."

Casper took the slip. It was thin paper, official blue, worth more than everything else she owned combined.

"One soul," she said again, and heard, beneath her own voice, the sound of arithmetic beginning — the terrible patient arithmetic that would not stop now until it finished dividing her family into what fit and what didn't.

* * *

She walked home the long way, along the canal, because the long way was colder and colder helped.

Five of them. The number kept assembling itself in her head no matter what else she thought about, the way a tooth finds its socket: five. Mother, who had sewn burial shrouds for the coming siege and joked about it in a voice that wasn't joking. Tomas, seventeen, strong as a dock rope, who talked about the north the way other boys talked about girls. Aunt Reva, who had come to stay when the eastern districts emptied and never stopped unpacking. Little Mira, Reva's girl, six years old, who asked whether the ships had beds. And Uncle Piet, Reva's brother, who had a bad lung and a good laugh and had already begun giving away his winter coat, saying he wouldn't need it where he was going, wherever that was.

Five. And in her glove, one blue slip.

*One soul.*

She stopped at the canal rail and looked down at the black water sliding under grey ice shelves, and let herself do the thing she had refused to do at Verel's desk: the arithmetic.

Her mother would say give it to the children, and mean Mira, and mean Nell Hale too if she knew about Nell, which she didn't yet. Tomas would claim it as duty — the future of the family, a young man's place on a new shore, planting the flag of the Voss survival in foreign soil. Reva would refuse it in a way that wasn't refusal, some arrangement of martyrdom so smooth it looked like logistics. Piet couldn't make the crossing anyway, everyone knew it, his lung wouldn't survive the northern sea, and he would say so cheerfully and thereby make the whole table complicit in agreeing. And Casper herself — Casper, who had bought the thing with stolen money and a dead man's fear — would stand at the head of the table holding the slip and discover that she had purchased not salvation but a fight.

Because that was the joke of it, the black joke underneath the whole transaction: she had imagined, walking out of the Harbor Commission, that she had solved something. That the impossible part was getting the berth, and once it was in hand the rest would arrange itself like a family packing a trunk. But the berth wasn't the answer. The berth was the question, sharpened to a point, brought home and laid on the dinner table where everyone she loved would have to look at it.

A bribe, she thought. People said *bought a berth* as if it were a loaf of bread. No one warned you that what you actually bought was the right to choose, and that choosing among the people you loved was a knife with no safe end.

An old woman passed her on the towpath, pulling a sled stacked with firewood, breath steaming. "Smiling at the canal, girl?" the woman said. "Only thing that ever smiled at me down there was a drowned cat."

"Bad habit," Casper said. "I'll break it."

But she was smiling, a little, helplessly — because for one hour, one whole hour between the Commission door and this rail, she had been a person who had *done it*. Who had walked into the machinery of the collapsing state with a pry bar and a lie and come out holding a soul's worth of escape. Whatever came next, no one could take that hour from her. The regime could take the city, the codes, the country, the names off every register in the census hall — but they could not un-ring the fact that Casper Voss, in the last winter of the Republic of Velden, had beaten Aldous Krein's audit and A. Verel's greed and the entire sinking arithmetic of the state, and bought her family a way out.

One way out. For five people.

The smile went away the way it had come, without permission.

* * *

The tenement smelled of onions and coal dust and, tonight, of her mother's glue pot, because Marta Voss was reinforcing the seams of every coat the family owned — a ritual Casper recognized and said nothing about. When the shroud-sewing was done for the day, her mother mended living clothes, as if the two trades were neighbors who borrowed each other's thread.

They were all there. That was the thing Casper noticed as she came up the stairs: all of them, gathered in the kitchen because the front room was too cold to heat, packed around the stove like the last coals of something. Reva darning by lamplight. Piet doing the crossword in pencil, badly. Tomas reading a tattered maritime primer aloud to Mira, who kept interrupting to ask whether the *Meridian* was bigger than the ferryman's barge. ("Much bigger." "How much bigger?" "Bigger than our whole street lying down.")

They looked up when she came in, all five faces, and Casper stood in the doorway with the snow melting off her shoulders and the blue slip burning a hole through her glove into her palm, and she understood that the moment she took it out, the family she was looking at would stop existing — not die, nothing so clean, but divide, forever, into the ones who left and the ones who stayed, and every dinner after this one, wherever dinners happened, would have a shape missing.

"Casper?" Her mother set down the seam she was sewing. "You're white as the window. Sit down before you fall down."

"In a minute." She unwound her scarf. Bought time. "I have news."

"Good news?" Mira asked, with the directness of the six-year-old, and the whole room stilled around the question, because good news was a currency none of them had handled in months.

Casper looked at them — at Tomas's open face, at Reva's needle paused mid-stitch, at her mother's hands folded in her lap with the needle still threaded through the cloth — and she reached into her glove and laid the blue boarding authorization on the oilcloth table, and the lamp flame leaned in the draft, and no one breathed.

"Passage on the *Meridian*," she said. "Sails in nine days. It's ours."

Piet laughed first, the great warm bark of him — and then stopped, because he had read the slip upside down from across the table, and Piet read everything, always had. "Ours," he repeated gently. Not a question either. The gentlest possible question.

"One berth," Casper said. "One soul. That's what the manifest allows. That's what there is."

The stove ticked. Out in the stairwell someone's boots went down past their floor and kept going, down and down, the way everything went down now. Mira, sensing the temperature of adult silence, slid off her chair and came to stand against Casper's knee, and Casper put a hand on the child's hair and looked across the table at her mother, and her mother looked back with an expression Casper had never seen on her before and would spend the rest of her life understanding:

Marta Voss was already deciding. And Casper saw, in that look, that whatever decision was forming behind those tired eyes, it was not going to be the one anyone at this table expected — least of all Casper herself.

"Take off your wet coat," her mother said quietly, "and sit down. All of us are going to talk about this properly. Tonight. Before anyone sleeps."

Tomas closed his maritime primer. Reva set down her darning. And the family council began.
