## 16. The Family Council

The berth was a slip of paper no bigger than a ration card, and it sat in the middle of the dinner table like a knife someone had forgotten to put away.

Casper had laid it there deliberately. She wanted them all to see it — the printed seal of the Meridian Line, the number thirty-nine stamped in red ink that had cost her everything she'd carried in her coat lining for three years. Her mother's hands were folded beside her bowl of barley soup. Tomas sat across from her, seventeen and hollow-cheeked, still smelling faintly of the tannery where he worked fourteen-hour shifts because skilled men had fled and boys were cheaper than soldiers.

Their aunt Marta had come up from the river district, which told Casper how seriously the family took the summons. Marta never climbed the hill to their tenement unless there was a funeral or a fortune involved.

"Say it again," Marta said. "Slowly."

"One berth," Casper said. "Number thirty-nine. Civilian passage on the Meridian when she sails. Which will be soon — I don't know the exact day, nobody knows the exact day, they keep it sealed even from the crew." She kept her voice level, courier's voice, the one that made bad news sound like weather. "It's real. It's paid. It's ours."

"And we are five people," her mother said.

Her mother hadn't touched the soup. That was the first thing Casper noticed, before the silence, before the way Tomas's spoon had stopped halfway to his mouth. Ilse Voss did not leave food untouched. In the worst winter of the border war, when Casper was nine and the grain convoys stopped coming, her mother had portioned out soup so thin you could read newsprint through it, and she had eaten every drop of hers, every night, without complaint, because she believed — she had *taught* them — that waste was a small treason against everyone who had nothing.

Now the soup steamed untouched under the single lamp, and her mother looked at the slip of paper the way she looked at bills they couldn't pay.

"Yes," Casper said. "Five people. One berth."

* * *

She had rehearsed this conversation four times on the walk up from the print shop. In the first version, her mother cried and embraced her. In the second, Tomas argued and was persuaded. The third and fourth versions were quieter and more honest: in those, no one said very much at all, because there was nothing to say. Five into one does not go. Arithmetic does not negotiate.

What she had not rehearsed was the speed at which love curdled.

Marta spoke first, because Marta always spoke first. "Then it goes to Mama. Obviously. She's oldest, she raised all of us—"

"Mama won't take it," Tomas said.

"I haven't been asked yet—"

"You don't need to be asked." Tomas set his spoon down with a click. "Look at her face. She decided before Casper finished talking. Same as she decided about Papa's pension money going to the church roof instead of new boots for me. Same as—" He stopped himself, jaw working.

"Tomas." Their mother's voice was soft. Soft was worse than sharp; sharp was just weather, but soft meant she'd already counted the cost. "Eat your soup."

"I'm not hungry."

"Liar. You're always hungry. Eat it while there's soup to eat."

He ate it. That was the thing about Tomas — he obeyed in the small things, furiously, because obedience in the large things had never once gotten him anything. Casper watched him swallow and thought: *he has already decided what he wants, and he hasn't said it yet, and when he says it, this table will break.*

She was right. She wished, later, with an ache that never quite healed, that she had been wrong.

* * *

"The berth goes to whoever the family agrees can use it best," Casper said, when the bowls were empty and Marta had poured tea from the pot that only came down when company visited. "I bought it with ministry funds routed through a shipping agent who thinks I'm someone else entirely. If any of you are questioned, you know nothing about how it was obtained. You received it from a charitable maritime society. Memorize the name — the Brotherhood of Saint Erasmus, Seamen's Aid. There are actual forms; I have them."

"You forged aid forms," Marta said.

"I borrowed a printing block."

"Borrowed."

"It will be returned." Casper let a beat pass. "The point is, the paper is clean enough. Whoever carries it boards clean enough. So the question isn't whether it's safe. The question is who."

"The question," her mother said, "is whether any of us should take it at all."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft from the window casement. Casper heard Tomas stop breathing.

"Mama—"

"A ship leaves," her mother said, to the table, to the lamp, to the dead husband whose photograph hung slightly crooked above the stove. "One person leaves. Four stay in a city that is being sold out from under us — and don't tell me it isn't, Casper, I sew burial shrouds for the Ministry of War, I know exactly how many coffins they've ordered for spring. What happens to the four? What happens to the one, alone, in whatever country takes the ship, knowing she left her family behind?" She turned her head then, and her eyes found Casper's with terrible directness. "You didn't buy a rescue. You bought a wound."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it? Tell me what Nell Hale eats for breakfast in ten years. Tell me if she remembers her mother's voice or only her grave. That's what a berth is, girl. It's a memory you give someone too young to carry it."

"Nell isn't part of this," Casper said, too quickly.

"No. Quentin Hale's niece isn't part of this. But you've been spending your evenings in that man's company, and you think I don't notice, because you think none of us notice anything." Her mother's voice stayed gentle, which made it unbearable. "You carry secrets the way other women carry handbags. We see the weight of you, Casper. We've always seen it."

Tomas pushed back his chair. The legs shrieked on the floorboards.

"She's right," he said.

Everyone looked at him. He stood by the window with his shoulders hunched, seventeen years old and built like a man already, tannery-hardened hands hanging at his sides.

"Not about the rest of it. About the ship." He swallowed. "The berth is mine."

* * *

The room went very quiet, the particular quiet of a family recognizing the shape of its own fracture.

"Tomas," Marta began, "you're the youngest—"

"I'm the future." His voice cracked and he hated it, Casper could see him hating it, and he pushed through anyway. "That's not pride, it's arithmetic, and everyone at this table loves arithmetic tonight. Mama is fifty-eight. Aunt Marta is fifty-three. Casper—" he gestured at her, and there was something raw and furious in the gesture, something that had been building for years, "—Casper is already gone. Don't look shocked. She's been gone since she was nineteen. She walks through that door twice a week wearing a stranger's coat and carrying other people's words, and half the time I don't think she sees us at all, she sees *assets*, she sees *logistics*. She's brave as a saint and she's leaving us either way — with the ship or without it, she'll die for this city or die for its ghosts, but she will not live here, not ever again. So it's not five people and one berth. It's two old women who won't leave each other, one sister who was never staying, and one boy the republic hasn't finished eating."

"Tomas!" Marta's voice cracked like a whip.

"He's right," Casper said.

And she was surprised to find she meant it. That was the cruelest part. She looked at her brother standing in the lamplight with his fists clenched, this boy she had taught to whistle, this boy whose boots she had resoled the winter their father died — and she saw it clearly, the way she could see the truth of a cipher once the key turned. He was the future. Not because he deserved it more, but because the word *future* only attached itself to the young, and mercy, like arithmetic, did not care what anyone deserved.

"The berth is yours," she said again. "If you want it."

"I want it." Then, quieter, and this was the part that would follow her down all the years after: "I don't want it like this. I don't want to be the one who left."

"There is no version," Casper said, "where somebody doesn't leave."

* * *

Her mother rose from the table without a word and began clearing the bowls. It was dismissal and refuge both; in that house, dishes were how grief got handled, water hot enough to scald, hands busy enough not to shake. Marta followed her to the basin, and the two sisters murmured in low voices, and Casper was left at the table with the red-stamped slip of paper and her brother, who came and sat down across from her again, close, elbows on the table, the way they used to sit when they were splitting stolen plums.

"How much did it really cost?" he asked.

"Everything I had."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only true one." She slid the paper toward him. He didn't touch it. "There are conditions. You go to the harbor with your papers and this, you present yourself at Pier Six between dawn and noon on sailing day, you say nothing to anyone, you do not go near the press gangs, you do not—"

"Cas."

"—do not stop to watch anything, do not help strangers, do not play the hero for some dock girl, you get on the ship and you do not look back at the quay, because looking back is how they catch you—"

"*Cas.*"

He had taken her wrist. His hand was enormous now, calloused, warm, and it stopped her mouth better than shouting would have. In the lamplight his eyes were their father's eyes, brown and patient and unbearably kind.

"I'll go," he said. "But not because I'm the future. And not because Mama refuses and Marta follows Mama and you were never staying." He turned the slip of paper over with one finger, studying the seal. "I'll go because someone in this family has to survive to remember all of you properly. Someone has to be alive in twenty years to say your names right. That's a job. I'm volunteering for it." A crooked smile, their father's smile. "Someone Voss has to be around when this country gets its reckoning, to tell them we weren't all like the men who sold it."

Casper discovered she could not speak. She nodded instead, and pressed her free hand flat over his, and for a moment the kitchen held still — the lamp steady, the sisters' murmur at the basin, the crooked photograph of a dead man watching his children divide themselves like rations.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the knock came at the door — three soft raps, the rhythm Quentin used, and Casper's whole body went cold before her mind caught up, because Quentin did not climb the hill after dark unless something had gone wrong.

Her mother opened it. Quentin stood on the landing with snow in his hair and Nell's mittened hand gripped in his, and his face was the face of a man who had rehearsed bad news on the stairs and failed to make it smaller.

"Forgive the hour," he said. "Casper — I need to talk to you. Tonight."

Tomas was already reaching for his coat.
