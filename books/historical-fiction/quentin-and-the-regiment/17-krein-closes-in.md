## 17. Krein Closes In

The Ministry of Transport still smelled of wet plaster and old men's cigarettes, and Aldous Krein found both comforting. Institutions in their death throes had a particular odor — decay sweetened by denial — and he had learned to breathe it the way other men breathed sea air.

He sat at a borrowed desk on the third floor, in an office whose occupant had fled south two weeks ago with a trunk of silverware and no forwarding address, and he spread the manifests out before him like a man laying out cards he already knew would win.

Forty civilian berths aboard the *Meridian*. He had the list. He had had it for eleven days, carried home folded inside his glove because he did not trust his own ministry's safes anymore — a reasonable instinct, as it turned out, since three days after he copied it, the safe in Records was drilled open over a weekend and nothing was reported missing except, presumably, whatever someone had wanted no one to count.

Krein counted.

That was, when it came down to it, all he did. People imagined that men like him spent their days breaking arms and kicking doors, and there were men in the Bureau who did exactly that, big pleasant animals like Dornik who enjoyed the work and were paid in kind. Krein's pleasure was quieter. Krein sat at desks. Krein read columns of figures. Krein asked the same question forty different ways until the numbers themselves grew nervous and confessed.

The question tonight — this morning, rather; the lamp at his elbow had burned down to its last finger of oil — was simple: who had paid for berth thirty-nine?

Thirty-eight of the forty civilian places had been allocated through channels so transparent they barely qualified as corruption. Ministers' wives. Undersecretaries' mothers. A deputy comptroller who had somehow listed his mistress as his "household dependent, aged 31." The invasion was coming up the coast road and the capital's ruling class was selling itself passage on the diplomatic hull, and they hadn't even bothered to disguise it well. That was the thing about a dying state, Krein thought, dipping his pen. In its last season, hypocrisy stopped being a discipline and became a habit, sloppy as any other.

Berth thirty-nine was different.

Berth thirty-nine had been purchased in cash — or what passed for cash now, treasury scrip at a rate of exchange that made honest men weep — and purchased quietly, through a broker on Vlach Street whose name appeared nowhere on any manifest. The payment had been routed through an account held by the Office of Census and Registration.

Krein set down his pen and looked at that line for a long time.

An office of census and registration had no business buying ship berths. It was a clerical organ, a counting house, a place where tired men stamped tired papers about the living and the dead. It handled money, certainly — fees for amended records, fines for late registration, the small grubby revenues of bureaucracy — but those sums went into the general fund and out again on requisitions for ink and coal and the repair of the roof, which had leaked for nine years and would leak until the invaders fixed it or let it fall.

It did not buy passages on evacuation ships.

Unless someone inside it did.

* * *

He began, as he always began, with the structure of the thing. Who worked in Census and Registration? One hundred and six names. Of those, how many had authority to move funds without countersignature? Fourteen. Of those fourteen, how many had reason — family abroad, political exposure, debts, fear — to want off this sinking island of a city?

Seven.

He took the seven and did what the Bureau called verticals and what Krein privately called peeling. He pulled their personnel files, their ration histories, their lodging registrations, the census sheets from their own districts — oh, the delicious symmetry of it, running the census against the censors — and he laid the papers side by side on the dead undersecretary's desk and read them the way a butcher reads a carcass.

Six of the seven were nothing. Two were drunks, one was a widower with no dependents and no ambition, one was so senior he'd have been evacuated on merit months ago if anyone upstairs remembered he existed. Two more were loyalists of such dull, unremarkable fidelity that Krein suspected even the invading power wouldn't bother arresting them; they'd simply be absorbed, like furniture.

The seventh was Quentin Hale.

Krein had pulled Hale's file before, back in the autumn, when the audit orders first came down and the Bureau decided that irregular census entries might conceal deserters, draft-dodgers, foreign agents, and — the phrase in the directive that made Krein smile every time he read it — "persons of ambiguous civic existence."

Hale's file had been clean. That alone had interested him. Nine years in the registration service and not a single disciplinary note, not a late report, not a complaint from a superior, not even a reprimand for untidy handwriting. Men like that existed, of course — quiet, careful, gray men who moved through bureaucracies like fish through water, leaving no wake. But in Krein's experience, perfect cleanliness in a dirty institution meant one of two things. Either the man was genuinely incorruptible, which made him useless and harmless.

Or he was corrupt in a way no one had thought to look for yet.

And now here was his office's account, buying a berth on the last ship, through a broker on Vlach Street, in scrip, in secret.

Krein did not leap to conclusions. Leaping was for Dornik and the arm-breakers. Krein walked. He walked around the conclusion, examined it from underneath, tested its joints. A berth could have been bought by Hale for himself — but no, Hale's name was not on the passenger list, and a man buying escape does not buy it for a stranger while staying behind. Unless the stranger was worth more to him than his own skin.

A berth could have been bought for a woman. That was the oldest story in the registry of human stupidity, and Krein had read every entry in it. Wives, mistresses, daughters, sisters-in-law with conveniently vague surnames. He checked the manifest again. Berth thirty-nine had been registered to a name — one name, single traveler, female, age entered as blank, which the harbor clerk had lazily transcribed as a dash.

A blank age. On a document where every other line was filled in with the fussy precision of people desperate to be seen as legitimate.

Someone had bought that berth in haste, or in secrecy, or both, and had not dared to fill in the details. And the purchase had run through Census and Registration, where Quentin Hale had processed death records for eleven years and knew precisely how a name could be made to exist, or stop existing, on official paper.

* * *

In the morning — gray, snowing again, the city's fourth winter of war pressing down like a lid — Krein went to Vlach Street himself. Not with soldiers. With his hat and his stick and his face, which was his best instrument. It was a mild face, people told him. A reasonable face. The face of a schoolmaster or an actuary, someone you could complain to about the drains. He had spent thirty years polishing that face and he wore it now like a coat of many pockets.

The broker's office was above a chandler's shop, up a stair that smelled of tallow and river rot. The broker was a woman named Ilsa Merrick, fifty-ish, sharp-eyed, with the hands of someone who had once done honest work and now did profitable work instead.

"Ministry business," Krein said pleasantly, laying his credentials on her desk without letting go of them. "You facilitated the purchase of a passenger berth aboard the vessel *Meridian*. I'd like to discuss the transaction."

"I don't discuss client transactions."

"Naturally." He smiled. "Let's discuss it hypothetically, then. Hypothetically, the funds came from a government account. Hypothetically, the account belongs to an office that processes the births and deaths of citizens. And hypothetically, Inspector Aldous Krein of the Bureau of Internal Security is asking the questions — the same inspector, incidentally, who will very shortly be empowered under the emergency statutes to seize accounts, freeze assets, and refer brokers to the provost marshal. Hypothetically."

Merrick looked at him for a moment. He watched her do the arithmetic she must have done a thousand times in the last weeks — weighing loyalty against survival, discretion against the gallows, the code of her street against the new arithmetic of the occupation to come. He watched her arrive at the answer he already knew she'd reach. They always reached it. Loyalty was a luxury good, and the market for luxuries was collapsing along with everything else.

"The payment came through a runner," she said. "Scrip, sealed envelope, no letterhead. But the seal wax was ministry violet, and the hand that wrote the covering slip—" She paused, and something almost like regret crossed her face, professional courtesy toward a colleague in the trade of paper. "The covering slip was written in a registrar's hand. You can tell. Registrar's hands are trained to make letters uniform. Schoolteachers write pretty; registrars write identical."

"And the name attached to the berth?"

"No name was given. That was the arrangement. The buyer specified the berth number and the class and paid the premium for anonymity. But—" she hesitated, and Krein waited, patient as sediment, "—the runner who delivered the payment was recognized. He's a boy who runs errands around the government quarter. Someone said he carries for the census office. Delivers their requisitions, picks up their post."

"Thank you," Krein said warmly, gathering his credentials. "You've been hypothetical beyond the call of duty."

At the door she said, behind him, "They'll be gone soon, your lot. Everyone knows it. Whatever you're hunting — is it worth what it'll cost you when the tide turns?"

Krein considered the question honestly, which was more than most men would have given her.

"It isn't about worth," he said. "It's about order. A state may die. Its ledgers shouldn't be allowed to lie." And he tipped his hat and went down the tallow-smelling stair into the snow, feeling better than he had in weeks.

* * *

Back at the Bureau, he built the case the way he always built cases: slowly, in duplicate, with every seam visible to himself alone. He requisitioned the census office's requisition logs. He pulled the duty rosters for the week of the purchase. He obtained — through a favor owed him by a customs clerk whose brother Krein had once kept out of prison, a debt he hoarded like coin — a copy of the harbor master's supplementary register, the informal one, the one where clerks noted things too awkward for the printed manifest.

Against berth thirty-nine, in a clerk's pencil, faded: *paid anon. see Mr. H.*

*See Mr. H.*

Krein sat back in the dead man's chair and allowed himself, for exactly sixty seconds by the office clock, the luxury of satisfaction. It moved through him like brandy, warm and slow. There it was. After weeks of ash and rumor and the general stink of a republic eating itself, a thread, and the thread was fine and strong and led directly to a gray, careful, perfectly clean man named Quentin Hale.

But satisfaction, Krein knew, was a currency that devalued if you spent it early. He had watched eager men ruin good cases by moving at the speed of their excitement. No. The thread would hold. Threads held better than people did; threads had no families to warn and no consciences to trouble them.

What mattered now was the shape of the whole animal. Hale had bought a berth for an unnamed woman. Who was she? A wife? Hale's file showed a widower — wife dead eight years, no remarriage, one household dependent listed: a niece. Nell Hale, aged nine, orphaned. The niece was not on the passenger list either.

So not the niece. Not unless the girl had been registered under another name, and there it was again, that word, *registered*, turning over in Krein's mind like a coin catching light.

Because that was the other thing he had been pulling at, all these weeks, half-consciously, the way a tongue returns to a sore tooth. When he had begun the audit of census rolls in the autumn, looking for draft-dodgers and ghosts, he had flagged a cluster of anomalies in the western districts. Amended entries. Deaths recorded late. One household in the Tannery Quarter where the family register showed five souls, then four, then — according to the amendment slips — five again, a brother restored to life on paper some years after his recorded death, as though the registry had decided to raise the dead.

He had set it aside then, filed under pending, because the audit was broad and his hours were not infinite. But now the sore tooth sang. A registrar's office buying anonymous berths. A registrar's trained hand on a covering slip. A household in the Tannery Quarter where the dead walked about on paper wearing living rations. Somewhere in that tangle of amendments and erasures was the unnamed woman of berth thirty-nine, and somewhere behind her was the reason a careful man like Quentin Hale had finally left a fingerprint on the world.

Krein opened the pending file. The Tannery Quarter household. Surname Voss. Mother, seamstress. Sons, daughters — and there, the resurrection: a son marked deceased in the plague year and reinstated on the rolls three winters later, reinstated by an amendment countersigned—

He turned the page. Read the countersignature. Read it again, though once was enough, once would always be enough, because the signature was neat and uniform and identical in its letters, a registrar's hand, trained to make every Q close the same way.

Countersigned: Q. Hale, Census and Registration, acting on verification.

Krein closed the file with great gentleness, as one closes a door on a room where something precious is sleeping.

* * *

He allowed himself one indulgence that evening, and it was this: he walked past the census office on his way home, unhurried, a citizen among citizens, snow settling on the shoulders of his modest coat. The building was lit against the dark, windows fogged, and through the nearest pane he could see clerks bending over their benches, and one figure at the end desk near the window — a lean man, gray at the temples, working late, stamping papers with the steady rhythm of a clock.

Krein stood in the falling snow and watched him for a full minute, and felt the peculiar tenderness that hunters feel toward prey that does not yet know it is hunted. It was not cruelty, or not only cruelty. It was a kind of respect. Hale was the best kind of adversary: intelligent enough to be worth the chase, careful enough to have left only a single thread — and careless in exactly one place, one warm human place, which was all Krein had ever needed from anyone.

Everyone had one. A sister. A daughter. A dead brother walking around in borrowed papers. A berth on a ship bought in the dark. Love was the flaw in every ledger, and Krein had built a career on reading love's handwriting.

Tomorrow he would request Hale's full amendment log — formally, through channels, so that the request itself became a record, so that when the moment came there would be paper behind the arrest, weight behind the warrant. Tomorrow he would pull the harbor master's final manifest and match the Voss amendments against berth assignments. Tomorrow he would begin, quietly, to learn everything there was to know about a nine-year-old girl named Nell.

Tonight he simply stood in the snow a moment longer, committing the lit window to memory, and then he turned for home through the whitening streets of a city that did not know how few weeks it had left.

Behind him, in the fogged window, Quentin Hale went on stamping his papers, steady as a clock, unaware that somewhere in the dark a second clock had started — one that counted not the hours of the working day, but the small remaining distance between a careful man's secret and a patient man's hands.

By Thursday, Krein told himself. By Thursday he would have a name.

He got it sooner than that. Before midnight, going through the day's last folder with cold fingers and a warming heart, he found the harbor master's final correction sheet — one page, misfiled, dated three days after the original manifest — and against berth thirty-nine, in fresh ink, a name had been entered at last.

Not a Voss. Not a Hale.

The surname was one he had never seen before, belonging to a woman who, as far as every registry in the Republic of Velden was concerned, did not exist.

Krein smiled in the lamplight, alone among the sleeping files, and reached for a fresh sheet of paper to begin her file.
