## 18. Night at the Print Shop

The basement smelled of ink, candle wax, and forty years of other people's secrets.

Casper stood at the top of the stairs with her hand on the door, listening the way she'd learned to listen in her first year as a courier — not for sound, but for the absence of it. A street that was too quiet was a street where someone had told someone else to be quiet. But Ropemaker's Lane held only its ordinary midnight noises: wind worrying the shutter of the chandler's shop, a cart two streets over, the sea doing what the sea always did, patient and enormous, indifferent to which government claimed its harbor.

She went down.

Sova had lit six candles and arranged them around the composing stone like an altar, though he would have bristled at the word. He was seventy if he was a day, built like something the tide had left behind — knobby, weathered, immovable. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow and his fingers were black to the second knuckle. He worked without looking up when she came in.

"You're late."

"Checkpoints on Foundry Street. Two soldiers checking papers." She set her satchel down under the stairs where she could reach it in three seconds. "They're not looking for pamphlets yet."

"They're not looking for anything yet. That's how you know they're about to look for everything." He lifted a piece of type between finger and thumb, squinted at it against the candlelight, and slotted it into the stick with a click like a bone setting. "Lock the door behind you. There's a stool by the press. You'll want it. We're going to be here until dawn, and my back is older than your whole government."

* * *

The press itself was a monster from another century, iron and oak, bolted to the floor as if Sova had expected someone to try to carry it away. Maybe he had. The walls were stacked with paper — some wrapped, some not — and the shelves above held trays of type sorted by size and face, each tray labeled in a hand so small and precise it looked engraved.

Casper took the stool and did what she did best: nothing. She made herself furniture. A courier who couldn't be invisible wasn't a courier; she was a corpse waiting for paperwork. She watched the room instead. The window at street level, painted over but leaking a grey seam of lamplight. The drain grate in the corner. The second exit Sova hadn't mentioned but she'd noticed anyway — a coal chute behind the stacks, boarded over, the boards cut so they could be pulled free from inside.

He'd done this before. Many times.

"How many?" she asked.

Sova didn't stop setting type. "How many what?"

"Printers they've closed down. How many shops before this one?"

He considered the question while he worked, the way he seemed to consider everything — slowly, thoroughly, as if rushing were a form of lying. "Four," he said. "Two in Velden City. One in Brast. One I won't tell you about because you don't need to know and neither does anyone else." Click. Click. Click. "This one is number five, or it will be, if we're careless. So don't be careless."

"I'm never careless."

"No." He glanced up at last, and his eyes were pale and quick in the candlelight, younger than the rest of him. "You're the opposite. That girl telegraph clerk you brought me through — what was it, Tuesday? — she said you checked the street twice before you knocked and once more after. She thought it was funny." He turned back to the stone. "I think it's the only reason either of us is alive tonight."

The dispatch sat between them on the workbench, unrolled now, its decipherment spread across four sheets of cheap paper in Mira's careful clerk's hand — the sympathetic telegraph clerk who had spent one long afternoon with Casper in the signal office, decoding line after line while the lamps burned low and no one questioned a courier reviewing her sealed orders. *Ministers. Defense contracts. Sold.* It still read like a wound every time Casper looked at it.

Sova was building the front page out of those four sheets, letter by letter, column by column. It would take all night. Type was slow; truth was slower. Two hundred copies by morning if everything went right, folded into flour sacks before first light, distributed through a chain of hands Sova refused to name in front of her.

"You should sleep," he said. "You'll stand watch better at three than at midnight if you've slept at eleven."

"I can't sleep in rooms with doors."

That got his attention. He set down the typestick and looked at her properly for the first time, elbows on the stone, ink-blackened fingers laced together.

"Every courier I've ever met says something like that," he said. "It's usually the first true thing they tell me."

"It's not the first true thing I'll tell you tonight."

"No," Sova agreed mildly. "I don't imagine it is. People who come to a printer at midnight carrying treason in their coat lining generally have a great deal saved up." He picked the typestick back up. "Talk if you want. I'm good company for talking. I don't answer, and I don't repeat."

* * *

So she talked.

Not at first. For the first hour she just watched him work and kept her eyes moving across the painted window and the coal chute and the drain, and let the silence settle around her the way snow settles — softly, then deep enough to hold weight. Sova's hands moved with a rhythm that was almost musical: pick, squint, place, click. Pick, squint, place, click. The candle flames leaned whenever the door upstairs shivered in its frame, and each time, her heart performed the same small cold trick, and each time, the wind was only the wind.

But silence, in a room with a stranger and a crime, eventually becomes a kind of confession booth. She supposed that was why he'd offered it.

"My name isn't Casper Voss," she said.

Click. Pause. Click.

"The real Casper Voss died nine years ago," she went on. "My brother. Fever, the winter the garrison hospitals filled up. He was twenty-two. He'd just gotten his first ministry posting — junior records clerk, Department of Transport. They issued him his identity folio in October. Green binding, gold seal. He carried it everywhere like it was made of glass." She heard herself smiling and didn't recognize the smile. "He was so proud of it. Our mother sewed him a special pocket inside his coat for it. She sews. Shrouds, mostly, now. She used to do wedding clothes."

Sova placed a capital T. Click.

"When he died, the ministry sent a clerk to collect the folio. Standard procedure. Dead men don't hold posts. And the clerk — I found out later his name was Emeric Dall — took one look at our family, at the tenement, at my mother with her hands full of other people's laundry, and decided the paperwork could wait. Or disappear. He wrote Casper Voss down as *transferred, provincial posting, records pending*, and the folio stayed in a drawer in the Department of Transport, alive and well and drawing a salary that went somewhere it shouldn't have."

"And you?"

"Took it back." She said it flatly, without drama, because there was no drama left in it — it had been worn smooth years ago, like a coin passed through too many hands. "I was seventeen. My mother couldn't feed five people on seamstress wages and the rations were already shrinking. I went to the ministry myself, dressed in Casper's coat, with his folio, and asked for a courier's certification under his name. Couriers travel light. Nobody looks hard at couriers. The examiner looked at the seal, looked at me, and stamped the paper." She shrugged. "I've been my brother for nine years. I've signed his name so many times my handwriting matches his better than mine does. Sometimes I dream in it."

Pick. Squint. Place. Click.

"Dall," Sova said at last. "Is he the one in the dispatch?"

"One of them. Assistant Undersecretary for Procurement now. Nine years of skimming and selling, and the man has never once been audited, because audits require a subject who exists, and officially, Casper Voss is alive in a provincial posting that doesn't exist, filing reports that don't exist, drawn against funds that don't—" She stopped. Breathed. "He stole my brother twice. Once when he let him die on paper and once when he made him a fiction to hide behind. I intend for the pamphlet to take his name off the wall of that ministry if it's the last thing I do."

"And after?"

"After what?"

"After it's done. After the ministers are named and the tribunals come, if they come, if any of us are standing when they do." Sova lifted a line of type with both hands, checked it against the copy, and locked it into the forme. "What happens to Casper Voss then? Does the dead brother get to die properly? Do you get your own name back?"

She hadn't expected the question to hurt. It went through her ribs like a splinter she hadn't known was there.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "I've worn him so long I'm not sure what's underneath. When I say my own name in my head it sounds like a word from a language I used to speak." She laughed, short and unamused. "There's a census taker — Quentin Hale — he came to my tenement in the autumn. Routine verification. And he asked about the brother on my registration, and for one moment I was so certain I was going to die in that doorway, over a name on a list. And he looked at me, and he marked the entry *verified*, and he left. And later I found out why. He keeps his sister's daughter off the rolls himself. Orphan. Unregistered. A child who officially doesn't exist, hidden inside a man whose entire life is making lists of everyone who does." She shook her head slowly. "Two liars. Both fluent. That's how we found each other. Not love — recognition."

"Love comes later," Sova said comfortably, "if the recognition holds."

"Do you have someone?"

"I had a wife who set type faster than I do and a daughter who could spot a bad kerning at ten paces. The wife died of age, which in our trade counts as dying in battle. The daughter prints leaflets in the southern provinces under a name I'm not going to tell you." He smiled for the first time all night — a small, crooked thing. "You see? Recognition. It runs in families. Even invented ones."

* * *

At two in the morning she went up to take her turn at the street.

The arrangement was simple: she watched from the chandler's storeroom next door, through a gap in the shutters, while Sova locked himself in and worked. If anyone came — soldiers, inspectors, the neighborhood watch, anyone walking with purpose rather than drunkenness — she tapped the pipe that ran down through both buildings, twice, slow, and gave him ninety seconds to kill the candles and get out through the coal chute.

The lane lay blue and empty under a sky clearing toward frost. Her breath hung in front of the shutter gap. Somewhere beyond the rooftops the harbor bells counted the half hour, and she found herself counting along, the way she counted everything now — berths, hours, names, lies. Forty civilian seats on the *Meridian*. Five in her family. One berth bought with money that could hang them all. An arithmetic that never came out even, no matter how many times she ran it.

Her mother's hands. Tomas's stubborn jaw. Nell's map of the harbor, drawn in a child's pencil, pier by guarded pier. Quentin's face in the tea house when he'd said *I lied for you because I know what it costs to lie for someone*. All of them balanced on a single sheet of paper being assembled letter by letter in a basement below her feet.

She tested the pipe with one knuckle. Solid. Ready.

And in the quiet, because there was no one to hear, she practiced saying it.

"Casper Voss." Her own voice sounded strange forming the words she'd borrowed for nine years. Then, softer, almost ashamed: "*Voss.* Just Voss. The way it was before."

The name sat in the frozen air and wouldn't warm.

She was still standing there when she saw the lantern.

It came down Foundry Street at a walk — not the stagger of a drunk, not the trot of a patrol late to its rounds, but a measured, deliberate pace, the lantern swinging at a man's knee. Behind it, three more shapes. Uniformed shapes. They stopped at the corner of Ropemaker's Lane, and the lantern rose, and the man holding it consulted something in his other hand — papers, she thought, or a map — and then the light swung slowly down the lane, past the shuttered baker's, past the cobbler's, past the chandler's, pausing.

Pausing at the paint-sealed window of the cellar below her feet.

Twice, slow. She rapped the pipe before she'd finished deciding to. Below her boots, faint as a heartbeat, she heard the scrape of a stool, the snuff of candles, the soft iron song of a press cover going down.

The lantern crossed the lane. Boots crunched frost. Four shadows gathered outside the print shop window, and the tallest of them raised the light to the painted glass — and through the gap in her own shutters, three buildings down, Casper Voss, courier, forgery, ghost, watched Inspector Aldous Krein tilt his head like a man reading a document he intended to sign.
