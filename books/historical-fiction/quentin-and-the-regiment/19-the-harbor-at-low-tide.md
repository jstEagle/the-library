## 19. The Harbor at Low Tide

The tide went out at four in the afternoon, and that was when the harbor told its secrets.

Quentin hadn't known this. He had lived in the capital his whole life and had visited the waterfront perhaps a dozen times, always in daylight, always on errands, always looking at ships rather than water. It took a nine-year-old to teach him that the mudflats were the honest part of the city — that when the sea withdrew it exposed everything the piers were built to hide: broken pilings, sunken crates, the rusted skeleton of a dredge nobody had bothered to salvage, and the long stone spine of the old customs wall that ran out beneath Pier Six like a backbone.

"That's it," Nell said.

She stood at the rail of the fish market's back gallery, mittened hands hooked over the iron, breath fogging. Below her the flats gleamed grey-brown under a low sky, stitched with the tracks of gulls. She had drawn all of it before she'd ever seen it — he'd found the map folded inside her arithmetic book three nights ago, inked in careful pencil: the piers numbered, the guard posts marked with little triangles, the patrol routes drawn as dotted lines with times written beside them in her small, exact hand.

He should have been alarmed. A child memorizing patrol schedules was either a prodigy or a catastrophe waiting to be reported. But Nell did not do things carelessly. She had inherited from her mother — from Marren, dead two years now, God keep her — a talent for watching without being watched. Quentin recognized it because it was his own talent too, the census taker's gift, the ability to stand in front of people and see straight through them while they saw only a clipboard.

"Where?" he asked.

"There." She pointed past Pier Five, where the Meridian would dock when she came — if she came; no one outside the ministry knew the schedule for certain, but everyone knew she was coming, the way you know a storm is coming by the smell of the air. "See the gap? The soldiers change shift at the end of the pier at six and six. Both shifts walk off together. For maybe four minutes there's nobody at the head of the pier but the gangway officer."

"Four minutes," Quentin said. "How do you know they both leave?"

"Because I counted." She said it the way other children said *obviously*. "I sat by the chandlery door for three days. Mama taught me counting games. I made it a game."

Three days. His niece had spent three days alone on the waterfront counting soldiers, and he had been at the office filing death registrations for men who would never need them, believing himself the careful one in the family.

* * *

They walked down among the flats, because Nell insisted the map wasn't finished until she'd seen it from below. The mud took their boots with a sucking sound, and the cold came up through the soles like something alive. Quentin kept glancing at the pier above them — the sentry boxes, the searchlight rig, the naval ensign hanging slack — but at low tide they were invisible, tucked into the shadow of the customs wall, and he understood suddenly why smugglers had loved this shore for three hundred years.

"This is where we'd go," Nell said, crouching, tracing the base of the seawall with a gloved finger. "If the papers are wrong again. If they don't let me on."

"Nell—"

"If they don't let me on," she repeated, patient as a teacher, "then Uncle Quentin carries me along here, under the wall, to the third ladder. There used to be fishing boats moored past the breakwater. If even one is still there, I can row. Papa let me row."

"You can't row out of a harbor under searchlights."

"I can row in the dark. The lights only come on after seven." She looked up at him then, and her face in the flat grey light was her mother's face, and it broke something in him that he would have to carry quietly for the rest of whatever time remained. "I'm not going to be left behind, Uncle Quentin. I've done the sums. There's forty berths and more than forty names, and the ones who paid get on first. So I made a different sum."

"What sum?"

"The gap sum." She stood, wiped her gloves on her coat, and produced the pencil stub she kept — like everything else she kept — in some hidden pocket of her person. In the margin of the map, which she unfolded against the seawall, she wrote: *6:00–6:04. Head of Pier Six. One officer. One gangway list.* And beneath it, in letters smaller than her smallness: *A girl who isn't on the list can be a girl who is, if the man with the list looks away for four minutes.*

Quentin stared at it. Four minutes. A forged line on a manifest. A child walking up a gangway on the strength of a glance and a lie.

It was exactly what he had done for Casper Voss in her tenement doorway — mark the entry verified, look away, let the living pass as the dead. He had told himself it was mercy. Standing in the cold mud with his niece's arithmetic in his hands, he understood it was also craft, and that the two had never been separate in him at all.

"We're not deciding anything today," he said. "Today we only watch."

"I know." She refolded the map. "But knowing where is how you decide later. That's what Papa said about fires. You learn the doors before there's smoke."

* * *

They climbed back up by the chandlery stairs and bought roasted chestnuts from a brazier cart, because Nell was nine and had been counting soldiers all week and deserved chestnuts, and because standing at a brazier gave them a reason to loiter with a view of the pier. The seller was an old woman with a face like a walnut and a voice pitched to carry no further than the coins.

"Heard you asking after boats," she said, without looking up.

Quentin went very still. "We weren't."

"The girl was. Tuesday. Asked my son which ladders were sound." The woman turned a chestnut with iron tongs. Her eyes stayed on the coals. "I didn't answer her. But you should know the press gangs started working the docks Monday night. They take men off the street for the wall crews. My son doesn't go out after dark anymore, and he's thirty-one and has a limp."

Nell's hand found Quentin's sleeve. He felt her grip through the wool — small fingers, fierce pressure.

"Thank you," Quentin managed.

"Don't thank me." She dropped hot chestnuts into a paper cone and held it out. "Eat those and walk toward the tram stop like a man going home. That's free advice too."

They ate and walked, and Quentin matched his pace to the rhythm of a man with nothing on his mind, while every nerve in him catalogued the street: the pair of gendarmes outside the harbor office, bored, stamping their feet; the navy launch idling off Pier Three with a petty officer scanning the flats through glasses; the poster, fresh-pasted on the kiosk, announcing in blocky official type that ALL ABLE-BODIED MEN AGED 16 TO 45 ARE HEREBY DIRECTED TO REPORT FOR LABOR SERVICE IN DEFENSE OF THE CAPITAL.

Defense of the capital. The capital that was negotiating its own surrender in rooms none of these men would ever enter. They were building walls for a government that had already sold the ground the walls stood on. Quentin thought of the ledger he'd seen in the Ministry of Last Things — the boarding order for the Meridian, berth by berth, each name followed by figures that could only be prices — and felt the old disillusionment turn, slowly, into something harder and more useful.

Anger, he decided, was a kind of clarity.

* * *

That evening he spread Nell's map on the table of their rooms and weighted the corners with cups, and Nell narrated while he copied it fair — a cleaner version, in his own hand, because a child's pencil work could be innocent but a copy in a grown man's writing was evidence if it was ever found, and he wanted the evidence to point at himself and nowhere else.

"Pier Six," she recited, kneeling on a chair to watch. "Two sentry boxes at the head. Shift change six and six. Gangway officer sits at the second box, keeps the manifest in a leather folder. There's a stove in the box — I saw the pipe. He'll be cold. Cold men look at stoves."

"Cold men look at stoves," Quentin repeated, writing it down. It sounded absurd and was entirely true; he had spent years reading clerks across desks, and he knew exactly how a cold man's attention moved.

"And the wall ladder is the third one, past the coal yard. Second rung is cracked but the rest hold. And—" she hesitated, and for the first time all day the certainty flickered. "Uncle Quentin. What if there's no boat past the breakwater?"

"Then there is no boat, and we make another sum."

"With what?"

He set down the pen. Outside, the city was going dark the way it went dark now — district by district, ordered, rationed, the streetlamps dead since November. Somewhere south a gun had been firing all day at intervals too irregular to be practice and too distant to fear properly, a sound the whole city had learned to hear without hearing.

"With whatever's true," he said. "That's all anyone works with now. Whatever's true and whoever you love. Everything else got burned months ago."

Nell considered this with the seriousness she brought to sums. Then: "Miss Voss," she said. "Your friend from the tea house. Is she making sums too?"

The question caught him off balance, which questions from children reliably do. "I think she's been making them longer than we have."

"Is she good at them?"

"She's very good at them," Quentin said. "Better than me, probably. She counts things the rest of us pretend aren't countable."

"Like what?"

Like berths, he almost said. Like brothers who died twice. Like the price of a place on a ship, written in a ministry ledger in a neat clerk's hand. Instead he touched the top of her head, between the braids her mother would have braided better.

"Go wash for supper," he said. "Tomorrow we check the ladder."

* * *

He did not sleep well, which was normal now, and rose before dawn, which was becoming normal too, and went out into the blue-black morning to walk the last stretch of the harbor road alone, because there was one thing on the map he wanted to verify without his niece beside him: the coal yard.

It sat between the customs wall and Pier Five's warehouses, a yard of black timber sheds behind a fence of salt-swollen planks, and according to Nell's observations the night watchman there was old and half-blind and warmed himself at a brazier at the far corner, leaving the gate unchained between rounds. Quentin confirmed all of it in twenty minutes of standing in a doorway pretending to wait for a tram that had stopped running weeks ago. Old watchman, brazier, unchained gate. The empire's vigilance, in miniature: a hundred thousand details half-tended by tired men, holding only because no one had yet pushed hard enough to find out.

He was turning away when he saw the wagon.

It came along the quay road at a trot, canvas-covered, escorted by four gendarmes on foot, and it stopped at the mouth of the fish market lane — the lane he and Nell had used yesterday. Not a supply wagon; supplies came at midday, and anyway the escort dismounted wrong, moving in pairs, checking doorways, the way men move when they are hunting rather than delivering. One of them carried a list. Even at this distance, in the bad light, Quentin could see the paper catching the lantern as the man turned it, ticking down the column.

Press gang. Working the morning streets now, not just the night. Working the lanes near the harbor, where the men they wanted — strong backs, no powerful families, no one to write letters — came early to unload fish.

His first thought, shamefully, was for the map in his coat: if the wagon searched anyone, the fair copy would hang him. His second thought, arriving a heartbeat later and drowning the first, was of every man on this quay aged sixteen to forty-five, and of Tomas Voss — Casper's brother, the young one, the one who worked the warehouse nights and walked home along this same quay road in the grey mornings, whistling, because he was nineteen and nineteen-year-olds believed the world noticed other people.

The wagon rolled on down the lane. The gendarme with the list spoke to the market porter, who shook his head and pointed — pointed east, toward the warehouses, toward the night-shift men just coming off.

Quentin ran.

He ran the way he had not run since school, coat flapping, boots loud on the cobbles, past the brazier woman hauling up her shutter who called something after him that he didn't hear, past the gendarmes at the harbor office who shouted and were ignored because the body in flight makes its own argument. The quay stretched out ahead, grey and endless, and somewhere along it a young man was walking home whistling, and the arithmetic of the morning had already changed: the gap at the head of the pier, the ladder past the coal yard, the four minutes at six o'clock — all of it suddenly worthless beside one new number, the number of minutes since the wagon had turned into the lane.

He reached the warehouse corner with his lungs on fire and stopped dead, hands on knees, because the lane beyond was empty except for a single dropped cap — a worker's wool cap, dark blue, lying in the gutter with the easy, abandoned look of a thing that had fallen in a hurry — and because from the far end of the lane came the sound of the wagon starting up again, heavier now, slower, carrying what it carried.

And walking toward him out of the warehouse shadows, whistling, capless, oblivious, came a tall young man with his sleeves rolled and flour or grain dust white to the elbows.

Tomas Voss.

Quentin's relief lasted exactly as long as it took to understand why the cap lay in the gutter. They hadn't taken Tomas. They had taken someone — the man whose cap that was, the man who'd run — and the wagon would circle the quarter all morning, and the lists would grow, and the lanes would close one by one, and every hour the city tightened like a knot around the throats of the people Quentin had promised, silently, in the mud below Pier Six, to get onto that ship.

Tomas saw him and lifted a hand, grinning. "Hale! What are you doing running about at this hour? You look like a man chased by ghosts."

"Where's your sister?" Quentin said.

Tomas's grin faded. "At home, asleep, I expect. Why? What's happened?"

Quentin looked past him, down the empty lane, at the cap in the gutter. The wagon was gone. The gulls were screaming over the flats where the tide was turning, coming back in, covering everything again.

"Get her," he said. "Wake her, bring her to the tea house on Cordwainer Street, and don't let him—" he stopped himself, rephrased it fast, because Casper's cover, her borrowed life, her dead brother's name, hung over every sentence like frost. "Don't let your family walk anywhere alone today. Not the quay, not the market, nowhere. Do you understand me?"

"No," Tomas said honestly. "What's happened?"

Quentin picked the cap out of the gutter and held it, and couldn't say. Behind them, at the mouth of the lane, the wagon reappeared at a trot, coming back around, the gendarme with the list scanning the waking street — and Quentin stepped sideways, casually, deliberately, putting his own body between the list and the young man beside him, the way you step between a child and a door with smoke behind it.

"Walk with me," he said, "and I'll tell you everything."
