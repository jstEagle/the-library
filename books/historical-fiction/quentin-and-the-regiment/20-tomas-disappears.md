## 20. Tomas Disappears

The morning Tomas disappeared began like any other in the last winter of the Republic of Velden — with bread that was mostly sawdust and a mother who pretended it wasn't.

Casper stood at the stove in her mother's kitchen on Ropewalk Lane, turning the flat gray loaves so they wouldn't burn on the bottom while the tops stayed pale. Her mother sat mending by the window, working a needle through the collar of a coat that belonged to a dead man. That was the trade now. The shrouds had been steady work since autumn; the coats came after, brought in bundles by neighbors who no longer said the names of the men they'd belonged to. They just handed them over and took the receipt for the mending fee and left.

Tomas was still asleep. He'd come in late from the harbor the night before, smelling of tar and cheap brandy, and he'd laughed too loudly about something Casper hadn't caught. Nineteen years old and laughing too loudly. She should have paid attention to that. She would spend the rest of her life knowing she should have paid attention to that.

"Eat something," her mother said without looking up.

"I ate."

"You turned bread over a fire. That's not eating." But she didn't push it, and Casper didn't sit down, because there was a dispatch to deliver by ten and a second one by noon, and the ministries didn't wait for anyone to eat, least of all couriers carrying the paperwork of a government's surrender.

She was buttoning her grey coat when Tomas shuffled out of his room, hair flat on one side, eyes still soft with sleep.

"There's a berth," he said.

Not *good morning*. Not *is there any coffee*. He'd been thinking about it all night; she could see it in his face, the way the thought had hardened into something with edges.

"We're not talking about this here," Casper said quietly.

"Mother's right there."

"Then let's not talk about it at all." She pulled her gloves on. "I'll be back before dark."

"Cas." He caught her sleeve at the door. Up close he smelled of the harbor, of rope and cold iron, and under that, faintly, of the brandy. "One berth. Five of us. You know what the arithmetic says. I'm nineteen. I'm strong. I can work anywhere they land. Mother's hands are bad — you've seen her drop the needle. Nell is a child. You're the one with papers worth something, and I'm—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I'm the one who's most replaceable. Say it out loud and we can stop pretending we haven't all done the sum."

"The sum has four wrong answers in it," Casper said. "Nobody in this family is replaceable. Least of all you."

"That's sentiment, not arithmetic."

"It's both." She pulled her sleeve free, gently. "Keep your strength. You may need it for something better than drowning."

He laughed — too loudly again, that same note from last night — and went to steal a heel of the sawdust bread, and she walked out into the snow feeling the shape of him standing in the doorway behind her, and that was the last whole memory she ever kept of that morning: Tomas in the doorframe, one hand raised in a lazy salute, mouth open around some joke she never heard the end of, because the door closed between them.

* * *

The city was quieter than it had been a week ago, and louder than it would be tomorrow. That was how the collapse moved — not as a roar but as a subtraction. Shops shuttered one by one. The tram on Vosk Street ran only until two in the afternoon now, because there wasn't coal enough for more, and the drivers had stopped ringing the bell, as if silence might make the schedule less shameful. Soldiers stood at corners in ones instead of threes, which meant either confidence or desertion, and nobody could tell which.

Casper made her deliveries. The first went to the Ministry of Ways, where the clerk who signed for it didn't look up from a newspaper whose headlines were all in smaller type than they'd been a month ago — the regime shrinking its own language along with its territory. The second took her past the fish market, and it was there, wedging through the crowd with her courier's satchel flat against her hip, that she heard it.

A whistle. Not a police whistle — those had a particular two-note fall she knew from years of stepping aside for patrols. This was shorter, sharper, repeated three times, and it came from the direction of the piers, and it was followed by shouting that had a practiced rhythm to it, the cadence of men doing a job they'd done many times.

Press gang. The word arrived in her mind fully formed, the way words do when they've been waiting.

They'd been sweeping the docks for a month, everyone said so. Conscription officers with quota sheets, taking men off the quays and out of taverns and once, famously, out of a funeral procession, marching them to the transport yards for the coastal defense batteries that were losing guns faster than they could name replacements. It was a rumor with weight to it, the way rumors get weight when everyone knows someone. But the sweeps had stayed south of the customs basin. They hadn't come near Ropewalk Lane. They hadn't come near Tomas.

The whistles sounded again, closer, and then she heard the other sound — the sound she would hear in dreams for years afterward — boots on wet cobblestone, a lot of them, moving fast.

She dropped the delivery schedule. Not literally — the paper stayed in her satchel — but in every way that mattered she dropped it, turned her back on the Ministry of Interior, and ran toward the water.

The fish market was dissolving ahead of her, stalls overturned, a woman gathering herrings off the cobbles with both bare hands, an old man standing very still against a wall with his eyes shut like a man waiting out weather. At the mouth of Pier Street a squad of conscription officers was moving in a loose line, and behind them, being marched in a huddle of eight or nine, were men from the docks. She looked for Tomas among them the way you look for a face in firelight — desperately, uselessly, seeing him everywhere and nowhere. A tall one. A young one. A coat with a torn shoulder seam her mother had mended twice.

The line of men was already at the corner. Already turning toward the transport yard. And then — she couldn't have said afterward what told her, some tilt of a head, some set of shoulders she'd known across dinner tables for nineteen years — she knew. Third from the end. Hands bound loosely in front with cord. Not struggling. Walking with his chin up and his mouth set in the expression he wore when he'd lost at cards and refused to admit it.

"TOMAS!"

Her voice cracked across the street like ice giving way. His head came up. Their eyes met for half a second — half a second in which his face did something terrible and tender, surprise and apology and *go home, Cas, don't be stupid* all at once — and then a conscription officer stepped between them, broad, gloved, bored.

"Courier business?" the officer asked. His eyes flicked to the grey coat, the satchel. Even now, even here, the uniform of the state opened doors and closed mouths.

"My brother," Casper said. "You can't—"

"Can't what?" The officer's boredom sharpened into something almost polite. "Conscription decree, article eleven. All able-bodied males sixteen to thirty-five present within the dock district are liable. Your brother was present. Your brother is able-bodied." He consulted a slate hanging from his belt, running a thumb down chalked columns. "Voss. T. Voss. Age nineteen. Occupation, rigger. There he is." He tapped the chalk. "There's the law of it. Clean as you like."

"He's my family's only—"

"Everyone's someone's only." The officer was already turning away, waving the column forward. "Take it up with the muster office at Fort Halven. Appeals take six weeks."

Six weeks. The Meridian sailed in eleven days. The Republic itself might not last six weeks. Casper stood in the trampled snow of Pier Street and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the arithmetic she'd been refusing had just solved itself in the cruelest possible way. One berth. Four of them now. The question hadn't dissolved — it had detonated.

The column disappeared around the corner toward the transport yard. Somewhere beyond the rooftops a ship's horn sounded, long and low, and the fish market began, slowly, to reassemble itself around her, vendors righting their stalls, the woman with the herrings counting her losses, the whole city performing its daily resurrection from small disasters.

Casper did not go to the Ministry of Interior. She went home.

* * *

Her mother took the news the way stone takes a hammer — no movement at first, then a single fracture line running through everything.

"Marta," Casper said, and couldn't finish it, and didn't need to, because her mother had gone white in a way that had nothing to do with the winter light, and her hands had stopped above the sewing, needle suspended, and then the needle went down into her thumb and she didn't notice, and Casper crossed the room and took both those ruined, capable hands in hers and held them, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

"They took him off the street," her mother finally said. Her voice was level, which was worse than screaming. "Off the street like a stray dog. Like a sack of coal."

"Yes."

"And you're going to get him back."

It wasn't a question, but Casper answered it anyway. "Yes."

Her mother nodded once, slow, and pulled her hands free, and picked up the needle, and went back to the dead man's coat. That was the household grammar of grief: keep the hands moving or they'll find your throat.

By evening Casper had learned the shape of the thing. She spent the hours between lunch and dark doing what she did best — moving through the city's paper veins, listening at the counters of offices that processed human beings as entries. The transport yard at Fort Halven held three hundred conscripts, give or take, awaiting shipment to the coastal batteries in lots of fifty. Muster rolls updated nightly. Releases required either a medical exemption stamped by a garrison physician, a deferral authorized by the Ministry of War, or removal from the roll entirely.

Removal from the roll entirely.

Casper stood in the freezing courtyard of the Records Annex, watching clerks hurry between buildings with lanterns, and felt the old familiar knowledge settle over her like her grey coat: the state was made of paper. It had always been made of paper. Men marched off to die because a line of chalk on a slate said their name, and lines of chalk could be smudged, and names could be traded, and somewhere in this building was the roll with *Voss, T.* on it, and somewhere else was a version of events in which that line had never existed.

She had given up her brother's identity once before, in a sense — had worn it herself for years, had lived inside his name the way you live inside a borrowed coat. She knew exactly what a name weighed. She knew what removing one cost.

The problem, she thought, walking home through streets going blue with early dark, was that she had exactly one identity's worth of leverage left, and it was the identity keeping her alive.

* * *

She found Quentin Hale after nightfall, in the tea house on Lamplight Row where they'd first talked honestly, sitting alone at the corner table with a census ledger open in front of him and a cup of tea gone cold beside it. He looked up when she sat down across from him, and whatever was in her face made him close the ledger immediately.

"Tomas," she said.

She told it plainly, because plainness was all she could afford: the press gang, Pier Street, the column of men, the officer with the slate, article eleven, Fort Halven, six weeks for appeals, eleven days until the Meridian.

Quentin listened without interrupting, hands folded, and she watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, watched it land on him the same way.

"The muster rolls for Fort Halven route through the central registry," he said slowly. "I've seen them. They cross my desk for reconciliation against the civil population lists — that's how they catch men hiding in the city. Every conscript gets reconciled. If a man's on the military roll, they check the civil roll, and if there's a discrepancy—" He stopped.

"If there's a discrepancy," Casper said, "someone comes asking questions. Like a census taker asking about a dead brother."

"Like me." Quentin rubbed his eyes. "Cas, listen to me. What you're thinking—"

"Do you know what I'm thinking?"

"I think you're thinking about the roll. About taking his name off it." He lowered his voice further, leaned in. "And I'm telling you that roll is guarded now. Krein's people audit it. After the manifest irregularities, after the berth bought with ministry money — Krein has clerks crawling over every register in the annex. You touch that roll and you don't just erase a line, you leave fingerprints on a document that a man like Aldous Krein is personally motivated to examine."

"Then I need better fingerprints than his clerks."

"That's not—" He exhaled. "That is not how it works, and you know it isn't, and I know you know because I taught you half of what you know about records, God help me."

"Then teach me the other half."

For a moment he just looked at her, lamplight catching the tired lines of his face, and she saw the war in him — the lawful man and the forger of souls, the clerk who had marked her entry *verified* with a stranger's lie still wet on his pen. She saw him lose. She watched him lose, and loved him a little for how much it cost.

"There's a transfer protocol," he said at last, very quietly, as if the walls might file a report. "Men deemed unfit for service get struck from the active roll and transferred to the invalid register pending medical review. The striking order needs two signatures — a muster officer and a registry clerk. The muster officer at Halven is a drunk named Pelder who signs whatever crosses his desk if you bring it to him after the third bottle. The registry clerk is the problem. Registry clerks answer to the audit chain now. Krein's chain."

"So we go around the registry clerk."

"You can't go around a signature, Cas. The order is void without it."

"Then we forge the signature."

The word sat on the table between them like a knife laid flat. Quentin stared at it. When he spoke again his voice had changed — steadier, lower, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge and finding it was a stair.

"I've been copying official signatures for six months to keep a dead woman's daughter alive," he said. "One more won't damn me any further. But understand what you're buying, Casper. Struck from the active roll, transferred to invalids — that takes Tomas off the transports, yes. It also puts him on a different list. Invalid registers get reviewed too. And if Krein audits the transfer order — and he will, eventually, because he audits everything — the trail leads backward. To Pelder. To the forgery. To whoever filed it." His eyes came up to hers. "To you. Or to whoever's name is on the filing slip. Which brings me to the real question."

"Which is?"

"Whose name will you put on the slip?" He turned the ledger around so it faced her, open to a blank page. "Because it cannot be yours. Yours is already burned into every register in this city — courier, sister, berth-buyer, ghost. If Krein finds your hand on a conscription forgery the same week he's hunting a ministry berth bought with stolen funds, he won't need to suspect you. He'll simply arrive."

Casper looked at the blank page. Somewhere across the dark city Tomas was sleeping on straw in a transport yard, bound for a coastal battery and a war the government had already sold. Somewhere north, a ship waited with forty civilian berths, one of them bought with money that would hang them all if traced. And here was a blank page, offering the oldest transaction in the republic's dying vocabulary: a name, traded for a life.

"Nell's father," Casper said.

Quentin went very still.

"Your sister's late husband," Casper said. "He exists on paper. He's dead in fact. He's of conscription age on paper and medically unfit in fact, and no one living will ever contest a filing slip in his name except a man who is himself a forger of six months' standing." She met his eyes. "You taught me half of what I know, Quentin. Give me the other half. Give me the hand that writes like a registry clerk."

Outside, the wind drove snow against the tea house windows in long hissing gusts, and somewhere far off toward the harbor a ship's horn sounded again, patient as fate.

Quentin uncapped his pen.

"One condition," he said. "When this is done — when Tomas is out of that yard — you stop. No more rolls, no more registries, no more ghosts. Because there's a third signature coming, Cas, sooner or later, and it's Krein's, and when it comes I want us both to be people the record can't find."

She reached across and turned the page toward him, blank and waiting, white as the snow filling the street outside.

"Write," she said.
