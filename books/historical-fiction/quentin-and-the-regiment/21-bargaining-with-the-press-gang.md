## 21. Bargaining with the Press Gang

The press gangs worked the docks in the last hour before dawn, when the lamps along the quay burned low and the fog came off the water thick enough to swallow a man whole. Casper had seen them twice from the upper windows of the shroud-maker's shop on Vell Street — grey-coated sergeants with ledgers under their arms and soldiers at their backs, moving through the fish market like men collecting debts.

They had taken forty-one names off the rolls that week. She knew the number because Quentin still had friends inside the census office who passed him the daily tallies folded into bread wrappers, and because her mother had begun sewing shrouds in batches of ten.

Tomas was seventeen. On paper he was sixteen, because their mother had shaved a year off his registration when he was born, the way mothers did in wartime, banking time against exactly this. But the new conscription orders didn't read birth years anymore. They read *able-bodied males between fifteen and thirty*, and they read them loosely, and the sergeants carried no records at all, only ropes and arithmetic.

He had been missing since Tuesday morning.

* * *

She found the trail by thinking like a courier.

A man taken off the street was not lost; he was filed. The gangs did not drag their catches straight to the transports moored beyond the breakwater — there were too many escapes that way, too many mothers clawing at the gangplanks. First they were marched to the customs shed on Pier Four, where a clerk entered them into a conscription roll, gave them a tin tag stamped with a number, and locked them in the holding pen behind the salt stores until the transport's complement filled. Forty men to a pen. Two pens a day. The ships left when the holds were full, and Velden's army, retreating east through the passes, ate men the way a fire ate timber.

Which meant that if Tomas had been taken Tuesday, he was either still in the pen at Pier Four or already gone, and there was one road to finding out which: you walked up to the clerk's window and asked, and asking required papers, and papers required a name.

Casper stood across the boulevard from the customs shed in the blue hour before dawn, her grey courier's coat buttoned to the throat, and took stock of what she carried.

In the lining, sewn flat against the wool beneath her left arm, the final dispatch from Handler Merrick — the codes she had half deciphered, the proof that could hang six ministers, wrapped now around her ribs like a second heart. That stayed. Nothing short of her own death would move it.

In her inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth: her brother Emil's identity book.

Emil had been dead nine years — drowned in the canal ice at fourteen, a boy's stupid accident, a hole in the world their mother never let close. But Emil's papers had lived on. Casper had used them for three years to hold the ration card that fed five people, the work permit that kept her mother's stall legal, the lodging registration that kept the census — until Quentin Hale, with his careful eyes and his careful mercy — from looking too hard. The dead brother was the roof over all of them. Every lawful thing the family possessed stood on a dead boy's name.

And now she walked toward the customs shed intending to spend it.

* * *

The queue outside the inquiry window was short at that hour: two women with laundry lists, an old man disputing a harbor tax. Casper joined it and let the cold settle into her bones while she rehearsed.

The clerk at the window was young, chapped-lipped, wearing a soldier's greatcoat over civilian clothes — a conscript himself, probably, given a desk instead of a rifle because he could write. Behind him the shed stretched back into lamplit shadow: shelves of ledgers, a pot-bellied stove, the smell of tar and brine. Through a barred door at the rear she could see the edge of the holding pen, and beyond the pen, in the grey light, the shapes of men standing shoulder to shoulder, breath rising off them like steam off cattle.

"Tomas Voss," she said when her turn came. "Taken yesterday morning near the fish market. He's my brother. I need to know if he's been entered."

The clerk looked up. His face had the flat patience of someone who answered this question forty times a day. "Name?"

"Voss. T-O-M—"

"I heard you." He pulled a ledger toward himself, licked his thumb, turned pages. Casper watched his finger run down columns of names and numbers and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very still, the way it went still when she crossed a checkpoint with contraband against her skin.

"Voss," the clerk said. "Tuesday, fish market sweep." He ran his finger down, stopped. "Number 3114."

Her knees nearly went. She put a hand on the sill and made it look like leaning. "Then he's here. In the pen."

"He's here," the clerk agreed, "until the transport fills. Thursday night, most likely. Then he's on the list for the eastern draft." He said it the way a man reads a train timetable.

"There's been a mistake," Casper said. "He's not of age. His registration says sixteen."

"Registration doesn't matter. New order. Fifteen to thirty, able-bodied. The doctors sort them at the depot." The clerk was already reaching for the next petitioner's paper. "Next."

"Wait." Her voice came out lower than she intended, and the clerk paused, hearing something in it. She leaned closer to the bars of the little window. "There's a procedure. For release on error. There has to be. My brother is deaf in his left ear — he failed the militia physical two years ago, it's in his file at the district office. A man with a discharge notation can't be drafted. It's in the regulations."

It was not true. Tomas's hearing was perfect. But she had spent three years memorizing regulations so she could move through ministries like weather through a room, and she knew the shape of the sentence that made clerks hesitate. The clerk hesitated.

"There's a form," he admitted. "Release on documented exemption. Needs the exemption certificate presented at this window before the transport manifest closes."

"Then I'll bring the certificate."

"You'll bring it today. Manifest closes at six." He looked at her then, properly, taking in the grey coat, and something shifted in his face — recognition without understanding, the look officials gave couriers, the neutral respect owed to anyone wearing the state's livery. "You're a courier."

"I'm a courier."

"Couriers don't carry other people's papers."

"No," Casper said. "We carry what we're told." And she stepped back out of the line before he could ask anything else, because the truth was already climbing up her throat and she needed somewhere private to swallow it.

* * *

There was no certificate. Of course there was no certificate. What there was, was Emil.

She understood the trade completely by the time she reached the end of the pier and stood looking at the black water. The exemption would have to be documented, stamped, entered — and the only identity book in her possession that could bear such an entry was a dead boy's, and once the entry was made, the fiction would collapse under its own weight. You could not claim an exemption for a man whose death record sat in another ledger in another hall. Sooner or later some clerk would cross-reference. Sooner or later the whole careful architecture of borrowed life would come down, and when it came down, it would come down on whoever's name was written in it.

So the choice was simple, the way arithmetic was simple.

Give them Emil — give them the name she had worn like armor for three years — and the name would be spent, burned, unusable forever. No more ration card. No more work permit. No more registered lodger at 14 Vell Street. Casper Voss, who did not exist on any roll except the one Quentin had marked *verified*, would become a woman made entirely of air. No papers meant no berth on the Meridian. No papers meant no legal self at all. If the soldiers stopped her on a corner, she was nobody, and nobodies in Velden that winter went where the state pointed them.

Keep Emil, and Tomas boarded a transport on Thursday night for the eastern front, where the casualty returns were running four in ten, and the snow was deep, and the ministers she carried proof against had sold the rifles those boys would die waiting for.

She thought about her mother's hands, quick on the needle, sewing shrouds in tens.

She thought about Tomas at fourteen, standing in the canal shallows trying to pull Emil's body out of the ice, screaming until his voice broke, and how he had never once said Emil's name aloud again, and how she was about to make him the reason the name died a second time.

She stood at the end of the pier until her face was numb, and then she went to find a stamp that would say what she needed it to say.

* * *

Sova's basement printer smelled of ink and candle smoke and, tonight, of fear, because Sova had heard the press gangs working Vell Street after midnight and had moved his type cases against the wall ready to flee. When Casper came down the stairs he looked up from the composing stick in his hands and read her face in the way old radicals read faces — quickly, and assuming the worst.

"You need something forged," he said.

"I need a medical exemption certificate. Discharge notation, district stamp, dated two years past. Deafness, left ear. For Emil Voss." She laid the identity book on the workbench between them. "And it needs to be good enough to survive one clerk's glance and one sergeant's signature. Not longer. One hour of credibility, that's all I'm asking."

Sova set down the composing stick. He picked up the identity book and turned it over in his cracked fingers, and Casper watched him understand it — watched him see that the photograph was hers, that the birth year had been inked, that this was the document that kept a family alive, that she was asking him to help her burn her own bridge while standing on it.

"This is your protection," he said.

"This is my brother's name."

"It's the same thing, girl. It's been the same thing for three years." He opened the book, studied the watermark against the candle, and shook his head slowly. "I can do it. I've got a district seal from the Third Ward, retired, but the wax pattern's right. The medical hand I'll have to fake — I know the format, I set type for a clinic once, before." He looked up at her. "But hear me. Once this stamp touches this page, the book is dead. Any cross-reference kills it. You'll be carrying a corpse in your pocket, and the corpse will be you."

"I know."

"Do you? Because last week you told me the Meridian takes forty civilians, and you told me your family needs five seats, and you told me—" He stopped. His hands, she noticed, were trembling slightly, though his voice was steady. Old printers' hands, stained black to the knuckle, shaking anyway. "You're spending your own exit. You understand that. Whatever berth you win, whatever place you buy — you won't be able to board it. A woman with no papers can't stand at a gangway. She can only run."

"I know," Casper said again, and this time it came out flat and final, and Sova heard the flatness for what it was and nodded once, the nod of a man who had spent fifty years watching people walk into fires for each other and had learned not to argue with the arithmetic of love.

He lit a second candle. "Then sit down and tell me everything about this district office. The stamp date, the doctor's initials, the file numbering. If we're going to lie to the Republic of Velden one last time, we're going to lie to it properly."

* * *

They worked through the morning.

Casper dictated details she barely knew and invented the rest with a courier's precision — the Third Ward medical office used blue-black ink, the examining physicians initialed in Cyrillic-derived shorthand left over from the old imperial schools, the file numbers ran by district and year with a letter prefix she had seen on a hundred documents passing through her hands. Sova cut the stamp impression into a soft block of cork with a heated needle, swearing softly under his breath, and pressed test after test onto scrap until the wax pattern matched the sample page Casper had copied from memory. By noon the certificate lay drying on the bench: official, banal, damning in its ordinariness. *Exempted from military service. Deafness, left ear, congenital. unfit for field deployment.* A dead boy's name above a live woman's forgery, sealed with a stamp that hadn't existed in six years.

Sova blew out the candles and sat back, rubbing his eyes.

"Now listen to me," he said, "because I'll only say this once, and then we never speak of it. When you walk out of that shed with your brother, you walk away and you don't look at the book again. You don't tuck it safe. You leave it in the clerk's hands if they want it, you let it enter their files, you let it die in the archive where it belongs. Because the moment it comes back to you, you'll start using it again — a ration line, a checkpoint, just once, just this once — and the first clerk with a memory will hang you with it." He slid the certificate and the identity book across the bench to her. "Dead names stay dead. That's the price. That's always been the price."

Casper picked up the book. Emil's face looked out of the photograph — her face, younger, unlined, a girl of nineteen wearing a dead boy's future like a coat. Three winters she had warmed herself in that name. Rations bought. Doors opened. A census taker's questions survived.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me." Sova was already turning back to his type cases, his shoulders hunched, his voice roughening. "Thank me when the pamphlets are on every barracks table in this city and the men who sold us are in chains. Until then I'm just an old man forging paper in a basement, same as I ever was. Go get your brother."

* * *

The customs shed at five o'clock was louder, meaner, fuller. Sergeants moved through the queue with the transport deadline pressing on them, and the holding pen at the rear had grown a second layer of prisoners, men standing on crates to see over the crowd. Casper joined the inquiry line with the certificate inside her coat, against the lining, next to the dispatch that could hang a government, and discovered that her hands were perfectly steady.

That steadiness frightened her more than trembling would have. Somewhere in three years of carrying other people's secrets she had learned to do terrible things calmly, and she understood, standing in that queue, that she would never unlearn it.

"Release on exemption," she said at the window, and slid the certificate and the identity book under the bars together.

The clerk — the same chapped-lipped boy, or his twin; they all looked alike by the third day of a sweep — examined the certificate, checked the stamp against a reference sheet, checked the initials, checked the date. One minute. Ninety seconds. The longest minute of Casper's life, and she had once spent twenty of them in a ministry corridor while a colonel read her sealed dispatch upside down.

"Third Ward," the clerk said finally. "Deafness." He glanced up at her, and there was something almost human in his tiredness. "He doesn't look deaf."

"The ear," Casper said, "is not the part that shows."

He grunted. He stamped the certificate, entered the release in the roll — she watched him draw a single line through *Voss, T., 3114*, one stroke of a pen unwriting a soldier — and pushed a chit through the window. "Presentation desk, rear gate. He signs out, he goes home, he stays indoors during sweeps. Next time the gangs take him there's no second miracle." Then he held out his hand, palm up. "The book. Identity verification requires the original retained pending audit."

*The book.*

There it was. The moment Sova had warned her about, arrived without ceremony, and she felt her fingers close around the oilcloth-wrapped shape in her pocket and refuse, for one full second, to move. Emil's face. The ration card. The roof. Three years of being someone, even someone stolen.

She thought of Tomas's voice breaking on the canal ice.

She took out the identity book and placed it in the clerk's palm, and the clerk dropped it into a wire basket with forty others, and that was all. No drumroll. No weighing of souls. The greatest theft of her life, committed against herself, processed in four seconds by a bored boy with chapped lips.

"Rear gate," he said. "Next."

* * *

Tomas came through the rear gate at quarter past six, blinking, unshaven, smelling of salt and panic, with the tin tag still hanging from his collar. He saw her and stopped dead, and for a moment neither of them moved, and then he crossed the distance in three strides and gripped her arms hard enough to bruise, and Casper held her seventeen-year-old brother against her coat with both arms and felt him shaking.

"How," he said into her shoulder. "Cas. How. They don't release people. Nobody gets released."

"Our brother," Casper said, "was deaf in his left ear. Congenital. It's all in his file."

Tomas went rigid. He pulled back and looked at her, and she watched him arrive at it piece by piece — the certificate, the name, the cost — because Tomas was not stupid, and because he had spent nine years not saying Emil's name and therefore knew exactly where it lived.

"Cas." His voice was very low. "What did you give them?"

"Nothing that wasn't already theirs." She took the tin tag from his collar and snapped the cord and dropped it in a puddle. "Come on. Curfew's at eight and Mother hasn't eaten since yesterday. We're going home."

They walked east through the darkening streets, past shuttered stalls and patrol lamps, and Tomas kept glancing at her sideways the whole way, wanting to ask and afraid of the answer, and Casper walked with her empty hands in her pockets and the dispatch flat against her ribs and nothing else — no name, no book, no legal shadow anywhere in the city — and felt strangely, terribly light, the way she imagined a snail must feel a heartbeat after losing its shell.

At the mouth of Vell Street she stopped. Under the broken lamp by the cobbler's, a notice had been pasted to the wall since noon, one of the regime's broadsheets, and in the torchlight she read it properly for the first time.

*CENSUS AUDIT — ALL RESIDENTS MUST PRESENT REGISTRATION PAPERS AT DISTRICT HALL WITHIN SEVEN DAYS. UNDOCUMENTED PERSONS WILL BE DETAINED PENDING IDENTIFICATION.*

Seven days. Detained pending identification. She had no papers. She had no identification to present. Somewhere in a wire basket in the customs shed, a dead boy's book was already filed against her, and in seven days the streets themselves would become the interrogation.

Tomas followed her gaze to the notice and read it, and she heard his breath catch.

"Cas," he whispered. "What are you going to do?"

Down the street, past the shroud-maker's darkened windows, a figure was coming toward them through the fog with a lantern held high and a canvas satchel over one shoulder, walking fast, walking like a man who had been searching doorways all evening — and even before the light reached his face, some part of Casper already knew it was Quentin Hale, and that whatever had brought him out at curfew with a bag on his shoulder was bad news wearing a familiar coat.
