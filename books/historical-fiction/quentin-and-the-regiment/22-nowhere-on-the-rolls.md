## 22. Nowhere on the Rolls

Quentin Hale had spent eleven years teaching himself to read paper the way other men read faces. A census roll told him things its writers never intended. The ink of a lie dried slightly differently than the ink of truth — a fraction too much pressure, a hesitation in the downstroke. He could find a forged entry in a thousand the way a shepherd could find one lamb gone wrong in a flock at dusk.

Which was why, on the third morning after Tomas Voss came home, Quentin stood in his own office at the census hall and understood that Casper Voss had ceased to exist.

He'd gone looking for her deliberately. It was not sentiment — or not only sentiment. Krein's auditors were working through the harbor manifests, and a berth bought with ministry funds left a trail, and everyone connected to that berth would soon be a name on someone's list. Quentin wanted to know what list she was on. He wanted to warn her.

She wasn't on any list. That was the horror of it.

He started with the ration registers, because rations were the state's most jealous record-keeping. Every soul in the capital drew flour and coal against a registration number, and the ledgers were updated weekly by clerks who feared nothing so much as an unbalanced column. He ran his finger down the Voss entry for their district — mother, Tomas, the two younger sisters, all present, all stamped. Casper's line had been struck through in red.

Not crossed out. *Struck.* The formal cancellation mark, used only when a registrant was proven dead, deported, or duplicated. Beneath it, in the auditor's neat hand: *Identity consolidated under deceased male registrant. See annex 44-C.*

Annex 44-C was the conscription correction file. He requested it through channels, because requesting it through channels was what a man like Quentin Hale did, and the clerk fetched it without curiosity, and there it was: the trade. One dead brother's papers, surrendered. One living woman, erased from the rolls in exchange. The press gang had taken Tomas's name onto their conscription list; Casper had bought him off it with the only currency she possessed, which was herself.

The transaction had been legal. That was the part that made Quentin's hands go cold on the desk's edge. Somewhere in the ministries, a clerk had processed her erasure with the same efficiency he brought to updating grain quotas, and had probably gone to lunch afterward feeling pleased with the tidiness of it.

He checked the ship manifests next, telling himself he wouldn't find her and needing to be wrong. Berth thirty-nine, purchased under the name Anton Voss — the dead brother's name, resurrected one last time as a vessel for a living woman's hope. But a berth belonged to a registered person, and registered persons were cross-checked against the rolls before embarkation, and Casper Voss no longer existed to be cross-checked. When the Meridian's passenger list was finalized, berth thirty-nine would be declared void. Reassigned, most likely, to some official's mistress or some minister's second cousin. The arithmetic of collapse always found room for the well-connected.

She couldn't board the ship. She couldn't draw rations. If she were arrested, she couldn't even be charged properly — you could not try a person who did not exist; you could only detain her indefinitely as an anomaly, and Quentin knew what happened to anomalies in the custody of Aldous Krein.

He sat back in his chair and looked at the gray light coming through the frosted window and felt something crack open in his chest that he didn't have time to examine.

*You alone know she exists.*

The thought arrived without drama, the way true thoughts do. Her mother knew a daughter, but the mother believed the sacrifice had saved only Tomas, not understood its full price. Sova might know a courier. But the state — the vast, indifferent machinery of paper that decided who ate and who sailed and who was real — held no record of her anywhere. She had walked into the machine to save her brother and the machine had swallowed her name.

Quentin closed the annex file, straightened his desk out of habit, put on his coat, and committed the small treason of leaving his office two hours early.

* * *

The print shop occupied the cellar of a shuttered chandlery off Salt Lane, and you found it by the smell of hot lead and lamp oil before you found it by any door. Quentin had been there twice — once to deliver Nell safely during the worst of the patrols, once to bring bread and a warning. Sova answered his knock the third time with a pistol held low and courteous, then lowered it when he saw who it was.

"You again." The old printer stepped aside. His eyes, white-ringed and sharp behind smudged spectacles, went past Quentin's shoulder to the empty stairwell. "Alone? Good. Fewer people know this door every day."

"Is she here?"

Sova's expression did something complicated. "Who?"

"Sova."

"She was here." The old man jerked his chin toward the back of the cellar, where type cases rose in wooden cliffs and the hand press sat under its canvas shroud like a sleeping animal. "Two nights ago. Set half a page of type with me, slept four hours in the corner there, left before dawn saying she had errands. Hasn't been back." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his apron, which made them no cleaner. "I assumed she'd gone to ground with her family."

"She can't," Quentin said. "Her family thinks—" He stopped, recalibrated. How much did Sova know? Enough, apparently; printers in basements knew everything eventually. "Her registration's cancelled. She traded her identity for her brother's freedom. As far as the state is concerned, there is no Casper Voss."

Sova put his spectacles back on and looked at Quentin for a long moment over their rims.

"And you're the census man," he said quietly. "The one man in this city whose whole trade is knowing who exists." Something that was almost a smile moved across his ruined face. "Then I expect you also know where a person goes when she has nowhere the law will admit to."

"I have an idea," Quentin said. "I'd like to be wrong about it."

But he wasn't wrong.

* * *

He found the tenement room first — her old room above the chandler's row, rented under the brother's name these three years. The landlady, a stooped woman with a face like a closed ledger, told him the tenant hadn't come back since the arrest trouble at the docks. "And good riddance, if you ask me. Room's needed. There's families coming in from the river districts every day." She said it without malice, the way people said everything now. The city was full of displacements, each one reasonable, none of them anyone's fault.

He tried the tea house where they'd had their wary conversation a lifetime ago — weeks ago, really, though it felt geological. Closed. Plywood over the windows, a municipal notice pasted to the door announcing requisition for military use. He tried the harbor, walking the length of the quay in falling dusk, scanning faces for grey courier's wool, and saw instead the press gangs working the taverns with their lanterns and their lists, and understood how completely a city could become a place where every doorway was either a shelter or a trap.

It was nearly midnight when he admitted where he should have looked first.

The print shop's back room was barely a room — a crawl space behind the paper stock, walled with bales and lit, when it was lit, by a single candle stub Sova permitted for emergencies. Quentin came down the stairs quietly, out of courtesy more than stealth; Sova had heard him from the street, obviously, and simply nodded him through.

In the candlelight, folded into the gap between two bales of newsprint like a letter slipped into an envelope, Casper Voss was asleep.

She looked smaller than he remembered. She had always seemed to him a person built entirely of competence — the grey coat, the level eyes, the way she could walk through a checkpoint as if the checkpoint ought to apologize for delaying her. Asleep, all of that was packed away somewhere, and what remained was a thin woman in a coat too heavy for her, one cheek pressed against coarse paper bales, her hands tucked up under her chin like a child conserving warmth. Her boots were off and set neatly beside her, toes aligned, because even in ruin she was orderly. There was flour dust in her hair. Or perhaps it was ash. In this city, lately, the two were indistinguishable.

On the floor beside her head lay a scrap of paper with figures on it — sums, crossings-out. Ration arithmetic, he realized. What a non-person could buy, which was nothing. What a non-person could eat, which was whatever kindness fell within reach.

Quentin stood in the doorway and did not move, because moving felt like a violation, and standing felt like a cruelty, and both were better than the alternative, which was turning around and leaving her to wake up alone in a cellar with no name.

He must have made some sound after all — a breath, a shift of weight, something — because her eyes opened. Not slowly, not with the soft confusion of ordinary waking. They opened the way a sentry's eyes open, already aiming. For one full second she was coiled to run, and then the second passed and she saw who it was and something in her face performed a small collapse, quickly repaired.

"Quentin." Her voice was rough with sleep. She sat up, pushing her hair back, assembling herself piece by piece. "What are you—"

"The landlady's letting your room," he said. "I went there first."

"I know. I watched her carry out my mother's mending basket yesterday." She said it evenly, reporting weather. "It's done, then. You've seen the rolls."

"Yes."

"Struck through in red?" A ghost of a smile. "I always wondered what color they'd use."

"Casper—"

"Don't." She raised one hand, and it was steady, and that steadiness hurt worse than weeping would have. "Don't do the voice. I've had two days to make peace with it and I've mostly succeeded. I traded a name for a brother. Names are renewable. Brothers are not. It was good arithmetic."

"It was terrible arithmetic," Quentin said, "because the name bought the berth, and now there's no name to claim it with. Berth thirty-nine is going to evaporate the moment the manifests are checked. You've lost yourself and gained nothing."

"I gained Tomas." For the first time her voice cracked, just at the seam. "Do you know what the press gangs do with dock boys? Do you know what the northern works are? He's nineteen. He would have lasted a year, Quentin. Maybe less. I'd burn ten names for that. I'd burn yours."

"I know," he said softly. "That's what frightens me about you."

She laughed — one short, broken note — and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and in the candlelight Quentin saw her shoulders shake exactly twice before she mastered them, and he understood that this was what her courage cost, paid in private, in installments, in cellars, where no one was supposed to see the ledger.

He crossed the room and sat down on a paper bale beside her. Not touching. Near. Nearness was the whole vocabulary he trusted himself with.

"My sister died in the fever winter," he said. "Eight years ago. And I falsified her death registration — delayed it, altered it — so that Nell could keep drawing orphan support under a household that officially still included a mother. I've been committing forgery on behalf of a child ever since. Every quarter, one careful lie, renewed like a subscription." He looked at his own hands. "So when I tell you I understand what you did, I'm not offering sympathy. I'm confessing membership."

"You covered for a stranger before you ever knew that about me," she said. "The census. My dead brother. You marked it verified and walked away. Why?"

He considered lying, considered some deflecting kindness, and discarded both. She had earned precision.

"Because you looked at me the way I look at my own reflection on audit mornings," he said. "Like a person waiting to find out whether the paper world was about to swallow them. And I thought: there but for one honest answer go I." He paused. "Also because your hands were shaking and you hid it beautifully, and I have never in my life admired anything so much."

She turned to look at him then, fully, and the candlelight caught the wet sheen in her eyes that she would deny existed, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the press stood shrouded in the dark beyond the partition like a patient witness.

"I don't exist," she said at last, wonderingly, as if testing the shape of the sentence. "Eleven years carrying sealed paper for a republic, and I'm not on a single roll. I can't buy bread. I can't board a ship. If Krein takes me, there's no habeas corpus for a ghost." Her jaw set. "And the codes are still in the lining of this coat, and Sova still has half a page set, and apparently the republic can erase me but it can't erase what I know."

"No," Quentin agreed. "It can't."

"So what does a person who doesn't exist do next?"

Quentin reached into his coat and drew out the thing he had carried all evening without quite admitting why: his own census notebook, the pocket-sized register he used for field verification, bound in black oilcloth. He opened it to a fresh page, uncapped his pen, and wrote, in his best official hand:

*Casper Voss. Verified. Residence: this address. Occupation: witness.*

He showed it to her. It was absurd. It was a child's game of government. It was worth precisely nothing in any court or ministry in the Republic of Velden.

She read it twice. Then she did the thing that undid him — she didn't laugh, though her mouth trembled toward it; she pressed her fingers briefly against the ink, as though checking whether it was real, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it was yours.

"Verified," she repeated. "By the only registrar left in this city I'd trust."

"It won't get you bread," he said.

"No."

"It won't get you onto the Meridian."

"No."

"But," Quentin said, and here he leaned forward, and the exhaustion in his own voice surprised him with its intensity, "it means one person in this city keeps your records. And I am very, very good at keeping records. So here is what happens next. You sleep — actually sleep, in a bed, because I'll take the watch and Sova will pretend he doesn't approve. Tomorrow we solve berth thirty-nine. There are ways to reassign a berth to a name that exists, and I spend my days among men who corrupt paperwork for profit, which means I know exactly how corruption moves through a form." He capped the pen. "You gave away your name to save your brother. Fine. Then borrow mine for a while. Hales look after their own."

Casper looked at him for a long time. Outside, distantly, a patrol whistle sounded and faded, the city breathing its anxious breath.

"You should go home to Nell," she said finally, gently, the way you decline a gift you can't afford.

"Nell," Quentin said, "told me this morning that I've been sad lately and that I should go find you, and then she went back to drawing her maps. Nine years old. Frighteningly perceptive. She drew you into the harbor map, you know. Little grey figure by pier six, labeled *the lady who knows ships*. She'll want a full report." He stood, knees cracking, and offered her his hand. "Come and sleep in a real room. We'll say you're my widowed cousin from the coast. I have a spare cot and a great deal of practice explaining family members who aren't officially real."

She took his hand. Her grip was strong despite everything — a courier's grip, a survivor's grip — and she used it to pull herself upright, and then, unexpectedly, she did not let go at once.

"Quentin. If Krein is auditing the manifests, he'll find berth thirty-nine, and he'll trace it, and when he traces it he'll find you standing between me and him. You have Nell. You should walk away from this while your name is still clean."

"My name," Quentin said, "has been dirty for eight years, one careful lie at a time, and it is the only reason a little girl eats regularly. Clean names are for people who've never had to protect anyone." He squeezed her hand once and released it. "Get your boots."

* * *

They came up the cellar stairs together into the blue hour before dawn, and Sova looked up from his type case, spectacles flashing, and said nothing, which from Sova was a benediction. At the top of the stairs Quentin paused with his hand on the door, and Casper stopped beside him, and for a moment they stood in the cold draft listening to the city — the far-off boom of artillery from the river front, nearer now than last week, everyone noting it and no one saying it.

"One condition," Casper said quietly. "Whatever we do about the berth — whoever ends up sailing — the pamphlet still gets distributed. All of it. Even if it costs us."

"That was never in question," Quentin said. "But Casper — the distribution plan requires a courier who can move through checkpoints unnoticed. Someone invisible. Someone the rolls have no record of, whom no patrol sheet will flag, because officially there is no such person to stop."

She stared at him. Understanding dawned slowly across her tired face, and with it something that was almost — almost — light.

"You're suggesting," she said, "that being erased might actually be useful."

Quentin pulled open the door onto the frozen street. "I'm suggesting," he said, "that the republic made one serious mistake tonight. It deleted you from its books. Which means that starting tomorrow morning, everything you do is officially impossible — and I intend to help you do all of it."

Somewhere across the rooftops, the sky over the harbor was beginning, very faintly, to turn the color of struck-through ink.
