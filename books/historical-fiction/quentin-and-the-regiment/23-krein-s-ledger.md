## 23. Krein's Ledger

Aldous Krein had a theory about corruption that he never spoke aloud: it was not a disease of the state but a form of housekeeping. Every government kept two ledgers — the one it showed the public and the one it actually lived by. The second ledger was not a scandal. It was the furniture. It was how the rooms were arranged.

His problem was that he had spent eleven years in the Ministry of Internal Verification being told he was furniture himself, and he had decided, somewhere in the last six weeks of snow and census rolls and shipping manifests, that he was done being furniture.

He dressed carefully for the meeting. Not well — well would read as ambition, and ambition in Velden's final winter was a smell that got men reported to their own subordinates. He dressed *correctly*. Grey suit, mended at the left cuff because everyone knew he had mended it. The ink stain on his right middle finger, permanent now, a kind of uniform. He looked like what he had always been: a man who counted things for other men who spent them.

The summons had come on Tuesday. A card, cream-colored, no seal. *Director Halven requests the pleasure of Inspector Krein's attendance, Thursday, nine o'clock.* The word *pleasure* doing its usual work, which was none.

Nine o'clock meant they wanted him nervous. Men summoned at nine were given time to imagine all night. Men summoned at seven were still buttoning their coats when they arrived. He understood the grammar of it. He had written enough of these cards himself.

He arrived at eight fifty-two and let them make him wait nineteen minutes in an anteroom where a clerk pretended to file things and actually watched him. He sat with his hands folded and his face arranged into mild administrative patience, and inside that face he rehearsed nothing, because he had stopped rehearsing years ago. Rehearsal was for men with something to lose. He had only something to trade.

* * *

Director Halven received him in an office that had once been impressive and was now merely heated. The carpets were rolled back from the windows — shell glass, someone had said, after the November shelling of the river district — and the bare floorboards gave the room the feel of a stage cleared between acts. On the desk: tea, untouched. A folder, closed. A revolver, holstered, which was itself a message; Halven was the sort of director who wore his sidearm to interviews the way other men wore their decorations.

"Inspector." Halven did not rise. "You've been busy."

"I've been thorough," Krein said. "There's a difference. Busy is what happens to you. Thorough is what you do."

Halven smiled the smile of a man who found proverbs useful mainly as targets. "And what have you done?"

Krein took out his own folder — thinner than Halven's, deliberately, so that the comparison would do its work — and laid it on the desk without opening it. He let it sit there between them like a fourth person at the interview.

"In October," he said, "I began a routine audit of the census rolls in the Third and Seventh Districts. Routine is the correct word. I want to be clear about that. I went looking for clerical error and I found a pattern."

"A pattern."

"Forty-one entries verified by a single census taker. Quentin Hale, Seventh District. Each entry verified against documentation that does not exist or has been altered. Three of the forty-one are outright fabrications — persons registered as living whose deaths are recorded elsewhere, or persons registered under names belonging to the dead." He paused. "One of those names belongs to a boy who died in the Tyne fever of '31. Casimir Voss. The registration currently claims him alive, resident, employed. The woman using his papers calls herself Casper Voss. She is, I believe, his sister."

Halven opened the folder at last. Krein watched his eyes move down the page and knew exactly what he was reading: the cross-references, the dates, the small beautiful machinery of it. Krein was proud of this work. That was the honest truth about himself, the one he permitted himself at night — he was proud. Anyone could find a lie. Finding a lie required only suspicion. But finding forty-one lies braided together across four years of paper, each one supporting the next, each one placed by a careful hand — that was authorship, and reading it back was almost as good as writing it.

"And the courier," Halven said. "The woman. Why does she interest my office?"

"Because she doesn't interest yours yet," Krein said, "and she should. She carries diplomatic dispatches. Sealed ones. She moves through six ministries a week, and her identity is a fiction I could unravel with one afternoon and a death registry." He folded his hands. "But that isn't why I'm here."

"No?"

"No." Krein leaned forward slightly. "Here is what else the audit turned up, Director, and here is why I requested this meeting rather than filing my report through channels."

He opened his own folder and turned it around.

"The civilian manifest of the *Meridian*. Forty berths. Thirty-seven assigned as of last Friday. I pulled the payment records — or rather, I pulled the absence of payment records, which is more instructive. Eleven of the thirty-seven berths were purchased through accounts traceable to ministry discretionary funds. Two through a shipping brokerage owned by the brother-in-law of Deputy Minister Arl. One berth — number twenty-two — was paid for in cash, but the cash passed through the same countinghouse, within the same week, as a withdrawal authorized under Undersecretary Brandt's signature. And berth thirty-nine—"

He watched Halven's face while he said it.

"—berth thirty-nine was purchased with funds drawn against a verification office disbursement account. My own department's account, Director. Signed with a countersignature that belongs to Deputy Director Ostrer."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a tram bell rang, absurd and domestic, a sound from a country that still believed it had mornings ahead of it.

Halven picked up the page. Set it down. Picked up his tea, remembered it was cold, set it down again. Krein catalogued each gesture the way he catalogued everything — automatically, completely, the way other men breathed.

"You understand," Halven said finally, "what you're describing."

"I understand precisely what I'm describing. I'm describing the evacuation of the republic's leadership, purchased with the republic's money, arranged through channels that will not survive scrutiny — except that there will be no scrutiny, because in three weeks the ships sail and in four weeks the archives drown, and every man on that manifest will be sitting in a neutral port telling stories about how he tried to save the country."

"That's treason."

"That's bookkeeping," Krein said. "Treason requires an enemy. These men required only a broker. The enemy simply provided terms." He let that sit. Then, softly, the way one sets down something fragile: "I have no interest in treason, Director. Treason trials are theater, and theaters burn. What I have is a list. And lists are portable."

* * *

They walked afterward. Halven suggested it himself — "these walls have ears, and lately some of them have mouths" — and they went out into the frozen garden behind the ministry building, two men in grey coats walking a gravel path between dead hedges, breath hanging in the air like punctuation.

Krein had played this walk in his head for three days and had concluded that Halven would offer him one of three things: money, silence, or inclusion. Money was an insult and Halven was not stupid. Silence was a threat, and threats required credibility Halven no longer had — a director who threatened a man holding a list was a director advertising how badly he wanted the list gone. Which left inclusion.

"You're a careful man, Krein," Halven said, watching the path rather than him. "Careful men survive. That's rare right now."

"Careful men also get blamed," Krein said. "When the tribunals come — and they'll come, if not here then wherever the government reconstitutes itself — the record will need a villain with a low rank. Auditors are excellent villains. We hold the pens."

"And what would you prefer to be?"

"Berthed," Krein said.

Halver laughed — one short bark, surprised out of him. Then he stopped laughing, because he understood.

"There are no berths."

"There are three unassigned. Forty minus thirty-seven. And there are at least four men on your own floor who would surrender theirs if your office suggested their continued presence posed a security risk." Krein's voice stayed level, almost bored, a clerk reciting inventory. "I don't need generosity, Director. I need arithmetic."

They walked another ten paces. The hedges clicked in the wind, dry stems knocking together like teeth.

"Hale," Halven said. "The census taker. You want us to move on him."

"I want you to authorize me to move on him. There's a distinction that will matter later. Your signature starts the process; my name appears nowhere until the arrest is effected. When it's over, Hale is in custody, the falsified records are sealed as evidence, and the Voss woman — the courier — becomes a loose thread that any competent investigator pulls." He paused at the corner of the path, where the hedge broke and the river showed through, grey ice with grey water moving under it, the city's whole condition in miniature. "And when she's pulled, Director, whatever she's carrying in those dispatches comes into our hands before it comes into anyone else's."

"You think she's carrying something."

"I think couriers at the end of wars don't carry orders. Orders stop mattering. They carry insurance." Krein turned back toward the building. "Someone taught her that. Someone in one of your ministries taught her that, which means someone in one of your ministries knows what she has, which means someone in one of your ministries is afraid of her, which means—" he spread his hands, an open book, a balanced column "—she is the most valuable undocumented person in this capital. And we know her address."

* * *

The authorization came back Thursday evening, signed, stamped, real. Krein read it twice at his desk with the lamp turned low, and felt — he examined the feeling honestly, turning it over like a coin — very little. Satisfaction, perhaps, in the way a lock feels satisfaction. Something had aligned. Something had turned.

He should have felt triumphant. He had traded a modest catch for a seat off a drowning ship; every instinct of self-preservation he had refined over eleven years had been honored. By Monday he would have Quentin Hale in a room, and by Wednesday he would have the full architecture of the forgeries, and somewhere inside that architecture was the courier, and inside the courier was whatever the ministries feared.

Instead, sitting there in the green lamplight with the authorization in his hand, Krein found his attention snagging on the wrong detail — the way an auditor's mind snags on wrong details, because wrong details are how ledgers confess.

Berth thirty-nine.

Ostrer's countersignature. He had put it in the report himself; it was the strongest single item, the hook on which the whole manifest inquiry hung. But tonight, going back through his notes, he checked the date on the disbursement against the date of the *Meridian*'s assignment list, and the numbers refused to sit straight. The berth had been purchased on the ninth. The funds had moved on the eleventh. Two days apart — which happened, occasionally, sloppily — except that Ostrer was not sloppy. Krein had audited Ostrer's expense accounts for six years. The man initialed his pencil stubs.

Which meant either the berth had been bought first and funded after — a loan, an arrangement, someone fronting money for someone — or the berth had been bought by whoever controlled the account before Ostrer ever touched it.

Krein sat with that for a long time. Outside, the snow had started again, fat slow flakes coming down through the lamplight of the yard, erasing the paths one by one.

It didn't matter, he told himself. It didn't change anything. He had his berth pending, his arrest authorized, his thread to pull. The rest was noise beneath the music.

But he wrote the discrepancy in his private notebook anyway — the small black one he kept sewn into the lining of his coat, because a man who hunted other men through their paper was a fool to keep no paper of his own — and he noted the date, and the amount, and the two-day gap, and beneath it, after a moment, he added a single line in his cramped auditor's hand:

*Find who held the account before Ostrer.*

Then he closed the book, put on his coat, and went out into the snow to arrange the arrest of Quentin Hale — never suspecting, even then, how far the trail from that little black notebook would run before winter ended, or whom it would lead him to on a quay at dawn, with a ship pulling away and a cipher key changing hands in the space between one heartbeat and the next.
