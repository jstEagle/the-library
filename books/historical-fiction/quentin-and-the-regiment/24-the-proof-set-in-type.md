## 24. The Proof Set in Type

The press finished its last revolution at four in the morning, and the silence afterward was so complete that Casper could hear the candle burning.

Two hundred sheets lay stacked on Sova's worktable, still smelling of ink and lamp oil. Two hundred copies of a document no court had authorized and no ministry would survive — *The True Account of the Defense Contracts of the Republic of Velden*, set in eight-point type on paper thin enough to fold into a coat pocket or swallow with water if it came to that. Casper had not known until tonight that she had memorized every line. She could have recited it blind. She had carried it against her ribs for eleven days before it ever touched type.

Sova wiped his inky hands on his apron and stood over the stack the way a man stands over a sleeping child he is not entirely sure he wants to wake.

"Count them again," he said.

"I counted them twice."

"Count them again."

She counted them again. Two hundred, in twenty bundles of ten, each bundle tied with plain twine because anything finer would tell a searcher this was treasure. "Two hundred."

"Two hundred," Sova repeated. He said it like a diagnosis. "In a city of four hundred thousand."

"It's enough."

"It is never enough." He pulled off his spectacles and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. He was sixty-one years old, and he looked it tonight; the candlelight found every line in his face. "Thirty years I have printed things the state forbids. Pamphlets about bread prices. Pamphlets about the war. Once, a poem. Do you know what happened after the poem?"

"No."

"Nothing." He laughed, short and dry, and put the spectacles back on. "Nothing happened. That was the lesson. You print the truth small enough, and the truth does nothing at all. The ministers read worse about themselves over breakfast and butter their toast anyway." He laid one hand flat on the top sheet, careful, as if the ink were not yet dry — it wasn't quite. "This will not be nothing. This names names. This shows the sums. This has the cipher key printed right there on the fourth page so any telegraph clerk in the capital can verify it against the ministry wires. When this goes out, it cannot be called rumor. It can only be called treason, or it can be called justice, and both of those words get people shot."

Casper said, "I know."

"You know it here." He tapped his own temple. "Do you know it here?" He tapped her sternum, lightly, through the grey courier's coat — the coat whose lining had carried the evidence for eleven days, now sewn shut again over nothing, an empty envelope where the truth used to live.

"Yes," she said.

"Then say what it costs. Out loud. I want to hear you say it, because my apprentice could not say it, and he left an hour ago, and I do not think he is coming back."

She looked at the stacks. She thought about the cost out loud, which was the hardest arithmetic she had done since the manifest of the Meridian.

"One copy reaches a barracks, and a soldier reads it, and he tells his corporal, and the corporal reports him, and the soldier hangs or shoots or runs. One copy reaches a ministry clerk who passes it up instead of down, and the clerk is arrested by nightfall. One copy falls into the wrong hands entirely — Krein's hands — and it leads them back through the flour sacks to this room." She paused. "It costs lives. Not maybe. It will cost lives."

Sova nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a debt is acknowledged. "And you print it anyway."

"We print it anyway."

"Why?"

Because her brother had died twice, once in fact and once on paper, stolen by men who then sold the city's guns to the power invading it. Because her mother sat upstairs from a shroud-maker's shop sewing burial linens by lamplight for neighbors who had not yet died but certainly would. Because Quentin Hale had spent his whole life keeping honest records inside a dishonest system and had finally, quietly, chosen dishonesty in the service of honesty, and was at this moment hiding in the attic above them with a nine-year-old girl who drew maps of guarded piers in the margins of her school exercises. Because the republic was dying either way, and the only question left was whether it would die knowing why.

None of that was the answer Sova wanted. He wanted the simple one, the one that fit on a pamphlet.

"Because they paid for the surrender with our money and signed it with our names," Casper said. "The least we can do is read the receipt aloud."

Sova studied her for a long moment. Then he began bundling the sheets with the brisk economy of a man who had decided something decades ago and was only now telling himself.

* * *

They hid the pamphlets in flour.

This had been Sova's idea from the start, and it was better than anything the resistance committees had proposed, which was to say better than anything involving actual resistance committees, half of which were infiltrated and all of which talked too much. Flour moved through Velden even now. The bakeries had contracts, the army had requisitions, the poor had ration cards, and sacks went from millers' warehouses to corner shops to cellar doors in a daily tide that no inspector wanted to slow down, because slowing the flour meant facing the women who queued for it.

Sova had a cousin — everything in this city eventually came around to someone's cousin — who drove a delivery cart for the Havelin Mill on the canal. Twice a week the cart made its rounds with genuine sacks of flour, and twice a week, at the third stop, two or three sacks came off the cart lighter than they'd gone on, traded to Sova for paper and coin, their contents decanted into bins behind his false wall. Empty flour sacks, stamped with the mill's mark, smelling honestly of grain.

Into these went the pamphlets. Ten per sack, folded flat along the bottom beneath six inches of real flour scooped back in from the bins. A searcher who slit a sack open would find flour. A searcher who poured it out would find more flour, and a fine, and move on. A searcher who weighed it might notice the heft — but inspectors did not weigh flour; they taxed it, and the tax was paid.

By dawn's first grey light coming through the loading hatch, Casper had packed nineteen sacks herself, her forearms white to the elbow, the smell of grain replacing the smell of ink on her skin. Quentin came down from the attic to help with the last five, moving slowly because of the bruised ribs he'd earned on the stairs the night the soldiers came for him, saying almost nothing, working with the same careful precision he must have brought to census forms for fifteen years. Nell stayed upstairs, under strict instructions, drawing. They could hear her pencil through the floorboards, a small persistent scratching, like a mouse building something.

"The distribution map," Quentin said at last, tying off a sack. He had drawn it himself, in census-taker's hand: forty drop points across the nine districts, each marked with the time of day the flour cart passed, the name of a shopkeeper or a sympathetic clerk or just a doorway where a sack could be "misdelivered" and picked up within the hour. Ministries got copies. The garrison canteen got copies. The telegraph office break room, courtesy of the sympathetic clerk named Marun who had helped decipher the dispatch, got six copies all by itself.

"Forty points," Casper said. "Over two days."

"Thirty-eight." Quentin's voice was flat with apology. "I struck the ones near the census hall. Krein has men watching that quarter since my audit went in. If a single sack turns up within four streets of his office, he stops looking at flour and starts looking at people."

"He's already looking at people. He's looking at you."

"Then let's not confirm his suspicions with geography." Quentin folded the amended map into quarters and handed it to her. Their fingers overlapped on the paper for a moment longer than the transaction required. Neither of them remarked on it. There had been very little time to remark on anything, and somehow that had made the unremarked things larger.

Sova appeared at the hatch with the cousin's cart driver, a broad silent woman named Petra with flour permanently in the creases of her knuckles. She looked at the twenty sacks lined against the wall, looked at Sova, and said, "Third stop tomorrow, and the rest the day after. Same price?"

"Same price," Sova said. "And the same answer if they stop you at a checkpoint."

Petra shrugged. "Nobody stops flour going to the garrison bakery. Soldiers eat. Even traitors eat." She said it without venom, without irony — as plain fact, and that plainness was somehow the most damning thing Casper had heard all winter. Even the men who sold the republic still expected their bread.

Petra took the first ten sacks. The cart wheels ground away over the cobbles into the fog coming off the canal, and Casper stood in the hatchway listening until the sound dissolved, thinking: *that sound is the sound of the government ending.* It didn't sound like gunfire. It sounded like a delivery.

* * *

Sova made tea on a spirit stove, three cups, and they drank it standing up because there was nowhere to sit that wasn't covered in lead type. Upstairs, Nell had fallen asleep mid-drawing; they could tell because the pencil-scratching had stopped sometime during the packing.

"Say it now," Sova said. "While it's quiet. While there's tea. Tomorrow there won't be."

Casper wrapped both hands around the cup. "You've said it already. It'll cost lives."

"That's the cost. Say the other thing. The thing you're afraid I'll refuse to hear."

She made herself look at him. Old radicals, she had learned, did not want comfort; they wanted witnesses.

"When the first sack opens," she said, "someone will die for it. Maybe a soldier who reads instead of reporting. Maybe a shopkeeper who takes coin to leave a sack in his back room. Maybe Petra, on the road, if Krein's net is tighter than we think. And every one of those deaths will be traceable to this room, this press, this decision — and we will have made it knowing, and we will not be able to give the lives back when the tribunals come and everyone suddenly agrees we were right."

Sova drank his tea. Steam rose past his face.

"In 'nineteen," he said, "we printed a call for a general strike. Fourteen thousand copies. It failed — the strike, I mean. Three organizers were arrested, one died in custody, and the strike failed, and for years I told myself the printing had been worth nothing, been worse than nothing, been vanity." He turned the cup in his hands. "Last month a man came to this door, maybe fifty years old, a dock foreman. He said: you don't know me, but in 'nineteen I read your strike sheet, and I didn't strike, and I have spent thirty years being the kind of man who doesn't strike. And then he gave me forty crowns and asked if I needed anything else." Sova smiled without much humor. "Truth is contraband, girl. Contraband keeps. It sits in cellars and flour sacks and the backs of people's minds, waiting. Sometimes it waits thirty years. That's not consolation. That's just how the weight distributes. We are not choosing whether anyone dies. That choice was made in the ministries, in cipher, months ago. We're only choosing whether anyone gets to know why."

Quentin set down his cup with a small precise click. "For what it's worth," he said, "the records support him. I processed death registrations for the whole Third District. People die anyway. The question the living always get wrong is whether the dead would rather be forgotten or explained." He glanced at Casper, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with the pamphlet and everything to do with a dead brother listed twice in the rolls. "Explained. Always explained."

Casper finished her tea. Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere around the nineteenth sack of flour, and she suspected they wouldn't start again until this was over — whatever *over* meant, in a story where the ship had sailed or hadn't, where the border was closed or wasn't, where her name existed or didn't exist on any roll in the republic.

"Then we finish it," she said. "Ten sacks remain. Petra takes them tomorrow. Distribution starts at the ministries by eight o'clock, before the clerks arrive, so the sacks are sitting in their own supply rooms when they do. By noon it's in the barracks. By evening—" she stopped, because *by evening the city turns* was hope, and hope was a currency she'd learned to hold loosely.

"By evening," Sova agreed mildly, "Krein knows it was us."

"He already knows it was someone."

"There's a difference between *someone* and an address." The old printer banked the spirit flame, and the room dimmed to one candle. In the low light the remaining stacks of pamphlets looked less like paper than like ammunition, which was, Casper thought, exactly what they were. "So hear the last instruction, and don't argue. After the final delivery, this press comes apart. The type goes into the canal in three separate bags on three separate nights. The plates go with me — I have a place. And the three of you are elsewhere. Anywhere. The attic above the shroud-maker holds for a night or two more, no longer; a neighbor saw smoke from the chimney during curfew and neighbors talk. You have perhaps forty-eight hours of being invisible, and then the city decides what it is, and either way you want to be near the harbor when it decides."

"The Meridian loads at dawn the day after tomorrow," Casper said.

"Then you have a schedule. Keep it." Sova blew out the candle, and for a moment they stood in the dark together, three conspirators and two hundred truths already rolling through the fog toward forty doorways, and the dark felt less like danger than like the inside of a held breath.

Upstairs, Nell murmured in her sleep — a word, maybe a name, maybe just the tail of a dream about harbors.

* * *

Casper did not go back to the attic immediately. She climbed instead to the print shop's loading loft, where a dirty skylight showed a slice of the capital: rooftops under snow, the ministry domes black against a sky the color of dishwater, and beyond them, faint, the cranes of the harbor standing in the mist like gallows or like masts, depending on the day's politics.

Somewhere out in that greyness, ten sacks of flour were riding toward the garrison, each one carrying its folded cargo of proof. Somewhere, Aldous Krein was waking, or not yet sleeping, in an office full of shipping manifests and suspicion. Somewhere, her mother was threading a needle by the first light, sewing another shroud for a city that kept needing them.

She thought about the fourth page of the pamphlet — the cipher key, printed plain, so that any clerk with access to the wires could verify the ministers' signatures themselves. *We didn't trust you,* the page said, in effect. *We trusted anyone who cared enough to check.* That had been her addition, argued for at midnight while Sova grumbled about layout. It was the whole philosophy in a single column of type: don't believe the pamphlet; believe the arithmetic.

A board creaked below, and Quentin's head appeared at the loft ladder.

"You should sleep," he said.

"Soon."

"That's what you said yesterday."

"And I was right eventually." But she came down the ladder, because there was no sense pretending the offer wasn't also a request. At the bottom he hesitated, then said, in the tone of a man filing a form he isn't sure is required:

"For what it's worth — and I know exactly what the records are worth, it's my profession — tomorrow, when the sacks open, the republic's true register begins. Everything before it was draft."

Casper looked at him. Fifteen years of honest entries in a dishonest ledger, and he had found, at the end, the one entry that mattered.

"Draft," she agreed.

Below them, faint through the floor, the city was beginning to stir — carts, bells, a patrol's boots on the next street, the ordinary percussion of the last days. And under all of it, if she listened the way she had learned to listen in eleven days of carrying secrets, she could almost hear it already moving: two hundred folded pages traveling through flour and fog and morning queues, toward the hands of soldiers and clerks and shopkeepers, faster than any law could run, faster than any warrant could follow.

By nightfall, everyone would know what she had carried.

By nightfall, she suspected, so would Krein — and he would know precisely where to look for the woman who had carried it.

From the street below came the sound she had been dreading since the first sack rolled away: boots stopping at the print shop's door, unhurried, deliberate, three pairs of them.

Someone knocked.
