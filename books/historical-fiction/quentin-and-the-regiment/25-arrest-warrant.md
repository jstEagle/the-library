## 25. Arrest Warrant

The morning it happened, Quentin Hale was correcting a spelling.

It was the kind of error he had spent eleven years hunting: a clerk in District Nine had written *Halvorsen* where the birth register clearly said *Halverson*, and somewhere downstream a pension claim would fail because of that single transposed letter, and some widow or veteran would spend months writing letters to offices that no longer answered letters. Quentin believed — had always believed — that the republic lived or died in these small mercies of accuracy. A state that could not spell a man's name correctly had already begun to lose him.

So he sat at his desk with the register open and the ink drying on his correction, and he felt, for the first time in weeks, almost calm. The audit had been submitted. His figures were clean. He had omitted Casper Voss's name and added three others — Krein's superior among them, Deputy Minister Ostrava Rell, whose signature appeared on falsified grain allocations going back four years — and he had dressed the whole thing in the language of thoroughness. *Discrepancies noted for cross-reference.* Nothing that read as rebellion. Everything that was.

Nell's berth papers were folded in his breast pocket. That was the one thing he allowed himself to think about when the fear came at night, standing in his kitchen with the lamp turned low while she slept in the next room: not the audit, not Krein's grey eyes across the table offering him immunity like a man offering poison in a teacup, but the folded paper against his heart. Berth thirty-nine. In a dead boy's name — in Tomas Voss's name, though the papers said otherwise now; Casper had worked her forgery so thoroughly that even Quentin, who knew what to look for, had to squint to find her seams. Nell was going to be on that ship. Whatever else burned down around them, Nell was going to be on that ship.

He set down his pen and reached for the next register, and that was when Marta Kessel appeared at the end of the row of desks with her coat already on and her face drained white as ledger paper.

"Quentin." She said it quietly, but the quietness itself was wrong. Marta did not do quiet. Marta argued about ration scales at full volume in front of supervisors.

He looked up. "You're leaving early?"

"The soldiers are in the stairwell." Her voice barely carried past his desk. "Two of them, plus a civilian I've never seen. They asked the duty officer for you by name. Not the audit files — you." She swallowed. "Quentin, they're coming up."

The room went very still around him, or perhaps it was only him who went still. Eleven years of desks and registers and ink-stained cuffs, and he found that his body understood before his mind did: the cold spreading outward from his sternum, the strange clarity, the way sound seemed to arrive from a distance. He looked at his hands. There was wet ink on the side of his right palm from the Halvorsen correction. He thought, absurdly, *I should wash that off*, and then thought, more truly, *They will find my fingerprints all over this building. Every page I ever touched.*

"How long?" he managed.

"A minute. Maybe less." Marta glanced over her shoulder toward the stairwell door at the far end of the hall. "Quentin, I didn't tell them anything. When Krein came asking about your entries, I told him nothing either. But if there's something you need to not be here for—"

"There is," he said. He was already standing, already sliding the register shut, already reaching for his coat on its hook. "Marta. Listen to me. If anyone asks—"

"I know nothing," she said flatly. "I'm a filing clerk. I know nothing about anything. That's been my career." And then, softer, with a flicker of the woman who fought about ration scales: "Go out through the records annex. The window in the map room still doesn't lock since the break-in last spring. Nobody uses that corridor after the morning delivery."

"Marta—"

"Eleven years, Hale, and I have never once seen you take an extra pencil home. Whatever this is, it isn't you being crooked. It's you being decent, which is worse, which is why they want you." She pulled her scarf up over her chin. "Go."

* * *

The records annex smelled of dust and camphor and the particular staleness of paper nobody would ever need again. Quentin moved through it fast, coat unbuttoned, briefcase abandoned under his desk — no, wait, he'd left the case, hadn't he, with the day's work in it, with his name stamped on the spine — and made himself stop caring. His name was on everything. His name was on eleven years of shelves. Running was not going to fix that; running was only going to buy the hours between their arrival and their discovery of where he wasn't.

The map room was at the annex's dead end, a narrow chamber of rolled survey charts and one tall grimy window facing the alley behind the census hall. The latch gave with a shriek of rust, loud enough that he flinched and waited, counting seconds, listening for boots. Nothing. The alley below held three feet of crusted snow and a single cat regarding him with imperial disdain.

He climbed out and dropped into the drift, and the snow took him to mid-shin, soaking his trousers instantly, and he stood there in the grey light of late morning feeling the strangest sensation: that he had just stepped out of his own life. Somewhere above him, boots were entering his office. Somewhere above him, men were opening his drawers, reading his correspondence, finding the photograph of Nell on his desk and drawing conclusions about a girl whose legal existence rested entirely on a lie he had written in careful copperplate four years ago.

*Nell.* The thought hit him like cold water and he started walking — then hurrying, then nearly running, boots crunching, breath smoking — cutting south away from the main avenues, away from his own street. Because they would go to his rooms. Of course they would go to his rooms. And if they went to his rooms they would find her, nine years old, alone until evening, with a name that would not survive ten minutes of checking against the rolls he himself had forged.

His mind worked the problem with terrible bureaucratic efficiency even as his heart hammered. They wouldn't arrest a child — probably wouldn't, not immediately; children were paperwork problems, and the regime, drowning in paperwork, deferred what it couldn't process. But they would hold her. They would put her somewhere official. They would ask her questions a frightened child answers badly. And every answer would lead them back to him, and every answer about her dead mother's registration would unravel the thread he'd spent four years weaving, and then it would be Nell in a cell instead of Quentin, and that outcome was not survivable in any sense of the word.

He needed to reach her first. He needed Casper.

* * *

He took the long way, doubling back twice, once through a covered market where he walked slowly between stalls of frost-bitten cabbage and let the crowd close behind him, watching reflections in a fishmonger's window until he was satisfied no one followed. His hands shook the entire time. He kept thinking of small things — the pen he'd left uncapped, whether he'd locked the annex door behind him (he hadn't), whether the map room window would betray him (it would; broken latches were evidence) — and he made himself stop each time, made himself think only of the next street, the next corner, the next hundred steps. One page at a time. It was how he'd survived eleven years of bureaucracy and it would have to be how he survived this.

Casper's street looked the way it always did: poor, patient, grey. Her tenement leaned slightly eastward like an old man listening. He climbed the stairs two at a time and knocked on her door with a rhythm he hoped sounded casual and knew sounded frantic.

She opened it within seconds — she must have heard him coming, or been waiting behind it — and whatever she saw in his face ended any greeting.

"They came to the office," he said. "Soldiers. For me. By name. I got out the annex window—" His voice cracked on the word *window*, which was ridiculous, and he pressed his lips together until it steadied. "They'll go to my rooms next. Nell is there. Casper, Nell is there alone."

He watched her take this in, watched the courier's stillness come over her — that quality he'd noticed the first time he saw her in the tea house, the way she could receive bad news without letting it move her face. Then she was moving, pulling her grey coat from its hook, sweeping a canvas satchel over her shoulder.

"Where does Krein think you are?" she asked, already counting on her fingers, already planning.

"He thinks I'm at the office. Until they search my rooms and find me gone."

"Then we have maybe half an hour before they connect you to anywhere else. Do they connect you to me? Officially?"

"You don't exist officially," Quentin said. "That was the whole point. You're nowhere on the rolls." He heard the bitterness in his own voice and hated it. "I made sure of that myself."

"Then we use it." She stepped into the corridor and pulled the door shut behind her, testing the lock. "My mother's shroud shop. The attic. No one goes there — people avoid it. Grief superstition. It's the safest room in the district." She looked at him, and her eyes were steady, and he realized she had already decided everything. "We collect Nell first. Which way?"

"East. Three streets. Casper—" He stopped in the stairwell, gripping the rail. "If they've already got there. If I'm too slow—"

"You won't be," she said. "Walk like a man going to work. We're faster than soldiers because we're not looking for anyone. We know exactly where we're going."

* * *

They were not faster. Or rather — they arrived at exactly the same time as the soldiers, from opposite ends of the street, and Quentin learned in those forty seconds what his whole body felt like at the edge of catastrophe.

He saw the transport wagon first: military grey, parked crooked outside his own building, engine idling, exhaust curling in the cold. Two soldiers on the steps, talking to his landlady, whose arms were crossed and whose voice carried — she was telling them, bless her obstinate heart, that Mr. Hale paid on time and bothered nobody, and what business was it of theirs. A third soldier was at the door of Quentin's second-floor flat, knocking.

And in the window — Nell's window, the small square of glass he'd replaced himself two winters ago — a shape moved. Small. Watching the street. Watching the soldiers knock on her door.

"She's seen them," Quentin breathed. "Casper, she can see them from the window."

"Then she's hiding." Casper's hand closed on his sleeve, hard. "Think. What have you taught her?"

"What?"

"When you drilled her on it. Last autumn, after Krein's first visit. You told me you'd practiced with her. What did you tell her to do if strangers knocked and you weren't home?"

Quentin's mind seized, blanked, and then produced it, whole, as if the memory had been waiting: *Don't answer. Don't make a sound. Go into the wardrobe, the one against the party wall, and stay there no matter what you hear. Count to five hundred. If someone opens the wardrobe, say you're hiding from your uncle as a game and you don't know where he keeps anything.*

"Don't answer," he whispered. "Wardrobe. Party wall. Count to five hundred."

"Then she has done it," Casper said, "because you told her, and she believes you, and children believe the rules that keep them alive." She was already turning him by the shoulder, steering him away down a side street, away from the wagon, away from the windows. "We can't go in. If we walk up to that door we confirm everything they suspect and we lose her anyway. We wait. They search, they find an empty flat and a frightened girl in a wardrobe, they take her to a holding station for processing — and processing takes hours, because everything takes hours, Quentin, the whole state runs on queues. We will be at the holding station before they finish her intake forms."

"And if they don't take her to a holding station?" His voice came out raw.

Casper was silent for three steps. Then: "Then I get her back. However it has to be done. I have spent six years moving sealed paper through this city without being caught, and I am very good at getting things out of places they aren't supposed to leave." She glanced at him sidelong. "Trust the arithmetic. It's cruel, but it works. Hours of bureaucracy versus one hidden child. Bureaucracy loses. Bureaucracy always loses, eventually, to anyone patient enough."

They waited in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery two streets over, out of the wind, watching the mouth of his own road. Quentin stood with his soaked trouser legs stiffening into ice and his heart performing its terrible work, and he catalogued, because cataloguing was all he had: the sounds of the city, ordinary and indifferent; a bell somewhere marking the quarter hour; the smell of coal smoke; the weight of Nell's berth papers still folded against his chest, absurd now, a ticket to a ship for a child currently sitting in a wardrobe counting to five hundred while strangers searched her home.

An hour. It took an hour. The wagon finally pulled away empty — empty, he confirmed it three times, staring, counting heads through the rear flap's gap — and the soldiers on foot went with it, and the street settled, and Casper said, "Now. Quickly, but not running."

His flat looked like weather had passed through it. Drawers opened, mattress lifted, floorboards pried at the corners. They had taken his files — all of them, every folder, including the private box where he kept his personal copies of the registrations he'd altered. They had taken the photograph of Nell's mother. They had left, with what struck him as a kind of contemptuous precision, everything of Nell's: her coat still on its peg, her cup in the drainer, her slate with half a harbor map chalked on it — her piers, her guarded docks, her careful childish cartography that he suddenly understood was worth more than everything the soldiers had confiscated.

The wardrobe doors stood open. Empty. The blankets inside arranged with a child's hasty tidiness, and tucked into the fold, deliberately, where he would see it, a scrap of paper in Nell's round pencil hand:

*I counted to 500 twice and then I climbed out the back and I am at Mrs. Ondra's cellar like you said for fires. I did NOT talk. Come soon. — N*

Quentin sat down on the edge of the stripped bed and laughed, one broken syllable of it, and pressed the note to his forehead, and discovered he was crying without any warning at all.

"She's at the neighbor's," he said. "Mrs. Ondra's cellar. We drilled that too — fires, I told her, for fires, because a fire drill doesn't frighten anyone." He looked up at Casper, and the tears were still coming and he let them. "She didn't talk. She hid and she climbed out the back and she left me a report, Casper. A report. She filed herself."

Casper crouched in front of him, and for a moment her composure slipped and he saw underneath it — the exhaustion, the fear, the fierce bright thing that kept her upright. "Get up, Quentin," she said gently. "Collect your niece. And then you both come with me, because as of this morning, none of us exists anymore — and I know exactly where the invisible people live."

* * *

Mrs. Ondra's cellar was warm, candle-lit, and smelled of pickled everything. Nell came off the potato crates like a shot and hit him hard enough to stagger, arms locked around his ribs, face buried in his coat, saying nothing at all, which frightened him more than crying would have. He held her and felt her shaking and understood that she had been shaking for an hour and had decided not to show it until it was safe.

"You counted twice," he said into her hair.

"Five hundred is easy," she mumbled. "I counted in fives. Like the ledger." She pulled back and looked up at him, and her chin was trembling and her eyes were furious with the effort of being brave. "Uncle Quentin. Are they going to arrest you?"

The question hung in the candlelight. Behind her, old Mrs. Ondra studied her jars with tremendous concentration.

"Yes," Quentin said, because he had decided, in the last hour, that he would never lie to this child about anything that mattered again. "Probably. Soon. So we're not going to be here when they try."

"Are we going on the ship?"

"Not yet." He knelt so their eyes were level. "First we go somewhere safe. Miss Voss knows a place. And then — then we make sure the ship happens. All right?"

Nell considered this with the gravity of a magistrate reviewing a petition. Then she nodded once, picked up her little canvas bag — packed, he saw, since some earlier night he hadn't known about; she had been ready for weeks — and took his hand.

At the top of the cellar stairs, Casper paused in the doorway, scanning the street with a courier's flat attention. When she turned back, her expression had changed, and Quentin felt the shift like a drop in pressure.

"Quentin," she said quietly. "There's smoke over by the census quarter. A lot of it. And people running toward it, not away." Her jaw tightened. "Whatever's happening — the city's starting to move. We need to move faster."
