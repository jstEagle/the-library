## 26. Three Fugitives

The attic smelled of camphor and cut linen. Casper lay on her back between bolts of burial cloth, staring at the underside of the roof, where the snow came through in thin grey light and the nails held frost like small silver teeth.

Below her, the sewing room of Marta Ilves went about its terrible business. The shroud-maker had taken them in at midnight without a question — she had known Casper's mother for thirty years, and grief, apparently, was a currency that bought silence. All day the treadle of the machine below rose through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat, stitching the city's dead into their last clean garments. The regime thought it was buying loyalty with fear. It had not counted on the seamstresses.

Casper turned her head. Quentin lay against the far wall under a horse blanket, Nell curled against his side, both of them asleep or pretending to be. The girl's boots were still on. She hadn't taken them off since they'd come up the ladder two nights ago, and when Casper reached over to loosen the laces, Nell's eyes opened — quick, wary, exactly her uncle's eyes — then closed again, letting herself be tended to only because tending was being offered.

* * *

They gathered around the candle at dusk, when Marta brought up bread, hard cheese, and a jug of barley tea, and pulled the ladder up behind her.

"Three days," Marta said, setting the tray down. "That's what I can give you before someone asks why my flour is going faster than my work. And the soldiers are asking questions at the corner now. A man with a grey coat." She looked at Casper. "Not your coat. Greyer."

Krein. Or one of his. Casper kept her face still. "We'll be gone by then."

"Gone where?" Marta folded her hands. She was a broad woman with forearms like a laundress and a voice that never needed raising. "I sew for the families of hanged men, child. I know how stories end. Tell me yours has a middle."

Quentin sat up, pushing the blanket off his shoulders. He looked thinner than he had a week ago, in the tea house with his careful census-man's posture, as though lawfulness were something you could wear. Now he looked like what he was: a man who had burned his own life down with three names on an audit form.

"We have pamphlets," he said quietly. "Two hundred of them. Printed and hidden."

"I know," Marta said. "Sova sent word by the flour boy. He also sent this." She produced a folded scrap of paper from her apron and handed it to Casper. On it, in Sova's cramped hand: *Distribution is the wound. Paper in a cellar heals nothing.*

"He's right," Casper said.

"So we open the vein," Quentin said.

* * *

They spread the map across the floor — not Nell's harbor map this time, but a street map of the ministry quarter that Quentin drew from memory, his pencil moving with the same unhurried precision he'd once used to falsify a census roll. Watching him, Casper understood something she hadn't let herself understand before: that his forgery had never been a fall from grace. It had been the same act as this. A man deciding, alone, at a desk, which version of the truth would survive.

"The ministries get their morning papers at seven," Quentin said. "Before the ministers arrive. Clerks sort it in the basement mailrooms — junior men, badly paid, badly fed. They're the ones who've been whispering since the pamphlets started moving."

"You can't just walk pamphlets into a ministry mailroom," Casper said. "Every entrance is checked."

"No," Quentin agreed. "But the paper deliveries aren't. The official gazette comes wrapped in bundles from the state printer. Same weight, same twine, if you know how to tie a knot." He looked up. "I spent eleven years auditing supply contracts. I know the knots."

"And the barracks?"

"Sova's people handle two barracks already. We take the third — the one on Foundry Street. The garrison there is half-conscripts from the river towns. Those are Tomas's people. Our people." Her mother's people, she almost said. The family had expanded its borders without anyone signing anything.

Nell had been quiet through all of this, sitting cross-legged with the tea cooling in her hands. Now she spoke.

"The Meridian docks Thursday," she said. "Pier Nine. Low tide is at half past five in the morning, and the patrol changes at six, but there's twenty minutes between the tide coming in enough for the gangway and the new watch. I measured it."

"You measured it," Quentin repeated.

"With my thumb steps. Two hundred and forty from the coal shed to the bollard, and the guard walks the other way." She said it the way other children recited multiplication tables. Casper felt something tighten in her chest — pride and grief braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which was which. This was what the republic had made of its children: little cartographers of escape routes.

"Pier Nine," Casper said. "Then that's our third thing. Pamphlets first. Pier second. And third—" She stopped.

"Someone," Quentin said softly.

"Someone." The word hung in the camphor-smelling air. One berth. One seat on the Meridian, bought with stolen ministry money and currently belonging, on paper, to a man named after her dead brother — a name she had given away to save Tomas and might have to spend again. "We don't know who yet. But we decide before Thursday. No arguments at the pier. Whoever goes, goes whole, and the rest of us carry it without looking back."

Her mother's face came to her unbidden — the refusal, flat and final, at the dinner table weeks ago; then the reversal, the secret fishermen's arrangement, the demand: *you take it, Casper.* She had refused that too, and forged it onto Nell instead. And still the berth sat in the arithmetic like an unpaid debt.

"There's another problem," Quentin said. He hesitated, and Casper saw him choose honesty the way a man chooses to step off a ledge. "Krein isn't searching for pamphlets anymore. He's searching for me. My audit had three added names. When the exposé broke, those names became evidence. He knows the audit was doctored, and he knows who could have done it."

"Then he comes here eventually," Casper said. "Marta's shop is on the route between the census hall ruins and the harbor. It's the logical place to sweep."

"Yes."

"Good." Casper took the pencil from him. "Then we don't wait for his schedule. We set ours."

* * *

They worked through the night by turns — Casper and Quentin dividing the pamphlet bundles into gazette-weight packets while Nell slept, or seemed to sleep, her eyes opening whenever the conversation touched the harbor.

It was near three in the morning, the candle guttering, when Nell spoke into the dark without preamble.

"When you put my name on the berth papers," she said, "was that the brother's name too? The one who died twice?"

Casper's hands went still over the twine.

"Yes," she said.

"Then I'm carrying somebody with me." Nell considered this with the gravity of a nine-year-old negotiating with the universe. "I can do that. I carried my mother's name until Uncle Quentin fixed the rolls. Names are just coats anyway. Marta says that. She makes coats for the last trip, and she says nobody checks the labels."

From beneath the horse blanket, Quentin made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the other thing, closely related.

"Nell," Casper said. "When you're on that ship, and you're whoever you need to be on the papers — remember that underneath the label, you're Nell Hale. That part doesn't go in any manifest."

"That's what Uncle Quentin said about you," Nell said. "After the census. He said some people are real even when the paper's wrong."

The candle flame leaned. Casper bent back over the bundles and tied the next knot, because if she didn't keep her hands moving she was going to have to let her face do what faces do, and there wasn't time.

* * *

Dawn came grey and early. Marta climbed the ladder with fresh bread and a look on her face that meant news.

"They found the print shop," she said. "Or what's left of it. Soldiers went through Sova's street before midnight. He got out ahead of them — my flour boy confirms it — but they took his press apart plank by plank and they're questioning everyone on the block." She set the bread down. "And there's more. They posted new orders at the harbor. The Meridian's civilian berths are being re-verified. Papers checked twice, at the gate and at the gangway. Starting tomorrow."

Silence. Somewhere below, the sewing machine started its day, stitching the dead.

"Re-verified means Krein," Quentin said finally. "He's narrowing the net."

"It means we move tonight," Casper said. "Pamphlets tonight, all of them, before they can burn whatever copies Sova saved and close every door. The truth doesn't wait for safe conditions — Sova told us that. Distribution is the wound." She looked at the two of them, this census-taker and this child, this family assembled out of lies told kindly and risks taken for strangers, and heard her own voice go steady the way it did on rooftops in the snow, running dispatches for a government that no longer deserved her.

"Tonight we bleed them," she said. "Thursday we reach the water. And somewhere between the two, we save someone."

Nell reached into her coat pocket and unfolded her harbor map, smoothing it on the floorboards between the bread crumbs and the candle wax, and put her thumb on the gap in the patrol line.

"Two hundred and forty steps," she said.
