## 27. The Distribution

The first pamphlet went out before dawn on a Tuesday, folded inside a loaf of black bread.

Casper carried it herself. That had been her condition when she and Sova argued through the night about method — that the first one be hers, hand-delivered, because she had sewn the dispatches of a dying republic into her coat lining for three winters and she would not now stand at a distance while the truth walked without her. Sova had relented in his way, which was to shrug and say that couriers made poor conspirators but excellent martyrs, and then he had gone back to setting type as if she hadn't spoken.

The bread went to Corporal Iven Dross at the Vodny Gate checkpoint, who had let her pass eleven times without searching her satchel because she always brought him something warm. He broke the loaf open at his post, read two lines, and looked up sharply — but she was already through the gate, already walking, already someone else.

She did not see him fold the paper small and put it inside his glove. She could only hope.

* * *

By noon of the first day there were forty copies in circulation, and by nightfall, four hundred.

They moved the way water moves through cracks. A flour sack delivered to the Ministry of Ways held six. A hymnal returned to the barracks chapel at Kessel Barracks held two more, tucked between the psalms of ascent. Quentin's former colleagues at the census office — the ones who had not yet been arrested, whose loyalty was a held breath rather than a conviction — passed them hand to hand under desks, each man or woman pretending to receive nothing while receiving everything.

Casper spent the day walking routes she knew better than the streets of her own childhood. The grey coat. The courier's stride. Nobody stopped a courier; the whole machinery of the state had been built on never stopping a courier, and now the machinery was being fed its own indictment one satchel at a time.

At the Ministry of Marine she watched a junior clerk unfold a copy behind his ledger, read for perhaps ninety seconds, and then look up at the portrait of Minister Havel Brandt that hung over the filing room door — the same Brandt whose signature appeared on page two, selling coastal gun emplacements to the power now shelling the eastern districts. The clerk did not tear the paper. He did not pass it on. He folded it very carefully, along its original creases, and put it inside his coat, against his chest, the way you'd carry a letter from home.

That was the moment Casper understood what they had made. Not an argument. An inheritance. Something people would protect.

She bought chestnuts from a cart on the quay and ate them standing up, and her hands shook, and it was not fear.

* * *

Quentin heard about the second day's distribution from a widow on Marrow Lane.

He was still technically employed — the warrant had not come, the audit had been accepted, Krein's suspicion had apparently been satisfied by the three names Quentin had fed him — and so he walked his rounds with his census ledger under his arm like any other gray functionary, counting heads in a city that was learning to count itself differently.

"You've seen the papers," the widow said. It wasn't a question. Everyone on Marrow Lane had seen the papers; Marrow Lane had received its bundle from the fishmonger, who received his from a boy who came off the grain barges, and no one asked questions because asking questions was how you got your name written down somewhere.

"I've seen something," Quentin said.

"My husband died at the eastern works," the widow said. "Two years ago. The ministry letter said an accident." Her chin trembled once, and steadied. "Page four says the eastern works were sold stripped. Says the accident was the price of doing business with their friends across the water. Says the minister signed for it."

Quentin said nothing. There was nothing lawful to say. His whole life had been lawful, right up until the winter it wasn't, and he had discovered that lawfulness and truth were different countries with no treaty between them.

"The men who did this," the widow said, "are they going to hang?"

"That's above my station," Quentin said. "I count people."

"Well," the widow said, "count faster. Before they're all gone."

He walked on. At the corner of Marrow Lane a soldier stood at his post with his rifle shouldered and his collar up against the cold, and as Quentin passed him the soldier was reading — openly, in daylight, in uniform — page two of the pamphlet, holding it in his bare hand as if the cold were the smaller danger.

Quentin kept his eyes forward and his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

* * *

In the barracks at Kessel, the pamphlet reached the mess hall at supper.

Sergeant Anselm Rook read it aloud. This was either bravery or calculation — Rook was nobody's fool, and Casper learned later that he had read it silently first, alone, twice, weighing it the way a man weighs a bridge before driving a wagon onto it. Then he stood at the head of the long table and read it aloud in a voice pitched for the whole room, and forty soldiers listened with their spoons down.

"—and whereas the contracts herein described were executed under seal of the Republic, we hold that a government which sells the walls of its own house has forfeited the house—"

"Who wrote that?" someone demanded.

"Don't know," Rook said. "Doesn't matter. What matters is whether it's true." He laid the page flat on the table. "Contract numbers are on it. Anyone want to check them against what we saw at the eastern works?"

Nobody wanted to check. Everybody already knew.

A lieutenant came to the door, heard the tail end, and stood very still. The room waited to see which way the evening would break. The lieutenant was young, from the river provinces, and his family's district had burned in October.

"Finish your supper," he said finally, and turned, and did not report it.

By morning, Kessel Barracks had nineteen copies and a shared silence that was louder than shouting. By the following evening, sentries were waving carts through checkpoints that orders said to search. Not all of them. Not everywhere. But enough that the word *enough* had entered the bloodstream of the garrison, and once a word like that gets in, it does not come out.

* * *

Casper slept four hours in the print shop cellar and spent the rest of the time moving.

The rhythm of the second day: bakery, chandler, laundry boat, the records annex where clerks took their smoke breaks in the yard. At each stop a bundle changed hands wrapped in something innocent — mending, onions, candle wax. Sova's network was thin but it was real: a printer's apprentice here, a dockworker's wife there, a telegraph clerk whose sympathies Casper had tested months ago with a falsified telegram and never told him about it.

At the records annex she nearly walked into trouble. Two inspectors from the Interior — not Krein's people, but cut from similar cloth — had set up a table in the yard and were questioning everyone leaving the building, collecting pamphlets into a growing pile with the grim efficiency of men sweeping up after a festival.

Casper adjusted course without breaking stride. She had a bundle of eight under her arm wrapped in a shawl she was supposedly delivering to a seamstress, and the seamstress — her mother's acquaintance, a woman named Petra Lunt who owed the family more than shrouds — was waiting at the gate, and between them they performed a small theater of delivery, chatter about hems and mourning crepe, while the inspectors' eyes slid past them because two women talking about clothing were furniture.

Inside the shawl, Casper's heart was a drum. Outside it, she was a courier again, invisible, essential.

"Eight," Petra murmured as they parted. "Where do you want them?"

"Third floor, records of the contracts office. Leave them in the privy. Men read in privies."

Petra almost smiled. "God help us, girl. You've thought of everything."

"No," Casper said. "I've thought of one thing. Everything else is luck."

* * *

The whispers started that afternoon.

Casper heard them in queues, at ration windows, on the tram steps — fragments, half-phrases, the sound a city makes when it is changing its mind. *"Did you see—" "*My cousin says the numbers are real, he checked, they're real—" *"They say the ministers' families are already packed, they're going out on the Meridian like rats on—"* 

And underneath it all, quieter, more dangerous, the sentence that meant the regime's foundation was cracking: *"Maybe somebody should make them answer for it."*

For years the capital had survived on the discipline of looking away. Now, everywhere she went, Casper caught people looking — at the ministries, at the uniforms, at each other — with a new arithmetic behind their eyes. Fear was still there; fear would outlast the republic itself. But fear had acquired a companion, and the companion was anger with a document in its hand.

At dusk she climbed to the roof of the print shop and looked out over the city. Smoke from the eastern districts still smudged the horizon. Searchlights swept the river. Somewhere below, four hundred pieces of paper were burning holes through the fabric of a state that had ruled by paper — censuses, permits, manifests, seals — and had forgotten that paper cuts both ways.

Quentin came up beside her, coatless despite the cold, Nell safely below with Sova's wife and a lesson in typesetting.

"It's working," he said, as if he didn't quite believe it.

"It's moving," Casper corrected. "Working comes later."

"A soldier thanked me today." Quentin laughed softly, shaking his head. "Thanked me. For the census. Said at least somebody was still keeping honest records in this city. If he knew what my honest records contain—"

"He doesn't need to know. That's the point. We don't have to tell them what's true. They only have to recognize it." She pulled her collar up. "Krein taught me that, in his way. A man can be undone entirely by his own files."

Below them, through the frosted skylight, the press thumped on. Sova was running a second edition — five hundred more — because demand had outrun supply, because clerks were copying pages out by hand when they couldn't get a copy, because a thing once loosed cannot be recalled.

"Two days," Quentin said. "It took the old governments years to fall and this one might go in a fortnight."

"This one's been falling since before we were born," Casper said. "We just handed it a push."

They stood together in the dark, and the city turned beneath them, slow and vast and irreversible, like a ship coming about.

* * *

But justice, Casper knew, had never once in history arrived without a bill attached.

She paid hers on the third morning, at the chandler's shop near Fish Gate, when she saw the notice pasted to the shutter — fresh paste, fresh type, official crest:

**BY ORDER OF THE MINISTRY OF INTERIOR. THE PERSONS RESPONSIBLE FOR THE SEDITIONS PAMPHLET ARE SOUGHT. INFORMATION LEADING TO ARREST WILL BE REWARDED WITH PASSAGE OUT OF THE CAPITAL.**

Passage. They were paying informers in berths now. The Meridian's manifest had become currency, and every desperate soul in the city had been offered a seat in exchange for a name.

And beneath the printed order, added later in a different hand — smaller, neater, chillingly precise — a line that made the cold walk up Casper's spine:

*The courier known to carry diplomatic materials is believed connected. All courier traffic will be subject to inspection at all gates.*

Krein. Even discarded, even hunted by his own superiors, the man was still working the problem — and he had narrowed the hunt to her.

She pulled her cap lower, turned from the notice, and began to walk — fast now, toward the print shop, because if all gates would soon be watched, then the plates, the remaining pamphlets, and everyone she loved were sitting in a basement with a clock ticking over their heads.
