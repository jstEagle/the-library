## 28. Krein's Reckoning

The pamphlet reached Krein before breakfast, which he took as a professional courtesy.

He was in his office at the Ministry of Internal Order by six, as always, working through the night's intercept reports by lamplight while the city outside lay blue and frozen under curfew lamps. The courier who brought the morning file set it down without meeting his eyes — they never met his eyes, that was part of the service he provided them all — and Krein sorted the stack with the mechanical pleasure of a man whose world still had categories.

Intercepts. Ration disturbances. A report on dockside sentiment at Pier Nine.

And then a single sheet, folded twice, printed on cheap stock in ink so fresh it smudged under his thumb.

*THE TRUTH ABOUT THE MERIDIAN CONTRACTS.*

He read it standing up. He read it twice. On the third reading he sat down, because his legs had made an independent decision about his future, and he preferred to be seated when his body betrayed him.

The document laid out everything. The defense contracts sold to the Vharen consortium at a tenth of their value. The names of the ministers who had signed them — four names, each spelled correctly, each followed by the sum received and the date of signature, transcribed from dispatches so sensitive that fewer than twenty people alive knew they existed. And beneath the ministers' names, in smaller type, a fifth entry:

*Facilitation of the above arranged through the Office of Census Audit, Inspector Aldous Krein, who supplied verification of personnel records for the contracting parties in exchange for consideration rendered.*

His name. His office. His *consideration* — the word chosen, he suspected, by someone with a sense of humor he would have appreciated in any other context.

Krein set the sheet down very carefully, aligned its edges with the edge of his desk, and looked at it for a long time. The stove ticked. Somewhere below, a clerk dropped a tray of files and swore.

He had built his career on the principle that paper did not lie but could be made to say anything. He had spent nineteen years teaching that lesson to subordinates, suspects, and superiors alike. Now someone had taught it back to him, on his own stationery stock — he recognized the watermark when he held it to the lamp, the faint crowned anchor of the ministry supply depot, third drawer, second shelf of his own requisition room.

They hadn't just published him. They'd published him *authentically*, with his own paper, in his own city, under his own nose.

Krein poured himself a glass of the plum brandy he kept for celebrations and interrogations, which had long since become the same occasion, and drank it without tasting it.

Then he began, methodically, to make a list.

* * *

By nine o'clock, three things had happened.

First, the Director of Internal Order summoned him. Krein climbed the four flights to the Director's suite past clerks who had developed, overnight, a fascination with their own paperwork; not one of them looked up, and the silence they manufactured around his passage was louder than shouting.

Second, the summons turned out to be a formality lasting ninety seconds. The Director did not ask him to explain the allegations. The Director did not accuse him of anything. The Director simply slid a typed memorandum across the desk — *Inspector A. Krein relieved of all duties pending inquiry; access to ministry archives, records, and premises suspended effective immediately* — and watched him sign the acknowledgment with a pen that didn't work, so that Krein had to press hard enough to tear the page, and the Director watched that too.

"Your lodging in the ministry residence is also rescinded," the Director said, as Krein stood. "You have until evening."

"I served this ministry for nineteen years."

"You served yourself for nineteen years," the Director said, without heat, "and were useful. You've stopped being useful. That's the entire history of your employment, Aldous, and I'm surprised you're only now hearing it." He returned to his papers. "There will be an inquiry. I'd encourage you not to be in the city for it."

It was, Krein reflected on the stairs, almost elegant. Not arrest — arrest required process, and process required the appearance of law, and the law had just been dragged through every barracks in the capital by two hundred copies of a pamphlet. No, this was simpler: strip him, evict him, and let the inquiry find whatever it found. If the mob outside burned the census hall tonight — and there were rumors it might — the inquiry would find ash, and ash accused no one.

The third thing that happened was that Krein understood, standing in the cold corridor with his memorandum in his pocket, that he was not being punished.

He was being *discarded*. There was a difference, and the difference was that punishment implied you still mattered.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he did best: auditing.

Not officially — his seals were dead, his keys confiscated, his name struck from the duty roster between breakfast and lunch, a bureaucratic erasure executed with a speed he would have admired if it hadn't been aimed at him. But nineteen years in the Office of Census Audit taught a man where the real records lived, and the real records lived in duplicate, and duplicates lived in places no suspension order reached.

He went first to the shipping annex near the harbor, where manifests for the Meridian were held in triplicate. The junior clerk on duty recognized him and hesitated — hesitation being the crack through which Krein had pried open half the secrets in this city — and in that hesitation Krein said, quietly, "I'll need forty seconds with the civilian berth ledger," and the man's training overrode his judgment, and Krein got his forty seconds.

Berth thirty-nine. Purchased in cash, ten days ago, through an intermediary whose name was a false name — Krein could smell a false name the way other men smelled rain coming — but paid from an account that was not false at all. It was a ministry discretionary account. *His* old discretionary account, or rather the one he had controlled until this morning, the fund inspectors used for informants and expenses and, occasionally, considerations rendered.

Someone had bought the berth with his money. With the ministry's money, laundered through his authority, using procedures *he* had designed to make exactly this kind of purchase untraceable.

He closed the ledger and felt something shift in his chest that was not quite fear and not quite admiration and settled, finally, into hatred, which was more durable than either.

The courier. It had to be. The woman — grey coat, grey gloves, the face you forgot on purpose. She carried sealed dispatches; she had carried *these* dispatches; she had carried them into the hands of whoever ran the press that produced the pamphlet. And she had needed a berth for her family, and she had taken his fund, and she had done it cleanly, professionally, with the same competence he had noted in her file months ago and dismissed as unremarkable.

Unremarkable. He had written *subject shows no initiative beyond assignment* in her dossier, and she had gone on to rob him, expose him, and topple four ministers, and the only reason he knew any of it was that she'd left his name off the list of heroes and put it on the bill.

But she hadn't worked alone. Nobody forged census entries alone, nobody moved a berth through the harbor system alone, nobody printed two hundred pamphlets on stolen stationery alone. And Krein had spent months auditing irregularities in the census rolls, and there was one name that kept surfacing at the edge of every falsification like a face at a lit window — the census taker. Hale. Quentin Hale. The mild one, the precise one, the one whose entries were immaculate except when they weren't, and the times they weren't were the times that mattered.

Krein walked out of the shipping annex into the grey afternoon and stood a moment among the crates and gulls, feeling the shape of it. The courier and the clerk. The woman who carried truth and the man who buried lies, working together, having found each other the way conspirators always found each other: through small mercies that grew teeth.

They had ruined him in a day. He intended to return the favor personally.

* * *

The problem with being discarded, Krein discovered as dusk came down, was that every door in the capital now opened inward against him.

He went to see Colonel Brant of the garrison, with whom he had exchanged favors for eleven years — Krein supplied the audits that justified Brant's budgets, Brant supplied the muscle that gave Krein's audits weight — and was kept waiting forty minutes in an anteroom and then informed the Colonel was unexpectedly occupied. Through the door he heard laughter. Brant had never laughed at anything Krein had ever said.

He went to Deputy Minister Ostrava, whose signature appeared on the fourth line of the pamphlet, and found the house dark and the neighbors loading a cart. Ostrava had gotten out ahead of it, then. Good instincts, Ostrava. Bad company.

He went, last, to the one man whose ruin might yet be traded for his own survival — Director Vess of the Meridian charter, the superior who had signed off on the bribed berths, the man whose name sat highest on the pamphlet's list of anyone still in the city. If Krein could deliver Vess to the inquiry — real evidence, dates, accounts, the whole architecture of the sale — he might buy his way back from discarded to merely disgraced. Disgraced men kept their heads. Discarded men lost them to mobs and tribunals with equal indifference.

Vess's office was empty. His desk drawers hung open, stripped to the felt. On the blotter, centered with a care Krein recognized as mockery, lay a copy of the pamphlet with a note pinned to it in pencil:

*You're next on the list. — friends of the truth*

Krein read it twice. His hands were steady. He noticed that his hands were steady and understood that he had passed through fear into some colder country beyond it, where the air was thin and clear and every object in the world stood out in outline, and in that country there was only one task, and the task had a name, and the name was not Vess.

Vess had run. Let him run; exile would do what the tribunal couldn't.

But the courier was still here. The clerk was still here. And somewhere in this freezing, turning, traitorous city, a printing press had his name set in type, and presses could be found, and printers could be persuaded, and persuasion was the one trade in which Aldous Krein had never once been outworked.

* * *

He went back to the ministry residence to collect his things and found the locks already changed, which was efficient of them. He stood in the corridor with his single valise — he had packed light for nineteen years, another professional habit — and listened to the building breathe around him, the radiators ticking, a telephone ringing in some distant flat and going unanswered, and he thought about the nature of the animal he had been and the animal he now was.

For nineteen years he had been a leashed thing. The leash had been the institution: its forms, its seals, its slow machinery of consequence. Everything he did had been deniable because the institution absorbed the denial. He had mistaken that shelter for strength. It was only shelter, and shelters burn.

Now the leash was off and there was no institution behind him, no audit trail to hide inside, no superior to absorb the blame. Just a man, a valise, a pistol in the valise, a head full of names and ledgers, and a hatred with excellent documentation.

He found it clarifying. He suspected most men would have found it terrifying, and he pitied them their terror the way he had always pitied the guilty their guilt — as a weakness of construction, a flaw he had been spared.

Down in the street, the city was coming apart in the ordinary way: queues, rumors, a barricade of sleds going up at the corner of the Avenue of Records, students chalking the pamphlet's four ministerial names on walls in letters a meter high. Someone had chalked a fifth name too, smaller: *HIS INSPECTOR*. Children, playing at treason. They scattered when he looked at them, and he watched them go without expression.

He walked to the harbor district because that was where trails ended and began. The Meridian would load at dawn — everyone knew it now, the pamphlet had seen to that — and everything the courier loved, everything the clerk protected, would funnel toward those piers in the next twelve hours like water toward a drain. People revealed themselves at gangways. Desperation was the finest interrogator he had ever employed, and tomorrow the harbor would be full of it.

In a tavern near Pier Seven, paying with coins counted out one by one, Krein bought two things: a meal he didn't taste, and a seat by the window with a view of the customs house, the manifest office, and the lane that ran between them — the lane any sensible fugitive would use to move papers or people unseen.

He ate slowly. He watched the lane. Behind his eyes, the ledger of his life rebalanced itself, column by column: what he had lost, what remained, what it would cost to take the rest.

Four ministers had bought their way onto a ship. One director had fled with the money. And two anonymous functionaries — a woman in a grey coat and a mild man with immaculate handwriting — had torn down a career nineteen years in the building, in a single night, with ink.

Aldous Krein finished his meal, checked the load of his pistol out of habit, and settled in to wait for dawn, when the Meridian would come to the pier, and the desperate would come to the Meridian, and he would be there to meet them — patient, thorough, and no longer leashed to anything but his own reckoning.

Somewhere across the dark water, a ship's bell rang the hour.

Klein smiled at the window, and began, in his head, to draft the first entry of a new ledger: *Item — one courier. Item — one clerk. Item — one child.*

The last item he underlined twice.
