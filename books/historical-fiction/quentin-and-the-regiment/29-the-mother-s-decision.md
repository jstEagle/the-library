## 29. The Mother's Decision

Mira Voss had sewn burial shrouds for forty years, and she had learned the trade's first lesson early: you do not weep over the cloth while you are cutting it. Weeping comes after. While the needle is moving, your hands belong to the work, and the work does not care about your heart.

She was thinking of this on the third night of hiding in the shroud-maker's attic because her hands had begun to shake, and shaking hands ruined seams.

Below her, the attic floorboards creaked under Casper's pacing. The girl had not slept properly since Tomas came back from the press gang with rope burns on his wrists — three days now of pacing, of whispering with Quentin Hale by candlelight, of folding and unfolding the forged berth papers as though the creases might spell out a different arithmetic than one seat for five souls. Mira knew that arithmetic better than anyone. She had done it herself, silently, every night since they'd come up into the attic: five bodies, one berth. Her son. Her daughter. A nine-year-old child who drew maps of guarded piers in the margins of old newspapers. A stranger who had lied to a census inspector to protect a woman he'd met once.

And herself. Fifty-eight years old, fingers gone stiff at the knuckles, a seamstress whose trade had died with the city that used to bury its dead in fine linen.

The arithmetic was not hard. That was what made it cruel.

* * *

Mira had made her decision eleven days ago, down at the fish market, before any of them knew she'd gone.

It had been a risk even to leave the attic — her face was known at the ration office, and half the neighborhood had been stopped and questioned since Krein began his sweep. But there were things a mother could do that no courier or census clerk could manage, and one of them was standing at a certain stall at dawn, haggling over herring, while an old man with tar-stained hands measured her with his eyes.

Joren Bakker had fished the Velden estuary for fifty years. He had buried a wife and two sons, one to fever and one to the last war, and he had no patience left for governments of any stripe. When the press gangs started sweeping the docks, he'd moved his boat's registration papers twice and bribed a harbormaster once, and when Mira Voss — whose hands had sewn his late wife's funeral shroud, whose husband had mended his nets through two hard winters before the republic killed him gently with debt — appeared at his stall with grey in her hair and terror held behind her eyes like water behind glass, he did not ask what trouble she was in.

He asked how many.

"Two," she said. "A boy of nineteen and a girl of nine."

"The girl isn't yours."

"No." Mira had prepared a lie and found she didn't want it. "Her uncle is a good man. Her parents are dead. I'm asking for all three of us to ask."

Joren had looked at her a long moment, the way fishermen look at weather. Then he'd said, "My boat goes out Thursday before dawn, tide and God willing. Smugglers' run, mostly salt and sometimes people. Two berths below, among the crates, and no papers asked. You understand there's no coming back if the patrol boats stop us."

"I understand."

"And payment?"

Mira had set on the wet boards the small bundle she'd been carrying: her wedding ring, her mother's silver thimble, and the coins she'd sewn into her coat hem twenty years ago against exactly this kind of day — the kind you cannot imagine until it arrives.

"That's not enough," Joren said, looking at it.

"It's everything," Mira said. "You'll have to decide what that's worth."

He decided it was worth it. He always would have. She'd known that before she came, which was why she'd come to him instead of to anyone else, and why she'd told no one — not Casper, not Tomas, not the Hale girl. Because Casper would have refused. Casper would have stood in that fish market with her courier's grey coat and her terrible arithmetic and said *Mother, no, the plan, the ship, the berth —* and the argument would have burned another week off a calendar that was nearly out of pages.

So Mira had kept her mouth shut and her needle moving, and for eleven days she had let her daughter pace the attic floor above her believing there was one way out for one person, when in truth there had been two ways out for three.

Now, tonight, with the pamphlets distributed and the city humming like a struck wire, with soldiers wavering at their posts and Krein hunted through his own labyrinth, there were no more nights to spend being gentle. The Meridian loaded at dawn tomorrow. Decisions had to be spoken aloud.

Mira threaded her needle, bit the thread clean, and went downstairs to ruin her daughter's evening.

* * *

They were gathered around the candle stub in the main room of the shroud-maker's house — the old man himself long fled to relatives in the hills, leaving them his stock and his silence. Casper sat with the berth papers flat before her, smoothing them again. Quentin Hale had Nell's boots in his lap, patching a sole by feel. Tomas was asleep sitting up, the way he'd slept since the press gang, one hand curled near his throat where the rope had been.

Mira took the chair opposite Casper and folded her hands in her lap, over the apron, the way she did when she meant business.

"Casper."

Her daughter looked up. In the candlelight she looked twenty again, and also sixty — the courier's face, the face that gave away nothing. But Mira had made that face's clothes. She knew what lived underneath it.

"Before you start," Casper said carefully, "we've been over—"

"We've been over it four times. This is the fifth, and it's mine." Mira kept her voice low, level, the voice she'd used on frightened apprentices. "I'm going to tell you something, and then you're going to listen without interrupting, because you've inherited your father's habit of arguing with me before I've finished my sentences, and I've outlived him, so I'll win."

Quentin set down the boot. Even Nell, half-dozing against his shoulder, opened her eyes.

"Eleven days ago," Mira said, "I went to the fish market."

"Mama—"

"*Without interrupting.*" She let the silence sit. "I spoke to Joren Bakker. He runs a salt-smuggler out of Pier Nine — the pier your niece there has memorized better than her catechism." Nell sat up straighter at that, eyes going wide and bright. "His boat leaves Thursday morning, before dawn. Two hours before the Meridian docks. He'll take two passengers below decks among the crates — no papers, no manifest, no questions. And he'll take a third if the cargo allows, which, as of Tuesday, it does."

Tomas was awake now, very still.

"You bought—" Casper's voice cracked and reassembled itself. "With what? Mama, what did you pay?"

"My ring. My mother's thimble. The coins in my coat hem." Mira said it flatly, refusing the drama. "It's cloth and metal, Casper. It was never going to keep anyone warm across the sea. People are what keep people warm, and I have spent two months watching you try to solve our family like a dispatch route — five names, one berth, choose — and I am telling you the problem is already solved. Tomas goes on the fishing boat. Nell goes with him, because a child alone on a diplomatic ship full of fleeing ministers is a hostage waiting to happen, and a child among crates with a boy who can carry her if the boats come is something else entirely. Quentin—" she turned to him, and her voice softened despite herself, "—you go too. Bakker owes me more than I owe him; I called in the rest. Three berths below deck, no names required."

Quentin had gone very pale. "Mrs. Voss. I — that's — Nell and I could never impose—"

"You're not imposing. You're cargo. There's a difference, and it's in your favor." She turned back to Casper. "Which leaves the Meridian. One civilian berth. Berth thirty-nine, purchased in a dead man's name, papers already forged, guard already paid once and payable again. And the only person left in this room who fits inside those papers is you."

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere outside, far off, a patrol whistle sounded and faded — the city's nervous pulse.

"No," Casper said.

"Yes."

"No. Mama, the whole point—the berth was for the family, the plan was—"

"The plan was a fantasy we told each other so we could sleep," Mira said. "Five seats' worth of love in a one-seat world. I know. I did the same sums. But here's what you never understood about the sums, because you were always the clever one and clever ones think numbers behave." She leaned forward. "Numbers don't behave. Mothers do. That's the entire job. Your father thought he was providing for us with his ledgers, and the ledgers drowned us, and what actually got us through every winter was me taking in piecework until my eyes bled and never once telling you children how bad it was. Protection flows downhill, Casper. That's the law of families. Parents to children. Not the other direction. Never the other direction."

"I'm not a child."

"You're my child. Those aren't the same thing and the second one outranks the first." Mira's hands, in her lap, had finally stopped shaking. "You will take that berth. You will board the Meridian tomorrow with the truth sewn in your coat lining — yes, I know about Sova's copies, but originals matter, Casper, originals are what tribunals believe — and you will deliver it to whatever exile government is sober enough to read it. You will live. That is not a request. That is the last order I will ever give you, and unlike the republic's orders, this one will be obeyed."

Casper stared at her. And Mira watched — with the particular grief and particular pride that mothers are issued at birth and may never exchange — as her daughter's face did the thing it had been trained never to do in front of anyone: it broke open.

"You can't stay here," Casper whispered. "Krein is finished but his men aren't, the city's falling, the mobs, the patrols—"

"I've buried a husband in this city and stitched shrouds for half its dead. The city and I understand each other." Mira reached across and took her daughter's hands — the courier's hands, ink-stained, steady again now, cold. "Besides. Who says I'm staying? Bakker offered me a crate too, and I told him I'd decide by Thursday. Maybe I sail with your brother. Maybe I walk north with the refugee columns and turn up on some dock in exile complaining about the fish. I'm a difficult woman, Casper. Difficult women survive wars; it's the agreeable ones who get monuments." She squeezed the cold hands. "But you go tomorrow, on the big ship, in the light, with papers in your name — your real name, at last, the one you should never have had to bury. Promise me."

For a long moment Casper didn't answer. Quentin had put his arm around Nell and drawn her close, and the girl was watching Casper with the fierce, unblinking attention of a child who understands that history is happening at the kitchen table.

Then Casper said, quietly, "If you're not on that fishing boat, I won't board either. Swear it. Swear you sail or walk — anything but stay."

"I swear I leave," Mira said. "When and how is a mother's privilege."

Casper exhaled, a long unsteady breath, and nodded once — the courier's nod, sealing terms.

And that was when Nell spoke up, small and clear in the dark:

"But Uncle Quentin can't go on the fishing boat."

Everyone turned. The girl sat forward, pulling free of Quentin's arm, her chin lifted in the exact stubborn angle Mira had seen on her daughter's face a thousand times.

"Whyever not?" Mira asked gently.

"Because the fishermen can't take him." Nell's voice wobbled and held. "Because Mr. Bakker said two berths and a maybe, and a maybe isn't a promise, and promises are what you make when someone's counting on you. And because—" she faltered, glanced at Quentin, and pressed on anyway, "—because if Uncle Quentin gets on a smuggler's boat with no papers and they catch him, they'll hang him for the audit, everyone knows what Krein's men do to census clerks who lie. But the Meridian takes officials. Diplomatic staff. It says so on the notice — *ministry personnel.*" Her eyes went to Casper, then to Quentin, then back. "Uncle Quentin worked for the ministry his whole life. He has his employment seal and his service card and everything. Couldn't he — couldn't he board as ministry staff? Evacuation priority? They're letting clerks on ahead of ordinary people, I heard the guards saying so at the pier when we were counting."

Silence filled the attic room like rising water.

Quentin looked at the child as if seeing her fully for the first time — this small creature he had fed and shielded and taught letters to, who had mapped the harbor in secret and memorized patrol rotations the way other children memorized songs. His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"Nell," he said hoarsely, "that's — the paperwork, the verification, an inspector at the gangway would—"

"Would check your seal," Casper said slowly, and Mira watched the idea move through her daughter like current through wire. "Your census seal. It's a ministry instrument. It carries weight — Krein himself treated your entries as official record. If the boarding officer sees a valid ministry seal, a service card, a clerk claiming evacuation priority..." She was already thinking aloud, already planning, the courier waking in her. "They're processing hundreds of people at dawn. Chaos. Half-verified manifests. It could work. It might even be *safer* than the fishing boat — no smuggler's hold, no crates, no patrol boats to outrun."

"But then the berth—" Quentin began.

"Tomas and Nell take the fishing boat, as Mrs. Voss arranged. You take the Meridian as ministry staff." Casper turned to her mother, and something passed between them — the old language, wordless, of women who have spent lifetimes reading each other across dinner tables. "And I..."

"You take berth thirty-nine," Mira said. "In your brother's name. As agreed. As *ordered.*"

"As agreed," Casper echoed — and then stopped, because her eyes had gone to the window, to the thin grey line just beginning at the edge of the rooftops, and her face changed.

"Mama. What day did you say Bakker sails?"

"Thursday. Before dawn. Why?"

Casper was already on her feet, already reaching for the coat draped over the chair, the coat with the lining, the coat that had carried a republic's sins across a snowbound capital. "Because today is Wednesday," she said, "and the Meridian loads at sunrise tomorrow — which means we have one night to forge a ministry credential, brief two children, and get everyone to the harbor before the city wakes up and decides what it wants to become."

Outside, the first bell of the morning rang out across the rooftops — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on the cold air from the direction of the river, came the sound of gunfire.
