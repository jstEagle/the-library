## 30. Refusal

The berth paper lay on the shroud-maker's table between them, and Casper understood that her mother had won the argument before it began.

"You'll take it," Maren Voss said. Her hands were still moving as she spoke, folding a length of linen with the automatic precision of fifty years at the needle. She could not stop working. She had not stopped working since the siege began — burial cloths for neighbors whose names she stitched into the hems so that someone, someday, would know who had died. "Tomas goes north with the fishermen on Thursday. Nell goes with him. And you take that berth on the Meridian and you get off this rock before it burns."

"No," Casper said.

It was the first time in her adult life she had said no to her mother and meant it all the way down.

Maren's needle paused. The attic room was cold enough that their breath hung between them like something written and immediately regretted. Downstairs, Quentin was reading aloud to Nell from a water-stained atlas, his voice pitched low and steady, the voice of a man teaching a child that the world still had coastlines in it.

"Casper." Her mother set down the linen. That, more than anything, told Casper how serious this was. Maren Voss did not set down her work. There had been days of mourning, days of fever, days when they'd carried Father's coffin through knee-deep snow, and her mother's hands had never once been empty. "I am your mother. I am telling you what will happen."

"And I'm telling you what has already happened." Casper picked up the berth paper. Berth Thirty-Nine, stateroom class, purchased in the name of one Aldric Voss — her brother, dead eleven years now, resurrected by ink more times than she could count. "This name bought it. Not me. Aldric Voss doesn't exist. He hasn't existed since the winter of the first mobilization, and every day he keeps existing is a day I'm stealing from a dead boy."

"Stealing—" Maren laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Child, you've been stealing your whole life to keep us fed. You think God is keeping a ledger now?"

"I don't think God is keeping anything." Casper folded the paper along its crease, carefully, the way she handled dispatches. "I think Krein is. I think the harbor police are. I think the moment anyone looks twice at that name, it unravels — and if I'm standing on the gangway when it does, I lose everything. But if it's Nell—"

"It will not be Nell." Her mother's voice dropped into register Casper knew from childhood: the voice that preceded beatings, or worse, forgiveness. "She is nine years old. She is not yours to spend."

The words landed where they were meant to. Casper let them sit there, hurting, before she answered.

"She's Quentin's," she said quietly. "Which means she's ours, if we're honest about what we've become. And she is the only person in this family — in either of our families — who is *clean*. No forged papers. No borrowed names. No record anywhere except the ones her uncle falsified to keep her fed, which burn with the census hall and are already ash in any case." She looked up. "Mother. Everyone else on that ship will be a minister or a bribe. One child among forty berths. Let her be the one thing this republic sends forward that isn't rotten."

Maren stared at her for a long time. Somewhere below them a door slammed; the fishermen's boys, coming early for Tomas's kit.

Then her mother did the last thing Casper expected. She picked up her needle, threaded it without looking, and said:

"If you do this, you do it properly. None of your courier scrawls. If those papers are going to carry my granddaughter onto a diplomatic ship, they're going to be sewn, not written." A pause. "I've been forging government seals since before you were born, Casper. Who do you think made your father's exemption papers?"

* * *

They worked by candlelight in the back of the shroud shop, among bolts of cloth that would wrap the city's dead if the pamphlets failed and the soldiers held.

Casper wrote; her mother unpicked and restitched. They had taken the berth documentation apart entirely — the manifest copy, the boarding authorization, the internal ministry chit that vouched for the passenger's identity — and rebuilt each one around a new name.

Aldric Voss was dead. Casper had killed him an hour ago, burning his registration card over the candle flame, watching her brother's borrowed face curl and blacken. It felt less like destruction than like burial — eleven years late, but done right at last. In his place, on the papers, went a girl.

*Nell Hale,* age nine. Ward of the state, orphaned, sponsored under emergency evacuation provision 12-c — a clause Quentin had found for them in the wreckage of the legal code, buried in a volume he'd rescued from the census hall before it burned. Provision 12-c allowed unaccompanied minors passage on diplomatic vessels with a single sponsor's seal.

The seal was the problem. It had to be a courier seal — first-class, gold-stamped, verifiable against the ministry cipher book. Casper had surrendered hers in everything but fact months ago; the brass tube sat in her coat lining beside the codes, and she had told herself a hundred times that keeping it was sentiment.

Tonight it stopped being sentiment.

"There," Maren said, pressing the wax flat with the flat of a knife. Gold powder, mixed with tallow and lampblack — her own recipe, older than the republic itself, from the days when seamstresses forged travel passes so conscripts could come home to bury their mothers. "Hold it steady. Don't breathe."

Casper didn't breathe. She pressed the courier seal into the wax, turned it a quarter measure, lifted it clean.

Nell Hale looked up from the document with her uncle's grey eyes and her dead uncle-by-marriage's sponsorship, and she was, on paper, real.

* * *

Quentin found them at dawn, asleep at the table — Casper with her cheek on the finished documents, her mother's hand still resting on her shoulder like a stamp of approval. He stood in the doorway a long moment, holding two cups of chicory, understanding without asking what had happened overnight.

"You gave her the berth," he said, when Casper woke.

"I gave the berth to a ward of the state named Nell Hale. Whether that's the same girl depends entirely on her uncle." Casper sat up, spine cracking. "He'll need to sign the sponsorship line. In his own hand — the ship's clerk will have boarding signatures on file from the census rolls, and yours are legible everywhere in this city."

Quentin set the cups down. His hands, when he took the pen she offered, were steadier than she'd ever seen them — steadier than in the tea house, steadier than at the pier, steadier than the night the soldiers came for him and he'd run with Nell's coat half-buttoned.

"You understand what you're doing," he said. It wasn't a question. "You spent your whole life being no one, so your family could be someone. And now you've made yourself no one again — legally erased, no papers, no name — so a child can be somebody. Casper, there's nothing left of you on any roll in this country."

"There never was," she said. "That was always the trick of it. The rolls only ever held the people the republic was willing to count." She touched the signature line, waiting, blank. "You counted me. First night, in that tenement, with one question too many. You looked at a ghost and decided she was worth saving. So save her again. Sign."

Quentin signed. *Q. Hale, sponsor.* Three words, small and neat, the handwriting of a man who had spent fifteen years making other people's existences official.

When he finished, Casper folded the papers into oilcloth, sewed them into the lining of a child's coat — her mother insisted, and for once Casper agreed — and woke Nell for breakfast.

The girl came downstairs rubbing her eyes, took one look at the three adults arranged around the table like witnesses, and said, "Something's happened."

"We've got a surprise for you," Quentin began, but Casper shook her head. Lies had gotten them this far; they wouldn't get Nell onto a ship.

"Nell." She put the child's coat across the girl's knees. "There's a ship leaving tomorrow at dawn. It's going somewhere safe. Your ticket is sewn into this coat, and it's real — as real as anything in this country gets. Your uncle signed for you. My mother made sure of it. And I need you to listen very carefully, because the men at the gangway may ask you questions."

Nell looked at the coat. Then at Casper. Then, with the terrible directness of children, at the space where the truth should have been.

"Whose name am I?" she asked.

Casper's throat closed. Eleven years of borrowed names, and no one had ever asked her the question straight.

"A girl called Nell Hale," she managed. "Who happens to be exactly who you are. That's the whole forgery. Everything else about you stays true."

Nell considered this with the gravity of a customs official. Then she shrugged, pulled the coat on, and asked the only question that mattered:

"Do I say goodbye to Tomas here, or at the boat?"

* * *

They told Tomas separately, in the yard behind the shop, while he loaded the fishermen's crates. He took the news of the rearranged plan — himself north by sea with the fishing fleet, Nell on the Meridian, his sister staying — with the flat stare of a nineteen-year-old who had already been conscripted once and escaped by luck.

"You're staying," he said. "For the pamphlets."

"For the truth," Casper said. "Same thing."

"They're not the same thing, Cas. Truth doesn't get people shot on quays." He threw the last crate onto the cart harder than he needed to. "Ma's letting you do this?"

"Ma's sewing me a coat for the road north. That's Ma's way of arguing."

Tomas was quiet a long moment. Then he hugged her — quick, bruising, the hug of a brother who'd learned affection from someone who rationed it — and said into her hair: "If the Meridian sails without you and the city holds, I'll find you. And if the city falls—"

"Then read the pamphlet," Casper said. "Know it wasn't for nothing."

* * *

That evening, Sova came up from the basement print shop with ink to his elbows and the news that changed the shape of everything: the type was set, the flour sacks were bought, and distribution would begin at first light — the same first light that would see the Meridian take on passengers at Pier Four.

"One push or the other," Sova said, warming his hands over the stove. "Can't be in two places, courier."

"I know where I'm needed."

Sova studied her with his old radical's eyes, the eyes that had outlasted three governments. "You know what I printed forty years ago? A leaflet about the grain requisitions. Cost me my hand—" he raised the mangled left one, "—and I'd do it again. But I'll tell you what I learned. The truth doesn't need you after it's loose. Children do. Papers do. The living do." He nodded toward the stairs, toward the sound of Nell practicing her new-old name under her breath, getting it right. "Take the berth. Let the pamphlets walk without you."

"No," Casper said, and found it easier the second time. "Someone has to see it through here. Someone has to stand on the quay when the ministers board that ship and watch their faces. Someone has to carry the original cipher key to the border if Sova's plates don't survive the week." She smiled, tired and certain. "Besides. I've spent eleven years being Aldric Voss. I'd like to find out who's left underneath."

Sova grunted, unconvinced but unsurprised, and went back down to his candles and his type.

* * *

Past midnight, with the household finally sleeping, Casper sat alone with the courier seal in her palm — the brass weight of it, warm from her pocket, the republic's eagle worn smooth by her thumb over four years of faithful service.

By rights she should have surrendered it when the government fell into negotiation. By rights it was worthless now, a stamp from a country in its final hours. By rights—

She thought of the guard at the gangway tomorrow, checking papers against seals. She thought of Nell's coat, and the oilcloth, and the thin margin between a child and the sea.

Casper turned the seal over once, twice. Then she did not put it away.

Instead she packed it — deliberately, knowingly, against every regulation she'd ever kept — into the inner pocket of the coat she would wear to the pier. A dead republic's authority, smuggled one last time, for one living purpose.

From upstairs came the faint sound of Nell turning in her sleep, murmuring her own name like a prayer she was learning.

Tomorrow at dawn, Casper would walk her to the water. Tomorrow the guard would ask his questions, and the papers would hold or tear, and everything her mother had sewn and Quentin had signed and a dead boy's name had purchased would come down to a single moment on a gangway.

She blew out the candle and, in the dark, finally said it aloud — the refusal complete now, witnessed even if only by herself:

"My name is Casper Voss. And I'm staying."

Somewhere across the darkened city, a bell rang the hour — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving toward the harbor, ahead of schedule.

The Meridian was loading early.
