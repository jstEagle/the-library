## 31. Krein at the Door

Quentin heard the knock at half past four in the morning, and he knew before he opened his eyes that it was not a neighbor.

Neighbors knocked the way people did everything in a dying city — apologetically, with pauses, with the soft apology of knuckles against wood. This was three blows, evenly spaced, struck by someone who had never once in his life waited to be invited anywhere.

He lay still on the shroud-maker's attic floor, Nell's breath warm against his collarbone where she slept curled against his side, and counted. Three more blows. Then a voice, muffled by two floors of old timber, saying something he couldn't make out — and then Mrs. Aldova's voice answering, high and cracked and too loud, the voice of a woman performing innocence badly.

Casper was already awake. He could tell by her breathing, which had gone shallow and even in the way it did when she was listening with her whole body. In the dark she was barely a shape, a grey suggestion against the grey window, but he saw her hand move to the coat folded beneath her head — the coat with nothing left in its lining but memory, since they'd surrendered the cipher key's twin to Sova's couriers — and then move away again, deliberately, as if reminding herself there was nothing left to hide inside it.

"Krein," she breathed. Not a question.

"Or soldiers acting for him." Quentin eased himself upright, one inch at a time, lifting Nell's head onto the folded blanket without waking her. The child slept like her mother used to sleep, he'd thought once — completely, trustingly, as though the world were a thing that could be relied upon. He hated that he was about to teach her otherwise.

Below them, Mrs. Aldova was still talking. She owned the narrow house on Vetchen Lane and let out its attic rooms to seamstresses and, lately, to fugitives, and she had taken them in with the unsentimental decisiveness of a woman who had buried a husband in the last war and considered fear a luxury item. But she could not lie. That was the trouble with honest people; their honesty showed in their voices like a limp.

"—no one up there but my sister's boy, and he's deaf, poor creature, you'll only wake him—"

"Madam." The voice from below was mild. That was the worst part. Quentin had spent eleven years reading the truth out of documents, and he had learned that violence always announced itself in the gentlest register available. "I am not here for your nephew. I am here for a census taker named Hale, a woman named Casper Voss, and a child whose birth registration lists a father dead four years before her conception."

The silence that followed had a texture. Quentin had heard silences like it before — in his office, across his desk, from people whose whole lives he held in a ledger line. It was the silence of arithmetic being done. Of odds being weighed.

Then boots on stairs.

* * *

There were two ways out of the attic and both of them were bad. The main stair came up through the house, past Mrs. Aldova's kitchen, and ended at a door that now had boots behind it. The back way was a skylight over the linen room, a drop to the yard wall, and beyond the wall the frozen canal — passable in daylight, suicidal in the dark, and anyway Casper's mother's hip would never survive the climb, and Tomas was still limping from the press gang's ropes, and Nell—

Nell.

"Take her," Casper said. Not whispered — mouthed, with such force that the shape of it was louder than sound. She pointed at the skylight, then at Nell, then at him. *You. Her. Go.*

"No," Quentin said.

It was the first time he had ever refused Casper Voss anything flatly, and he watched surprise cross her face like weather. She was, he had come to understand, a woman who had built her entire survival on the principle that every attachment was a debt that could be called in, and so she paid her debts early — gave away berths, surrendered names, cut herself off the rolls before anyone else could do it. She expected everyone to run. She had arranged her whole heart around the expectation.

"I'm not leaving you," he said. "Either of you." He looked at her mother, small and straight-backed in the corner, already wrapping her shawl as though she were preparing to receive visitors rather than arrest. At Tomas, fifteen years old and holding a boat hook he'd found somewhere, gripping it like a man who had already been conscripted once this winter and did not intend to repeat the experience. "Besides. There are five of us and however many of them, and one stair. Stairs favor whoever holds the top."

"That's not a plan, Quentin."

"It's a position."

She almost smiled. In another life, he thought, he would have liked to spend thirty years learning all the variations of that almost-smile.

The door at the bottom of the attic stair shook. Once. A fist this time, or a boot heel, and Mrs. Aldova's voice rose again — braver now, or more desperate, hard to tell — *"You can't come up here, I told you, I have my rights—"*

"Your rights, madam," Krein's voice said, still mild, still terribly patient, "expired with the constitution. Open it or I will open it, and the difference will appear in how many of your lodgers walk down these stairs alive."

Quentin moved. Later, he would not be able to reconstruct the decision — it arrived the way Nell's maps arrived, whole and certain, drawn by some part of him that had been quietly memorizing terrain while his conscious mind worried. He took the boat hook from Tomas, set the heavy iron table the shroud-makers used for cutting against the top of the stair door, and wedged its legs into the rail brackets. Then he put his shoulder to the skylight frame instead.

"The roof," he said. "To the chimney stack. We go along the ridge to the neighboring house, down through the tanner's loft—"

"And if they've circled the block?" Casper was beside him, hands already on the skylight, testing. "Krein doesn't knock on doors without covering exits. He's a bureaucrat. Bureaucrats check the footnotes."

She was right, of course. Through the frost-furred glass Quentin could see, far below, the blue-white smear of lantern light moving at the mouth of the alley behind the house. Two men. Maybe three. Patient as clerks.

The door at the bottom of the stairs splintered on its second blow. Not open — just split, a dark seam appearing in the panel, and through the seam came lamplight and the smell of wet wool and gun oil.

"Casper." Quentin kept his voice level because Nell had stirred, and because nine-year-olds woke frightened or calm depending entirely on the first voice they heard. He touched her hair until her eyes opened. "Nell. Love. Do you remember the game? The quiet game we played coming from the print shop?"

Her eyes went wide, went to the splitting door, went to him. He watched terror arrive in her face and watched her master it — master it, at nine, in the space of one breath — and something in his chest tore cleanly along an old seam.

"Good girl," he said. "Behind the fabric bolts. Don't come out unless it's my voice or hers." He nodded at Casper. "No one else. No matter what you hear."

* * *

They came up the stairs fast after that, having abandoned pretense. Quentin got one clear look through the widening gap: two soldiers first, rifles leveled at nothing, young faces under caps pulled low; and behind them, unhurried, a long grey figure that could only be Aldous Krein.

He had imagined Krein often enough during the flight from the census hall — in the shroud-maker's attic, in the print shop basement while Sova set type by candlelight, in every hour when the pamphlets were moving through the city like blood through a body. He had imagined him cruel, or theatrical, or sneering. What stood in the doorway now, ducking under the lintel, brushing plaster dust from his sleeve with two precise fingers, was none of those things. Krein looked tired. He looked like a man doing the last of his filing.

"Hale." His eyes found Quentin immediately, the way an auditor's eyes find the altered column. "And Voss. And — the widow Voss, is it? And the brother who died twice. My congratulations on your resurrection, young man; the paperwork must have been hellish." His gaze swept the attic, snagged on the fabric bolts where Nell hid, and moved on with deliberate care. "Where is the child?"

"There is no child here," Casper said.

"There is always a child here," Krein said. "In every one of these cases there is a child. That is how I know which names matter." He stepped further into the room, and the soldiers fanned out with the graceless competence of men who had done this too many times. One of them kicked the boat hook from Quentin's reach. Another began pulling shrouds off their hooks and dropping them on the floor — burial linens, white and folded, falling into the dust like snow that had given up halfway.

"You read it," Quentin said. "The pamphlet. You must have. Your name isn't in it, but the ones above you are, and you know what happens to men whose superiors are exposed. They don't get promoted, Krein. They get erased. You're not arresting us. You're applying for a job with the same people who sold the defense contracts, and you've seen what they do to employees."

Something flickered across Krein's face — quick, quickly mastered, but Quentin had spent his career catching exactly that flicker, the micro-expression between the true entry and the false one.

"You mistake me," Krein said, "for a man with options. I have a warrant. I have witnesses. I have a shipping manifest showing berth thirty-nine purchased with ministry funds routed through a census clerk's cousin. I have you, Hale, standing in an attic full of seditionists. Whatever happens to me afterward, tonight I am simply the law arriving late." He lifted two fingers, and the soldiers raised their rifles toward the room generally — toward Casper, toward Tomas, toward the fabric bolts. "Everyone downstairs. Quietly. The child especially. I want the child unmarked."

"Why?" Casper's voice was ice over deep water. "So she can be a bargaining chip, like Quentin was for your berth?"

"So she can be *processed*," Krein said, and for one moment the mildness cracked and Quentin saw underneath it: not cruelty but panic, cold and professional, the panic of a man who has realized the building is on fire and has decided to be found holding the correct bucket. "Do you imagine the tribunals will distinguish between me and the ministers? They won't. They'll hang us alphabetically. Unless I hand them something worth more than my name is worth." He looked directly at Quentin. "The original cipher plates. Sova's press. Give me those, and the three of you vanish from my report. Every report. I am very good at vanishing things, Hale. You've seen my work."

"He has," Casper said. "My brother's identity. Eleven years of my life. You vanished those beautifully."

"Efficiency," Krein said, "is not a crime yet. Ask me next month."

* * *

That was when the knocking started again.

But it was wrong. Quentin's head came up. It was wrong in every particular — too many fists, too scattered, no rhythm, no warrant behind it. It came from the street door below, and from the yard gate, and from the tannery wall, a dozen hands at once, and beneath it he could hear voices. Not soldiers' voices. Ordinary voices. Tired ones.

Mrs. Aldova's cry rose through the floorboards — joyous, astonished, cracked clean through: *"It's the neighbors! It's the whole street, what are you—"* and then, indistinctly, a name. Someone shouting a name over and over.

Krein's soldier by the window leaned out into the cold and reported back in a flat murmur: "Crowd, sir. Forty, fifty. Civilians. They're — sir, they're filling the lane. They've got lamps."

"What do they want?" Krein snapped.

The soldier hesitated. Then, reluctantly: "They're saying the inspector should come down and read them his warrant. Out loud. To everyone."

Quentin closed his eyes for half a second. *Of course.* The pamphlet. Two hundred copies in flour sacks, and flour went everywhere, flour went into every kitchen on every lane, and Mrs. Aldova had bought her flour from the miller on Vetchen Lane herself, and the miller's wife read faster than anyone gave her credit for. Somewhere in the last forty-eight hours the street had read page three — the list of ministers, the contract prices, the tonnage of shells sold unbuilt to the enemy — and page six, the smaller list, the one naming the inspectors and auditors who had made the selling possible. Krein's name sat at the bottom of page six in type an inch tall.

They hadn't come to fight. That was the extraordinary thing Quentin saw when he reached the attic's one small window and looked down. They had come to *stand*. Fishermen still in their oilskins, the tanner in his leather apron, the widow Brask from the corner house holding a lamp in one hand and a rolling pin in the other with equal seriousness. Old Ferenc, who had signed his census form every year for a decade with a shaking hand and a joke about the government owing him a new pen. They stood shoulder to shoulder in the lane in the freezing dark, fifty of them, sixty, and they did not shout slogans. They simply did not move.

"Disperse them," Krein said. His voice had lost its mildness entirely now. "Clear the lane. That's an order."

"Sir." The younger soldier had gone grey. "Sir, they're civilians. Some of them are — Private Dorn's family live on this lane, sir. That's his aunt with the lamp."

"Then Dorn can explain it to her. Move them."

The soldiers didn't move. All three of them stood in the ruined attic among the fallen shrouds, and Quentin watched the moment happen — watched obedience fail not with a crash but with a sag, the way a shelf fails, one bracket at a time. The oldest soldier shifted his rifle to parade rest without being told to. The youngest looked at the aunt with the lamp and looked away.

"Sir," the oldest one said, "with respect. Page six."

Krein turned on him. For a moment Quentin thought he would strike the man, thought any of a hundred ugly endings might arrive at once — and then the crowd below began, very quietly, to sing.

Not a anthem. Nothing so dangerous. It was a work song, an old hauling song the fishermen used on the nets, tuneless and endless and impossible to arrest anyone for, and it rolled up the walls of the lane from sixty throats, and under it came the sound of more feet arriving, more lamps, the tanner's apprentices climbing onto the wall to see. The song didn't threaten anything. It simply made clear that the night was going to be very long, and that the lane belonged to itself.

"We can shoot our way out," the youngest soldier said, not to Krein, not to anyone — just saying it, testing it aloud, hearing how it sounded. Nobody answered him, which was answer enough.

Krein stood very still in the middle of the attic. Quentin watched him do the arithmetic — he was, whatever else, a superb accountant — watched him total the columns and find the sum unbearable. Fifty civilians. Three wavering soldiers. A city turning beneath his feet like a slow wheel. And upstairs in a shroud-maker's attic, the only assets he had left: a disgraced census clerk, a courier with no legal existence, and a press he couldn't find.

"You'll hang anyway," Krein said to Quentin. Quieter now. Almost conversational. "When the tribunals come, they'll want someone to blame for the audit, and it will be you. Mark it. I keep records."

"I know," Quentin said. "So do I."

For a moment — one strange, suspended moment — Krein almost smiled, as if Quentin had passed some final examination he hadn't known he was sitting. Then he straightened his coat, took his hat from the nail by the window, and walked down through the broken door and the fallen shrouds and out of the house, past Mrs. Aldova, past the soldiers who did not follow him, down into a lane that sang and would not let him pass until his own escort pushed through it ahead of him, heads down, like men wading upstream.

* * *

Afterward, the street wouldn't disperse either. They posted themselves — Ferenc at the corner, the tanner's boys on the wall, the widow Brask refusing to give up her post or her rolling pin — and Mrs. Aldova climbed the attic stairs carrying hot barley coffee and an expression of profound grievance about her door, and found them all still standing where the soldiers had left them, as if none of them quite believed it yet.

Tomas sat down slowly on a bolt of linen and started, absurdly, to laugh. Casper's mother put her arms around Nell, who had come out of hiding only when Quentin's voice called her, and who was now examining the splintered door with the critical air of a cartographer surveying newly mapped territory.

"They were afraid to shoot," Tomas said. "Did you see? Big rifles and they were afraid of *Ferenc*."

"Not of Ferenc," Quentin said. He went to the window and looked down at the lamps in the lane, at the ordinary faces turned up toward the dark attic, at old Ferenc raising his fist in a salute that was mostly a wave. "Of being the only ones. That's all it ever takes. One person deciding not to be the only one, and then the next, and then it's a street, and then—" He stopped. Below, at the far end of the lane, where the lamps ran out and the darkness toward the harbor began, a light was moving. Not approaching. Receding. But it paused once, at the corner, and held there a long moment — a single lantern, lifted high, the way a man lifts a lantern when he wants to be seen looking.

Casper came to stand beside him. She saw it too.

"He knows about the harbor," she said quietly.

"He knows about the pier. He knows about the Meridian." Quentin's mouth had gone dry. "He can't stop the ship. But he doesn't need to stop the ship. He needs one name at the gangway, one paper challenged, one guard he owns—"

"Dawn," Casper said.

Dawn. The ship loaded at dawn, and the harbor was a mile and a half of frozen streets away, and somewhere between here and the gangway, a hunted man with nothing left but his records and his rage was going to be waiting.

Quentin picked up his coat. Around him the attic was suddenly all motion — Casper waking her mother gently, folding papers, taking Nell's mittens down from the nail where they'd dried overnight; Mrs. Aldova pressing bread into every pocket within reach and complaining the entire time, because grief and courage were both, in her view, best conducted alongside complaint.

Below, the singing had stopped. In the sudden hush, Ferenc's voice carried up clearly through the frozen air:

"They're lighting the harbor beacons early! Get your people moving, clerk — the tide won't wait, and neither will the navy!"

Quentin took Nell's hand. It was very small, and very cold, and it gripped his with the fierce certainty of a child who had drawn the piers from memory and trusted the map.

"Which way?" she asked.

"Down," Quentin said. "Through the middle of the crowd. And Nell — whatever you see at the water, whatever happens — you hold the map in your head, and you don't stop walking."

She nodded solemnly, and together they started down the stairs, past the broken door, out into a lane full of lamps, toward the sea.
