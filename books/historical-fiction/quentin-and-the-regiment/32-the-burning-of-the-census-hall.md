## 32. The Burning of the Census Hall

Quentin Hale had spent eleven years believing that paper was the most durable thing in the world.

Ink outlasted flesh. A record, properly kept, survived the man it described. He had built his whole life on that faith — the neat columns, the cross-referenced registers, the census rolls he had maintained with a scrupulousness his colleagues mistook for ambition and which was, in truth, something closer to piety. Every name entered correctly. Every death registered in its proper hand. Every birth witnessed by the state's cold blue stamp. He had believed that if the world burned down around Velden's ministries, the records would remain, and some future clerk — honest, patient, unnamed — would reconstruct the truth from what he had written.

He was standing in Census Square when he learned how wrong he had been.

* * *

It began, as most things in the capital now began, with a rumor moving faster than an official could stamp it dead.

Quentin had gone to the hall at midmorning because Krein's audit was still open — because even now, three days after the pamphlets flooded the barracks and the bread lines and the ministries themselves, some part of him insisted on procedure. If the regime was dying, let it die in order. Let the last entries be true ones.

The square outside was crowded in the way squares got crowded in the final winter: not with demonstrators but with people waiting. Waiting for ration offices to open. Waiting for ships that were no longer coming to piers that were no longer manned. Waiting, without quite saying so, for the end.

He heard the shout before he understood it.

*"The Meridian manifests!"* A voice from the steps of the grain exchange, cracked and furious. *"They sold us the contracts, they sold the piers, and forty seats on one ship for five hundred thousand of us — and their names are all written down! Written down in there!"*

A arm swept toward the census hall.

Quentin felt the wrongness of it before the crowd did. The manifests weren't in the census hall. The manifests were at the Harbor Office, three districts away, behind two locked doors and a garrison checkpoint. The crowd had the building wrong.

But crowds do not check references.

He should have run. Later, in the years after, he would try to explain to himself why he didn't — why he instead pushed forward through the press of bodies toward the wide stone steps, shouting the truth into faces that didn't want it: *wrong building, wrong records, nothing here but names.* As though names were not, in the end, exactly what the crowd had come for.

The first window went in at half past ten.

* * *

He was inside. That was the thing he could never fully account for afterward. When the glass broke on the second floor and the smell of smoke reached the registry room, Quentin Hale — a man who had once walked four streets out of his way rather than jaywalk — turned and walked back up the stairs into the burning building.

Because the ledgers were there.

His ledgers. Eleven years of them, shelf after shelf of bound registers, the whole population of the ninth district rendered in his careful hand: births, marriages, deaths, the movements of families tracked across decades like weather. Somewhere in those shelves was the entry he had falsified for Nell, marked in his own ink. Somewhere was Casper Voss's brother, dead twice over. Somewhere was every name Krein had ever wanted, catalogued and cross-referenced and ready to be weaponized by whoever held the city next.

He got as far as the registry room door before the smoke drove him back.

Through the doorway he could see the far wall of shelves, and along it a line of fire moving with terrible unhurried patience, the way fire moves through things that have waited years to burn. The spines blackened first. Then the covers curled. And then — this was the moment, the one that stayed — the pages themselves caught, and the light changed.

Burning paper gives off a light he had never seen before and has never seen since. Not orange. Whiter than that. Almost holy. It lit the registry room like noon, and in that light Quentin stood in the doorway and watched eleven years of his work rise off the shelves in sheets of flame and go out through the broken windows into the winter air.

Pages. Hundreds of thousands of pages. Names lifting free of their bindings and sailing up over the square, glowing, each one legible for a heartbeat — he could see the handwriting turning in the wind, his handwriting, the careful Hs and the ruled lines — and then curling into ash and coming down over the crowd like grey snow.

Someone in the square below was reading them as they fell. He could hear it — a woman's voice, catching fragments out of the air. *"—born March — daughter of —"* And then the page was gone, and whatever name had been written on it existed nowhere on earth except in the memory of the person it had named, if that person still lived.

Quentin put his hand against the doorframe and found he was crying.

Not the way he had cried at his sister's funeral — that had been grief, heavy and specific. This was something with no name he knew. It came up through him like water finding a crack, silent and enormous, and it took him several minutes to understand that it was not sorrow.

It was relief.

* * *

Here is what eleven years in the census office had taught him, though he had never said it aloud, never let himself think it in full sentences:

Paper is not neutral. A register is not a mirror. From the day he had taken the post, every line he entered had been a small act of definition — this person exists, this person owes, this person may board, this person may be conscripted, this person is verified. He had told himself he was recording the world. But the world had bent around his records, not the other way round. Men had been marched to the docks because of a column he had ruled. Families had starved in the correct category. Nell had been safe only so long as his forgery held — safe inside a lie he had written, threatened by truths he had never set pen to.

And Krein. Krein had taught him the final lesson: that in a state like theirs, the ledger was not a memory. It was a fist. Every name in the hall was ammunition waiting for whichever hand picked the book up next.

Now the books were burning, and the fist was opening.

He watched the fire take the conscription rolls — the great green volumes, the ones that had fed Tomas Voss to the press gangs — and felt no grief at all. He watched it take the tax registries, the residency files, the index of political associations that Krein himself had compiled, and he thought: *let it be illegible. Let every child born in the ninth district start again as simply a child.*

A beam groaned somewhere above. Heat pressed against his face like a palm. He knew he should leave; some calm, clerical corner of his mind noted that he had perhaps ninety seconds of remaining judgment, and that men who overstayed their judgment in burning buildings became entries in someone else's register.

But he allowed himself one more minute. Because there on the near shelf, not yet touched by flame, sat Volume Fourteen — the current year. His year. The year of Nell's forged entry, of Casper's brother, of the audit he had submitted with three names added and one omitted. The year everything had happened.

He crossed the room — six steps through thickening smoke — and took it down from the shelf.

He didn't know why. He stood there holding it, feeling its weight, the familiar give of its binding, and understood that he was not saving it. Whatever mercy or vanity he'd imagined, it wasn't that. He looked at the cover a long moment, at the state seal stamped in gold, and then he carried it to the window where the fire breathed its white light, and he threw it back into the flames himself.

It caught almost immediately, eager, and the last thing he saw of it was his own name in the clerk-of-record line, going up.

*There,* he thought. *Now it's true.*

Then the ceiling spoke in a language of cracking timber, and Quentin Hale ran.

* * *

He came out through the side entrance into snow and ash, coughing, his eyebrows singed, a scorch mark up one sleeve that he would keep on that coat forever. The square had transformed while he was inside. The crowd was no longer a crowd; it was a congregation. People stood in the falling ash with their hands open, palms up, catching the pages. Some wept. Some laughed. An old man had built a small pile of unburned fragments and was feeding them piece by piece to a candle flame, methodically, like a priest completing a rite.

And everywhere, everywhere, the ash coming down.

Quentin stood at the edge of it, breathing hard, and searched himself for ruin and could not find it.

They had taken his life's work, and it felt like being handed his life back. Eleven years of careful, complicit precision — erased in forty minutes by people who couldn't read a ledger line but understood perfectly what the ledger was for. He had spent a decade keeping the regime's memory accurate, and accuracy had served the regime. Now the memory was gone, and for the first time since his sister died, Quentin felt the future become a place he might actually live in rather than merely record.

Nell. He thought of Nell suddenly, sharply — her map of the harbor pinned inside his coat, her nine-year-old certainty about which piers were guarded. Her whole existence had lived in those burning volumes, one forged line among millions, protected by paper. And now her existence lived nowhere but in him. In his memory, his testimony, his stubborn living body.

That was better. He understood that now, standing in the ash. She was safer as a story than she had ever been as an entry. Entries could be audited. Stories could only be believed.

A woman beside him caught a large fragment — half a page, mostly intact — and read it aloud, wondering: *"Hale, Marguerite. Deceased."* She frowned at him. "Do you know her?"

"My sister," Quentin said.

The woman looked at the scrap, at him, at the burning hall behind him. Then she held the page out. "You should keep it, maybe."

"No," Quentin said. And he took it from her gently, and walked to the nearest fire — a barrel someone had lit against the cold at the square's edge — and gave his sister back to the flames himself. "She's where she belongs."

When he turned around, Casper Voss was standing in the crowd ten feet away, grey-coated, ash in her dark hair, watching him with an expression he had learned to read across a hundred narrow rooms: fear held very still, and underneath it, calculation, and underneath that, something warmer that neither of them ever named aloud in daylight.

"You're alive," she said.

"The building disagreed," he said. "I won."

Behind her, at the far edge of the square, a man in a good coat that no longer looked good stood motionless, watching the fire with the fixed attention of a creditor at an auction. Even at that distance, Quentin knew the posture. Aldous Krein had come to watch his life's instrument burn — and Quentin saw, in the way the inspector's head turned slowly across the crowd, hunting, that the instrument's destruction had not freed Krein. It had only made him desperate.

Krein's gaze passed over them and moved on. For now.

"Casper," Quentin said quietly, not looking away from the distant figure. "The Meridian loads at dawn. And I think we just ran out of places to hide."

She followed his eyes, and when she answered, her voice was steady in the way he had come to trust more than any document he'd ever filed.

"Then we stop hiding," she said. "Tomorrow we walk her straight down the gangway."
