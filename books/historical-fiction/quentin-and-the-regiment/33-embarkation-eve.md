## 33. Embarkation Eve

The harbor smelled of coal smoke and salt and fear.

Casper stood at the mouth of Pier Nine with Nell's hand in one of hers and a canvas bag over her shoulder, watching the *Meridian* take on her first light cargo under a sky the color of wet slate. The ship was smaller than the rumors had made her — a grey-hulled diplomatic packet with a single yellow funnel and forty civilian berths that had once seemed like an arithmetic problem and now seemed like the only number in the world that mattered. Dock cranes swung crates aboard with the slow patience of things that did not know they were living through the end of anything.

"They're small," Nell said.

"What is?"

"The portholes." Nell had gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was memorizing something. "If you were inside and you looked out, you'd only see a piece of the water at a time. Not the whole sea."

"You don't need the whole sea," Casper said. "Just enough to get across it."

Nell considered this with the seriousness she gave to everything, then nodded once, filing it away. Nine years old and already fluent in the language of making do. Quentin had taught her maps; the city had taught her the rest.

They had walked since four in the morning. Through streets where shopkeepers nailed boards over windows they would never reopen, past a bakery queue that had dissolved into argument when someone shouted that the bread was the last of it, down the long hill to the harbor district where every wall wore fresh paint: KEEP ORDER. THE REPUBLIC ENDURES. Someone had crossed out ENDURES in charcoal and written, beneath it, in a child's unsteady hand, *for now*.

Tomas was already at the pier gate. He'd gone ahead an hour before them because five people traveling together drew eyes and two did not, and because he insisted — with his mother's stubbornness and none of her grace about it — that he would not be carried onto that ship like luggage. He stood now among the crowd pressing against the barrier, taller than Casper remembered him being, wearing their father's old coat with the collar turned up, scanning the crowd for them with an expression that was trying very hard to be bored and failing completely.

When he saw her he didn't wave. He just lifted his chin, the way men in this family acknowledged each other across distances, as if emotion were something you paid customs duties on.

But his eyes were bright. And when Casper reached him he gripped her arm above the elbow, hard, once, and let go, and neither of them said anything about it.

"Checkpoints are worse than yesterday," he said quietly. "Two at the pier gate instead of one. They're checking papers twice."

"I know. I watched."

"And?"

"And we do what we planned." She shifted the canvas bag on her shoulder. Inside it, wrapped in Nell's spare dress, lay the folder that had cost more than anyone should ever have to pay for a seat on a boat: the berth documents, made out in the name of Marius Voss — her brother, dead eleven years, whose name she had borrowed so long it sometimes felt like it fit better than her own. Berth Thirty-Nine. One adult, one minor dependent.

The arithmetic had been done months ago, in a kitchen, in tears, in silence. It came down to this: one berth, two names on its papers, two children who could pass for what the papers said they were. Tomas, twenty-two and lean enough to read young if he kept his mouth shut. Nell, small for nine, reading even younger. An orphaned niece listed as the daughter of a man who had never existed, guarded by a boy carrying the identity of a ghost.

It was theft of the purest kind — stealing from the dead to buy the living a future. Casper had stopped apologizing to herself for it somewhere around the third sleepless night.

"The second checkpoint," Tomas said. "The one at the gangway. That's the one that worries me."

"It worries me too."

"There was a captain there this morning. Real uniform, real sidearm, not one of these—" he tipped his head at the militia men slouching by the barrier, boys with armbands and rifles they held like shovels. "A naval officer with a manifest. He was turning people back. Two families sent away while I watched."

"Then we give him nothing to check but paper," Casper said. "Paper is honest. Paper says exactly what we taught it to say."

Nell tugged her sleeve. "Aunt—"

She'd caught herself. They'd agreed on the train platform three days ago: no names that could be overheard, no titles, nothing for a listening ear to pull a thread from. But Nell had looked up at Casper with an expression that needed no thread pulled, and Casper crouched so they were level, and Nell put both arms around her neck and held on the way children hold on — entirely, without any of the adult arithmetic of how long the embrace can last or what it will cost.

"I'll write," Nell whispered into her collar. "I'll write the first day. I'll write so many letters the postman will hate me."

"You'd better." Casper's voice held. She was almost proud of that. "And you'll mind your uncle Quentin, and you'll eat everything on your plate even the fish, especially the fish, because growing girls who are going to see the whole sea need—"

"Aunt Casper." Nell pulled back far enough to look at her, and her eyes were dry and fierce and utterly without self-pity, which was the worst thing about it. "I know why you're staying. I'm not stupid. Papa wasn't stupid either, and Mama wasn't, and you're all the same kind of not-stupid." Her chin trembled once and steadied itself. "So don't talk to me like I'm nine."

"You *are* nine."

"That's different."

* * *

Her mother met them at the fish market, at the corner stall that sold nothing anymore because the boats had stopped going out, and gave each of them a parcel wrapped in brown paper and string — food for the journey, though the journey would be measured in hours, not days. Then she did the thing Casper had known she would do, which was turn away and fuss with the parcels so that no one could see her face while she got it back under control.

Edith Voss had sewn burial shrouds through the siege years, stitching the city's dead with hands that never shook until after the work was done. She understood, better than anyone alive, the discipline of finishing before you grieved.

"Tomas." She handed him the largest parcel. "There's bread underneath the cheese. Eat the cheese first, it won't keep."

"Yes, Mama."

"Nell." A parcel for her too, and a kiss on top of her head, and a murmured word Casper couldn't hear and knew better than to try to.

Then Edith turned to Casper, and for a moment the discipline wavered — Casper saw it go through her mother like weather, the whole storm of it — and then Edith took her daughter's face in both hands, palms rough as sailcloth, and looked at her the way she had looked at her across forty years of mended coats and unspoken things.

"You'll come north," she said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite a plea. It was an order issued in the tone mothers reserve for orders they know may not be obeyed.

"When the road's clear."

"Casper."

"Mama. When the road's clear. Sova has the plates safe, the pamphlet is doing its work, there's nothing left for me here but—" she gestured, helplessly, at the harbor, the ship, the grey water beyond the breakwater, "—but leaving. And I've done my leaving. This is yours."

Edith's jaw worked. Somewhere behind them a crane bell rang and a voice shouted numbers, and the great indifferent machinery of embarkation went on grinding.

"Your father waited once," her mother said finally. "In '09. Waited for the road to be clear, and it never was, and by the time he moved it was too late to move well." Her thumbs traced Casper's cheekbones, once each, like sealing an envelope. "Don't wait for clear roads. There are none. There never were. Go when it's merely possible."

"Yes, Mama."

"Liar," Edith said, without heat, and kissed her forehead, and stepped back, and became again the woman who finished the work first.

* * *

The line at the pier gate moved like a wound clock — slow, jerky, occasionally running backward when someone's papers failed and they were turned out of the queue to begin the humiliation again. Casper watched it for twenty minutes from the shadow of the customs shed, cataloguing.

Two militia at the gate, checking papers against a list. One naval officer inside, at the foot of the gangway, with a clipboard and a face that had surrendered nothing to the collapse of nations. A fourth man — plainclothes, soft-bodied, watchful — drifting along the barrier with the particular stillness of someone paid to notice things.

That fourth one was the problem. Militia could be bored. Officers could be procedural. But a watcher who noticed things would notice a courier's walk, a courier's eyes doing the work of mapping exits, a courier's bag held in the wrong hand for its weight.

"We change the plan," Casper said quietly.

Tomas tensed. "Which part?"

"Not the papers. The walking." She crouched to Nell's level, adjusting the girl's scarf, buying the moment to think. "Families get through. Families are boring. So we're not three fugitives with a forged manifest — we're a big sister taking her little brother and his cousin to meet the ship, and we're late, and we're annoyed about it, and we don't look at soldiers, because nobody looks at soldiers unless they have to."

"I can be annoyed," Nell said. "I'm good at it."

"I know you are, love."

"And I'm the cousin?" Tomas asked.

"On the papers you're her guardian. In the street you're whatever gets us through. Don't perform it. Just be tired. Be a boy who wants this over with."

"I can manage that without performing."

"I know." She straightened. Under her coat, against her ribs, the courier's seal rode in its leather wallet like a coal that wouldn't cool. She should have surrendered it weeks ago. Every day she kept it was another count of treason, and she kept it anyway, because a seal was a key and a key was a door and somewhere ahead there would be a door worth opening. *One more use,* she told it silently, the way you'd quiet a dog. *One more, and then I swear.*

They joined the line.

The queue pressed them forward in small increments, and the crowd around them was a country in miniature: an old professor clutching a bookcase's worth of books tied with rope, arguing with a soldier that learning constituted luggage; a woman with twin babies and no visible papers at all, talking her way toward the gate with a fluency that suggested she'd talked her way through worse; dockworkers loading crates with the deliberate slowness of men stretching the republic's final hour as long as it would go. Everyone was leaving or wished they were. Everyone had a story shaped like a door closing.

At the gate, the militiaman took their papers — all three sets, Casper's included, the false ones, the ones that said she was a seamstress named Verra Koln with no brother and no history — and checked them against his list, and his finger slid down the column and paused.

Casper's heartbeat did not change. Eleven years of courier work had trained that out of her. But some older animal under the training went very still.

"Voss?" the militiaman said.

"Koln," Casper said. "These say Koln."

His finger tapped the list. "There's a Voss party cleared for the Meridian. Party of two. Minor dependent." He looked up. "You related?"

"Everyone's related," Casper said, with the flat irritation of a woman who had queued an hour in the cold. "My name's Koln. Check the papers you've got."

He checked them. They were good papers — Sova's network had made them, and Sova's forgers were artists — and after a moment he waved them through with the contempt of officialdom for the unremarkable. Casper walked on with her heart hammering now, delayed action, the body catching up to what the nerves had refused to feel in the moment.

Because there it was. On a government list, at a government checkpoint: *Voss, party of two.* The berth documents working. The dead brother opening doors. Her own false name passing through the same gate her true name was waiting behind, and the two halves of her divided by ten feet of frozen mud and a bored teenager with a rifle.

Nell's hand found hers and squeezed. The girl had heard. Of course she'd heard. She said nothing, because she was nine-going-on-eternal, and they walked toward the gangway together.

* * *

The naval officer at the gangway was worse than Tomas had described.

Not cruel. Worse than cruel: exact. Lieutenant's boards, gloves white and clean, a clipboard held at regulation angle, and eyes that went through each traveler the way a customs officer goes through a suitcase — politely, thoroughly, missing nothing. Behind him, the plank rose to the *Meridian*'s deck, where sailors waited with outstretched hands, and above them the funnel breathed thin smoke, impatient, alive, counting minutes.

Ahead of Casper's little group, a merchant family — father, mother, grown son — presented papers that gleamed with money. The officer examined them for a long time.

"This endorsement is from the Ministry of Trade," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"The Ministry of Trade ceased functioning Tuesday." He handed the papers back. "Next."

The father began to argue, then thought better of it, then argued anyway, quietly, desperately, and was steered aside by a sailor with the gentle firmness of a man who had done this forty times today and would dream about it later.

"Next."

Tomas stepped up. He laid the folder open to the berth page without being asked, smooth as a card trick: **MARIUS VOSS. PARTY OF TWO. ONE ADULT, ONE MINOR DEPENDENT. CLEARED — MINISTRY OF MARINE AFFAIRS.**

Casper watched the officer's gloved finger trace the ministry stamp. Watched him compare faces to the photograph — Tomas's, then Nell's — and saw the precise moment his eyes narrowed, a fraction, a hair's width of doubt.

"These dependents," he said. "The girl. Relationship to the bearer?"

"Niece," Tomas said. "Orphaned. She's in my care."

"Where are her parents' death registrations?"

"In our district office." Tomas's voice stayed level, admirably. "Which burned."

It had. Half the city's records had burned, one way or another; the census hall's fate was common knowledge now, and half the country's paperwork with it. It was the perfect answer. It was also, Casper realized a half-second too late, an answer that invited verification, and the officer's eyes flicked past Tomas's shoulder — to her.

To her, standing close, the way family stands close.

"And you are?"

Here it was. The door. The coal in her pocket warming. *One more use.*

She opened her mouth to deliver the seamstress — dull, forgettable, a neighbor helping carry a bag —

—and the crowd surged.

It happened all at once, the way crowds do: a shout from the barrier, a whistle, the crack of something heavy falling, and then the whole mass of the queue moving as one animal. Casper caught fragments — *"they're closing the pier," "the tide, they say it's the last high tide,"* — and then bodies pressed around them, and a woman screamed about her husband already aboard, and the militia at the gate started swinging their rifle butts flat-side to hold the line that had stopped existing.

Through the chaos, the officer was shouting for order, his clipboard pinned under his arm, his attention torn between the mob and the manifest — and in that tearing, that one instant of divided focus, Casper made the oldest decision in the courier's repertoire.

She put her hand flat against Nell's back and pushed, gently, irresistibly, toward the plank.

"Go," she said into the girl's hair, under the noise. "Now. Don't run. Walk like it's boring. Walk like your uncle's right behind you."

"He isn't—"

"He's right behind you," Casper lied, steady as a signature. "Walk."

Nell looked back at her — one look, the whole of it compressed into a single gray-eyed glance, nine years old and ancient and furious and brave — and then she turned and walked up the gangway into the noise and the smoke, small shoulders square, a forged niece carrying a forged name toward a real sea, and a sailor's hand reached down from the deck and closed around hers and drew her up and out of Casper's life.

Tomas hesitated at the plank's foot, half-turned, mouth opening—

"Tomas." Casper seized his sleeve. "The papers. Go. *Go.*"

"But you—"

"If you miss this ship I will never forgive you, and I mean that as your sister and as the person holding your ticket." She shoved him, actually shoved him, toward the plank, and he stumbled a step, caught himself, and went — up into the smoke, taller than she remembered, always taller than she remembered, until the deck swallowed him too.

The officer was fighting free of the crowd, shouting, and the gangway was about to close, and Casper Voss stood alone at the bottom of it with empty hands and a full heart, watching the rail where two small figures had appeared, pressed together, looking down.

Then a gloved hand closed on her shoulder and spun her around, and the lieutenant's exact, exhausted face was six inches from hers, and behind him, cutting through the mob with the unhurried certainty of a man who had all the time in the world and intended to spend it on her, came a figure in a grey overcoat that she knew the way you know a nightmare.

Aldous Krein smiled.

"The courier," he said, over the roar of the departing tide and the dying crowd. "I've been looking everywhere for you."
