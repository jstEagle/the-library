## 34. The Last Berth

The Meridian came in on the dawn tide like a grey animal too tired to fight.

She sat low in the water, her hull streaked with salt and old paint, and even from three streets away Casper could hear the groan of her engines turning over — a sound like a giant clearing its throat before saying something it would regret. Around the pier the crowd had already begun to gather: families with trunks tied in rope, clerks clutching portfolios they would never need again, an old man carrying nothing but a birdcage with a dead bird inside because he could not bring himself to leave it behind.

Casper walked with Nell's hand in hers and Tomas's bag over her shoulder, and she kept her eyes forward and her pace unhurried, because hurried people were remembered and unhurried people were furniture. The courier's coat was gone — burned two nights ago in Sova's stove, the lining stripped of its last secrets — but the seal was not gone. The seal rode against her sternum now, flat and heavy as a second heart, tucked into a leather pouch on a cord around her neck.

She should have surrendered it at the Ministry of Last Things on Tuesday. She had stood outside the building for eleven minutes, according to the clock above the door, and then she had turned around and walked home, and no one had stopped her, because no one was left at the ministry who cared about seals when their own names were on the pamphlets being read aloud in the barracks.

*Voss,* her mother had said this morning, pressing bread into her hands in the shroud-maker's attic kitchen. *If they shoot you, I want you shot on a full stomach.*

That was her mother's way of saying goodbye. It had always been her mother's way of saying everything.

* * *

The checkpoint began where Fisherman's Row met the quay — two sawhorses, a trestle table, four soldiers, and a sergeant with a clipboard, which in the final winter of the Republic of Velden was a more frightening weapon than any rifle. Beyond the checkpoint, the gangway ran up to the ship's side, and at the top of the gangway stood two more soldiers and a shipping clerk with a manifest, and beyond *them*, the deck, and beyond that, the sea, and beyond that — Nell had asked once, on the walk down, in her flat, practical way — what exactly? A neutral port. A school that still held classes. A bed that belonged to no one yet.

Casper had answered: *A place where your name is just your name.* And Nell had considered this for a long moment and said, *But mine isn't. Mine is Anton Voss's.* Because that was true. That was the truth Casper had forged for her, in her dead brother's name, one final time, on paper so good that even Quentin — who knew forgery of the soul better than any living soul — had looked at it twice and said quietly, *He'd have wanted this. If he'd known her.*

They joined the queue behind a woman with twins and a soldier's widow who kept straightening a hat that did not need straightening. The line moved in small, reluctant increments. Each family reached the table, surrendered papers, endured the sergeant's slow reading, and either passed through or did not. Casper watched the ones who did not. A young couple turned away over a stamp missing from a marriage certificate. An old woman whose name had been struck from the rolls during the purges of the autumn — Casper saw the sergeant's mouth form the word *irregular* and saw the woman's face do something terrible and quiet, folding up like a letter being put away.

Nell's hand tightened in hers.

"Don't watch them," Nell said. "Watch me."

Casper looked down. Nine years old, hair braided by Tomas that morning with more enthusiasm than skill, wearing a coat her grandmother had sewn — a good coat, a coat with a future in it — and looking up at Casper with the level gray eyes of a girl who had memorized guard rotations on piers for sport.

"I'm watching you," Casper said.

"Good." Nell faced forward again. "Because I'm going to be fine, and someone should verify it."

Tomas laughed beside her — a short, startled sound, the laugh of a boy discovering he still owned one. He was sixteen and had been free from the press gangs for six days and still flinched at boots on cobbles. He carried his own papers now, real ones, arranged by fishermen in smoke-filled rooms, and he kept touching his chest where they sat as if checking that his heart had not been swapped out for documents.

"Forty-first in line," he murmured. "We're forty-first."

"You counted?"

"Nell counted. I just agreed with her."

* * *

The queue crawled. The sun came up properly and found nothing to warm; the light lay on the water like spilled milk. Somewhere behind them in the city, bells were ringing — not alarm bells, not yet, just the ordinary bells of a morning that did not know it was extraordinary, and under the bells Casper could hear, faintly, the sound of the crowd at the census hall ruins, where people had been gathering every day since the burning to read the pamphlets aloud to whoever hadn't heard them yet. Two hundred copies, hidden in flour sacks, and now ten thousand copies in every mouth in Velden. Words traveled faster than ships. Words had already won. What remained was only to get two children past a clipboard.

Thirty-ninth in line. Fortieth. The sergeant yawned, and Casper catalogued him automatically — tired, not cruel, left-handed, more afraid of his own officers than of the crowd — and then they were at the table.

"Papers," the sergeant said.

Casper laid them out with steady hands. Three sets. Her brother Anton Voss's registration, transferred to guardian status — the finest forgery of her life, aged with tea and creased with care, stamped with a ministry seal Sova had reproduced from memory. Tomas Ferenc's conscription release, countersigned by a harbor master who owed the fishermen more than he owed the state. And beneath both, her own hand resting on them, the gesture of a guardian presenting wards.

The sergeant read. He was slow. He was thorough. Casper let him be both.

"Anton Voss," he said. "Age thirty-one." His eyes flicked up. "You're the sister?"

"Aunt," Casper said. "Guardian. Their parents are registered deceased — autumn bombardment, eastern district. Documentation enclosed."

It was enclosed. It was flawless. Quentin had helped her build the death notices himself, and had wept while doing it, though he'd pretended it was the candle smoke.

The sergeant grunted and moved to Tomas's release. Read it. Grunted again. And then Nell stepped forward — small, composed, her braid crooked, her chin at the exact angle of a child who has been told she is traveling and intends to travel — and laid her own paper on the table with both hands, the way children are taught to present things to officials.

And the sergeant's face changed.

* * *

Later — much later, on a road north, in the dark, a thousand times over — Casper would replay the moment and never find the exact hinge of it. Perhaps the ink was wrong. Perhaps the tea-aging had gone one shade too far on the corner fold. Perhaps the sergeant simply had a cousin in the census office and had seen a hundred genuine registrations and knew, in the wordless way that tired men know things, that this paper smelled of candlelight and desperation instead of bureaucracy.

Or perhaps it was none of those things. Perhaps it was only that the world, having spent all winter teaching Casper Voss how much one life costs, decided to present the invoice at the gangway.

"This registration number," the sergeant said slowly, "was issued in March. To a boy. In the northern parishes."

The quay noise seemed to step back from them. The gulls went on screaming, indifferent.

"It was reissued," Casper said. "After the bombardment. Records were consolidated. There's a ministry directive—"

"There's a lot of directives lately." The sergeant picked up Nell's paper and held it closer to the light, and his voice did not rise at all, which was worse. "Sorrel. Kessler. Come here a minute."

Two soldiers detached from the sawhorses. They came over without urgency. One of them had done this before; Casper could see it in his shoulders, the settled patience of a man whose job at checkpoints was to stand slightly behind people and make the arithmetic obvious.

"Problem, Sergeant?" Sorrel asked.

"Papers don't sit right." The sergeant set Nell's registration down on the table between them, face-up, like a card he suspected of cheating. "Girl says she's Anton Voss. Girl looks nine. Registration says eleven, issued to a male ward." He rubbed his eyes. "I've got forty berths to fill and a colonel on the manifest ahead of me, and I haven't slept, and I am asking one simple question." He looked at Casper directly now, and there was no malice in it, which somehow made it harder. "Who are these children to you, honestly?"

Behind him, the line stirred. Someone craned to see. Somewhere a baby started crying, and the crying sounded very far away, as if it were happening in another country.

Casper felt the pouch against her sternum. Felt the seal through the leather — the courier's mark of the Republic of Velden, the crossed quill and key, the authority she had carried for three years through snow and suspicion and ministries full of men signing away the country one dispatch at a time. She should have surrendered it Tuesday. Every hour she had kept it had been theft from a government that no longer deserved the word. But it hung at her neck now, and it was the only thing in the world heavier than fear, and she understood suddenly — with the strange clarity that arrives precisely when it can do the most damage — why she had kept it.

Not to protect herself. She had already erased herself; there was no Casper Voss left on any roll in Velden, and the soldiers could arrest a ghost if they liked. She had kept it because somewhere inside her, below thought, below plan, she had known there would be a table like this one, and a tired sergeant, and a piece of paper that didn't quite sit right, and one moment in which a lie would need to become larger than itself.

She lifted the cord over her head.

"Before you answer me, Sergeant," she said, "you should know who I am."

* * *

The seal came out of the leather pouch and she pressed it flat to the table, beside Nell's imperfect paper, and the sight of it changed the air. Sorrel actually took half a step back. Kessler's hand drifted toward his rifle and stopped, uncertain whether courier seals counted as weapons. Even the sergeant straightened.

"Do you know what this is?" Casper said. Her voice came out low and even, and she was grateful to it, because her pulse was a drumline. "This is the diplomatic courier's seal of the Republic of Velden. With this seal I have carried dispatches through every ministry in this city, including the one that issued that registration you're holding. With this seal I could walk onto that ship without showing you a single paper, and everyone on this quay knows it, including your colonel."

"That seal was supposed to be surrendered Tuesday," the sergeant said. "There was a notice."

"There was. I didn't surrender it. Draw whatever conclusion serves you." She kept her eyes on his. "Here is my conclusion, offered freely. This girl's registration has an error in it — an error made by the same collapsing ministry that sold the contracts, stole the money, and printed the pamphlets your own barracks have been reading for three days. You've read the pamphlets, Sergeant. Don't bother lying; everyone has. So you know that every record that office ever touched is worth exactly the paper it's printed on, and the girl standing in front of you is worth considerably more than that."

The baby was still crying. The tide was still coming in. The Meridian's engines ground on, patient as fate.

"If I'm wrong," Casper said, "arrest me. Take the seal. Write it up. In a week there may even still be an office to write it up *in*. But if you're wrong — if you turn this child back onto this quay, into this city, today — then you'll spend the rest of your life knowing exactly what you chose, and I promise you the rest of your life will include finding out what happened to the people who missed that ship." She paused. "I've seen the manifests, Sergeant. I've seen who bought the berths and what they paid. Do you want to be standing on this pier when those people come back to settle accounts?"

Kessler shifted. Sorrel looked at the sergeant. And the sergeant — tired, left-handed, more afraid of his officers than of her — looked down at the seal, and then at Nell, and Casper watched the whole war pass across his face in miniature: the regime and the republic and the rumor and the rope, all of it weighed against one nine-year-old girl with a crooked braid who had not blinked once.

It was Nell who ended it. Of course it was.

"I *am* Anton Voss," Nell said clearly, in her flat, practical voice. "The records are wrong. Everyone knows the records are wrong. You read the pamphlet." And she held out her hand for her paper, palm up, the way one does with a shopkeeper, as though the matter were merely transactional and the transaction long since complete.

The sergeant looked at her hand for a long moment.

Then he picked up a pen, drew a single stroke through the erroneous entry on Nell's registration, wrote *corrected per courier verification* in the margin, initialed it, and slid the paper back across the table.

"Board," he said. "Both of them. And you—" his eyes flicked to Casper, and something moved in them that might have been respect or exhaustion or the beginning of a story he would tell for years, "—the seal stays with the ship's clerk. Rules are rules."

* * *

The gangway was narrow and iron and rang under their feet. Halfway up, Tomas stopped and grabbed Casper's arm hard enough to bruise.

"You're coming," he said. Not a question. Worse than a question — a demand, a boy's whole heart aimed at her like a lantern. "You're coming on after us. You have to. You—" His voice cracked along a fault line sixteen years deep. "You gave us everything. You don't get to stay."

Casper took his face in her hands. His jaw was already changing, losing its softness; in two years it would be a man's jaw, and she would not see it happen.

"I'll be right behind you," she lied, warmly, easily, the best forgery of her life. "Fishermen. Your mother's arrangement. North, then overland. We'll write. Now go — go on, before I make a scene in front of these nice soldiers."

He hugged her then, fierce and quick, the hug of someone who suspects the truth and cannot afford to inspect it. Then he took Nell's hand, and they went up.

At the top, Nell turned. The morning light caught her fully for the first time — lit her hair gold at the edges, lit the rail, lit the grey water running out toward the edge of the world. She did not wave. She pressed one small hand flat against the rail, fingers spread, and held Casper's eyes, and Casper understood that this was Nell's version of waving: verification. *I am here. Confirm receipt.*

Casper raised her hand from the foot of the gangway. *Confirmed.*

Then a sailor pulled the gangway's head inward, and the rail turned, and the children were folded into the deck's confusion of crates and coats and were gone.

* * *

Casper stood at the bottom for a long time. Long enough that the sergeant, collecting his papers, muttered something almost kind. Long enough that the mooring lines came off the bollards one by one, thick ropes sliding into the water like snakes deciding to swim. She did not move until the Meridian's hull began to separate from the pier — a gap first wide enough for a hand, then an arm, then nothing.

Only then did she feel it: a hand on her shoulder, firm, familiar, smelling faintly of ink and candle smoke.

Quentin. Standing beside her on the emptying quay with the crowd roaring gently behind them and the city smoking softly to the east, and his face was white and his eyes were wet and he was smiling anyway.

"They made it," he said.

"They made it."

"Casper." His grip tightened. "The sergeant sent a runner toward the harbor office five minutes ago. I saw him go."

She turned. Down the quay, moving fast against the current of the crowd, a man in a colonel's greatcoat was striding toward the checkpoint — and behind him, pushing through the press of bodies with the fixed, terrible patience of a man who has lost everything except his anger, came Aldous Krein.

Their eyes met across fifty feet of winter morning.

"Casper Voss," Krein called out, and his voice cut through the crowd like wire. "You have something of mine."

Her hand closed around the seal at her neck — and found, already waiting in her pocket, folded flat, the decoy.
