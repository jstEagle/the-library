## 35. What Casper Carried

The Meridian did not look like a ship of salvation. She looked like what she was — a diplomatic vessel stripped to her rivets, her brass fittings dull with salt, her funnel coughing black smoke into a sky the color of wet ash. Forty berths below decks for men who had signed the surrender, and one civilian berth bought with stolen ministry money, and a gangway wide enough for all the lies Casper Voss had told in her twenty-nine years.

She walked Nell toward it through a crowd that had stopped being a crowd and become a pressure. Refugees pressed against the harbor barriers, soldiers shoved them back with rifle butts held sideways, not quite striking, not quite merciful. Somewhere behind the customs shed a woman was screaming a name over and over, and no one was answering it.

Nell's hand was in hers. Small, chapped from the cold, gripping hard enough to hurt.

"Your papers," Casper said for the fourth time. "When they ask."

"Vale," Nell recited. "Vale Arden, age nine, ward of the estate of Corin Voss, deceased. Berth thirty-nine, paid in full." She'd memorized it in the attic in an hour, the way she memorized piers and patrol gaps and the faces of men who mattered. "And if they ask about you?"

"You don't know me. You're frightened. Cry if you have to."

"I won't have to." Nine years old, and she said it flat as a census entry.

The checkpoint stood at the foot of the gangway: two marines in the grey-green of the Provisional Office and a customs clerk at a folding table with a manifest weighted down by a brass lamp against the wind. Beyond them, the ship's rails were already lined with silhouettes — officials' wives, clerks with leather cases hugged to their chests, a man in ministerial black who would not turn his face toward the quay.

Casper felt the coat first. The weight of it. The courier's grey wool, mended twice at the shoulder, and sewn into its lining the flat oilcloth packet that had crossed the city three times in the last week, from Sova's basement to a safehouse and back, because Sova insisted the plates needed a second proofing before he trusted them to flour sacks. Two hundred pamphlets already loose in the ministries. But the plates themselves, and the cipher key that unlocked every dispatch Krein's masters had ever sent — those still rode against her ribs like a second heart.

*After the children board,* Quentin had said, *you give it up. Promise me.* He was somewhere behind her in the crowd with Tomas and her mother, holding their places at the barrier, because a man with a child's hand in his drew fewer eyes than a woman alone.

*Promise me,* he'd said, and she had lied with her eyes, which by now was almost a kindness between them.

They reached the table. The clerk looked up with the dead patience of a man who had stamped ten thousand lives this morning alone.

"Papers."

Nell produced them herself, both hands, the way they'd practiced. Vale Arden, nine years old, ward of a dead man's estate, berth thirty-nine, paid in full. The clerk turned each page under the lamp. The wind gusted; he pinned the manifest down and squinted.

"Ward of Corin Voss." His eyes came up. "Relation present?"

"She has none present," Casper said. "I'm escorting her on behalf of the estate trustee. I'm not boarding."

The clerk looked at her — grey courier's coat, courier's satchel, courier's nothing face — and whatever he saw made him lose interest. He stamped the manifest. "Berth thirty-nine. Up the gangway, girl. Hold the rail."

Nell didn't move. For one second her grip on Casper's hand tightened to something that wasn't practice at all.

"You'll write?" Nell said. Loud enough for the clerk to hear. A child asking her escort to write to the trustees. Nothing more.

"First chance the roads allow."

"That's what my uncle says too." And then she let go, and went up the gangway with her chin level and her small fists closed on the rail, and Casper watched her all the way until the deck swallowed her, and then watched longer, at the place where she had been, which was harder.

That was when the voice came from behind her left shoulder.

"Courier."

She knew it before she finished turning. Some voices you learn in your spine.

Aldous Krein stood at the edge of the checkpoint, hatless, his coat buttoned wrong — one button off, and she noticed it because Krein was a man who had never once in his life been one button off, and the fact of it told her more than any report. No escort. No soldiers. The Provisional Office had burned him out of its ledgers four days ago along with the ministers he'd served; the pamphlet had done the rest, printing his name beside theirs in type set by candlelight. He was a hunter with no pack, and a hunter with no pack is the most dangerous animal on any quay.

"The original key," he said.

Around them the crowd flowed, checked papers, wept, boarded, was turned away. Nobody looked at a courier talking to a fallen inspector. Invisibility was her oldest coat and she wore it now with everything she had.

"I don't know what you mean," Casper said.

Krein smiled. It was the worst thing she'd seen all day, and she had put a child on a ship leaving a drowning country.

"Madam Voss." Her name, spoken softly, in the open, on the quay where names were currency and hers was counterfeit. "Please don't insult us both. I audited this republic for eleven years. I know what couriers carry. I know that the dispatches your handler gave you in the final week were not diplomacy — they were insurance, and someone very high up sewed them into your lining believing you were too grey to ever be searched." He stepped closer. Wind snapped the collar of his wrong-buttoned coat. "The pamphlet cost me everything. The pamphlet came from plates. Plates require a cipher. And there is exactly one cipher key in this city that opens the ministry's sealed traffic, and it has been riding around in a courier's coat for nine days while I tore my own offices apart looking for it."

"So you want to sell it," Casper said. "To whoever's still buying."

"I want what I was promised." For a moment the smile dropped and underneath it was just a tired middle-aged man with salt-and-pepper stubble and eyes red from sleepless nights. "A berth. A life. I traded eleven years of service for the promise of passage, and the men who promised it are now named criminals in a pamphlet, and the ship—" he nodded at the Meridian, smoke climbing from her funnel— "sails in forty minutes, and I am standing on a quay with nothing but my name, which is worth less than the paper it used to be written on." He extended one gloved hand, palm up. Patient. Almost gentle. "The key, Madam Voss. Give it to me, and I walk away and you never see me again. Refuse, and I call over those marines and tell them a courier is carrying state secrets off the soil they're dying to defend. They will search your coat. You know what they'll find. And your niece's berth will not survive the questioning — paid berths can be revoked, and revoked berths go to officers' widows. I've seen the list."

*Your niece.* He thought Nell was hers. Good. Let him think the leverage reached her heart; it did, but not along the seam he imagined.

Casper looked at his open palm. Then past it, at the gangway, where a small figure in a borrowed coat stood at the rail with her hand pressed flat against it, looking down at the quay, looking for her.

*After the children board, you give it up.*

She had promised Quentin. And here was the moment arriving early, wearing a wrong-buttoned coat, offering her the exit she'd sworn to take — hand over the key, walk away clean. Except the hand was Krein's, and the key in her lining was not one key. It had never been one key.

Sova had taught her that on the third night in the basement, tapping the oilcloth packet with a pipe stem. *Never carry the true thing, girl. Carry the true thing's twin. A decoy isn't a lie — it's a message. It tells the enemy you expected him.* And so the packet against her ribs held two documents: the cipher key, genuine, copied faithfully — and beneath it, folded around it, the decoy, a forgery of Sova's own devising, near-perfect, subtly wrong, keyed to a cipher that had been retired eighteen months ago and whose every use since had been logged, dated, and archived by the telegraph office's sympathetic clerk. Anyone who used the decoy would sign his own confession in every message he sent.

*It doesn't just mislead,* Sova had said. *It testifies.*

The marines were twenty feet away. The crowd was thick. If she refused, Krein would shout, and shouting would bring rifles, and rifles would find the lining, and the lining would hold the decoy anyway — but the search would also delay the ship, and delay meant manifests re-examined, and re-examined manifests meant berth thirty-nine.

If she gave him nothing, she lost everything on this quay.

So she gave him something.

Casper reached into her coat — slowly, the way you move when soldiers are watching, and the marines were watching now, she made sure of it — and drew out the oilcloth packet. Inside it, wrapped in wax paper, lay the folded sheet. She took out the top document. The decoy. Sova's masterpiece. The retired cipher, elegant, authentic-looking, damning to anyone who trusted it.

She placed it in Krein's glove.

"This is the original," she said. "Ministry seal, courier registration, chain of custody intact. I was ordered to destroy it at the border. I'm choosing to trade it instead."

Krein's fingers closed around it. For a long moment he only looked at her, and she understood that he was reading her the way he read ledgers — hunting the irregularity, the line that didn't balance. Eleven years of audits. And what he found, apparently, was a grey courier with nothing left to hide, because the best forgery in the world is a truth delivered flatly by a tired woman on a cold quay.

"They told me you were nobody," he said finally. "A grey coat. Invisible." He pocketed the paper inside his glove, close against his palm. "Whoever told them that committed the last honest act of this government."

"Take my advice," Casper said. "Don't send anything on that cipher. Not one word. Use it once to verify, and burn it."

"Why would you advise me?"

"Because I'm the only person on this quay who wants you gone quietly instead of loudly."

He almost laughed. It died in his throat. Down the harbor a whistle blew — five minutes — and the crowd surged, and Krein glanced toward it, and in that glance she saw the whole of him: the man who believed he had finally, after everything, purchased his way out. He had. He'd paid with a forged key that would hang him the first time he used it, and Sova's people would read his signature in the traffic logs within a week, and the tribunals forming across the water would have one more name handed to them gift-wrapped by his own hand.

Justice outside the law. It looked like a tired woman handing a condemned man the rope and watching him thank her for it.

"One question," Krein said, turning back. "Professional curiosity. The berth — thirty-nine. The money that bought it came out of ministry accounts. I traced it. Sloppy work, whoever did it." His eyes narrowed a fraction. "Was it the printer?"

"Sova doesn't have two coins to rub together."

"No. He doesn't." Krein studied her a moment longer, and whatever conclusion he reached, he filed it in that vast internal ledger of his, and the ledger, today, was short one auditor. "Then enjoy the sailing, madam. One of us should."

He touched his hat brim — he wasn't wearing one, and the gesture completed itself anyway out of eleven years of habit — and turned into the crowd, wrong button and all, walking toward the customs offices where he would presumably try to buy his passage with a dead cipher, and Casper stood in the wind and let him go, and did not breathe fully again until the crowd closed behind his grey shoulders.

* * *

The whistle blew again. Final boarding.

She found her mother and Quentin and Tomas at the barrier, pressed against the iron railing among hundreds of others who could only watch. Her mother's hands found her sleeve — the seamstress's hands, quick even now, straightening the coat's collar with an old unconscious tenderness, and feeling, Casper saw her feel it, the flat absence where the packet had ridden for nine days.

"It's gone," her mother said. Not a question.

"Gone where it does the most good."

Quentin was watching the gangway. "Where's Nell?"

"On deck. Starboard rail, blue coat." Casper pointed. "See her? She hasn't cried once. Not once. She gets that from no one in my family."

Tomas laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a boy pulled out of a press gang six days ago who still flinched at whistles. Then the laugh caught. "The ship's moving."

She was. Lines splashing into the water, the gangway rising on its chains, tugs nosing against the Meridian's hull like dogs herding a reluctant ox. Along the rail, faces. Casper found the blue coat and held it.

Nell was looking down. Searching the quay. Casper raised her arm — not waving, just raising it, the way you signal across a census hall to someone you've verified — and saw the small hand lift from the rail and press outward, palm flat, held there against the widening air.

Hold it there, Nell. Hold it as long as you can see me.

The gap widened. Ten feet. Twenty. The tugs pulled, the screw churned the grey water white, and the Meridian began to draw away from the country that had failed her passengers, carrying one forged identity, one orphan, one boy's future, and not one ounce of the truth — because the truth stayed here, in a printer's basement, in flour sacks, in the coat lining of a woman who had just handed a hunter the instrument of his own conviction.

"Come on," Quentin said quietly. His hand settled on her shoulder, warm through the grey wool. "The north road won't wait, and neither will the columns. We should be at the grain market by dark."

Casper kept her eyes on the shrinking ship a moment more. Somewhere out on that water, a nine-year-old girl was learning the shape of leaving. Somewhere behind them, in a customs office, a ruined man was unfolding a cipher that would betray him by breakfast. And ahead — north, overland, through the falling republic — a border waited where, if she lived long enough to reach it, she intended to stand up and say her name.

Her real one. Out loud. Once.

"By dark," Casper said, and turned from the water, and walked.
