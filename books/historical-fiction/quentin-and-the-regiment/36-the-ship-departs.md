## 36. The Ship Departs

The gangway went up at half past six, and Casper counted every minute of it.

She stood on the quay in the grey courier's coat, the one that had made her invisible for four years and had finally, at gunpoint, made her visible again. Her hands were empty. That was the strangest part. For the whole length of this winter she had carried something — codes, papers, a brother's name, a nation's shame sewn into her lining — and now her hands were empty and the weight of everything she'd ever carried sat instead on the deck of a ship two hundred meters of water away from her, pressing its rail with nine-year-old fingers.

Nell was at the rail. Of course she was. Quentin had told her to stay below with the other children in the hold, and Nell had done exactly what any child raised on forged maps and memorized piers would do: she had come up the moment no one was looking.

Casper could see her clearly even across the widening water — the small dark coat, too big, bought three winters ago for a girl with more growing left in her; the hair escaping its braid in the harbor wind; the hand pressed flat against the white rail as if she could hold the ship to the dock by grip alone.

*Don't look for me yet*, Casper thought. *Look at the water. Look at anything else.*

But Nell looked straight at her. Children always did. They hadn't yet learned that looking was dangerous, that some things could only survive by not being seen. Nell stared across the gap between quay and ship with the ferocious directness of someone who had spent a winter learning which piers were guarded and had decided, in her own private cartography, that her aunt-by-no-blood was worth guarding instead.

Casper raised her hand. Not a wave — waves were for people you expected to see again. She pressed her palm flat against the air, the way Nell pressed hers to the rail, and held it there.

Then she mouthed it, slowly, so there could be no mistake: *Be good. Be brave. Write.*

Nell mouthed something back that Casper couldn't read at that distance, and didn't need to. It was enough that her mouth moved. Enough that the words existed somewhere in the cold air between them, even if they never landed.

A whistle blew on the bridge. The engines changed their note — that deepening hum, like a held breath being let out after four years. The Meridian began to move.

* * *

On deck, Nell kept her hand on the rail because Uncle Quentin had said, before he handed her over to the steward with the brass buttons, *Hold onto something solid when she goes.* He hadn't meant the ship. She'd understood that. But the rail was what she had, so the rail was what she held.

The water opened up between the ship and the quay like a page turning.

She had watched the city her whole life from low places — from the tenement window, from the fish market steps, from the roof where she'd drawn her map of the harbor in charcoal on butcher paper, marking the guard posts with little X's because Uncle Quentin had shown her how census men marked absent households. From up here, the city was different. Smaller. The ministries were just gray rooftops. The census hall — the burned one, the one all the clerks talked about in whispers on the gangway — was just a black tooth in a row of teeth. Even the smoke hanging over everything looked small from here, like steam off a kettle.

*It doesn't look like the place where Mama died,* Nell thought. *It looks like a place somebody drew.*

That was the trick of it, she decided. You got far enough away and everything became a drawing. Maybe that was why grown-ups cried at harbors. They couldn't get far enough away inside their own heads.

The steward with the brass buttons came back along the deck. He'd been counting them earlier, walking up and down with his little book, and Nell had watched him count because watching was what she did best. Now he stopped beside her and frowned at the wind pulling her braid apart.

"Below decks, miss. We're coming round the mole."

"I'm saying goodbye," Nell said.

He looked out at the quay, at the crowd still standing there — hundreds of them, the ones who hadn't gotten berths, the ones who'd sold coats and rings and years of their lives for a place on this ship and lost, the ones who'd simply come to watch the republic leave without them. The crowd wasn't waving. That was the worst thing about it, Nell thought. Nobody down there was waving. They were just standing, all of them, like a photograph of a crowd instead of a real one.

"Two minutes," the steward said, and moved on.

Nell found the woman in the gray coat again. She was easy to find; she stood the way soldiers stood in Nell's drawings, feet planted, facing the thing coming at them. Except nothing was coming at her now. Nothing except the water getting wider.

Nell lifted her hand off the rail — just for a second, just long enough — and pressed it against her own chest, then held it out flat toward the shrinking quay, palm down, the way you'd press a flower in a book.

*You're in here,* the gesture said. *I'm taking you with me.*

* * *

The Meridian cleared the mole at 6:51 by the station clock on the harbor master's wall, and Casper checked it because checking things was the last discipline she had left.

Quentin stood beside her. He had not spoken since the gangway went up. His hands hung at his sides with the particular emptiness of a man whose life's work had been paperwork and who had put the only two documents that mattered onto a ship with a nine-year-old girl — her berth chit, made out in a dead man's name; and the letter Casper had written her, sealed, addressed to be opened "when the mountains are behind you." Everything else he'd ever written or filed or verified had gone up with the census hall, and he had watched it burn and called it freedom, but freedom apparently had a cost collected at docks.

"She's holding the rail," Casper said.

"She always holds on," Quentin said. "Since she was small. Stair rails, cart rails. I used to think she was frightened." He paused. "She's not frightened. She's anchoring herself. She does it when she wants to remember where she is."

The ship's bow swung out toward the channel. Somewhere behind them in the city, a siren started up — not an air raid, not anymore, just the evening shift siren at the gasworks, running on habit in a country that had stopped having evenings that mattered. The sound crossed the water and slid under the ship's engines and disappeared.

Casper mouthed the goodbye. She saw Nell answer it with the gesture — the hand to the chest, the palm held out — and something in her chest turned over like a key in a lock that had been rusted shut all winter.

"She learned that from my mother," Casper said. Her voice came out wrong, scraped. "The pressing-down. My mother does it with dough. And with shrouds. You press the cloth flat so the folds will hold." She swallowed. "I didn't know Nell was watching. I thought none of them were watching."

"People are always watching," Quentin said. "That's what the census taught me. Everyone thinks they're invisible, and every single one of them is in my files." He looked out at the ship. "Was in my files."

The Meridian's stern came around. The wake spread behind it in a long white V, cutting the harbor's gray skin open, closing again, leaving nothing.

"They'll be past the fortifications by midnight," Casper said. "If the channel mines were swept like the manifest claimed."

"You read the manifest?"

"I read everything now. It turns out that's the only skill I have." She almost laughed. Almost. "Four years of carrying sealed paper for men who were selling us, and the whole education comes down to this: I can tell you exactly which ships are safe and which ministers are traitors, and neither piece of knowledge can get me a cup of soup."

"Soup's at the shelter on Cordwainer Street," Quentin said. "Sova's people run it. They asked after you twice today."

"Asked if I was alive or asked if I was useful?"

"Both, I think. In Sova's world those are the same question."

The ship was smaller now. A hundred meters of water. Two hundred. The crowd on the quay had begun to break up in ones and twos, drifting back toward the burning city the way water finds its level, and Casper stayed planted while they flowed around her, because moving felt like abandoning a post and she had never once abandoned a post, not through checkpoints, not through Krein's interrogation, not at the gangway with a pistol three meters from her face and the courier seal in her raised hand.

The seal was still in her pocket. She should have surrendered it weeks ago. It was worthless now — the ministry it authenticated had ceased to exist as a functioning body sometime around the burning of the census hall — but she kept it the way Quentin must once have kept his first census ledger: as proof that a system had existed, and that she had moved through it, and that it had failed to make her into what it wanted.

* * *

From the stern, Nell watched the city fold itself up.

First the cranes went — sliding behind the warehouses, then behind the mole. Then the smokestacks. Then the dome of the old exchange, which had seemed like the top of the sky her entire life and now sank down under the horizon line like a coin dropped in deep water. She counted the things as they vanished. It was what she did with maps: fix the landmarks, mark the order, so you could find your way back.

*Cranes. Smokestacks. Exchange dome. Gasworks tower.*

The gasworks tower lasted longest, thin and black against the sunset, and Nell kept her eyes on it until it went, and when it went something in her throat closed hard and stayed closed.

Because the gasworks tower was the last thing, and after the last thing there was only water, and water in every direction meant that the city was not behind her anymore. It was nowhere. Places you could see were places you could go back to. Places that slipped under the edge of the world were just stories.

"Miss?" The brass-buttoned steward again. "Storm canvas going forward. You'll want to come below."

"Is Tomas below?" she asked. "Tomas Voss. He's sixteen, he has a—"

"He's in the aft hold helping stow cargo, miss. Been asking after you too." The steward hesitated, and then, in the softer voice adults used when they'd decided a child had earned the truth: "Your brother cried at the rail this morning. Before loading. Thought nobody saw."

"Tomas doesn't cry."

"No, miss," the steward agreed, and touched his cap, and went forward.

Nell went back to the rail. Below decks there were bunks and blankets and a stove and Tomas, and all of it would keep. Up here the light was doing something to the water she needed to see — turning it from gray to copper to a color she didn't have a name for, the color of the inside of a shell, the color maybe of wherever ships went when they left dying countries.

She took the stub of charcoal pencil from her coat pocket — she'd carried it since the attic, since the night the plans were made, since before — and flattened a piece of wrapping paper against the rail with her forearm, the wind trying to take it, her hand holding it down, holding everything down, the way she did.

And she drew the quay as it had been. The crowd standing silent. The gray-coated woman with her palm raised. The whistle. The wake opening like a page.

She wrote the date at the bottom, small, in the corner, the way Uncle Quentin dated his ledgers. Then, underneath, because maps needed titles:

*Last view of Velden. From the Meridian. Drawn true.*

* * *

By full dark the ship was a set of lights losing an argument with the distance, and Casper still hadn't moved.

"Cordwainer Street," Quentin said quietly. "They'll lock the shelter doors at ten. And Casper—" he turned to face her fully, and in the lamplight from the harbor office window his tired face looked suddenly older and younger at once, the way faces did when they'd been stripped down to what they actually were, "—the road north leaves at dawn. Sova says the refugee column forms at the flour mill. If we're not in it, we walk alone, and alone is what Krein's people are best at finding."

"I know."

"He has nothing left, Casper. A man like that with nothing left doesn't grieve. He hunts."

"I know." She watched the last light of the ship — the stern lamp, riding high, steady. "Quentin. Do you think she'll write? Really? First chance, from whatever port, whatever camp, whatever cousin-of-a-cousin takes them in — do you think the letter will actually come?"

Quentin considered it with the seriousness he brought to everything, the gravity of a man who had spent his life verifying what was true and refused, ever, to offer comfort he couldn't stand behind.

"She memorized the guarded piers of an armed harbor at eight years old," he said. "She kept your secret before she knew she was keeping one. She draws maps that save lives and dates them so they can be checked." He looked out at the water. "Yes. The letter will come. It will probably arrive before we do anywhere."

The stern lamp winked once — a swell between them and it, nothing more — and steadied, and slid smaller, and then the sea had it, and the sea had all of it, and the Republic of Velden's last diplomatic vessel was a darkness among darknesses heading for a horizon that no longer belonged to anyone.

Casper lowered her hand at last. It ached from being held up. She flexed the fingers, one by one, like a woman testing whether she still owned her own body, and found that she did — that whatever else the winter had taken, the hands were hers, and tomorrow they would walk north.

"All right," she said, to Quentin, to the empty water, to the city smoldering behind her. "Dawn. The flour mill."

She turned her back on the harbor — the first time in her life she had ever turned her back on a departing ship, and it felt less like surrender than like setting a compass — and together they walked up out of the docklands, toward Cordwainer Street, toward sleep if sleep would have them, toward the long road and the border and the name she would one day speak aloud.

Behind them, unseen by either of them, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the customs shed and watched them go.

Krein had missed the ship.
