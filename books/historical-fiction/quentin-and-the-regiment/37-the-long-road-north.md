## 37. The Long Road North

The road out of the capital was a river that had forgotten how to flow.

Casper walked in it anyway, one body among ten thousand, her mother's arm looped through hers, Quentin a grey shape at her shoulder with Nell's absence sitting on him like a second coat. Behind them the city burned its own archives and rang its bells wrong — three strokes where four should be, a signal she didn't want to understand. Ahead of them, somewhere past the frozen marshes and the burned granary at Vellmark, there was a border pass with a neutral flag over it, and beyond that a country where no one would ask her name.

She had given away every name she owned. She was walking north as nobody, and nobody, it turned out, could walk very fast.

"Left foot," her mother said, the way she'd been saying it for two days. "Right foot. That's all a road is."

"I know how roads work, Mama."

"You know how cities work. Cities end. Roads don't." Her mother, Ilsa Voss, sixty-one years old, seamstress, shroud-maker to the coming siege, had packed for exile as though packing a parcel: one needle case, one spool of waxed thread, two changes of linen, and the mended coats on their backs, each seam reinforced with the double stitch she used on funeral garments because — she'd said, tightening the last knot — *these clothes have hard work ahead of them.*

The column moved at the speed of its weakest cart. There were carts: handbarrows stacked with sewing machines and photograph frames and, in one heartbreakingly careful arrangement, an entire tea service wrapped in a quilt. A farmer drove a cow. Children ran the ditches like dogs let off leads. Somewhere behind them, artillery spoke in short bored sentences, and everyone pretended not to hear, the way you pretend not to hear a couple arguing in the next room.

Casper counted exits. She couldn't stop counting them. Every bridge had a second crossing point if you knew the marsh paths; every village had a back lane; every checkpoint had a clerk who wanted a bribe or a woman who wanted a story. Ten years as a courier had taught her that every wall has a door, and every door has a price, and most prices are cheaper than people think.

But Krein's men wouldn't be looking for doors. They'd be looking for her.

* * *

They heard about the first killing on the second night, in a barn outside Drenholt where forty refugees slept standing up because there wasn't floor enough to lie down.

A man from the fish market — Casper had noticed him earlier, broad hands, a voice like a foghorn — had gone back for his wife's loom. He'd reached the city gates before the curfew closed around him, and what came back down the road instead of the man was a boy of perhaps fourteen who'd seen it happen. The boy told it in fragments by candlelight, and the barn went very quiet, the way water goes quiet before it freezes.

"They weren't soldiers," the boy kept saying. "They weren't wearing anything. Grey coats. They asked him his business and he showed them his papers and they laughed."

Grey coats. Casper's old uniform. Someone had taken the courier's livery and turned it inside out, which was exactly the sort of thing Aldous Krein would find funny now that the pamphlets had stripped him naked. Two hundred copies hidden in flour sacks had become twenty thousand copies passed hand to hand; the ministries had read their own treason at breakfast; and Krein — discarded, exposed, hunted by the same machine he'd serviced — had done what wounded predators do. He'd stopped hunting for the ministers and started hunting for the couriers.

For her.

"He can't cross the border," Quentin murmured. They stood in the barn doorway, watching snow come down like static. "Neutral ground means nothing to him, but he knows he can't follow past it. So everything he does, he does here, in the next hundred miles."

"He doesn't need a hundred miles." Casper watched her breath drift. "He needs a roadblock and a list. He knows my description. He knows your face from the census rolls — half those rolls burned, but not the ones he copied. And he knows my mother's shop." She said the last part flatly, because saying it any other way would cost more than she had.

Quentin was quiet a moment. In the dark his profile was just an idea of a man, patient and angular, a draughtsman's idea. "Then we shouldn't travel as ourselves."

"We can't forge papers anymore. I traded my brother's identity to get Tomas off the conscription roll. There is no paper left that says I'm anyone."

"No." Quentin turned to look at her, and there was something in his face she'd seen only twice before — once in the tea house when he admitted what he'd done for Nell, once the night the census hall burned and he'd watched nine years of careful records go up in sparks and called it freedom. It was the face of a man about to use his knowledge of paperwork as a weapon. "You're thinking like a courier. Think like a census taker. What do the columns ask?"

"Name. Age. Occupation. Place of origin. Next of kin."

"And what did I spend nine years learning? That the column is never checked against the person. Only against the other columns." He said it gently, as if offering her something fragile. "We don't need forged documents, Casper. We need consistent answers. A family of three, travelling to relatives in the north, whose names match from village to village. The papers will burn or rot or be demanded and surrendered. The answers are the document."

Ilsa appeared between them with the silent authority of a woman carrying a pot of something hot. "Then decide what our names are before morning," she said. "I'm too old to be invented twice."

* * *

They became the Brandt family. Anton and Maretta Brandt of Lowden, a town far enough south to explain their accent and destroyed recently enough to explain their lack of papers — the fire at Lowden was real, three weeks old, and half the road was using it. Quentin took the surname because he argued, with census-taker logic, that a widow and her daughter's husband would be registered as a household, and households drew less scrutiny than single travellers. Casper was Maretta now. Her mother wore the name like an old coat, testing the sleeves.

"Maretta," Ilsa repeated, chopping the word in half with her knife at supper, bread and salted pork shared among six strangers under a lean-to. "I had a cousin named Maretta. Terrible with money. Died falling off a horse she couldn't afford."

"That's perfect," Quentin said seriously. "If anyone asks, we know details."

Casper almost laughed — the first time in weeks — and the laugh came out cracked, and her mother put a hand over hers without looking, and that was worse than tears, so she looked at the fire instead and ate.

Tomas and Nell were a hundred miles behind them now, on a fishing boat out of Salt Harbor with nets stacked over the hold, and Casper thought about them the way you touch a bruise: briefly, deliberately, to confirm it still hurts and therefore is still yours. Nell would be drawing maps of the boat. Tomas would be pretending not to be afraid and failing beautifully. They were alive. They were north. Everything else was arithmetic.

The third day the road climbed into pine country and the column thinned, families peeling off toward farm cousins and timber villages, until the walkers were mostly the stubborn and the wanted. Casper learned to read the road the way she'd once read ministries: who walked loose and who walked hunched, who answered questions quickly and who rehearsed them, which pairs of men in grey coats were deserters and which were something else. Twice she spotted watchers on ridgelines. Both times they were gone when she looked again, and both times she said nothing, because panic moves through a column faster than cholera and kills more slowly but just as surely.

On the fifth day the watchers stopped being subtle.

* * *

It happened at the ford at Kessel's Mill, where the river ran shallow over slate and the column funneled down to a track barely wide enough for the cow. Four men waited on the far bank. Not soldiers — no insignia, mismatched boots, but rifles held with the ease of practice, and one of them holding a folded paper he consulted between arrivals like a priest with a breviary.

A list. She knew it in her stomach before her eyes confirmed it: the way he glanced up at each face, down at the page, up again. Names and descriptions. Krein had typed his revenge.

"Walk," Casper breathed. "Don't slow. Don't look at the water. We cross like everyone else."

They joined the knot of families pressing down to the ford. The slate under the shallows was treacherous; a woman ahead slipped and swore magnificently, and the small human comedy of it — the cow bellowing, a child shrieking with delight — gave cover, noise and chaos, everyone jostling. Casper put her mother upstream of her and Quentin downstream and they crossed in the middle of the crowd, heads down, ordinary, three more tired faces in a river of them.

The man with the list caught Ilsa's elbow as she stepped onto the far bank.

Casper's heart went off like a dropped lantern.

"Name?" he said. He was young. That was the first mercy — young, chapped lips, the rifle slung carelessly, a hired man rather than a believer.

"Brandt," her mother said, with the mild annoyance of a woman whose feet hurt. "Maretta Brandt. And unless you've made the rivers toll-free since yesterday, I paid at the last bridge like everyone else, so this had better not be about money."

The young man blinked. Down the page. Up at her face. Whatever Krein's list said about Ilsa Voss — and Casper knew with sick certainty it said something, *seamstress, shroud-maker, Ropewalk Street* — it evidently didn't say *woman who argues like a tax collector*, because the young man's certainty wobbled.

"It's not about money."

"Good. Because exile has ruined me thoroughly." She pulled her elbow free with dignity and limped on, and Casper took the offered arm and Quentin fell in behind, and the three of them walked up the far track at exactly the pace of exhaustion, neither hurrying nor dawdling, while Casper counted her own heartbeats and got to forty before her lungs agreed to work again.

At the top of the rise Quentin exhaled. "Your mother," he said, "has been forging herself for seventy years. We merely wrote it down."

"She raised me on stories that weren't true so I'd learn how true ones worked." Casper glanced back. The four men were still at the ford, working through the crowd, checking, checking. One of them looked up at the ridge, at the three figures silhouetted against the sky, and held there a moment too long.

Then the snow came down harder and the figure became weather, and the road bent north, and they walked.

* * *

That night they slept in the loft of a chapel that had stopped being a chapel when its congregation scattered, and Casper lay on hay that smelled of dust and old hymns and listened to the wind worry the roof, and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do for five days.

Krein had a list. Lists were checkable. Checkable things were dangerous — she had built her whole life on that principle, had smuggled state secrets sewn into coat linings because a sealed dispatch gets logged and a seam doesn't. But a man with a list on a road full of refugees could only check so fast, and every day the border came closer, and every day his own faction rotted further behind him. Sova's pamphlet had named names; tribunals were forming in exile cities she'd only ever delivered codes to; the men who sold the republic were discovering that paper, once loose, does not go back in the drawer.

Krein was running out of country. She just had to be faster than his despair.

Beside her, Quentin slept the way he did everything — carefully, on his side, hands folded like a man conserving himself for some final audit. She watched him a while and felt the strange new weight of it, the tenderness that had grown between print-shop nights and attic days, unannounced, undiscussed, durable as the double-stitched seams in her coat. When this was over — if this was ever over — there would be time. Her mother had already begun referring to him as "your Quentin" in a tone that permitted no correction.

Sleep took her eventually, dreamless, the sleep of the genuinely spent, which is the only honest sleep there is.

* * *

The mountains announced themselves on the eighth day as a white line on the horizon that didn't move no matter how far they walked, and the whole column seemed to lift a finger's width, all ten thousand hearts recalibrating at once. The border pass was two days beyond. There would be guards there — neutral guards, indifferent guards, guards who cared about tariffs and tuberculosis and nothing else in the world — and there would be a desk, and a question, and an answer required.

Casper had been practicing her answer for eight days. *Maretta Brandt.* It fit her mouth fine now.

But on the ninth evening, in a timber camp where refugees slept between stacked logs and the smell of cut pine was sharp as vinegar, a girl came down the track asking for them by description — not by name, by description: *a tall man who walks like a schoolmaster, an older woman with a needle case, a woman with a courier's stride*. Sova's network, still alive, still passing messages the way Casper had once passed ciphers: person to person, mouth to ear, nothing written down.

The girl was twelve and fearless. "There's a man at Vellmark asking after you," she recited. "Not grey coats anymore — he travels alone now, his men gone over the border or dead. He shows a photograph. He pays in silver. He told the ferry keeper he only wants to talk." She delivered it flat, memorized, and then held out her palm for the biscuit tradition demanded.

Quentin and Casper looked at each other across the firelight.

"Alone," Quentin said.

"Krein alone is worse than Krein with men," Casper said. "Men can be bribed, dodged, misdirected. Alone means he's stopped caring about capture. Alone means—" She stopped. She remembered the quay, his hand clamped on her arm, his breath sour with ruin: *the cipher key, courier, and I'll forget your face.* She'd handed him the decoy and watched him walk away believing he held the truth, and she had known even then that a man like Krein does not stay deceived. He verifies. And when he verifies — when he discovers the plates were never in his hand, that the exposé lives in exile ink because of her — verification becomes fury.

"He wants the key," she said quietly. "Or he wants me. Possibly he no longer distinguishes."

Her mother, darning by firelight, did not look up. "How far is Vellmark from here?"

"A day behind us. Maybe less, if he has horses."

"Then we don't sleep tomorrow," Ilsa said, and bit her thread.

The pass was a day and a half ahead. Krein was a day behind, gaining, alone, carrying a photograph of her face and whatever was left of his purpose. Between them: frozen marsh, the burned granary, one road narrowing as it climbed, and a border desk where someone would ask her name.

Casper fed the fire another log and began, in her head, to plan the last hundred miles — and found, to her surprise, that her hands were steady. Fear had been her companion so long it had become a kind of competence. Let him come up the road. She had crossed borders all her life with contraband in her coat lining, and she had never once been stopped carrying the only thing that mattered:

The truth, moving faster than the man chasing it.

Far to the north, past the white line, the neutral flags waited. And somewhere on the dark road behind them, hooves struck frost, keeping pace, patient as winter.
