## 40. Letters to the Meridian

The letter came on a Tuesday in April, when the exile town of Harrowmere was still deciding whether to believe in spring.

Casper saw it first from the window of the room she shared with Quentin — a narrow attic room above a cobbler's shop, with a view of the harbor that was wrong in every particular and right in one: it faced east, toward the sea, toward everything they had left. A girl in a grey coat stood at the foot of the street reading a paper by lamplight, and even before the postman turned away Casper knew. She knew the way you know weather coming over water.

She took the stairs too fast, caught her elbow on the doorframe, did not care.

"Quentin."

He was at the table with his mending — he mended now, endlessly, a habit the road had given him, as if keeping things whole were a discipline he could practice against all the things that hadn't stayed whole. He looked up. She held out the envelope without speaking, because if she spoke she would have to say something true about her hands shaking, and she wasn't ready.

The envelope bore a Veldenish stamp, the old republic's seal, the one with the broken crown that had meant nothing for fifty years until suddenly it had meant something again. The postmark was smudged. The address was in a child's careful hand, each letter formed like it was being graded: **Mrs. C. VOSS** — and beneath it, added in different ink, smaller, almost apologetic: *and Mr. Q. Hale*.

They had not told anyone aboard the ship what they were to each other. Nell had guessed anyway. Children always knew; they simply lacked the paperwork.

"Open it," Quentin said. His voice was steady. His hands were not.

Casper opened it with the bread knife because her fingers wouldn't cooperate, and the smell of the paper reached her before the words did — salt and coal smoke and the specific industrial sweetness of a ship's hold, carried across four hundred miles of ocean in an envelope. For one absurd moment she pressed the page to her face like a child with her mother's shawl.

Then she read it aloud, because some things are not meant to be read alone.

---

*Dear Aunt Casper and Uncle Quentin,*

*I am writing this myself and not asking the schoolmistress to check it, so if there are mistakes you must forgive them and me both.*

*Tomas says hello. He has grown a full inch since December, I measured him against the doorframe in Mrs. Aldous's boarding house where we live, which is very nice except the stairs squeak and Tomas says they squeak in Veldenish so we pretend we are home. He is apprenticed to a boatbuilder. He says wood is honest, unlike ministries. I think he is funnier than he used to be. Grief made him funny, which the schoolmistress says is common.*

*We got your parcel. The mittens fit. The book is too advanced for me but I am reading it anyway because you said it was important and I do everything you say, which you should remember.*

*School here is strange. They teach us the history of Velden already, as if it is finished, like a country is a book you can close. They say the Republic fell because it was weak. I raised my hand and said it fell because certain men sold it, and I know their names, and the teacher said young lady where did you learn such things, and I said my aunt is writing the true record and when it is published you will have to teach from hers instead. I got kept after class. It was worth it. Tomas laughed for ten minutes.*

*Mama Voss —*

Here the handwriting changed, larger, slower, the letters of a woman who had learned penmanship for invoices and never for love.

*— sends her own regards below. She is well. We both are. The sea agreed with her better than anyone expected. She sews now for the widow's society and has opinions about every coat in Harrowmere and tells me yours are cut wrong, which I am instructed to convey verbatim.*

*Uncle Quentin: I still have the map. I drew a new one of this harbor. There is a gap in the patrol route on the north quay on Sundays. You will understand why I mention this. Some habits are inheritances.*

*Aunt Casper. I will write the rest only to you, but Mama will read it over my shoulder, so everyone will know it anyway, which is how families work.*

*You gave me your seat. I have thought about this every day for five months. At first I thought I would have to spend my whole life earning it, and that thought was very heavy. But then I understood: you did not give me a debt. You gave me a job. The job is to be alive, properly, all the way, for all of us who could not be. I am doing the job. I get up early. I eat everything on my plate. I learn things. When people are unkind I am kind back, which annoys them extremely.*

*Tell Sova the pamphlet reached us even here. A sailor had a copy folded in his kit. He read it aloud in the forecastle and half the crew cried and the other half swore oaths. He let me keep a corner of it, the corner with the names. I keep it inside my atlas, on the page with Velden, so the names stay home even though we couldn't.*

*Write back. Write long. Tell me everything, especially the parts you think are too much for a child, because I decided last winter that I am not a child anymore, and Mama agrees, mostly.*

*I press my hand to things for you. Rails, walls, windows. You know why.*

*Your niece,
Nell*

*P.S. — I have decided to be a printer when I grow up. Nobody asked my opinion about this. I am informing you as a courtesy.*

---

Casper read the postscript twice and then set the letter down on the table with exaggerated care, the way you set down something explosive.

"She's going to be a printer," she said.

"I heard."

"Sova will be insufferable. Wherever he is. He'll say *I always said the trade runs in strange blood.*"

Quentin was smiling in the way she'd learned meant his chest hurt. "She signed it *your niece*. Both our names. She's assigned us kinship in writing. That's legally binding in at least nine jurisdictions, I checked once, professionally."

"Then it's settled."

"It was settled on the gangway." He reached across the table and took her hand — the left one, and looked pointedly at it, and she felt her ears go hot like a girl of nineteen.

Because that was the other thing that had happened in Harrowmere, the quiet thing, the thing done without pamphlets or witnesses beyond a landlady and a fishmonger who'd lent them his shop for twenty minutes on a wet morning in February. There had been no white dress. Casper had worn her grey courier's coat, because it was the only garment she owned in which she had ever been entirely herself, and Quentin had worn the good jacket she'd re-stitched for him, and the vows they exchanged were short and slightly illegal in two particulars, having been adapted by Quentin from census forms — *to have and to hold, to record and to keep, to count each other always, and never again to mark either of us verified by anyone else's hand.*

The fishmonger had cried. The landlady had brought a cake with a filing error in the icing — her word, and they'd adopted it.

"We should tell them properly," Casper said now. "In the reply. Mother first. Then Tomas. Then Nell last, with a line she'll pretend not to be pleased about."

"You've drafted it already."

"Three drafts."

He laughed, and got up, and kissed the top of her head on his way to put the kettle on, and Casper sat in the lamplight with her family's voices still salt-damp in her hands, and let herself have the moment — the whole moment, uncensored, unrationed — before turning to the work.

Because there was always the work. That was the last thing the war had taught her and the first thing peace confirmed: grief doesn't end, it gets transcribed.

* * *

The archive occupied the back room of the Harrowmere lending library, granted to her by a librarian who had read the pamphlet in a flour sack in another life and never forgotten it. Three shelves, growing. Casper worked there six days a week under a green-shaded lamp, and the work had a name now, stenciled on a card taped to the shelf end: **THE TRUE RECORD OF THE REPUBLIC OF VELDEN, VOL. I: THE CONTRACTS**.

It was slow, painstaking, unglamorous labor, and she loved it with a ferocity that frightened people who hadn't seen what its absence cost.

Sova's plates had survived — smuggled north in those same flour sacks, hidden in a cooperage outside the capital while the city burned and fell and changed hands. From them, and from the cipher key she'd kept sewn into a new coat lining (the decoy copy long since served its purpose on Krein's desk, wherever Krein was now; the tribunals in exile had issued a warrant for him in March, and the last report placed him running, which Casper found she could not celebrate, only note), she and a committee of three exiled clerks had spent the winter reconstructing the ledger entire. Every contract. Every signature. Every berth on the Meridian purchased with ministry funds while civilians drowned at the docks or scattered into conscription columns.

Names went into the record. Not accusations — names, dates, figures, sources. Quentin had insisted on the distinction, sitting across from her the first week with his auditor's patience.

"A pamphlet accuses," he'd said. "An archive testifies. Anyone can call it lies. No one can call a shipping manifest a lie; they can only wish it were."

So she testified. Page by page. And the strangest thing, the thing she wrote to Nell about and then crossed out and then wrote again, was that the work had healed something she hadn't known was broken: her brother.

Not Tomas — Tomas was whole, was measuring himself against doorframes, was learning honest wood. Her other brother. The dead one whose name she had worn like a borrowed coat for seven years. In the archive, in Volume One, chapter four, there was a section headed **USURPED IDENTITIES**, and in it, documented with dates and clerk signatures, was the full accounting of how a ministry registrar named Ostrava Helt had appropriated the identity of Joren Voss, deceased 9 years prior, to open accounts, sign contracts, and eventually purchase Meridian berth number eleven. Helt sat now in an exile detention house awaiting tribunal. Joren Voss sat now in a bound volume in a library in Harrowmere, restored — his death recorded truly, his name cleared of every transaction committed in its shadow.

Casper had written that section in one sitting, fourteen hours, no lamp oil spared, and when she finished she had gone home and slept twelve hours and woken lighter than she had been since the first snowfall of the last winter of the republic.

Her brother had died twice. Now he had, in the only way available to any of them, come back.

* * *

In May, the first copies of Volume One came off a rented press in the cellar of the lending library, and Casper insisted on setting the type for the title page herself. Sova would have wept at her kerning. She didn't care.

Two hundred copies. One for the exile government's tribunal office. One for the Veldenish school in the next town over — addressed, per Nell's instructions, *to the attention of the teacher who keeps my niece after class*, with a note: *For correction of your curriculum. You're welcome.* One for the widow's society, where her mother presided over hems and grudges in equal measure. One for a sailor named Brannic, care of the forecastle of whatever ship would have him.

And one, wrapped in brown paper and twine, that she and Quentin posted together on a bright cold morning, walking to the mail coach hand in hand like the ordinary married couple they had finally, formally, become.

"That one's not going anywhere," Quentin observed, reading the address upside down. "It's going to the ruins of the census hall. Which is rubble. Which has no postal delivery."

"It's going to Velden," Casper corrected. "To the city. Someone will find it. Someone always finds paper. It's the one thing our century has taught me." She handed it over the counter, watched the clerk frank it, and felt the small seismic satisfaction of a document dispatched toward the place it was sworn to reach. "Let it sit in a ruin until the city stands again. It'll wait. Truth keeps better than most things. Better than bread, better than promises. It just needs someone to come along and read it."

Quentin squeezed her hand. "And then?"

"And then whoever reads it starts Volume Two."

On the walk home the gulls were loud and the harbor light was gold and wrong-shaped and theirs, and Casper thought about rails — ship rails, harbor walls, the backs of chairs — and about a girl somewhere across the water pressing her palm flat against each one, doing the job, being alive all the way through.

There was ink on her fingers that wouldn't wash off entirely anymore. She had stopped trying.

At the door of the cobbler's shop, Quentin paused. "Letter came for you at the library today," he said, too casually. "Foreign post. Heavy envelope. Official seal."

Whose seal, she asked.

"The tribunal," he said. "Veldenish crest. And Casper—" he was smiling, that chest-hurt smile, handing it to her on the doorstep in the gold light— "it's addressed to *Madame Voss*. Your real name. Spelled correctly. First time anyone official has ever managed it."

She weighed the envelope in her hand. Somewhere inside it was a summons, or a commission, or an apology from a republic that no longer existed — and any of the three would mean more work, more testimony, more pages.

Casper Voss opened her mail standing up, as always, ready.
