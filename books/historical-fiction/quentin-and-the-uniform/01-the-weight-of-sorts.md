## 1. The Weight of Sorts

The bell over the door had a crack in it, and so it rang flat — a dead note, like a coin dropped on felt. Sebastian Vane heard it forty times a day and never got used to it. Everything else in the shop she had tuned herself: the press's treadle oiled to a whisper, the type cases reglued and rebrassed, the ink stones ground smooth as river rock. The bell her father had bought cheap from a salvage barge, and she could not bring herself to replace it. A shop ought to sound like itself.

"Morning, Master Vane," called Mrs. Okonkwo from the doorway, breath steaming, a basket of washing on her hip. "Is the notice ready? They're burying him Thursday."

"It'll be ready by noon," Sebastian said, keeping her voice where it lived — low, unhurried, a young man's voice with a cold in it that never quite went away. Three years of practice. She no longer thought about the pitch; only about not thinking about the pitch.

"The widow wants his rank on it. Sergeant, they said."

"I'll set it large." She came out from behind the counter with the galley already in hand. "Come see if the spacing pleases you before I cast it."

Mrs. Okonkwo leaned over the tray of composed type — mirror-wise, backwards to every eye but the press's — and read the inverted letters the way the whole neighborhood had learned to, squinting, lips moving. *In Memory of Sergeant Tomas Ellery. Fell in Service at the Mill Gate. Beloved Husband. Father of Four.* The widow had come yesterday with the details written on the back of a laundry list, weeping too hard to dictate, and Sebastian had sat her down with tea and copied out the names of the four children one at a time until the woman could speak again.

"That's him," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "That's our Tomas." She touched the edge of the galley, careful, the way you'd touch a coffin rail. "You've a gift, Master Vane. Your father had it too. He'd be proud."

"Thank you," Sebastian said, and meant none of it and all of it, because the pride was real and the *Master* was a costume fitted so long ago she'd forgotten what her own shoulders looked like without it.

She printed the broadside while Mrs. Okonkwo waited — fifty copies, black ink on the good rag stock she kept under the counter for exactly these jobs, the stock that cost her margin and bought her something better than money. The press took the impression clean. She pulled each sheet, hung it on the drying lines strung across the back room like strange grey laundry, and handed Mrs. Okonkwo six still-damp copies wrapped in newsprint.

"For the house, for the church, for the mother," Sebastian said. "And one extra, in case the wind takes one off a fence."

"You think of everything." The old woman pressed a few coins into her palm — fewer than the price, more than she could spare, and both of them pretended otherwise, which was its own kind of currency in Aldenmoor. "God keep you, boy."

*Boy.* Thirty-one years old last month, and the word fit her like a glove turned inside out. She pocketed the coins and went back to the bench.

* * *

The bench was the true heart of the shop, and everyone who knew anything about printing knew it. Customers saw the press — the great iron Adams with its flywheel and its romance, the machine they pointed their children at through the window. But Sebastian spent her days here, at her father's type-cutting bench, where the steel punches were made that became the matrices that became the sorts, each letter a small column of lead alloy cast in hand moulds and set by hand into pages. Her father Gideon Vane had been famous for it in a small way: the Pemberton face, cut when he was an apprentice, still used by three shops along the river for legal work because it stood up to bad paper and worse handling. Sebastian had been cutting since she was nine, standing on a crate, filing burrs off her father's rejects. Now the files were hers, and the gravers, and the little oil lamp whose flame she bent with a jeweler's breath to inspect a shoulder at magnification.

This morning the job was a new face, or half of one — a titling sort for funeral broadsides specifically, because the standard capitals looked wrong above a name like *Tomas Ellery*. Too proud. Grief wanted a letter that stood straight without boasting. She had been turning the problem over for two days, sketching on scraps between jobs, and this morning she finally cut the first punch.

It went slowly. It always went slowly. She took a blank of type steel the size of her thumbnail, annealed it soft in the lamp, and began driving the graver into the face of it, cutting the letter in reverse, in relief, working by feel as much as sight because a slip of the graver ruined a day's work and there was no undoing steel. Outside, Aldenmoor woke up: carts on Cordwainer Street, the fishwives arguing prices, the garrison band practicing something martial and slightly out of tune from across the canal. Inside, there was only the bite of steel and the small percussion of her own breathing.

She thought, as she always did at the bench, about her father's hands. How he'd held a graver like a pen, how he used to say the alphabet was the most democratic thing ever made — *every man's property, girl, and no man's master* — and how he'd laughed when nine-year-old Seb had asked why the letters had to be backwards. *Because the truth reads right only from one side,* he'd said. *Your job is to be the side it reads from.*

He'd been talking about type. She had taken it as scripture.

The punch emerged by midmorning: a capital T with a slightly lowered crossbar, serifs trimmed to a human modesty, the stem carrying just enough contrast to catch light without showing off. She smoked it, pressed it into softened wax to check the impression, held it up against the window light. It was good. It was better than good — it was the best thing she'd cut since the memorial face she'd made the winter her father died, the one nobody knew existed because she'd cut it alone at night and printed nothing from it but a single proof she kept folded in the drawer with her mother's letters.

She set the punch aside to harden and struck the matrix, and by the time the noon bell rang from St. Anselm's she had cast twenty sorts of the new letter, cooling in their little rack like silver ingots. Twenty letters of grief, measured in points. She weighed them in her palm out of habit. Her father had taught her that too: *a page of mourning should weigh less than a page of celebration, or the mourners will feel it in their hands and resent you.*

* * *

The afternoon was commerce, which was to say the neighborhood came through the door in its usual procession and Sebastian performed the role of Sebastian.

Mr. Havel the chandler wanted five hundred circulars for lamp oil, and haggled over the price of paper until Sebastian showed him two stocks side by side and let him discover for himself that the cheap one would drink his ink and print his prices looking like apologies. He paid full rate, grumbling, satisfied.

Two boys from the ropewalk brought a copybook page for their sister — the schoolmistress required fair copies, and the shop charged a penny for a model hand. Sebastian wrote it out in her careful upright script, the one that belonged to no gender in particular, and sent them off with a warning not to drag their nibs.

A clerk from the customs house brought legal copy for the Pemberton face, because the Pemberton face was required by statute for bills of lading, and the statute was the nearest thing her father had left behind besides her. She set the job while the clerk watched, as clerks liked to do, and felt the familiar double awareness: his eyes on the craft, admiring, and his eyes on her, reading a young man, seeing nothing. That was the arrangement. The whole city admired the shop's hand and none of them knew whose hand it was. Even the customers who'd known Gideon Vane — and there were a dozen old men who still came in to tell stories about him — even they believed only that the son had inherited the gift. The daughter had died, they supposed, or married away, or never existed. Papers didn't follow girls out of trades. Girls followed papers out.

There had been a girl once, in the first weeks after the fever took her parents. There had been a girl who answered the knock of the assessor's men with her hair pinned up and her voice pitched wrong, and who had seen in the first man's bored glance exactly how fast a household could lose its trade license, its lease, its standing — a widow's son could inherit a shop; an orphaned daughter would be packed off to an aunt in the country or a position in service, and the presses would go to some cousin or creditor within the season. The girl had lasted eleven seconds. Then she had said, in a voice scraped low, *I'm Sebastian,* and the name had stuck to her like size to paper, and the girl had gone wherever unrecorded things go.

Sebastian Vane kept the books, paid the rent, drank ale at the Anchor when the guild met, shook hands with rough palms she kept deliberately roughened with pumice, and went home to the rooms above the shop where she unlaced the binding and let her hair down and ate supper alone at the table where three settings had been the custom once and then two and then one.

Loneliness was not quite the word. Loneliness implied wanting company, any company. What she had was more specific: she was known by two hundred people and known by no one. Every soul on Cordwainer Street would swear the Vane boy was solid, generous, the finest hand on the river. Not one of them had ever heard her actual laugh. She wasn't sure she remembered it herself.

* * *

At dusk she locked the door and turned the sign and climbed to the back room where the drying lines held the day's work, and she took down one of Sergeant Ellery's broadsides and read it right-way-round at last. *Beloved husband. Father of four.* Good spacing. The new capital T sat above his name like a held breath, dignified, restrained, exactly what she'd wanted. Somewhere across the district a widow would pin this to a wall and touch the letters, and she would never know that the letter above her husband's name had been invented that morning by a woman she'd called *boy* twice in one day.

That was the trade. That was the whole of it, some evenings: she gave the neighborhood its record — births and deaths and debts and marriages, the fair copies and the funeral sheets — and the neighborhood gave her cover, and neither transaction was acknowledged, and both were indispensable.

She was reaching for the snuffer when she noticed the slip of paper that had been pushed under the door sometime during the evening. Not customer paper. Cheap grey stock, torn unevenly, the handwriting hurried:

*Master Vane — a government man has been asking after the households on our street. Taking names. Asking ages. He was at the Okonkwos' an hour past and asked after your family too, and how long the shop has been yours. Thought you'd want to know. — F.*

Ferryman's son, probably. One of the boys who loitered by the canal.

Sebastian stood holding the note a long moment while the lamp guttered. A census taker. They came every few years; they were supposed to come; there was nothing in a census taker's visit that a legitimate household need fear.

But her household rolls said what her father had written them to say seven years ago, and the dates had a seam in them if anyone looked closely, and Quentin Ashe — for she knew already who the new census man was; everyone on the street remarked on the pale, precise stranger with the ledger who counted doors like a man counting sins — Quentin Ashe had a reputation, the ferryman's boy said, for looking closely.

She folded the note and put it in the drawer with her mother's letters, next to the single proof of the face she'd cut for her father, and stood a moment longer in the dark shop, listening to the flat cracked silence where the bell would have rung if anyone had come in.

Then she banked the lamp and went upstairs to look at the household book, and to decide which lies needed reinforcing before morning.
