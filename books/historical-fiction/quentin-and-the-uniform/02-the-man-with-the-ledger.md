## 2. The Man with the Ledger

Quentin Ashe had learned to make the ledger boring.

That was the trick of it, the thing no one told you when they handed you the satchel and the stiff-backed book and the little brass-tipped pencil that was supposed to last the season. Boredom was armor. A census taker who moved through a district like weather — noticed by everyone, interesting to no one — could count eleven hundred souls in a winter without once being invited inside for tea or threatened over a disputed chimney. Quentin had perfected the art of being forgotten before he finished writing down your name. He'd been practicing longer than most men practiced anything.

*Name of head of household. Trade. Persons abiding therein, their ages, their condition.*

He wrote small. He wrote fast. He did not look up more than courtesy required.

The morning was the kind Aldenmoor specialized in come February: a gray so complete it seemed less like weather than like the city's own opinion of itself. Sleet ticked against the canvas of his satchel. His boots were soaked through at the seams, and by noon his left heel would be raw, because it was always raw by noon. The river smelled of coal and dead fish and, faintly, of the tanneries upwind, which meant the wind had turned against him again.

Quentin worked the river wards because no one else wanted them. The hill districts were warm houses and long lunches; the merchants' quarter tipped you to go away quickly. The river wards gave you neither comfort nor coin, but they gave you something Quentin valued more, though he'd have denied it under oath: they gave you accurate answers. Poor people lied about their ages sometimes, and about how many children still lived, but they almost never lied about what they did. Work was work. It was the only honest column in the whole book.

He started on Tanner's Lane, where the doors stood open despite the cold because the dyeworks vented heat into the street and the street had learned to take what it could get. He counted the Ossler family — six persons, one lodger, the lodger's age guessed at and admitted as such. He counted Widow Ferren, who made him wait while she found her spectacles and then read her own entry back to him twice, checking his spelling of *Ferren*, which he had spelled correctly both times. He counted the dyers' bunkhouse, fourteen men in two rooms, and wrote *condition: exhausted* in the margin before catching himself and striking it out.

By midday he'd filled four pages. His pencil was down to a stub. The sleet had graduated to snow, fat and indifferent, gathering in the gutters like the beginning of an argument.

It was on the way back along Cordwainer Street — taking the long route, because the short one passed the garrison wall and he disliked being looked at by soldiers, a dislike he considered professional rather than personal — that he came to the print shop.

Everyone knew the print shop. Even Quentin, who made a discipline of knowing nothing about anybody, knew Pemberton & Vane. You couldn't live in the river wards and not know it. The window glowed amber even on a day like this, and the smell coming off it — ink and hot metal and paper dust — was the closest thing the neighborhood had to incense. Funeral broadsides came out of that shop. Wedding notices. Petitions about the grain weights. When the mill girl had drowned at the sluice gates two summers past, it was the print shop's broadside that named her, in letters fine enough to be read at arm's length and bold enough to be read across a square, and half the ward had stood in the snow outside reading it, and several had wept, and nobody had asked who cut the type.

Quentin had asked. Once. Silently, to himself, standing across the street with his ledger under his arm. It was the kind of question he was not permitted anymore.

He pushed open the door.

The bell above it was a printer's sort, he realized later — an actual piece of cast type, some enormous wooden letter, repurposed — and it knocked a dull wooden knock instead of ringing. Inside, the warmth hit him like a hand. The shop was narrower than its reputation suggested, and deeper, a corridor of shelves and cases with a press at the far end squatting under its leather cover like a sleeping animal. Candles burned in jars. Paper hung from lines overhead to dry, sheets of it ghosting in the draft from the door, and Quentin walked through them the way you walk through a curtain of other people's words.

A young man stood at the composing bench with his back to the door, sleeves rolled, setting type by hand with a speed that looked less like labor than like breathing.

"Closed to orders till Thursday," the young man said, without turning around. "Broadside run. If it's the grain petition, the answer's the same as November."

"It's not the grain petition." Quentin unwound his scarf. "Census."

That got him a turn. And here is where Quentin Ashe, who had counted eleven hundred faces without retaining one, made his first mistake of the season: he looked.

Not glanced. Looked. The young man at the bench had ink to the second knuckle and sawdust in his hair and the particular posture of someone who has spent ten thousand hours bent over a case — shoulders forward, neck slightly bowed, a craftsman's stoop worn into the spine like a watermark. He was perhaps twenty. Perhaps twenty-one. His hands were quick and certain and there was a burn scar across the back of the left one, old and shiny.

And his face —

His face stopped Quentin the way a misprinted page stops a reader. Something familiar in it, something he couldn't place, some arrangement of bone and expression that rang a bell in a room he hadn't entered in years. He felt the wrongness of his own attention and corrected it, dropping his eyes to the ledger, opening it, wetting the pencil stub against his tongue out of habit.

"Pemberton & Vane," he said. "Household. I'll need names and ages of all persons abiding here."

"You've got it in front of you," the young man said. "You lot came round in autumn."

"The autumn enumeration was incomplete for this address." This was true. Quentin knew it was true because he had checked it twice, in the evenings, for reasons he had not examined closely. "The registrar flagged it."

"I doubt that."

"Nevertheless."

The young man set down his composing stick with exaggerated care, the way a man puts down a weapon he's decided not to use. "Sebastian Vane. Proprietor. Age nineteen. One person abiding herein. My late father's shop. Will that do?"

Quentin wrote it. *Vane, Sebastian. Printer. 19.* The pencil scratched. The candles guttered in the draft. And then he made his second mistake, which was larger, and which he would spend many sleepless hours afterward trying to understand — not why he said it, but why he had wanted to say it since the moment the boy turned around.

"Sebastian Vane," he repeated. "Any relation to Edmund Vane?"

The silence that followed had texture. Quentin had spent years listening to silences — in registries, in hearing rooms, in the office where they'd struck his own name from the rolls while he stood in the corridor counting floor tiles — and this one was not confusion and it was not grief. It was calculation. Fast, cold, weighing him.

"He was my father," Sebastian Vane said.

"Your father." Quentin kept his voice level, clerk-flat. "Edmund Vane died in — I have it somewhere — forty-four? There was an obituary. The *Gazette* ran one."

"People die. Newspapers notice. What's your question?"

"No question." Quentin smiled the smile he used on hostile households, the one that apologized for existing. "Routine confirmation. Families are frequently misrecorded."

"Then record me correctly and be gone." But the young man's hand had drifted, casually, terribly, to rest flat on the composing stick, and Quentin understood two things in the same instant. First: that Sebastian Vane knew exactly what Quentin was really asking. Second: that Quentin did not himself know yet what he was really asking — and that this frightened him more than any answer would.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing Quentin had carried since autumn, folded small at the bottom of the satchel like a letter he couldn't bear to post.

In the autumn enumeration, taken by a different man, a bored man, a man who drank, the household at this address had been recorded as: *Pemberton & Vane, printers. Pemberton, Josiah, partner, 61. Vane, Sebastian, apprentice, 16.*

Josiah Pemberton had died the following spring. Everyone knew that too; the whole ward had drunk to his memory at the Anchor. Fine. Dead partners happened.

But sixteen-year-old apprentices named Sebastian Vane had also happened — specifically, they had happened in the burial register of Saint Anselm's, in the winter of '43, where Quentin had once, years ago, in another life, in a life with a desk and a title and a name that appeared in official documents, transcribed the entry himself: *Vane, Sebastian, son of Edmund, aged 12, of fever.* Aged twelve. Dead of fever. Buried in the north plot beside his mother, whose name Quentin had also transcribed, because in those days he transcribed everything, faithfully, compulsively, the way other men drank.

Twelve then. Nineteen now. Seven years between the grave and the composing bench, and a face that rang a bell in a room Quentin hadn't entered since the university.

He should have written *confirmed* and left. Every professional instinct he owned — every instinct that had kept him invisible, unremarkable, safe — said *write confirmed and leave.* The flag on this file wasn't even his. He'd inherited it from the autumn man. He could pass it along unexamined and never think of it again, and in exchange keep the only thing he had left, which was his own unexamined life.

Instead Quentin Ashe closed the ledger, which was the gesture of a man concluding business, and then did not leave.

"How long have you run this shop alone?" he asked.

"Since spring."

"A young proprietor. That's unusual."

"Not so unusual as a census taker who reads obituaries."

The candle nearest them guttered and steadied. Somewhere behind the press, water dripped from a roof leak into a bucket with the patience of a clock. Sebastian Vane had not moved his hand from the composing stick, and Quentin had not moved toward the door, and between them hung the whole weight of the question — the one question too many, the one that separates a clerk from an inquisitor, the one that gets people erased.

Because Quentin knew what happened next, if he asked it. He'd sat on the other side of it once. A registrar with a thin mouth and a clean collar, tapping a file, saying *explain the discrepancy,* and Quentin explaining, and the explanation not mattering, because discrepancies don't want explanations, they want verdicts. He knew exactly what machinery he held in his hands right now. One line in a ledger. One word in a margin — *investigate* — and the registrars would come, and they would not come alone, and the shop with its amber window and its funeral broadsides and its grieving neighbors would simply stop appearing in records, the way certain pamphlets stopped appearing, the way certain names stopped appearing, the way his own name had stopped.

*Vane, Sebastian, aged 19, printer.*

*Vane, Sebastian, aged 12, of fever.*

One of these entries was true. Both of them were in the books. And the books, Quentin knew better than anyone alive, always won — unless somebody did something about the books.

"Mr. Vane," he said quietly. "When I confirm an entry, I put my mark against it. My mark says: I have seen this household, and it is as written." He opened the ledger again, slowly, and turned back through the pages — past the dyers' bunkhouse, past Widow Ferren and her spectacles, past eleven hundred souls rendered in a stub of pencil — until he reached the autumn record, the flagged one, the one with the registrar's thin annotation in red: *verify identity of apprentice.*

"And if I put my mark against yours," Quentin said, "and anyone ever looks — a supervisor, an auditor, a historian, thirty years from now, some clerk with nothing better to do — they will find what I found. They will find a boy buried at Saint Anselm's running a print shop seven years after his own funeral." He met the young man's eyes. "So before I sign anything, I'm going to ask you one question. Only one. And I want you to consider very carefully whether you'd rather lie to me badly or tell me the truth at all."

The shop was silent except for the bucket. Snow ticked at the window. On the drying lines overhead, a thousand borrowed words stirred in the draft, waiting for ink.

Sebastian Vane's hand came off the composing stick.

"Ask it, then," the young man said — and the voice, the voice itself, was suddenly the answer arriving ahead of the question, low and steady and pitched just slightly, deliberately, a half-step below where nature had set it.

Quentin took a breath. Outside, the snow was filling his bootprints already, erasing the evidence that he had ever walked this street at all.

"Who is buried in the north plot at Saint Anselm's?"
