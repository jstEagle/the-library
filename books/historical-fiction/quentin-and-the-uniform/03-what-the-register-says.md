## 3. What the Register Says

The knock came at half past seven in the morning, and by the time Sebastian reached the door the street was empty.

She stood in the doorway of the shop with her hand still on the latch, looking out at Aldenmoor Lane as it woke around her: the baker's boy hauling his basket up from the cellar, Mrs. Corrigan shaking ash from her stoop, the milk cart rattling toward the square with its cargo of thin blue morning. Ordinary. Unremarkable. Nobody running. Nobody watching her door.

Except somebody had knocked, and nobody had waited for an answer.

On the step lay a folded paper, weighted down with a pebble against the wind. She let it lie for a full minute while she made herself breathe like a person with nothing to hide. Then she picked it up, closed the door, and turned the bolt — a habit she'd never needed before and suspected she would need often now.

It was an official form, printed on the government's own stock, which meant it had come off a press not unlike hers, set by hands not unlike hers, and she hated it a little for that intimacy. *Notice of Inquiry*, the heading read. *Household of Pemberton & Vane, No. 9 Aldenmoor Lane.* Below it, in the cramped hand of a clerk trained to write small so the record could hold more souls per page:

*Discrepancy noted between returns of 1841 and present enumeration. Sebastian Vane, son of the late Edmund Vane, listed age eleven (b. 1837) in prior return; present occupant gives age nineteen. Requesting verification of birth register entry. Household flagged pending review.*

She read it three times. The words didn't change. That was the cruelty of print — she had built her life on the principle that ink was honest, that a line of type said what it said and kept saying it, and now the whole machinery of her craft was aimed at her like a loaded barrel.

Eleven. In 1841 she had been eleven, and her father had written *Sebastian Vane, son, aged 11* on the census roll because by then the decision had already been made, sealed two years earlier when her mother sat her down on this very floor among the type cases and explained what the world did to girls who wanted a trade, and what they would do instead. Her mother had cut her hair herself, with the shop's shears, over newspaper spread on the floorboards, and swept the dark curls into the fire, and said, *There. Now you're whoever you say you are.* It was the only gift her mother ever gave her that cost nothing and everything at once.

And now some clerk with neat handwriting wanted the birth register.

Sebastian carried the notice back through the shop, past the press — old Hargrave's iron, older than her father, older than the war that had brought printing to Aldenmoor in force — past the composing stone where yesterday's funeral broadside still sat locked in its chase, waiting for the paper run. She set the notice down on the bench beside the type cabinet and put her palm flat on the wood, feeling the grain, feeling the hundred thousand hours of her life soaked into it like oil into a whetstone.

Her father's bench. Her father's punches and gravers, his lead alloy recipe written nowhere but inside her skull, his name still lettered on the window glass in gold leaf gone cloudy: PEMBERTON & VANE. He had been dead six years. Pemberton had been dead nine. And she was the Vane, the whole of the Vane, compressed into a false name and a bound chest and a voice she kept low and even the way her father had taught her to keep his tools — oiled, sharp, out of sight until wanted.

*Requesting verification of birth register entry.*

There was no entry. There never had been. Her mother had paid the parish clerk to look away in 1837, and the clerk had looked away so thoroughly that Sebastian Vane existed in no book anywhere in Aldenmoor except the ones her father had falsified himself. Which meant the inquiry could go one of two ways: either the register would show nothing and the absence itself would be the evidence, or worse — the registrar's memory, the midwife's ledger, some aunt's letter — and the truth would surface whole and undeniable.

Either way, she was finished. A woman practicing a guild trade under a man's name wasn't fined or shamed. She was erased. Struck from the rolls, barred from the shop, forbidden the press, and the neighbors who loved her broadsides would cross the street to avoid her eyes. She had watched it happen once, to a woman named Harrow who had tried to take her late husband's boat license; within a season Harrow was selling matches on the bridge and pretending not to hear her own name.

Sebastian filled the kettle because her hands wanted work, then stood watching it instead of working, and finally admitted what she had known since the first reading of the notice:

She would have to run.

* * *

Packing a life is a lesson in arithmetic.

She started with the obvious things and abandoned them one by one. Clothes: two shirts, the good coat, the wool stockings — enough. Money: the tin behind the third drawer, four pounds eleven and what her father called the funeral sovereign, sewn into a coat cuff years ago and never spent; it seemed right that it should buy her escape instead of her burial. Tools: this was where the arithmetic turned brutal.

A graver weighed almost nothing. So did a burnisher, a file, a jeweler's loupe. But a punch — a steel punch, cut by hand, the master pattern from which every piece of type in a face is struck — a punch was a few ounces of steel that contained months of labor and, in her case, her entire inheritance. Her father's punches. The Aldenmoor text face he'd cut in the year she was born, the display capitals he'd finished the winter before he died, each one wrapped in waxed paper and nested in cedar like reliquary bones. Two hundred and forty-one pieces. She had counted them as a child the way other children counted sheep.

She couldn't carry them all. She couldn't carry half. She knelt at the cabinet and lifted them out one at a time, weighing each in her hand as if the metal might tell her which letters mattered most, and understood that she was being asked to choose which parts of her father would survive.

E. T. A. R. S. N. O. The common sorts first — a language reduced to its skeleton. Then the lowercase, then the figures, then, after a long argument with herself, the ampersand, because her father had cut it as a joke, two serpents kissing, and had laughed until he coughed when he showed it to her. She wrapped them in a flannel shirt and packed them into a satchel that had held her schoolbooks when she was small enough to have schoolbooks.

That left the type cases themselves. Three hundredweight of lead and wood, sorted and labeled in her father's hand, holding not just the foundry stock but the shop's whole capacity — the means of every bill, every ballad sheet, every wedding notice and funeral card the lane had asked of her for six years. You cannot flee with a print shop. You can only flee from one.

She stood in the middle of the room and let the silence argue with her.

*Run,* said the notice on the bench, in its neat clerk's hand. *Run tonight, before the registrar answers, before the inquiry comes down the lane in boots.*

*Stay,* said everything else. Stay, said the forme still locked on the stone, the funeral broadside for little Tam Whistler, dead of the fever, whose mother had wept with gratitude because Sebastian had cut the boy's initials into a new ornament rather than use the standard fleuron — *he liked birds,* the mother had said, *he was always in my garden shouting at the sparrows,* and so Sebastian had spent three hours cutting a sparrow, wings up, beak open, mid-shout. It was the best thing she'd cut all year and it existed for one grieving woman on one street that would vanish if Sebastian vanished. Stay, said the widow Corrigan's rent receipts and the cooper's apprenticeship papers and the debt notes she held informally for half the lane because people trusted the shop the way they trusted a church. Stay, said the press itself, Hargrave's iron, bolted to the floorboards her father had reinforced, immovable as a headstone.

And stay, most treacherously, said her mother. Not any ghost with a voice — her mother had left no voice behind, only a smell of lye soap and a way of standing with her weight on both feet — but the memory of her, kneeling on these same boards with the shears, saying *now you're whoever you say you are.* Whoever. Not wherever. Her mother hadn't fled when the plague year came and took her; she'd stayed and nursed the street and died in the bed upstairs with the window open to hear the presses. Running felt like a betrayal of something her mother had never said aloud: that the shop was not where Sebastian hid. It was where she lived.

But a press can't testify for you at an inquiry. A press can't explain why the birth register has a hole where you should be.

By noon she had stopped packing and started pacing, which was how Quentin Ashe found her when he returned at one o'clock with his ledger under his arm and his knock, this time, patient and repeated.

* * *

She nearly didn't answer the door. She stood behind it with her ear against the wood, listening to him wait, and thought about the back stair, the coal yard, the river path — all the routes she'd mapped since morning and not taken, because taking one meant the thing was real.

He knocked again. "Master Vane," he said, in the flat, careful voice of a man reading from a form. "I've a correction to make to your household return. It will take a moment."

A correction. She turned the bolt.

He came in the way he had come before, hat in hand, eyes going everywhere and settling nowhere — a professional habit, she realized now, the gaze of a man who reads rooms the way she read proofs, scanning for errors. He was younger than his tiredness suggested. Thirty, perhaps, with the kind of face that offices forget the moment it leaves them.

"The registrar," he began, "has been slow in answering my request, and I find I dislike loose ends." He set his ledger on the corner of the bench, precisely parallel to its edge, and opened it to a page marked with a slip of blue paper. "So I'll ask you directly, and spare us both the paperwork."

"Ask."

"Your father registered your birth late. Or never. I've been to the parish house twice." He looked at her then, straight on, and there was no triumph in his face, which frightened her more than triumph would have. There was only a kind of exhausted attention, the look of a man carrying something heavy and deciding whether to put it down. "I know what a missing record usually means, Master Vane. Illegitimacy, mostly. Sometimes debt. Sometimes worse. I've flagged hundreds of households and slept well after every one of them."

"And mine?"

He didn't answer at once. Instead he reached into his coat and produced the notice — her notice, the twin of the one on her bench — and held it up between two fingers. Then, slowly, deliberately, watching her face the whole time, he tore it in half. And in half again. And set the pieces on the bench like a small dead thing.

"There's been an error," he said. "The household of Pemberton & Vane presents no discrepancy. The clerk misread the prior return. Eleven was a transcription fault for seventeen. These things happen constantly; I'm astonished the office wastes its paper." His voice never changed register, never warmed, but his hands were not quite steady, and she saw that the steadiness of his voice was costing him something. "You'll receive no further visits from me. As far as the census is concerned, this household is exactly what it claims to be."

Sebastian discovered she had stopped breathing somewhere around the second tear. She made herself start again, slowly, the way she'd taught herself to pull a proof — even pressure, no jerks, nothing wasted.

"Why," she said. Not thank you. Thank you would have been a confession, and besides, she genuinely wanted to know. Men didn't tear up government paper out of kindness. Kindness was expensive and clerks were poor.

Quentin Ashe closed his ledger. For a moment he looked at the window, at the gold-leafed name gone cloudy, at the street beyond where Mrs. Corrigan was arguing cheerfully with the milk cart driver about a broken crock.

"Because eight years ago someone struck my name from the university rolls for a theft I didn't commit," he said, "and I have spent every day since learning precisely how easy it is, and how permanent, and what it does to a person to have their existence decided by other people's paperwork. And because—" he paused, and she watched him decide whether to spend the next sentence, and spend it he did— "because when I asked you about your age last week, you looked at me the way I used to look in mirrors. Like a man checking whether the disguise was showing."

The kettle, forgotten since morning, had gone cold on its stand. Somewhere in the type cabinet the ampersand lay wrapped in flannel, packed for a flight that suddenly had no destination.

"I don't know what you mean," Sebastian said.

"No," Quentin agreed mildly. "I expect you don't. Well." He tucked the ledger under his arm and moved toward the door, and at the threshold he stopped, half-turned, and delivered the sentence that undid the whole morning's arithmetic — the packing, the punches, the four pounds eleven, the cold kettle, the river path she'd never taken.

"I'm told the shop takes on apprentices sometimes. If you ever find yourself short-handed, Master Vane, I set a fair hand, and I ask no questions I don't want answered myself. Good day."

Then the door shut behind him and Sebastian Vane stood alone in her father's shop, surrounded by everything she had failed to abandon, listening to his footsteps fade down Aldenmoor Lane — and understanding, with the slow cold certainty of ink drying, that the man had just handed her his own erasure as collateral, and that she would spend the rest of her life deciding whether that was a gift or a trap.

She went to the cabinet, unwrapped the ampersand, and set the two kissing serpents upright on the bench where she could see them.

Then she unlocked the door again, and left it unlocked.
