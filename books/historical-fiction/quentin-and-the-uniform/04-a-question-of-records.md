## 4. A Question of Records

Quentin Ashe had spent eleven years learning that the most dangerous thing a man could do was look too closely at anything.

The census rolls rewarded the opposite habit. You read names, ages, trades, and you wrote them down, and you did not ask whether the cooper on Cordwainer Lane was truly fifty-three or whether his wife existed or whether the three children listed as "scholars" could read. You transcribed. You moved on. The ledger did not care about truth; it cared only for completeness, and even completeness it accepted in approximate form.

But he had been an archivist before he had been nothing at all, and some habits survived erasure the way roots survive a burned field.

So when he closed the door of Pemberton & Vane behind him and stood in the slush of Printer's Row with the household file in his satchel, he did what the file instructed him to do: he flagged it.

*Discrepancy — head of household. Sebastian Vane, printer, aged 24. Cross-reference: Vane, Sebastian, deceased 1841, age 17. See parish register, St. Aldhelm's.*

It was not even detective work. Any clerk with a memory and a grudge could have found it. The dead boy's name, worn like a coat by a living stranger. A household of one where there had once been four. A trade passed down through no lawful inheritance he could trace, because women did not hold type-cutting benches and the registry knew it, and the registry always knew things, that was its whole nature and its whole menace.

He flagged it because flagging was his function. He told himself this on the walk back to the census office, past the chandlers and the pie shops and the pawnbroker whose window displayed other people's desperate winters. He told himself it again while he inked the marginal note in his small, precise hand: *Refer to parish records for verification.*

He told himself it a third time that night, lying in his rented room above the tallow merchant's, listening to the building settle around him like an old man into a chair, and the telling did not work any better than it had the first two times.

Because he had seen the hands.

That was the thing. That was the thing he could not flag away. When he had asked — one question too many, he'd known it as it left his mouth, *your father's bench, is it? I understood his son died* — the young man behind the counter had gone very still. Not guilty-still. Craftsmen went still differently than liars. Liars performed stillness; craftsmen simply stopped, the way a clock stops, all the motion gathered into some interior decision. And then Sebastian Vane had said, in a voice pitched low and even, "The son died. The trade didn't," and turned back to the case of sorts as if Quentin were a draft to be shut out.

And Quentin had looked down at those hands — ink in the creases, a fresh nick along the thumb, the left forefinger callused in the particular way of someone who set type by feel in bad light — and something in his chest had turned over with an awful, familiar weight.

He knew what it cost to be unrecorded. He knew it from the inside.

* * *

He waited three days, which was either prudence or cowardice; he never fully settled which.

Three days gave the flag time to travel up through the office's channels, and channels meant clerks, and clerks meant eyes, and eyes meant that if he was going to do anything he had better do it fast. He learned on the second day that his supervisor had pulled the Pemberton & Vane file and read it twice. On the third morning he learned that a copy had gone to the registrar's annex with a request for the parish register pages.

After that, waiting stopped being prudence.

He went in the late afternoon, when the light through Printer's Row came long and amber off the wet cobbles and the respectable shops were shuttering. The sign above the door said PEMBERTON & VANE in letters cut by someone who loved letterforms — the ampersand alone was worth framing — but the window was dark except for a lamp burning deep inside, past the press, past the racks of drying broadsides, where a figure bent over the type cases.

He knocked. No answer. He knocked again and let himself in, which he would later understand was exactly the right move, because a man who announces himself gives the other person time to become someone else.

Sebastian Vane straightened up from the cases with a composing stick in hand like a weapon, and for one unguarded instant the face above the collar was not a young man's face at all — it was open, startled, undefended — and then the shutters came down so fast and so completely that Quentin almost doubted what he'd seen.

"Shop's closed."

"I know. I came anyway." He set his satchel on the counter. "My name is Quentin Ashe. I take the census. I also took your name down last week, and I want to tell you what happens next, because I don't think anyone else will."

The composing stick lowered an inch. "What happens next."

"The flag I entered has been copied to the registrar. By Friday they'll request the St. Aldhelm's register and compare it against your household roll. When they find that Sebastian Vane died in 1841 of the fever, they'll come here, and they won't send a clerk. They'll send the constabulary, because impersonation of a registered person isn't a curiosity to them, it's a crime, and crimes are how they justify themselves." He kept his voice level, archival, the voice of a man reading aloud from a record. It was the only way he could say it at all. "You'll lose the shop. You'll lose the trade. Depending on the magistrate's temper, you may lose considerably more."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the row a cart rattled past, and the sound seemed to belong to another city entirely.

"You've thought about this a great deal," she said at last. Not *you're wrong.* Not *I don't know what you mean.* She had made her calculation in the space of a breath and decided that denying it would waste both their time, and Quentin recognized that arithmetic because he had done it himself every day for eleven years.

"A great deal," he agreed. "Mostly on the walk over. Some of it standing outside your door being a coward."

"Why?" And there it was — the question under the question, the one she actually needed answered. Not *what have you done* but *what do you want.* People who had lived hidden developed a fine ear for the difference between a threat and a transaction, and she was listening for the transaction.

He opened the satchel and took out the file. Household roll, his marginal note, the flag slip attached with its red wax seal half-cured and lumpy. He laid it on the counter between them like a card turned face-up.

"My name was Quentin Ashe-Marlowe," he said. "I was a fellow of Aldenmoor Collegiate, archives division. I spent six years among the municipal records — births, deaths, land grants, trials. I was good at it. I was, if it isn't vanity to say so, the best they had, because I understood that a record is never neutral. Every line in a register is a decision someone made about what deserves to exist. Most archivists think they're custodians. They're authors. They just don't sign the work."

She watched him without moving. The lamp threw his shadow long across the floor, across the press with its iron frame like some patient beast.

"In 1837 I authenticated a land grant. Standard work. Except the grant was forged — well, not forged, amended, quietly, by a family with enough money to amend history — and my authentication made the theft lawful retroactively. I found the forgery two years later. I brought it to the college. I expected them to strike the grant." He smiled, and it was not a pleasant expression; it was the smile of a man handling an old break in himself to check whether it had healed. "They struck me instead. My name came off the rolls. My letters of credential were voided. The family's solicitors saw to it that no archive in the city would hire a disgraced archivist, and the registrar's office — the same office that will receive your file on Friday — hired me instead at a wage fit for a copying machine. I count people now. I write down what I'm told and I am not told much, because I am a man who officially does not quite exist. Do you understand?"

"Yes," she said quietly.

"No," he said. "You understand it from the outside. I'm telling you I understand it from yours."

He picked up the file. For a moment he held it, feeling the cheap paper, the bureaucratic weight of it — less than an ounce, and heavy enough to end a life. Then he gripped the flag slip and tore it. The wax seal cracked with a small sound like a knuckle. He tore the slip again, lengthwise, and again, and let the pieces fall onto the counter, red wax and blue paper, the machinery of verification reduced to confetti.

Then he took out his pen, crossed out his marginal note in the household roll itself, and wrote beneath it in his small precise hand: *Verified. No discrepancy. Clerical error in initial entry.* He initialed it. In the census office, an initial was a signature; it was the closest thing he had to one anymore.

"There," he said. "As far as the record is concerned, Sebastian Vane, printer, aged 24, is exactly who the register says he is. The parish comparison request will find my correction ahead of it and die in a drawer somewhere. Drawers are where truth goes to sleep in this city. I should know. I put it there for years."

Sebastian Vane had not spoken through any of this. Now she set down the composing stick with great care, the way one sets down something fragile, and looked at him — really looked, the disguise slackening around her face like a garment unbuttoned, though perhaps only for him, only here, only in this lamplit room where the presses kept everyone's secrets.

"You destroyed your own credibility," she said. "If anyone checks—"

"They won't. And if they do, I'm a census clerk who made a clerical error. There are forty thousand households in this city. Errors are expected. What they don't expect—" he gestured at the shop, the press, the racks of funeral broadsides and price lists and the little devotional pamphlets that paid the bills, "—is this. I watched you measure grief last week. You were cutting a new face for a coffin-maker's broadside and you adjusted the counters three times because the widow wanted her husband's name to look *kind*, and you understood instantly that kindness in a letterform is a matter of thirty-thousandths of an inch. Nobody teaches that. Nobody learns it by fraud. Whatever else is true in this room, the craft is true, and I have spent eleven years around people whose only skill was making false things look real. This is the opposite. I know the difference now."

The silence stretched. Outside, full dark had come down over Printer's Row, and the gas lamps made orange islands in the fog.

"What do you want?" she said finally. "No one tears up a flag for free."

"No," he agreed. "I want work."

Her eyebrows rose — the first genuinely unperformed expression he'd seen from her, and it was worth the whole terrible evening.

"I can lift a forme. I can sweep floors. I can learn to set type, though I warn you I'll be slow, because I've spent my life reading printed things backward through mirrors and never touching them. But I'm meticulous, I keep hours, I can't be bribed by anyone who matters, and I am very, very good at making sure records stay lost." He paused. "Also, I have nowhere else to be. That's not sentiment. It's inventory."

"That's not how apprenticeships work," she said. "There are papers. Guild enrollment. The registry—"

"The registry," Quentin said, "believes you are a young man named Sebastian Vane, because I have just sworn to it in ink. As far as I'm concerned, the registry and I have reached a mutually satisfactory understanding, and I see no reason to complicate it with additional paperwork."

For a long moment she studied him, and he had the strange sensation of being the document under examination — of being read, weighed, assessed for discrepancies, the way he had spent weeks assessing her. Then something in her posture shifted, some load redistributed, some decision arrived at.

"Can you start tomorrow?" she said. "Dawn. There's a job for the rope-walk that has to go out by noon, and I need someone to distribute type after. It's filthy work."

"It's the best offer I've had in eleven years," Quentin Ashe said, and meant it more than he could safely say.

She blew out the lamp, and in the sudden dark her voice came quiet and dry:

"One condition. Whatever you were before, whatever name you carried — in this shop, you leave it at the door. Everyone here does."

Quentin stood in the darkness of Pemberton & Vane, surrounded by ten thousand small metal letters that existed only to give other people words, and felt — for the first time since a committee of seven men had struck his name from a book he had devoted his life to keeping — that he might someday be written into something again.

"Dawn, then," he said.

Neither of them knew yet that within the year, the government would burn the last record that Quentin Ashe had ever existed — nor that the shop they were standing in would become the only archive in the city that mattered.

That was still ahead. That night there was only the lamp going dark, the smell of ink and oil, and two erased people shaking hands over a counter in the fog-bound dark of Printer's Row.
