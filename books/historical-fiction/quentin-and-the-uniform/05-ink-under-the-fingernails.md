## 5. Ink Under the Fingernails

Quentin Ashe set his first line of type on a Tuesday and got it backwards.

Not mirror-wise — every compositor's work reads mirror-wise until it meets the press — but *wrongly* backwards, the letters reversed in their own bodies, the n's facing the wrong way like men turned to watch a procession pass. He held the composing stick out at arm's length and studied the little metal sentence with the expression of a man who has been cheated by it personally.

"It's mocking me," he said.

"It's lead," I said. "It doesn't mock. It only waits."

"Then it waits with intent." He turned the stick over twice more, frowning, before he found his error and began again, picking each sort from the case with the exaggerated care of a man defusing something. His fingers were wrong for it — long and pale, an archivist's fingers, made for turning pages rather than pinching type — but they were learning. By the end of that first week they'd stopped trembling when he reached into the job case. By the second week he could find the e's without looking.

I had not meant to teach him anything. That should be said plainly, because everything after grew from what I only intended as a kindness of short duration. He had torn the flag from my file, burned it in the shop's stove while I watched, and asked for work in the same breath, as if one act purchased the other. I told him no. I told him the shop had no wages to give and no need of hands. He came back the next morning with bread and a lump of beeswax for the press rails, and stood in the doorway holding them like offerings, and I understood that I was not going to win this the way I won most things — by being unanswerable.

So I put him on sweeping. Then washing formes. Then, because he watched everything the way a crow watches a market stall, setting type.

* * *

The shop took on a second rhythm, the way a house does when a second heartbeat moves into it.

Mine had always been a solitary music: the creak of the treadle, the slap of the platen, the hiss of ink rolled thin. I rose before light, worked through the afternoon deliveries, ate standing at the bench. Customers came to the counter, paid the boy they saw — the quiet young Vane, clever hands, father's chin — and left, and the shop exhaled around me like a held breath released.

Now there were two sets of footsteps on the floorboards, two cups steaming on the ledge above the stone sink, and Quentin's voice asking questions in the pauses between impressions. He asked questions the way other men breathed. What was the point of quads versus spaces. Why I cut new punches instead of buying from the type foundry in Marlowe Street. Why the funeral broadsides were always set in the old Aldenmoor face and never in anything newer.

"Because grief doesn't want novelty," I told him. "Grief wants to be told it's happened before and will be survived."

He wrote that down. Actually wrote it, in a small notebook he kept in his breast pocket, along with a hundred other things I said without thinking. It should have unsettled me, a census man keeping notes in my shop. Instead I found I liked it — the strange flattery of being recorded by someone whose whole trade was recording, who chose what deserved the page.

He learned badly, is the thing. Eagerly, generously, but badly. He spaced lines too loose, as though the words needed room to be comfortable. He inked the rollers heavy and spattered the tympan so often I finally taped a square of waste paper over the worst stain and printed a single word on it: *NO*. He broke a piece of my father's twelve-point — snapped the shank clean dropping the stick — and went white as paper stock, standing there holding the corpse of it in both palms.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry, I'll pay for it, I'll—"

"You'll learn to carry the stick level," I said. "That's the whole payment. My father broke four sorts in his first month. He told me so himself, and he was the finest cutter in the district."

Quentin looked at the broken type a moment longer, then at me, and something in his face shifted — some assessment completed, some ledger balanced. "You talk about him like he's in the next room."

"He is, most days." I took the dead sort from him and set it in the kind box where broken things waited to be recast or melted down. Nothing in a print shop is ever truly lost; even mistakes go back into the pot. I didn't say that aloud. Some comforts are better shown than spoken.

* * *

It was in the third week, over the wash-up, that he first mentioned the pamphlets.

My father had died owing money to everyone except the people who mattered, and among his effects — such as they were — I'd found a locked drawer in the cutting bench that opened to a key I'd never seen before. Inside were three pamphlets. No imprint. No date. Set in a face I recognized as his own hand-cut work, tight and severe, the letterforms leaning forward like runners at a mark.

I had read them once, the night I found them, and locked the drawer again, and did not open it for two years.

"The Vane pamphlets," Quentin said, casual as weather, scrubbing ink from the press bed. "They're in the municipal archive. Or they were. Restricted access, which is why you've never heard anyone mention them — restricted collections don't get cited, and uncited things effectively don't exist. That's the quiet genius of suppression. Nobody burns your book anymore. They just file it somewhere no scholar bothers to look."

The rag stopped in my hand. "How many?"

"Four that I've catalogued. There may have been more — the accession records show gaps, deliberate ones, pages razored out of the intake ledger itself." He rinsed the rag, wrung it, laid it over the rail with the particular neatness he'd developed around my tools, careful not to presume ownership of any of them. "The first is about the grain contracts. 1841. The second concerns the garrison's land purchases along the river — land the city needed for the flood levees. The third—" he hesitated, and I realized he'd been deciding all week whether to tell me this part, "—the third names names. Magistrates. A customs commissioner. Your father documented payments. He wasn't writing polemic, Sebastian. He was writing evidence."

I stood very still, the way you do when the floor proves less solid than advertised.

"He never spoke of it," I said finally. "He'd come home smelling of ink and river fog and he'd eat whatever my mother left for him and he'd talk about *kerning*, about a new ligature he was chasing, and never once—" My voice did something I disliked, so I stopped it. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you have a right to it. Because those pamphlets are the best-set things in the entire restricted collection — I've handled them, the impression is extraordinary — and whoever cut them wanted to be read." He dried his hands and faced me fully, and I saw that underneath the archivist's caution there was something hotter. "And because the men he named are still in office. Two of them sit on the committee that funds this district's relief. If the flood comes this spring the way the engineers keep warning—"

"You've thought about this."

"I think about records," he said. "It's the only thing I know how to do. Most of the time it's useless knowledge. Occasionally it isn't."

* * *

We opened the drawer together that evening.

I want to record that I felt triumph, or vindication, or the clean fire of purpose. What I actually felt was smaller and stranger: my father's presence, sudden and physical, in the smell of the old paper and the particular slant of his setting. Here was his anger, typeset. Here was the man who taught me to hold a burnisher saying the unsayable in the only voice he trusted — the composed voice, the mirror-voice, words that only speak when pressed to paper.

"He couldn't say any of it out loud," I said. "Could he."

"No," Quentin said gently. "People like us rarely can. We say it in ink or we don't say it."

*People like us.* He said it without ceremony, the way he said everything, and it landed in me like a coin dropped down a well — a long time falling, then a small bright splash. People like us: the struck-from-the-rolls and the never-on-the-rolls, the two of us making ourselves legible to each other across a workbench while the city went about its business outside, unaware that its historians and its printers were conspiring in a back room off Cooper's Lane.

I cut a punch that week. First new work in months — I'd been living on repairs and reprints, the craft equivalent of reheating soup. But the pamphlets wanted answering, or at least acknowledging, and I am my father's daughter however the world spells me. I cut a small fleuron, a printer's flower, and hid it in the tailpiece of a wedding announcement for the Halloway girl. No one would ever see it. That wasn't the point. The point was that my hands remembered how to make new things instead of only preserving old ones, and that Quentin stood behind my shoulder while I graved the steel and did not offer a single opinion, which may be the truest kindness one craftsman can pay another.

* * *

The rhythm of the shop changed again, subtly, the way a river changes around a new stone.

He began arriving earlier. I began staying later. We argued about spacing — he believed in air, I believed in color, and we settled it per job like two nations negotiating a border. He learned to cast good joints on the press and bad ones everywhere else. He memorized the cases faster than any apprentice I'd heard of and still, infuriatingly, called the em quad "the big one" under his breath.

And the neighbors adjusted. Mrs. Ostrer, who brought her funeral orders and her suspicions in equal measure, began bringing two meat pies instead of one. The Ostrers' boy hung around the door asking Quentin questions about the archive until Quentin lent him a book of engravings, which the boy returned with grease marks on every plate and a devotion thereafter that bordered on religion. Old Tammsen, who hadn't bought so much as a handbill in a year, came in to complain about the noise and stayed forty minutes talking about the flood levees, and left having ordered two hundred circulars for the ward meeting.

A household of one becomes a household of two without either party signing anything. There's no register for it. No census taker comes round asking do you live alone, and since when, and who is the man who laughs in your shop at six in the morning. Though I supposed someone had come round once, and looked at my rolls, and asked one question too many — and the man who'd done it now slept in the loft above the type store and whistled while he swept.

One evening in the fourth week I caught him at my father's bench, the cutting bench, which I had never let another soul touch. He wasn't cutting. He was just looking — at the gravers racked in their leather roll, at the magnifier hanging from its hook, at the drawer with its key back in my pocket. His hands were folded behind his back like a man in a gallery.

"She'd have taught you," I said from the stairs. "If she'd lived. My mother. She ran the counter for thirty years and corrected my father's spelling and nobody outside this street ever knew her name."

Quentin didn't turn around. "Is that why you do it? Keep the shop under a name that isn't quite yours?"

"That's why I keep it at all." I came down the last steps. "The name is just the door it happens to knock on."

He nodded slowly, and I watched him turn the sentence over the way he turned everything, looking for the error in the line, finding none. Then he picked up the broom, because the floor wanted sweeping, and the presses would run in the morning, and between us we had a shop to keep — half a truth each and the whole of the work.

I was carrying the last forme to the stone when he spoke again from the loft stair, casually, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather.

"The grain contracts. From the first pamphlet. I checked the current filings yesterday." He paused. "Sebastian — the same clerk signed both. Forty-one and now. And next week he sits on the tribunal that sets the winter bread price."

The forme went cold in my hands. Outside, far off toward the granary quarter, a crowd was gathering — I could hear it, the low animal sound of too many people and too little bread — and Quentin came down the stairs already reaching for his coat.

"We should see this," he said.
