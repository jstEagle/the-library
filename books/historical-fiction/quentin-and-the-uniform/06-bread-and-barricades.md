## 6. Bread and Barricades

*Sebastian*

The price of bread went up twice in one week, and the second time, the baker's boy came to the shop not to order a broadside but to tell me about it, standing in the doorway with flour on his sleeves and something wild in his eyes.

"Four sous," he said. "Monsieur Aubert raised it again. Four sous for a loaf that was two and a half at harvest."

"Was it wheat, or was it him?"

"He says it's the barges. Says the river's low and the millers are robbing him." The boy wiped his hands on his apron, leaving pale streaks. "My mother says he's lying. My mother says everybody's lying."

His mother was usually right. I gave him a handbill to carry back — a notice for a widow's auction, nothing political — and watched him go down Corderie Street with the paper folded under his arm like a weapon he hadn't learned to use yet.

The shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and, lately, of too many bodies. Word had gotten around, the way word does, that Pemberton & Vane would print things other shops wouldn't, and things other shops couldn't. Not sedition — not yet, not exactly — but the gray territory between: a petition about grain weights, a circular from the dockworkers' mutual aid society, a list of prices so the neighborhood could prove to itself it wasn't being robbed by memory alone. I set these jobs at night when the front shutters were closed, and by morning they were gone, distributed into coat pockets and market baskets, and the shop was only ever a print shop, only ever respectable.

That was the arrangement. Everyone understood it without saying it. Madame Ossart brought me coffee and never asked what I printed after dark. Old Ferrier, who repaired our press rollers, whistled while he worked and looked at nothing. The neighborhood had decided to have an institution, and institutions don't ask questions.

Quentin had been with us six weeks by then, and he'd stopped setting his letters backward out of habit. He still held the composing stick wrong sometimes, gripping it like a pen, and I'd reach over and turn his wrist without thinking about it, the way you'd correct a younger brother, and he'd flush and fix it and say nothing. We had developed a silence between us that was more comfortable than most conversations I'd ever had.

On the evening the garrison mutinied, we were both at the bench.

I heard it before I understood it: a sound from the direction of the citadel, not quite music and not quite shouting, a rolling murmur like a tide coming in somewhere you can't see. Quentin put down his stick first. He had better ears for trouble than I did; years of archives teach you to hear the moment a record is about to be broken.

"What is that?" I asked.

He went to the window and lifted the shutter a crack. The light of the street lamps came in and cut across his face in bars.

"Sergeants' mess," he said. "Or a riot. Or a revolution. From here they all sound alike."

We stood together and listened, and the murmur resolved itself into voices, hundreds of them, and then into a name being chanted, though I couldn't make out which name. It didn't matter. In 1848, every city has a name it wants to shout, and every government has a reason to be afraid of it.

"They'll come to us," Quentin said quietly. "You know that."

"I know."

"Not tonight. But soon. Every uprising needs a press, and this is the only one in the quarter that isn't owned by someone who'll say no."

I looked at my press — my father's press, the iron frame he'd bolted to the floorboards himself, the stone he'd leveled with shims and patience. I thought about how a thing can be yours and still not be safe, how ownership is just another word for exposure.

"Then we'd better decide what we say yes to," I said, "before someone decides it for us."

Outside, the chanting grew louder, and somewhere a drum began, badly, the way drums begin when civilians pick them up.

* * *

*Quentin*

I want to record, while I still can, what the mutiny actually sounded like, because I know now that everything I write will be contested, and I would rather contest it early.

It sounded like relief.

That is the thing none of the official accounts will say. When the garrison broke — when the sergeants threw open the armory gates and let the crowd take what was inside — the sound that came off the parade ground was not fury. It was the sound of men who had been holding their breath for years finally letting it out. I heard a corporal laughing. Actually laughing, sitting on a cannon carriage with his boots off, as though he'd just remembered a joke from childhood.

Sebastian and I went out into it, against my better judgment, which she overruled by putting her coat on. The streets were full but not yet dangerous; there is a window in every insurrection, an hour or two when it is still a festival, before the shooting starts and the accounting begins. We walked as far as the fountain in Marché Neuf, where a man was standing on the rim reading a proclamation from a sheet of paper, and the crowd was passing copies hand to hand, and I took one and read it by lamplight.

Provisional Committee of the Fourth District, it said. Demands followed: bread at a fixed price, the registers opened, the prisoners released. And at the bottom, in small type, a line that made my chest tighten:

*Copies may be had at the printing house in Corderie Street.*

Someone had already volunteered us.

"We didn't print this," Sebastian said beside me. Her voice was flat, careful.

"No."

"But they've put our address on it."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a moment. Around us the crowd surged and sang, and a woman near the fountain handed her baby to a stranger so she could clap.

"Then it's done," she said at last. "Whether we agreed or not. That's how history works, apparently. It signs your name to things and tells you afterward."

I knew something about having names signed to things. I kept it to myself.

We walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and gun oil, past barricades already rising at the ends of the smaller lanes — carts overturned, paving stones pried up by men working with the cheerful competence of men who had done this in dreams for twenty years and were surprised to find their hands remembered. One of them nodded to Sebastian. Another called out, "Is it true? The printer in Corderie? You'll print for the district?" and Sebastian said, "Bring me clean copy and payment in advance," which was so exactly her that I nearly laughed, and the men laughed too, and for one hour that winter night the revolution felt less like a fire than like a trade opening its shutters.

The dread came later, when the festival thinned and the lamps burned low, and I lay in the room above the shop listening to the presses of my own heartbeat and understanding precisely what I had agreed to by staying. Archives burn. Printers hang. I had read enough files to know how both stories end.

And still, falling asleep, I caught myself smiling. God forgive me, I was happy.

* * *

*Sebastian*

They started coming the next morning, and they did not stop.

First the committee men — three of them, led by a shoemaker named Brissac with ink-stained fingers and the air of a man who had waited his whole life for a crisis worthy of him. Then the students, then the women from the market, then a lieutenant from the mutinied garrison who wanted five hundred copies of the soldiers' own statement of grievances and paid in army scrip that I suspected would be worthless within the month. I took it anyway.

By noon the shop had reorganized itself without anyone deciding anything. Brissac's men carried the type cases from the back room to the front bench so the work could be seen through the windows — a display, a promise, proof of good faith. Madame Ossart arrived with a pot of coffee large enough to bathe in and appointed herself quartermaster of it. Ferrier came to check the press and stayed to work the tympan. Quentin stood at the stone, learning to lock up a chase under pressure, his hands steady even though I could see the fear moving underneath his skin like weather under water.

"Copy," I kept saying, and copy kept arriving: handwritten pages, half-legible, crossed out, passionate. I taught a dozen people in two days how to write for a compositor — one side of the page, mark your deletions clearly, do not change your mind after I have set it. The district had never been literate about its own machinery before. Now there were men who knew what an em quad was and women who could read a galley upside down, and this, more than the barricades, felt like the actual revolution: the neighborhood learning to speak in its own voice instead of waiting for someone else to transcribe it.

But at night, when they were all gone, I locked the door and stood in the empty shop and felt the other truth, the cold one underneath.

Presses are targets. I had always known it the way you know your own mortality — abstractly, politely. Now it was specific. If the government retook the quarter, everyone who had touched a pamphlet would be a name on a list, and mine — the false one, the fragile one, the whole architecture of Sebastian Vane that I had built plank by plank since my father died — would be the first to collapse under questioning. A press run leaves witnesses. Witnesses leave testimony. And testimony, examined closely enough, would find the girl under the apron faster than any census taker ever had.

I thought about running. I had packed before; I could pack again. There was river traffic south, and southern cities, and other names.

Then I looked at the formes left standing on the stone — the soldiers' grievances, the grain-price schedule, the district's demands, each one a small stubborn brick in a wall the neighborhood was building out of its own language — and I understood that I could not carry them, and I could not abandon them, and those two impossibilities meant I was staying.

Quentin found me like that, at midnight, staring at the locked-up chase.

"You're doing the arithmetic," he said.

"What arithmetic?"

"The one where you count what a name costs." He came and stood beside me at the stone. "I did it for eleven years. It doesn't balance. It never balances. You just choose which column you can live in."

"And which column is this?"

He looked at the type — mirror-wise, reversed, unreadable to anyone who couldn't read it — and said, "The one where the words get out."

* * *

*Quentin*

On the fourth day of the uprising, the man from the provisional government came to Corderie Street.

Not a censor — not yet. An envoy. Well-dressed, unhurried, with the particular courtesy of a person delivering a summons he expects you to accept gratefully. He waited outside while the queue of petitioners emptied, then stepped into the shop, removed his hat, and introduced himself as Monsieur Delorme, attached to the Bureau of Public Information.

"Information," Sebastian repeated, from the bench, without looking up. She was distributing type with perfect calm. "How novel. A bureau of it."

"There is a great deal of information now, madame— monsieur—" His pause was brief and surgical. "Forgive me. There is a great deal of information now, and the government wishes it to be orderly."

"Orderly," said Brissac, from the corner, in a voice like a dropped anvil.

"Printers who cooperate will be recognized," Delorme continued smoothly, as if Brissac were furniture. "Licensed. Protected. There will be official bulletins requiring distribution, and the Bureau prefers to work with established houses. There may even be commissions." He produced a card and laid it on the counter. "The alternative, of course—" he smiled, apologetic, a man regretting the weather "—is that in wartime conditions, unlicensed presses are occasionally confused with enemy assets. Mistakes occur. I should hate for a mistake to occur here."

He let that sit in the room like smoke, tipped his hat, and left.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Madame Ossart, who had heard everything from the doorway, marched to the counter, picked up his card, and held it over the candle flame until it curled.

"That's that," she said.

"That's nothing," Quentin said. "That's a warning with a visiting card. They'll send someone worse next time, with actual authority, and he won't stand on ceremony." He turned to Sebastian. "How long to print the district's full account? Everything — the mutiny, the barricades, the dead when there are dead. A true chronicle, dated, signed by witnesses."

"Why?" Brissac demanded. "What good is paper against soldiers?"

"Paper outlasts soldiers," I said. "I spent eleven years in archives. Believe me — the victors write first, but they cannot write last, if someone else has already written truly. A record in a thousand hands cannot be recalled."

Sebastian had stopped distributing type. She was looking at the press, and then at me, and I watched her arrive at the same place I had arrived, by a different road.

"How many names," she asked quietly, "do you suppose this street will lose before this is over?"

Neither of us answered. We didn't need to. Outside, the drums had changed their rhythm — no longer the festive stumbling of the first night, but something martial, measured, coming from the direction of the bridge. Somewhere beyond the barricades, an army was remembering itself.

Brissac went to the window and looked out, and when he turned around his face had aged ten years in four days.

"They're bringing cannon to the Place Royale," he said. "Our own guns, taken from the citadel, turned toward our own streets."

Sebastian pulled the first forme toward her and began to lock it down.

"Then we print fast," she said.
