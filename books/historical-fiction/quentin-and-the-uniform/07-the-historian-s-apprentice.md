## 7. The Historian's Apprentice

The summons came by hand, which was the first strange thing about it. A boy in a government sash — too new to have earned it, too clean to have fought for it — stood in the doorway of the print shop holding out an envelope sealed with wax that had never been near a fire.

"For Mr. Ashe," the boy said.

"There's no Mr. Ashe here," I said, and took it anyway.

Sebastian was at the stone, inking a forme with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and she watched me break the seal without asking. That was one of the things I had come to value about her: she understood that some letters are read aloud and some are read alone, and that a man who has been struck from every register ever kept develops opinions about which is which.

*Mr. Quentin Ashe,* the letter said, in a hand I knew before I read a word of it. *You are requested to attend Dr. Aldous Crane at the Provisional Secretariat, Rue de la Monnaie, at four o'clock on Thursday. The matter concerns your rehabilitation.*

Rehabilitation. The word sat on the page like a coin someone had polished for the occasion.

"Crane," Sebastian said. She hadn't stopped inking, but her roller had stopped moving.

"You know the name?"

"He published my father's pamphlets. Before they were suppressed." She lifted the roller, looked at the ink on it as if checking its temper. "Your mentor."

"My jailer's clerk, once. My teacher after." I folded the letter along its original creases. "He wrote to the university on my behalf three times. Then he stopped writing."

"And now he's attached to the provisional government."

"They always are. Revolutions don't staff themselves. They take whoever can spell."

She set the roller down. "Are you going?"

I thought about lying to her, and then thought better of it, because she could read a lie the way she read a galley — upside down and backward, and faster than you'd believe. "Yes."

"Then wear the good coat. Not because you owe them respect. Because they'll judge the shop by your cuffs."

It was such a practical thing to say that I almost laughed. I wore the good coat.

* * *

The Provisional Secretariat had been a customs house in the old administration, and it wore its new purpose like a borrowed coat itself: the imperial eagles chiseled off the lintel, leaving pale scars in the shape of wings; tricolor bunting strung across a staircase built for men who'd never once climbed it in a hurry. Clerks moved through the corridors with the particular urgency of people whose employment depends on appearing employed.

Crane received me in a room with a window overlooking the river. He had aged into his authority the way some men age into their clothes — comfortably, without ever questioning whether either still fit. His hair had gone white at the temples since the university, but the hands were the same: long, dry, precise, the hands of a man who had spent forty years telling other people what happened.

"Ashe." He said it carefully, tasting it. "Quentin Ashe. Do you know how long it's been since anyone said that name to my face?"

"Six years. Give or take."

"Give or take." He gestured to a chair. "Sit. You look well. Printing agrees with you, I hear."

"You hear a great deal for a man in a customs house."

"I hear everything. That is the entire profession." He folded his hands on the desk between us. On it lay a single sheet of paper, face down, weighted with a paperknife older than both of us. "Do you remember what I told you the last time we spoke? In the visitors' room, before they walked you out."

"You told me to keep my head down and my hands clean, and that history would sort itself out."

"I told you history is written by the survivors. I've since refined the thesis. History is written by the survivors who can write quickly." He turned the paper over.

It was a draft. Of course it was a draft. It bore the heading *An Account of the Late Disturbances in the City of Aldenmoor, Prepared for the Instruction of Posterity*, and beneath the heading, in Crane's own hand, the opening paragraph, already composed:

*"On the morning of the twelfth, certain agitators, largely foreign-born and acting without the sanction of the city's respectable citizens, assembled near the granary and disturbed the public peace—"*

I read it twice. The second time was not for comprehension. It was for the absences.

"How many dead?" I asked.

"In the disturbances? The provisional figure is eleven."

"The figure from the parish registers is two hundred and six. I've been counting."

Something moved behind Crane's eyes — not guilt; I want to be fair to him, and guilt would have been easier to forgive. It was the flicker of a man recalculating. "Parish registers can be inflated. Grief inflates. You above all people should understand how records lie."

"I understand how records get made to lie. There's a difference, and it has a name, and the name is authorship."

He smiled, thin and patient, the smile he used on undergraduates who thought they'd caught him. "You haven't changed. Good. That's precisely why you're here." He slid the draft aside and produced a second document, this one bearing a seal and a signature and all the machinery of legitimacy. "Full restoration. Your name restored to the university rolls, your degree conferred retroactively, a stipend, and a post — archivist to the provisional commission on civic memory. There will be archives again, Quentin. Someone will have to decide what goes in them."

"Someone like you."

"Someone like me, yes, until I'm dead, and then someone like you." He leaned back. "All you have to do is finish the account. My draft is sound; it merely lacks a certain texture. Eyewitness detail. Names, even — the respectable names, the ones that flatter the narrative. You lived among those streets for weeks. You know how the common mind works. Write it so posterity reads it correctly."

"And the two hundred and six?"

"Will be remembered by God," Crane said, "who keeps the only register that matters. We keep the other kind."

I looked at the restoration order for a long time. I won't pretend otherwise. Six years I had been a man without a country of paperwork — unable to sign a lease, marry, inherit, be buried. I had stood in queues and stepped out of them when the ledger came around. I had learned to move through the world the way water moves through cracks: everywhere, officially nowhere. And here was a piece of vellum that would make me exist again, signed and sealed, one signature away.

Crane watched me look at it, and he knew exactly what he was doing. That was the cruelest part. He had taught me palaeography; he knew the weight of a document better than any man alive.

"You were innocent," he said quietly. "We both know it. The whole business at the university — the forged donation, the missing folios — you were the convenient man, not the guilty one. This is the state correcting an error. All corrections cost something. This one costs you a paragraph."

"A paragraph."

"Perhaps two hundred words. The disturbances will be remembered either way — that much is true whether we print it or not. What is *not* inevitable is whether they're remembered as a riot or a martyrdom. A riot produces order. A martyrdom produces another riot, ten years hence, with worse weapons. I am offering you the chance to buy peace for this city at the price of your silence about numbers that cannot be verified anyway." He tapped the restoration order. "And your own name back. Which, if I am not mistaken, is the thing you have wanted more than air since the day they took it."

* * *

I walked back across the bridge instead of taking the ferry, though the ferry was free to government guests and my feet were blistered by the time I reached the far bank. I needed the walk. I needed the wind off the river, and the sight of the neighborhood rising up the hill — crooked roofs, smoke from a hundred modest chimneys, the print shop's windows catching the late light like a held breath.

They had put bread and cheese out on the step for me. That was Sebastian's doing, or Mrs. Halloran's, or both; the household had developed habits around my comings and goings that nobody had voted on and everybody observed. I stood in the street eating bread like a vagrant, looking at my own front door, thinking about the word *rehabilitation.*

Here is what I did not tell Crane. Here is what I have not entirely told Sebastian.

When I first read his letter, in the shop, before I showed it to anyone — for perhaps the length of ten breaths — I wanted it. I wanted the name. I wanted to walk into a registry office and say *Quentin Ashe* and watch a clerk write it down without flinching. I wanted a grave someday with my actual name on it, cut in stone by an honest stonecutter who had checked the spelling against something official. Ten breaths. Maybe twelve.

A man who has been erased does not stop wanting to be written back in. That is the wound's whole mechanism. They cut you out, and the cut keeps reaching for the pen.

But standing there on the bridge, I found myself thinking about the census rolls — the ones that had started all of this, the ones where I had found a dead boy's name attached to a living woman and asked one question too many. I had torn the flag from that file myself. I had done it because I recognized the arithmetic: a record is a weapon, and whoever holds the pen decides who exists. Crane holds the pen now. Crane has always wanted to hold the pen. And Crane was offering me the chance to become the kind of man I had spent six years hiding from — the man with the ledger, walking door to door, deciding whose household counts.

There was one more thing. In the corner of his draft, in the margin, in pencil — a note to himself, forgotten there, the kind of thing I had been trained to catch: *Vane press — watch.* Two words. He knew about the shop. He had known before he invited me. Perhaps the invitation was the test; perhaps the account was the trap; perhaps both. Crane did nothing without a margin note.

* * *

The shop smelled of ink and lamp oil when I came in. Sebastian was setting type by lamplight, quick and sure, the stick rattling softly as each piece locked home. She didn't look up.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me everything. Name, degree, position. In exchange for writing the official account." I set the copy of Crane's draft on the composing stone beside her case, and she read it the way she reads everything — head tilted, lips moving slightly, faster than seemed possible.

"'Certain agitators, largely foreign-born,'" she quoted. "Old Tomas Ferenc has lived on Garland Street for thirty years. He carried sandbags at the barricade until his hands bled." She set the draft down. "And the dead?"

"Eleven, officially. He says grief inflates the registers."

"Grief doesn't inflate anything. Grief remembers exactly." She looked up at me then, and the lamplight caught her the way it catches everyone eventually, without mercy and without judgment, just light. "What did you tell him?"

"That I needed time to consider. Which was true. And that I would give him an answer within the week. Also true."

"And what will the answer be?"

I should have answered immediately. A better man — a simpler man — would have said *no* on the bridge and walked in here clean. But I have learned that in this household we do not perform our virtues for one another; we confess our temptations, because unconfessed temptations grow teeth.

So I told her. About the ten breaths. About wanting the name back, wanting it the way a drowned man wants air, and how the wanting hadn't died just because I'd found other things to breathe. I told her about the marginal note — *Vane press — watch* — and watched her go very still, the way type goes still in a locked forme.

"He knows about the shop," she said.

"He knew before the summons. The invitation may have been the point."

"Then why offer you rehabilitation at all? If he suspects the shop, why dangle restoration in front of its printer's apprentice?"

"Because a man who accepts is a man owned. And a man who refuses tells Crane everything he needs to know about where the shop's sympathies lie." I took the draft back from the stone. "Either answer serves him. That's the elegance of it. He taught me that trick himself, years ago, over a forged charter. I should have recognized the lesson."

Sebastian was quiet for a while. Then she went back to her case and began setting again, and I understood that for her, work was thinking — the hands processed what the mouth couldn't.

"You'll refuse," she said finally. It wasn't quite a question.

"Yes."

"But you're going to write to him anyway."

"I'm going to ask him for paper," I said. "Official stock. Government allotment. A ream or two, for — as I'll phrase it — private study. He'll grant it. Refusing costs him nothing, and granting it lets him believe I'm considering."

"And the paper?"

I nodded toward the back room, where under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the growing stack of scraps the neighbors had been bringing us. Names on butcher's paper, on the backs of playbills, on the flyleaves of psalters. Two hundred and six names, and counting, because the fighting wasn't finished and neither was the dying.

"The paper," I said, "will hold the truth. His stock, his seal's provenance, his own allotment — carrying the names he's omitting. There's a justice in that I think even Crane would appreciate, if he weren't on the wrong side of it."

Sebastian laughed — short, surprised, real. Then she sobered, because the next thought arrived in both of us at once, the way thoughts do now, as if we shared a single compositor.

"If he granted you paper, he's watching what comes off it. Or he will be."

"Then we print fast," I said, "and we print small, and we print everywhere at once. But first—" I picked up the pencil, and on the back of Crane's own draft, in the margin, directly beneath *Vane press — watch*, I wrote the number. *206.* "First, we finish the list."

Outside, the curfew bell rang early, which meant the garrison was nervous, which meant the neighborhood would be at our door by morning with more names. Sebastian banked the lamp low. Somewhere down the street a patrol's boots kept uneven time, and above them, faintly, someone was singing — one of the grain-riot songs, the forbidden ones, sung soft enough to deny and loud enough to carry.

I sat down at the bench beside her and reached for the case.

Two hundred and six. And the night was young, and the ink was fresh, and somewhere across the city a historian was drafting a lie in a fair hand — and I had been, once, upon a time, before the fall, before the erasure, his best apprentice.

Let him see what the apprentice writes when the master isn't holding the pen.
