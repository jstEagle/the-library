## 8. Names in the Margin

The first death came on a Tuesday, and it came to the tanner's boy.

Sebastian heard it before she understood it — a sound from the direction of Cordwainer Street, not quite a shout and not quite a bell, that low collective intake of breath a crowd makes when something irreversible has happened. She was standing at the stone with a brayer in her hand, ink half rolled, the morning's job — fifty invoices for Halloran's dry goods — waiting in its forme. The sound passed over the shop like weather.

By noon the whole neighborhood knew: Josiah Tanner, seventeen years old, shot through the chest at the mouth of Cordwainer Street when the garrison's patrol decided a crowd of hungry men outside the grain factor's warehouse was a mob and the mob needed dispersing. Not dispersed. *Discouraged.* That was the word the sergeant used, they said. Discouraged.

Josiah had set type once. Two afternoons, the previous spring, when his father thought printing might be steadier work than tanning and brought him round to see. He'd had ink under his nails for a week afterward and wore it like a decoration. He had been terrible at it. He'd laughed too much to hold a composing stick level.

Sebastian stood at the window of the shop with her hands flat against the glass and watched the street below, where people moved differently now — faster, in clumps, glancing over their shoulders. The barricade at the corner of Weir Lane had grown overnight from three overturned carts to something with iron bars in it. The grain price chalked on the baker's board had climbed again, each numeral taller than the last, as though writing it large enough might shame it back down.

Quentin was in the back, sorting type he still sorted badly, his fingers learning the cases the way a man learns a river by falling into it. She could hear him muttering the layout to himself. *Lower left, a through z; upper case, capitals; the devil's boxes, k, q, x.* He'd been at it three weeks and he still reached for the wrong compartment one time in five, but he reached faster every day, and there was something in the way he bent over the cases — that hunger, that gratitude — that made her want to keep him forever and never say so.

She did not tell him about Josiah yet. Some things wanted to be carried alone first, weighed before they were shared.

* * *

They came at dusk.

Not all together. One at a time, mostly, which is how grief travels in a poor neighborhood — quietly, sidelong, wrapped in an errand. A woman who needed a handbill printed for a room to let, though everyone knew she didn't have a room to let anymore since the rent tripled. An old man wanting a card for a christening that had already happened two years ago, standing at the counter turning his hat in his hands until Sebastian said gently, *sir, is there something else,* and he looked at her with wet red eyes and put a scrap of paper down on the counter as if it were nothing, as if it were a receipt, and left without another word.

On the paper, in a shaking hand: *Josiah Tanner, b. 1831, d. 14 Feb. Shot at Cordwainer St. He was somebody.*

*He was somebody.* Not a cause. Not a martyr of the people, not a casualty of reaction, none of the words the pamphleteers would use by Friday. Just that. Somebody.

Sebastian stood holding the scrap for a long time after the door had closed, the lamp hissing above her, the presses silent. Then she lit a second candle, because one wasn't enough light for what she was about to do, and she read it again, and she went to the drawer beneath the cash box where she kept things that were not business — her mother's thimble, a lock of hair folded in waxed paper, the first piece of type her father ever cut, a lowercase *v*, worn nearly smooth — and she laid the paper in beside them.

It was the second scrap. She realized that only later, counting back. The first had come that very morning and she had almost thrown it away, thinking it litter blown in from the street: a corner of a playbill, pencil-written, *Marta Kessel, weaver, trampled near the Corn Exchange.* She had swept it toward the fire before something stopped her hand. It was still there, wedged under the ink stone, when she went looking for it at midnight and found it and understood.

The neighborhood was using her shop the way it had always used her shop — as the place you went when you needed words fixed, made permanent, made official. Births. Marriages. Funerals. Bills of sale. And now this. The authorities had their own ledger, kept at the provost's office, and the authorities had decided that Marta Kessel and Josiah Tanner were disturbances, not deaths, and disturbances went into no ledger at all.

But scraps of paper came to Pemberton & Vane. Scraps of paper had always come to Pemberton & Vane.

She sat down at her father's old desk and pulled a fresh sheet toward her and wrote at the top, in her careful engraver's hand: *A True Register of the Dead of Aldenmoor, as recorded by their neighbors.* Below it, the two names. Then she sat looking at the page, at how small it looked, two names on a whole white sheet, and felt the sheet asking its question: *is that all?*

It would not be all. She knew that the way you know weather coming. The city had been leaning toward this for weeks, grain prices and mutinies and barricades growing like ice across a pond, and when the pond finally broke, it would not take one boy and one weaver. It would take dozens. Hundreds, if the histories of other cities held true, and Quentin had told her about other cities, reading to her from memory in the evenings while she cleaned the press — Paris in '30, Brussels, the Polish towns, places where the count of the dead had been written by whoever won and everyone else had simply been subtracted.

*Subtracted.* That was the word Quentin used, and she had hated it and understood it completely, because subtraction was the thing that had been done to her own name her whole life, done gently, done by herself, but done.

No. She would not be a party to subtraction. Not of others.

* * *

Quentin found her at two in the morning still at the desk, surrounded by scraps. There were seven now. Word traveled fast in a neighborhood of ten thousand souls; someone had seen the old man leave the shop and told someone else, and by eleven the scraps had begun arriving properly — tucked under the door, handed over the counter, pushed through the gap in the shutters by children who ran off before she could thank them. A fishwife's son, drowned — no, *beaten*, the scrap corrected itself in a second child's handwriting, *not drowned, they lied about the drowning*. A journeyman cooper. A woman whose name even her neighbors only knew as Old Bess of Weir Lane. Each scrap different: pencil, charcoal, a nail scratched into bark paper, one written in what could only be berry juice, already fading brown.

Quentin stood in the doorway in his shirt sleeves, hair flattened on one side where he'd slept on the pallet in the back room, and looked at the desk the way he looked at everything now — as an archivist first, a man second.

"How many?" he asked.

"Seven."

"And the official count?"

"Two," Sebastian said. "An inquest ruled both 'deaths by misadventure during civil commotion.' No names published. I sent a boy to the provost's office to ask."

Quentin came forward slowly. He picked up the berry-juice scrap, tilted it to the candlelight, and his jaw tightened. "This will fade entirely within the month."

"Then we shouldn't wait a month."

He looked up at her. In the candlelight his face had the particular stillness it got when his mind was moving fast behind it, sorting, cataloguing — the archivist's reflex, except that lately the reflex had turned from cataloguing things away to cataloguing things *back*. "You're thinking of printing them."

"I'm thinking of more than printing them." She spread the master sheet out where he could see it. "I'm thinking of a register. Names, ages, trades, how they died, where. Everything the scraps give us and everything the neighbors will tell us. Set in type. Printed. Not one copy — hundreds. Enough that it can't be unprinted."

For a long moment Quentin said nothing. She watched him read the sheet, watched his lips move faintly around the names the way he did when he was memorizing, and she remembered what he'd told her about himself, that night three months ago when he tore the flag off her file and asked for work: *I know what it is to be struck from a record, Mrs.— Mr. Vane. The world doesn't stop existing for you. But you stop existing for the world, and that's a kind of death the coroner never certifies.*

"It'll be treason," he said quietly. "Printing unauthorized accounts of deaths caused by the garrison. If they find the forme—"

"They'll find a lot worse than a forme if they burn the shop, and they'll burn the shop either way once the fighting comes here. You've seen Weir Lane." She stood up. Her back ached; she'd been at the desk six hours and her body had begun to register protest. "Quentin. My father printed seditious pamphlets in '31 under three false imprints and never lost a night's sleep, or so my mother claimed. This isn't sedition. This is bookkeeping."

That surprised a sound out of him — not quite a laugh, but its cousin. "Bookkeeping."

"The most honest kind. Someone died; the account must balance." She touched the edge of the sheet. "Every trade has its scripture. Mine says you don't let a lie stand in type. These aren't even in type yet. That means there's still time."

Quentin set the berry-juice scrap down with exaggerated care, aligning its edges with the others, squaring the little pile the way he squared everything. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into the register he used for facts, and she had learned that this was how he handled feeling too large for any other container.

"Then we do it properly," he said. "Verification first — I spent nine years in archives, I can chase a rumor to its source better than any historian in this city. Every name corroborated by two witnesses where possible. Dates confirmed. Causes stated plainly, no rhetoric, no blame attached — the names themselves are the accusation, we don't need to add a syllable. Format—" he paused, eyes going distant, reading some imagined page, "—single column, small pica, generous leading so old eyes can read it. No ornament. Ornament would make it a manifesto. Without ornament it's a *record*, and records are worse than manifestos. Manifestos can be argued with. Records can only be denied."

"And denial requires witnesses," Sebastian said, "and we intend to manufacture witnesses by the hundred."

"Ten thousand copies," Quentin said, and there it was again, that hunger, that gratitude, the look of a man who had been erased finding the eraser and turning it around.

They shook hands over the desk like partners signing articles, which in every way that mattered, they were.

* * *

The next days taught her what a reliquary was.

She had thought of churches, of course, gilded boxes and finger bones, when the word crossed her mind. But a reliquary, it turned out, could be a wooden drawer under a cash box in a print shop on Tallow Row, and its relics were not bones but paper — scraps accumulating now at a rate that frightened her. Eight names became fourteen became twenty-three in four days, as the skirmishes spread along the river and the garrison's patrols grew bolder and the crowds grew hungrier and the arithmetic did what arithmetic does.

Neighbors began to linger at the counter. That was the change she noticed most. Before, they had come with their jobs — a bill, a notice, a funeral broadside — paid, thanked, gone. Now they stayed. They leaned on the counter and talked, low and urgent, and the talk always circled back to the same place: *did you hear about the cooper's apprentice? Did you hear what they're saying about how he died? They're saying nothing happened at all. They're saying nobody died.*

Nobody died. As though Josiah's mother hadn't sat in her kitchen with a boy's Sunday coat across her lap. As though Marta Kessel's loom weren't standing silent in a rented room her sister couldn't afford to keep.

Sebastian took the names and set them. That was the discipline she imposed on herself, the craft-grief, the transmutation: every scrap became a line of type before the day was out, set mirror-wise in a forme she kept covered with a cloth at the back of the shop, behind the stack of harmless invoice paper. Name. Trade. Age where known. Date. Place. Manner of death, in plain words, no adjectives. *Shot by garrison patrol, Cordwainer St.* *Struck by trooper's saber, Corn Exchange steps.* *Died of wounds, refused treatment, garrison surgeon would not admit civilians.*

Twenty-three lines of type. Twenty-three small leaden bodies lying in their bed of furniture, awaiting the press, and every time she slid the composing stick along and locked another line in, she thought: *this is what I am for.* Not the invoices. Not the wedding cards. Not even the beautiful faces she cut at her bench late at night, the letterforms that made connoisseurs write letters to the shop addressed to "Mr. Vane" that she answered in her steadiest hand. This. Holding the names of the unholdable. Being the box the relics go in.

Her mother would have understood. Her mother, who had kept the shop's books for thirty years in a hand so fine it looked engraved, who had entered every transaction, every debt forgiven, every loaf of bread given to the widow Muller and never collected — her mother had believed, with a ferocity she expressed only through ledger lines, that what was written truly could not be undone. *Ink is a promise,* she used to say, wiping her pen. *That's why bad men fear printers.*

Bad men were going to fear this printer. Sebastian felt no pride in the thought, only a cold settling certainty, like type locking into its chase.

* * *

On the sixth evening, Quentin came back from the provost's office district gray-faced and walked straight past the counter into the back, and she knew before he spoke.

"They have the count," she said.

"Forty-one," he said. "Confirmed dead since the fourteenth. Their internal roll — I saw it through a friend of a friend, a clerk who owes me." He sat down heavily on the type-case bench, which no one was allowed to sit on, and which he sat on anyway because rules relaxed around Quentin the way knots relax in warm water. "Forty-one dead, and the public statement issued today says, quote, *order restored with minimal loss of life.* Minimal." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd ever heard him make. "Forty-one is minimal. What's the threshold, do you suppose? Fifty? A hundred? At what point do the dead become worth mentioning?"

"We have twenty-six," Sebastian said. "We're missing fifteen."

"Then we find them." He looked up, and the grayness in his face had burned off into something harder and cleaner. "Sebastian. Listen to me. They are writing the history right now. Tonight. In warm rooms, with wine, by lamplight, men I trained with, men who taught me — they are deciding what February meant, and in their version nobody died. In their version your street slept peacefully through a mild season of unrest. And in fifty years that version will be in every schoolroom in this province unless—"

"Unless it's already on a thousand doors before their history leaves the printer's hands."

"Yes." His voice cracked slightly on it. "Yes."

She went to the drawer and lifted out the scraps, all twenty-six, and dealt them onto the desk between them like a tarot reader laying out fates. Quentin gathered them up with both hands, carefully, reverently, and began to sort them into order — by date, then alphabetically, the archivist's instinct — and she watched his hands move and thought about the strangeness of it, that a man who had been struck from the rolls should be so gentle with the scraps of the struck-from, that two people made of erasures should spend their nights building a monument out of paper.

Outside, distantly, a bell began to ring. Not the church bell — the foundry bell, rung only for fires and for musters. Somewhere down by the river, the city was tipping further, the ice going out all at once, and neither of them moved toward the window. They bent instead over the scraps, over the names, and worked.

At midnight she cut a new piece of type at her father's bench — she couldn't help it, the ritual demanded it — a single word to head the broadside when it was ready. She chose a plain roman, no flourish, honest as a fence post:

**REMEMBERED**

And as she wiped the punch and set the new sort in its place at the head of the forme, the shutter at the front of the shop rattled softly, and a child's voice whispered through the gap: "*Mister Printer? I've got another one. It's my da.*"

Sebastian put down the burnisher and went to the door.
