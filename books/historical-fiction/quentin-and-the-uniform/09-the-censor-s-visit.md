## 9. The Censor's Visit

The knock came at half past nine, and Quentin knew before he opened the door that it was trouble, because nobody knocked at half past nine. The neighborhood had its rhythms — the baker's boy at five, the first customers at seven, the widow Ostrand's complaints about her funeral cards by eight — and none of them involved a man standing on the step with a leather satchel and an expression of official patience.

Through the glass he could see the gray coat. Provisional government issue, cut well enough to say the wearer was somebody's clerk and badly enough to say he wasn't anybody.

Quentin wiped his hands on his apron out of habit, though his hands were already clean, and opened the door.

"Morning," said the man on the step. He was young. That was the worst of it. Twenty-five at most, with the soft chin of someone who had never missed a meal and a folder under his arm thick enough to ruin lives. "Censorship Directorate. I'm here for a routine inspection of printing premises." He produced a paper stamped with a seal Quentin recognized from a former life — he'd helped design the marginal flourishes on it, years ago, as a graduate assistant who still believed seals meant something. "Name of Delacroix."

"Vane," Quentin said. "I'm the compositor." It came out smooth. Six weeks of answering to Vane, of being Vane's man, had worn the name into him like type set deep in a forme. "My employer isn't available."

"Then you'll do." Delacroix stepped past him without waiting to be invited, which was the whole job description of a censor in one gesture, and stopped in the middle of the shop floor, turning slowly, taking inventory with his eyes.

The shop looked good, Quentin thought. Sebastian had seen to that. After the first deaths in the street fighting — after the boy Corven, after the two men shot on Tanner's Row whose names were already circulating on scraps of paper — she had stripped the back room of everything but legitimate work. Funeral broadsides, price lists, the almanac pages they'd been contracted for since autumn. What remained of the memorial work lived in exactly one place: locked in the stone forme under the press, composed mirror-wise, invisible unless you knew to lift the chase.

Delacroix lifted it anyway.

Not the chase — not yet. He lifted instead a printed sheet off the drying rack, held it to the light, read it with the speed of a man who read for a living. Funeral notice. Widow Ostrand's husband. Approved text, filed format. He set it down.

"You've been busy," he said.

"Almanac season."

"Mm." He walked the length of the shop, trailing one finger along the type cases the way a customs officer trails a finger along luggage. Quentin followed him with his eyes and did not follow him with his feet, because following a censor around your own shop looked like guilt, and guilt was the one thing they were actually looking for. "The Directorate has noticed an increase in unauthorized material in this district. Handbills. Lists." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Lists of names."

"Funeral trade is brisk this winter," Quentin said. "Sad arithmetic, but there it is."

"Sad arithmetic." Delacroix smiled at the cases without turning around. "You were at the university, Mr. Vane."

The floor did not move, but Quentin felt it move. "A long time ago."

"Archival studies. You were struck off the rolls in '41." Delacroix turned then, and his face had changed — not unkindly, which was worse. There was something almost apologetic in it, the look of a man about to spend money that wasn't his. "I know your file. I requested it before I came. Which means my director knows your file. Which means, Mr. Vane, that you are not a man in any position to be clever with me."

* * *

They sat in the front office, at the desk where Sebastian kept the accounts, and Delacroix laid out his papers like a card player laying out a hand he already knew would win. The inspection order. The district report. And beneath them, squared and neat, a copy of a university register with one line ruled through in red ink: *Q. M. Ashe, matriculated 1838, struck 1841.*

Quentin looked at his own name and felt the old cold come up through his boots. Eight years, and the line was still red. They never faded those lines. That was the point of them.

"I'm going to speak plainly," Delacroix said, "because plainly is cheaper than the alternative, for both of us. The Directorate believes this shop is producing seditious lists. If I find evidence, the shop closes, the compositor — you — is detained, and the owner—" he consulted a page, though Quentin was certain he needed no consultation, "—one S. Vane, type-cutter, inheriting from the late G. Pemberton — is summoned for questioning. You understand what questioning means this winter."

"I understand."

"If I find no evidence," Delacroix went on, "I write a clean report, and everyone goes on with their morning. But." He held up one finger. "But I am required to note in my file that the premises employ a man struck from the university rolls, currently working under a name that does not appear on any census I can locate." He let that sit. "That note costs me nothing to write. It costs you a great deal. Do you see?"

Quentin saw. It was extortion conducted with perfect legality, which was the provisional government's principal innovation over the old one. "And what would spare me the note?"

"A name." Delacroix folded his hands. "The Directorate doesn't believe a shop this size produces these lists alone. Someone supplies the plates. Someone coordinates the district printers. Give me a name — any name above yours in that chain — and the note disappears. Better than disappears. Your file comes back across my desk marked *cooperative.* There are people," and here his young face did something complicated, something almost wistful, "who have come back from worse than a red line. With cooperation."

Quentin thought of the archive burning. He had watched it from the bridge three nights ago, the municipal records going up in orange sheets, and somewhere in that fire had been the only document on earth proving Quentin Ashe had ever matriculated at all. The Directorate had erased him twice now — once by pen, once by flame. This boy wanted him to complete the trilogy himself.

"There's no chain," Quentin said. "There's me and Mrs.— and Vane. We print almanacs and funeral cards. If there are handbills in the district, we didn't cut them."

Delacroix studied him for a long moment. Then, softly, almost kindly: "Your hands are inked to the second knuckle, Mr. Vane. Almanac season or not, you've been setting type all night."

* * *

He asked to see the press.

There was no legal ground to refuse — the inspection order covered the premises, all of it — and refusing would have been the same as confessing. So Quentin stood aside and watched the boy walk to the Albion press in the corner, watched him run his thumb along the stone bed, and felt his heartbeat arrange itself into the slow, deliberate rhythm he'd learned in examination halls: panic is noise; think in silence.

The forme lay under the frisket. To anyone who lifted it, it was plain as scripture: two hundred names, mirror-reversed, causes and dates in Sebastian's tightest setting, the memorial edition that forty district printers were waiting to reproduce. One lift. One glance. One red line that would not be Quentin's alone.

Delacroix's fingers found the edge of the frisket.

"The chase is locked," Quentin said. His voice came out level, bored even, and he marveled at it from a distance, the way he'd once marveled at his own signature on documents that ruined him. "Full forme of the March almanac. If you open it, I have to re-lock and re-register the whole bed, and we lose the day's impression. Vane will take it out of my wages and I'll take it out of the Directorate's goodwill, such as it is."

Delacroix paused. "You're asking me not to do my job."

"I'm telling you what it costs. You seem like a man who keeps accounts." Quentin stepped closer, close enough to lower his voice into the register he'd used once upon a time persuading archivists to release misfiled boxes. "Look at the register again. Look at the dates. The lists circulating in this district started appearing three weeks ago. My file — the file you pulled — shows me employed here six weeks. Whatever your Directorate thinks it knows about me, it doesn't know enough to make the arithmetic work, and you know it doesn't, because if it did, you wouldn't be here trading threats. You'd have sent soldiers."

He was gambling everything on a bluff built out of his own disgrace, and the terrible thing, the thing he would carry afterward, was how good it felt. Eight years of being nobody's authority, and here was the residue of it, still spending.

Delacroix's hand came off the frisket.

"Here is what I'll do," the boy said quietly. "I'll write the clean report. No seditious material found. But I'm noting the irregular employment, Mr. Vane, and I'm noting it under my own name, which means when this district goes the way districts go this winter, my name is attached to whatever happens in this shop. If there's a next visit, it won't be me, and it won't be polite. Do you understand the shape of the favor?"

"I understand."

"And I want an answer to one question. Not for the file. For me." Delacroix shouldered his satchel, and for a moment the official mask slipped entirely, and underneath was just a tired young man in a bad coat doing a dirty job in a city eating itself. "The lists. The names of the dead. Whoever prints them — do you think it helps? Honestly. Does ink change anything, or does it just make more names for someone else to cross out?"

Quentin thought of Sebastian at the composing stone at midnight, her shoulders bent, setting the dead in reverse so the press could read them forward. He thought of the widow Ostrand touching the printed card with both hands, as if checking whether grief could be made solid.

"It's the only kind of help left," he said. "Everything else got burned."

Delacroix held his eyes for a moment longer. Then he nodded — a small nod, private, nothing official in it — and wrote his report standing at the counter, in a fair round clerk's hand: *Premises inspected. No contraband matter discovered. Irregular employment noted.* He blotted it, left the copy on the counter, and at the door he paused.

"For what it's worth, Mr. Vane — the flourish on the Directorate seal. The laurel border. Someone designed that very well."

"I know," Quentin said. "I did."

The door closed behind him.

* * *

Quentin stood in the empty shop for a long moment, listening to his own pulse slow down. Then he went to the press, lifted the frisket with steady hands, and looked at what he had nearly lost: two hundred names, backward, patient, waiting for ink.

Sebastian came up from the basement at noon, smelling of metal polish and the cold, and read the report copy on the counter in silence. When she reached the line about irregular employment she looked up, and Quentin saw her do the same arithmetic he had done: *irregular employment noted* was a hook set in their flesh, barb and all. Every future visit would pull on it.

"You spent yourself buying us the day," she said.

"I spent the last coin I had." He managed something like a smile. "Whoever comes next won't stop to talk. Delacroix was the polite version. There are no other polite versions."

She took the report, folded it once, and slid it into the drawer where her father's suppressed pamphlets' proofs were hidden — the drawer that was becoming, page by page, an archive of everything the city wanted forgotten.

"Then we don't wait for the impolite version," she said. "We print tonight. All of it. Before whoever comes next gets a name worth putting on a list."

Outside, the garrison drums had started again, and they were closer than yesterday.
