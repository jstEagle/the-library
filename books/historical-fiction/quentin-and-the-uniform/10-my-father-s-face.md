## 10. My Father's Face

The bell over the door had a cracked note in it — had had one since before I was born, when my father decided that replacing it cost more than the character it lent the shop. Customers stepped through into that broken chime and were, for a moment, part of the shop's long history whether they knew it or not.

I was setting a wedding announcement for the Hollis family when he came in.

I knew before I looked up. There is a particular quality to silence from a man who has stopped moving in a doorway, and I have learned to hear it the way a compositor hears a loose quoine — something out of true, pressing where it shouldn't. I kept my eyes on the stick. *Mr. and Mrs. Aldous Hollis request the pleasure* — the *q-u-e* giving me trouble because I'd let my hands go lazy on the lower case, and lazy hands betray you faster than any face does.

"Good morning," I said, in Quentin's register. Lower than mine. Flatter. The voice I'd built the way Father built a face: cut by cut, until the counterforms held.

"Morning." A pause. Footsteps, unhurried, crossing the floorboards toward the counter. "I'm looking for — " and then he stopped again, and this time the silence had a different shape. Not a customer gathering his errand. A man looking at something.

I looked up.

He was perhaps sixty, gray at the temples, dressed in the good-but-mended coat of a clerk or a schoolmaster — someone who worked with paper for a living but could not afford to have it tailored. His eyes were pale, watery blue, the kind that water further with age, and they were fixed on my face with an attention that made my skin feel like wet paper under a press plate.

"Vellum," I said, to fill the space. "If you're after vellum we're low until Thursday. Paper stock's come up all winter."

"No," he said. "No, not vellum." He came closer. He did not take off his hat, which marked him as foreign to the neighborhood — everyone took off their hat in Pemberton & Vane, everyone, even the garrison officers who came for requisition forms, because the shop had that effect on people, the smell of ink and oil and the warm mineral breath of the type cases. It made them polite without knowing why.

"What can I do for you?" I asked.

"I'm looking," he said slowly, as though each word were a sort he had to find by hand in a dark case, "for the compositor."

"You've found him."

Another pause. His gaze went from my face to my hands, resting on the composing stick, and stayed there a moment too long. My hands are my father's hands — I've thought so since I was twelve, watching them move over the cases with a fluency I hadn't earned yet. Long fingers, ink in the knuckle creases that no lye would ever fully lift, a scar across the left thumb from a knife slip in '39 that I wore now like an inheritance.

"Yes," he said quietly. "I believe I have."

---

His name was Mr. Corliss. He taught mathematics at the Aldenmoor Academy, he said, and needed two hundred copies of an examination paper, and he said all of this while looking at me the way a man reads a difficult proof — line by line, testing every step, waiting for the place where it fails.

I quoted him a price. He agreed too quickly. He gave me the copy — fair hand, cramped, the handwriting of a man who wrote fast and thought faster — and he still did not leave.

"They tell me," Corliss said, "that old Vane died some years back. The type-cutter."

"He did." I set the copy on the spike. My voice held. I have spent ten years making sure my voice holds; it is the most reliable piece of equipment in the shop. "I keep the bench now. Cut my own faces. If there's anything unusual in your examination — fractions, symbols — I'll cast what's wanted."

"And where did you learn?" he asked. "The cutting. That's not a trade you pick up at fourteen."

"My father taught me." True. Every word true. That is the trick of it — I have learned to build my life almost entirely out of true words laid in false lines. Ask me nothing directly and I will never lie to you once.

"Your father," Corliss said, and then, softly, with the gentleness that is worse than suspicion, "Elias Vane had a daughter."

The composing stick in my hand weighed four hundred pounds.

I want to record, here, what happened in my body in that instant, because I have read enough broadsides about courage to know that courage is mostly bookkeeping — a rapid accounting of exits, weights, witnesses, the distance to the door, the hour, who else is on the street. The bell had cracked its note when he entered and no one else had entered since. Quentin was at the river market buying lead. Mrs. Okonjo next door was boiling laundry; I could hear her copper through the wall. The street outside was empty of anything but sleet.

And somewhere under the fear, treacherous and warm as a coal wrapped in cloth, something else. *Elias Vane had a daughter.* Said aloud. In the shop. In the room where she had set her first letter at six years old, standing on a crate, her father's big hands cupped over hers on the stick. Nobody had said it aloud in ten years.

"A daughter," I said. I turned back to the case and began setting his examination paper, because my hands needed work or they would shake, and because turning away is what a man does when a remark doesn't interest him. "Dead, I heard. With the mother. Fever year."

"That was the story."

"Stories are usually all there is."

He was quiet a moment. When he spoke again his voice had changed — the reading-a-proof tone gone, replaced by something older and wearier. "I sat for Elias Vane's types once. At the Exhibition of Manufactures, the year before the fever. He'd cut a face he called Aldenmoor, after the city — said a city should be able to read itself in its own letters. He showed me how the *a* had a little spur on the bowl, like the bridge piers. He talked about his daughter the whole time. How she had the eye. How she could see a hair's width of error in a letter at arm's length, and how she'd better never marry a printer, because she'd run his shop inside a year." A dry, sad laugh. "He said it as a joke. It wasn't one."

I kept setting type. *Mathematics Examination, Michaelmas Term.* My hands knew the work. That is the mercy and the horror of craft: it continues when you cannot. The letters went into the stick clean, justified, square. Inside me nothing was clean, justified, or square.

"If you're asking me something, Mr. Corliss," I said, "ask it."

"Am I?"

"You're standing in my shop talking about a dead man's daughter instead of telling me how many reams of paper you need."

"Two hundred sheets," he said automatically, and then: "You have his hands."

"It's a common enough arrangement of bones."

"It isn't." He took off his hat — finally, belatedly, the gesture of a man conceding a point — and stood there with it in both hands like a penitent. "I'm not here to make trouble. I want you to understand that. I taught mathematics to a girl named Sabine Vane for two terms when she was eleven. Best eye for pattern I ever saw in a child, and I told her father so, and he said *I know it, damn it, what am I to do with it.* And then the fever came and the household rolls recorded three deaths in this building, and I grieved for a clever girl and her parents, and life went on, the way it does. And today I walked past this shop because I always walk past this shop, the way one walks past a grave, and I saw through the window a young man at the case with Elias Vane's hands and Elias Vane's way of tilting his head at the stick, and I thought — well. I thought what I thought."

I set the last line of the header and locked it off. My hands, I noted distantly, were steady. Ten years of practice buys you that much: the surface performs even when the whole structure underneath is screaming.

"The boy who keeps this shop," I said, "is called Sebastian Vane. He's Elias's nephew, come down from Kirkwall after the deaths. He learned the trade from his uncle, same as anyone might. He's no daughter of anybody. He's a printer, and he has two hundred examination papers to print by Friday, and if you'll leave the copy and half payment, they'll be done."

The lie went into the air between us and lay there, obvious, unconvincing, a mislocked forme waiting to spill.

Corliss looked at me for a long time. Then he put his hat back on, took out his purse, and counted coins onto the counter — exact, to the farthing, the habit of a schoolmaster.

"There," he said. "Half payment. From one keeper of records to another." He moved toward the door, and stopped with his hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "For what it's worth — and I know precisely what it's worth, which is nothing, and everything — the Aldenmoor face was the finest thing cut in this city in fifty years. Whoever keeps that bench alive honors him. Whatever name they do it under."

The bell cracked behind him.

---

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that the sleet against the window changed its rhythm. Long enough that the ink on the locked forme began to skin at the edges, and I had to wipe the rollers down twice before I could print his proofs, and my mind was nowhere near the press either time.

*Whatever name they do it under.*

Here is what nobody tells you about wearing a disguise for a decade: it stops being a disguise. It becomes a coat you sleep in, a coat your body grows to fit, and then one day a stranger puts his hand on the collar and says *this isn't yours,* and you feel the terrible double grief of it — grief that you were seen through, and a worse, shamefuller grief that being seen through felt, for one hot second, like relief.

Because it did. I won't write it any other way, not even in a page meant for no one. When he said *Sabine Vane*, something in me rose like a dog hearing its name after years in a strange yard. Eleven years old. Two terms of mathematics. Someone remembered. Not the census rolls, not the parish register, not the ledger of the living — just one old man's memory, unprinted, unarchived, carried around in a watery-eyed head for ten years because a child was good at patterns.

That is the only archive that cannot be burned. I understood it standing at my own counter, and I hated understanding it, because it meant every careful erasure I had engineered was also a small surrender — I had taken myself out of the paper record entirely, and the paper record is the one that gets read aloud, the one that survives, the one that decides whose children get to know their grandparents' names.

Quentin came back at midday with forty pounds of lead and a bruised look I initially attributed to the price of metal.

"You're printing Corliss's examinations," he said, reading the forme upside down from across the room, which after weeks of practice he could do, badly. "Academy work? We never get Academy work."

"We do now."

"Corliss. Thomas Corliss? He came himself?" Quentin set the lead down and frowned. "I know that name. He was — " and I watched the recollection arrive in his face the way weather arrives over the river, slow and total. "Archives. He consulted at the municipal archives when I was still — before. He's the one who first showed me the suppression lists. The pamphlet seizures." He looked at me sharply. "Sebastian. What did he say to you?"

"Nothing."

"You've got your father's face on."

"I haven't got anything on. I have a shop to run."

"Sebastian."

"*Nothing,*" I said, louder than I meant, and the word rang against the type cases and came back to me sounding exactly like what it was: the sound of a woman guarding a door she didn't know she'd left open.

Quentin didn't push. That is one of the things about him I was learning to trust — he pushes at records, at officials, at locked drawers, and then goes absolutely quiet around people. He picked up the lead and started sorting it, and after a while he said, to the type case rather than to me:

"Whoever it was. However he said it. You're still here. The shop's still here. That's the whole of what matters today."

But it wasn't, and he knew it, and I knew it, and that night after he left I sat at Father's cutting bench with a graver in my hand and a blank of type metal clamped in the vise and cut nothing at all. I just sat there, in his chair, in his lamplight, wearing his son's name like a borrowed coat gone thin at the elbows, and I thought about a girl of eleven who could see a hair's width of error at arm's length.

What I could not stop seeing was the error in my own composition. Ten years locked tight, justified, square — and one old man with a hat in his hands had shown me the gap in the line.

Three days later, when I delivered Corliss's examination papers to the Academy myself, there was a sealed envelope tucked in with the payment. No note on the outside. Inside, a single sheet in that same cramped, racing hand, and I stood in the Academy courtyard in the sleet and read it twice, and by the third reading my hands were shaking too hard to hold it flat.

It was a list of dates, file numbers, and drawer locations in the municipal archives — the seizure registers of 1841 through 1844, the years of Father's political pamphlets, the ones confiscated and suppressed, the ones whose plates were supposed to have been destroyed.

At the bottom, one line:

*They were never destroyed. They were filed. I checked twice. — T.C.*

And beneath that, smaller, as though written later, pressed hard:

*The provisional government's men have been in the archives all week. Whatever is going to happen to those records is going to happen soon.*
