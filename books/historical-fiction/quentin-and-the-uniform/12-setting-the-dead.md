## 12. Setting the Dead

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when I set the first name.

*Marguerite Ossler, aged forty-one. Baker's widow. Shot in the doorway of her own shop, the eleventh of March.*

Twenty-six pieces of type for that one line, each one a small leaden tooth I picked from its case and seated into the composing stick upside down and backward, because that is how type lives — mirrored, wrong-reading, legible only to itself until the press kisses paper and the world gets it right. I had been setting type since I was nine years old. My father taught me that you do not look at the letter. You feel for it in the dark of the case, your fingers knowing where every character sleeps, and you trust the metal to become true only when it meets the ink.

I had never set anything like this.

Two hundred and six names so far. The scraps of paper kept coming even now — pushed under the door at all hours, folded into loaves of bread, pressed into Quentin's hand by women who would not meet his eye. A boy of fifteen, drowned in the canal with his wrists bound. A lamplighter who stepped between a soldier and a child and took a bayonet for it. An old man whose heart gave out running from men who were never punished, because they were on the winning side, and the winning side does not count its own dead, only ours.

"Ossler again," Quentin said from across the bench. He was working the second stick, slower than me but steadier every night, his scholar's fingers finally learning the geography of the cases. "The sister came back. There was a second name inside the first one."

He slid the scrap across. Two pieces of paper, one nested in the other like a seed in its husk. *Marguerite Ossler*, said the outer. And within: *Tomasz Ossler, aged nine. Her son. Died the same hour. The soldiers did not wait to see if he was armed. He was not.*

I sat with that for longer than a compositor should sit with anything. The candle fluttered. Somewhere above us the floorboards of Mrs. Halloway's rooms creaked, her insomnia keeping its own vigil.

"Set them together," I said finally. "Mother and son. Same entry. If the official record will split them apart, we won't."

Quentin nodded and went back to work.

That was the whole of our conversation for an hour. This was night work in the truest sense — the shutters barred, the lamp hooded with tin so no light spilled onto the street, the press silent except when we pulled a proof, which we did rarely, because proofs make noise and noise makes widows. We worked by touch as much as sight now. My fingers walked the cases. Quentin's followed, learning. Between us the forme grew, column by column, two hundred names standing on their heads in lead, waiting to be turned right side up.

* * *

You should understand what it means, setting a name in type.

It is not like writing a name. Writing is fluid, provisional, a thing the hand can disown. But type is commitment. Each letter is cast metal, identical to every other instance of that letter ever cut from that matrix, and once you seat it in the stick it holds its place with a stubbornness no eraser can touch. When I set *Tomasz Ossler, aged nine*, those letters existed before I was born and will exist after everyone who knew him is gone. The same *o* that spelled his name has spelled ten thousand other names. It does not care. That is the mercy of it, and the terror. The type is indifferent. What it says depends entirely on what hands assemble it.

The government had assembled its own account, of course. We'd seen the first broadsheets come off the official press three days after the bridge fighting: *isolated disturbances*, *regrettable excesses*, a total of eleven deaths acknowledged citywide, most of them attributed to "crossfire of uncertain origin." Eleven. We had forty-one names before the end of the first week of collecting, and the scraps were still coming.

"They're not even lying well," Quentin had said, reading the official broadside with the flat expression he wore when something had gone past anger into some colder country. "A good falsification requires craft. You have to account for the witnesses, structure the omissions so they reinforce one another. This is just—" he searched for the word— "*laziness*. They think nobody will check."

"Will anybody?"

"Everybody," he said, "if we give them something to check against."

So here was the arithmetic of our nights: two hundred and six names, average of perhaps thirty pieces of type per line including the dates and causes, which came to better than six thousand individual sorts, plus spacing, plus leads, plus the furniture to lock it all up tight in the chase. My father's type inventory, counted honestly, held perhaps four hundred pounds of foundry type across five faces. We were going to strip the shop bare to say these names once. Every em quad, every hair space, every ligature would be in that forme or wasted. After this printing there would be barely enough left to set a handbill.

I found I didn't mind. There is a poverty that is clean.

* * *

Past midnight, my hands began to fail me.

Not dramatically — nothing so useful as a cramp that would force rest. Just a slowing. A hesitation at the case, the fingers hovering over the *h* box while the mind forgot which word came next. I caught myself reading the same scrap three times: *Anselm Prue, carter, crushed beneath a wagon the garrison overturned to block the street. He lived four hours. His family could not reach him.* Four hours. I had set worse tonight and I still had to stop and put both palms flat on the cold stone of the bench and breathe.

"You should sleep," Quentin said without looking up.

"So should you."

"I don't dream anymore. Sleeping's just a way of losing time." He set a period with unnecessary force. "You dream. I've heard you, through the wall. So sleep matters more for you."

I hadn't known he could hear me. I filed that away with the other small exposures of shared walls and shared secrets — the growing inventory of things Quentin Ashe knew about me that no census roll contained. It should have frightened me. Once, it would have. Lately it felt more like ballast.

"The mirror trick," I said instead, because if I spoke about dreams I would have to speak about what was in them, and Marguerite Ossler's face had been in them last night, asking me why her son's name needed a second piece of paper to be believed. "Show me where we are with the locked formes."

He cleared space on the far bench and lifted the cloth. Three chases stood in a row, each holding a locked forme face-down, and this was the part of the work I loved best and trusted least. To any eye — a censor's eye, a soldier's eye, the informer's eye we both pretended we weren't worried about — the benches showed nothing but ordinary jobbing work. Funeral notices, a price list for the flour merchant, half-set almanac pages. The ledger formes sat among them wearing their disguises, mirror-wise, gibberish to anyone who read them as they lay. Only the press could translate. Only iron and pressure could turn the dead right side up.

"We can't leave them locked, though," Quentin said, and I heard him arrive at the thought as he said it, the way he often did, thinking aloud in that archivist's cadence. "Locked formes are evidence. Loose type is just... type. If anyone comes through that door, we distribute the forme back into the cases. Ninety seconds, if we practice."

"Ninety seconds to unmake two hundred people."

"Yes." He met my eyes across the bench, and I saw he understood exactly what he'd said and had said it anyway. "That's the cost of keeping them alive. We unmake them every morning and remake them every night, until they're out in the world where no one can take them apart again."

I looked at the formes a long moment. All those reversed letters, standing in their columns like a congregation seen through the back of a mirror.

"All right," I said. "Practice at dawn. Before the deliveries."

* * *

The third watch of the night belonged to the dates.

This was Quentin's province, and I confess I watched him at it the way my father used to watch me — with that particular pride that hurts a little, because it contains the knowledge that the skill is leaving you and entering someone else. He had built himself a chronology on scrap paper, cross-referenced three ways: date, location, cause. Where a neighbor's account conflicted with another neighbor's account, he did not smooth the difference. He printed both. *Some say shot on the bridge; some say in the water below it.* Truth, he told me once, is not a single line. It is a knot, and you print the knot.

"Here's one that isn't a death," he said, holding up a scrap. "Should it go in?"

"What is it?"

"A woman named Ilse Brandt. Taken from her lodgings on the ninth. No body returned. The garrison lists her as transferred to the river prison; the river prison has no record of her." He turned the scrap over, as though the answer might be written on the back. "She's neither dead nor accounted for. Most histories would drop her. She doesn't fit a category."

"Then we make her a category." I reached for the stick. "Missing. That's a cause too. Maybe the cruelest one. Set her last in the day she vanished, and let her stand there with no period after her name. Like a sentence that hasn't ended."

Quentin looked at me for a moment. Then he handed me the scrap without argument, and I felt the weight of it pass between us — not the paper's weight, the other kind. He had spent years in archives where missing persons were clerical problems. I could see him deciding, line by line, night by night, that they were griefs instead.

Ilse Brandt went into the forme without her period. I checked twice to be sure.

* * *

Near four in the morning, the candle died, and we worked the last hour by the banked coals of the stove and whatever gray light leaked through the shutter seams.

There is a state I get into at that hour, at the end of a great setting, and my father called it *the deep water*. Your hands know the cases better than your eyes do. Your mind stops translating between the reading self and the setting self, and the words simply flow from paper to stick as if the type were already in them, waiting. Time goes strange. The ache in your shoulders becomes a kind of music. You are no longer composing the page; you are the page, being composed.

I set the last dozen names of that night in the deep water. I couldn't tell you afterward which ones they were, only that my hands were steady and my throat was tight and somewhere in the middle of it I realized I was crying, quietly, the tears doing nothing to slow the fingers, because grief and craft are separate departments and only occasionally send messages to each other.

When I came up out of it, the sky beyond the shutters had gone the color of dishwater, and Quentin was standing over the finished forme with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a graveside.

"How many?" I asked.

"Two hundred and nineteen." He didn't turn around. "Plus Ilse Brandt, unperioded. Plus eleven entries marked *disputed*, because honesty costs extra and we can afford it."

Two hundred and nineteen. In eight nights. My whole body hummed with the fatigue of it, a sacred exhaustion, if exhaustion can be sacred — and sitting there in the ash-gray dawn with my fingertips blackened to the first knuckle and every quad in the shop committed to the dead, I believed it could. No customer had ordered this edition. No payment waited at its conclusion. If the government learned of it, it would not merely confiscate the forme; it would confiscate us. We were printing for no profit and no permission, which meant we were printing for the only reason that has ever mattered: because the truth required a body, and ours were the bodies nearest to hand.

"It needs a title," Quentin said.

"It has one." I wiped my eyes with the heel of my hand, smearing ink across my cheekbone, and didn't care. "My father had a phrase for the register he kept of jobs done. *A True Account of the Work.* We'll use it straight. No ornament. Let the ornament be the names themselves."

"*A True Account of the Work,*" he repeated, testing it. Then, softer: "He'd have printed this, your father. The pamphlets I found traces of — he never stopped, even when they suppressed him. Even alone."

"He wasn't alone at the end. He had me." I stood, and my knees announced their grievances, and I ignored them. "And now the shop has you. Which means the work has hands again. Both pairs."

We locked the final chase together, his hands and mine on the same quoins, tightening the last of the furniture until the forme rang faintly under the tap of the mallet — the sound every printer knows, the sound of a page made solid, of words that will not scatter. Two hundred and nineteen people, locked fast in lead.

Then, because we had promised ourselves, we spent ninety minutes distributing it all back into the cases. Unmaking them. It was harder than making them had been. Each letter went home to its compartment still warm from my fingers, and I whispered the names as I broke the lines — Marguerite, Tomasz, Anselm, Ilse — a printer's requiem, a mass said in reverse, gathering the dead into safekeeping until nightfall, when we would raise them again.

The knock came as I was banking the stove.

Three raps, uneven, urgent — Mrs. Halloway's pattern, but faster than I'd ever heard it, and underneath her knuckles I could hear her voice pitched low through the door, saying my father's name. Not mine. My father's. The name I had buried with the boy I used to be.

Quentin and I looked at each other across the dark shop, two hundred and nineteen names sleeping in their cases between us.

"That's not a delivery knock," he said quietly.

"No," I said, and moved to open the door.
