## 13. Quentin's Confession

The confession sat in me for eleven days before I could lift it out.

I counted them, the way I counted everything now — in pages set, in names entered into the forme, in the hours between the last customer leaving and the lamp being trimmed low. Eleven days since Aldous Merrick's letter had come to the shop, folded inside a delivery of legal paper we had no use for. Eleven days since I'd read it twice by the light of the shuttered window and then hidden it behind the loose board where Sebastian kept her father's private correspondence, as if proximity to honest paper might make it honest itself.

It did not. It was still what it was: an offer.

*Restoration of full standing. Reinstatement in the rolls of the University, with arrears noted and expunged. A position in the provisional government's bureau of records, under my direct supervision. In exchange: your hand on the official account of the February disturbances.*

*Disturbances.* As if the dead in our ledger were a noise complaint.

Merrick had written it in his old careful hand, the one that had corrected my marginalia when I was nineteen and hungry for exactly this kind of attention. He had even remembered my weakness for flattery disguised as duty. *You have a gift for order, Quentin,* he'd written at the bottom, *and order is needed now more than ever.*

He was right about that much. Order was needed. The wrong kind was simply easier to come by.

I set type all day with the letter burning through my shirt like a coal I'd swallowed. Sebastian worked across the bench from me, her hands moving through the cases with that economy I'd spent weeks failing to imitate — wrist, finger, eye, done — and every time she glanced up to check my spacing I felt the letter shift against its hiding place like a living thing. She trusted me. That was the arithmetic I couldn't balance. She had shown me the ledger. Two hundred and six names now, each one set mirror-wise so only the press could read it, each one a small act of treason committed at her bench with her ink and her father's type. And she'd shown me because she believed I was the kind of man who tore flags out of files and kept tearing.

I was. Mostly. But there was a part of me — I would not name it until day eleven, and naming it did not shrink it — a part of me that had read Merrick's offer and felt something other than disgust first. Something warm. Something that sounded like *they want you back.*

That was the thing about erasure that no one who hadn't suffered it could understand. It didn't make you noble. It made you hungry. Eight years struck from the university rolls — no degree, no standing, no name that any official hand would recognize — and I had learned to live on scraps of unofficial existence: odd clerkships, borrowed lodgings, a census taker's commission that paid almost nothing but let me carry a book with my name stamped on the spine. When Merrick offered to un-erase me, some starved animal in my chest sat up and wagged.

And here was the filthiest part, the part I made myself say aloud eventually, alone, at night, rehearsing: the people offering to restore me were the same people burning the archives. They had fed my file to a stove in the courtyard of the record office while I stood in the crowd and watched. My proof of existence, gone up with the parish registers and the debt rolls and the true casualty lists from the barricades. And my heart had still leapt at their letter. That is what they do to you. That is what erasure does. It teaches you to be grateful to whoever holds the pen.

On the eleventh day, Sebastian caught me looking at the loose board.

She didn't say anything. She just looked — at the board, at me, at the board again — and went back to her composing stick. But she knew. Of course she knew. She had grown up reading faces the way I read registers, out of necessity, and I had never once managed to keep a secret from her longer than a week. The whole shop seemed to lean in around us: the press silent, the ink smell thick, the winter light coming through the front window in a long dusty bar that fell exactly between us like a line neither of us wanted to cross.

"Close the door," she said finally.

There was no customer to close it against. We closed it anyway.

* * *

"I'm going to tell you something," I said, "and then you're going to look at me differently, and I'd rather you did it now than after the plates are finished."

She took off her apron. Folded it. Set it on the bench. These small domestic motions of hers, performed while deciding whether to be furious, always frightened me more than shouting would have.

"Merrick wrote to me," I said. "Eleven days ago."

Her face didn't change. "The historian."

"My mentor. My former mentor." I heard myself correcting the word the way I used to correct dates in a ledger, and hated it. "He runs the bureau of records now. He's drafting the official account of the uprising. He wants a second hand on it. He wants mine."

"And what does the account say?"

"You know what it says. You've seen the proclamations. Isolated disturbances. Regrettable excesses. A handful of casualties, regretfully unavoidable, most of them regretfully soldiers." I made myself go on. "Two hundred and six people, Sebastian. He'll give them a paragraph. Maybe a sentence. 'Order was restored.'"

"Order was restored," she repeated, flat, tasting it. "And he wants you to write that."

"He wants me to sign it. Writing is nothing; anyone writes. Signing is authorship. He taught me that. He said—" I stopped. Started again. "He said a man with my gift for order shouldn't waste it on pamphlets."

The silence went on long enough that I heard the river through the walls, the ice groaning somewhere down at the wharves. Then:

"How long have you known?"

"Eleven days."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise, arithmetic. Eleven days of setting the dead together. Eleven days of her handing me names she'd taken from widows at her own door, tears still wet on the paper, trusting my hands with them. Eleven days.

"Why are you telling me now?" she asked.

"Because you caught me looking at the board."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have that's true." I pulled the letter out from behind the board myself, rather than let her do it. Held it out. "Read it. All of it. Including the part I can't say properly."

She read it standing, the way she read everything, fast, her lips not quite moving. When she reached the bottom her jaw tightened, and I braced for the anger, and I got it — but not first.

First she asked, quietly, "Did you want it?"

And that was the moment the whole confession turned on. Because I could have lied. I was, by trade and temperament, a good liar; I had survived eight years on carefully incomplete answers. But she had built a life out of an incomplete answer too, and she deserved better than the thing we both knew how to do.

"Yes," I said. "Part of me did. Not the part I'm proud of. But it's there, and it doesn't leave just because I want it to. He offered me my name back, Sebastian. My actual name, in the actual rolls, the degree, the position, the— " My voice cracked and I pushed through it. "Do you know what I thought, the first time I read it? Not *how could he.* I thought *finally.* Finally someone with power looked at the hole where I used to be and decided to fill it in. I stood in that courtyard and watched my file burn and I still—" I pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes. "I wanted to be restored by the very men holding the matches. There it is. There's your craftsman of truth."

She set the letter down on the bench between us, face-up, Merrick's careful hand gleaming.

"Say the rest," she said.

"There isn't any rest."

"There's always rest. You're an archivist. Nothing is ever just the surface layer." She came around the bench. "You kept it eleven days. Why? If you'd already decided to refuse him, you'd have burned it the first night. So either you were deciding—" she held up one finger, "—or you'd decided and you were afraid to tell me." A second finger. "Which?"

"Both," I said. "In sequence. First the deciding. Then — God help me — the fear wasn't of losing you. It was of you being right. If you forgave me too easily, I'd know I hadn't really been tempted. If you threw me out, I'd deserve it, and I'd lose—" I gestured at the shop, the press, the forme waiting under its frisks, the two hundred and six names. "This is the first thing I've belonged to since the rolls spat me out. I'd rather confess to a crime than risk it carelessly."

"So you confessed to a different crime instead."

"Yes."

She looked at me for a long time. I watched her do the thing she does — the thing I had watched her father's portrait do above the type cases, the measuring look, weighing a piece of type in the hand to feel if it's true.

Then she picked up Merrick's letter and held it out to me over the flame of the trimming lamp.

"No," I said.

"Burn it yourself, then. I'm not doing your penance for you."

I took it. My hands were steady, which surprised me. I held the corner to the flame and watched Merrick's careful sentences curl and blacken — *your gift for order, restoration of full standing, arrears expunged* — and dropped the last burning scrap into the ink stone, where it died with a small sound like a sigh.

"You understand," Sebastian said, "that he'll send another."

"He'll send worse. He'll send threats next. Or he'll send someone."

"Probably." She was already rolling up her sleeves. "But you told me. That's the difference between you and him, and I needed to see it with my own eyes before I could believe it. Quentin Ashe, census taker, liar by profession, coward by self-description — you put the true thing on the table when keeping it hidden would have cost you nothing I could detect." She took up her composing stick and nodded at mine. "So pick it up. We've got forty-one names left to set, and I'm not doing them alone."

I didn't move. "You're forgiving me. Just like that."

"Just like that?" Her laugh had no humor in it, but it had iron underneath. "Quentin. Do you think I don't understand wanting your name back? I've been wearing a dead boy's name for six years. Every morning I lace on Sebastian Vane like a coat, and every morning some part of me thinks: today someone will offer to let me stop. Today the world will make it easy. And if the world ever did — if some magistrate said, admit what you are and keep your trade, your shop, your press, all you must do is sign their paper — do you imagine I wouldn't feel the pull? I'd feel it in my teeth." She set a capital S in her stick without looking at it. "The temptation isn't the sin. Everyone tempted is innocent until they act, and everyone acts sometimes. What matters is which ledger you bring it to. You brought yours here. To this bench. That's the only test I know how to give, and you passed it eleven days late, which is human timing."

I found I couldn't speak for a moment. When I could, all I managed was: "You should be angrier."

"Oh, I am. Don't mistake this for softness." She pointed the stick at me like a blade. "If you had signed his draft — if I'd found my neighbors' deaths rendered as *disturbances* under your signature — I'd have put you out myself, and I'd have burned whatever you left behind. This isn't forgiveness in advance, Quentin. It's forgiveness retroactive. You haven't earned tomorrow yet. Come earn it."

So I picked up the stick. And we set names.

* * *

We worked past midnight. That was becoming the shape of our lives — the shop after dark, shutters drawn, lamp turned low, the two of us bent over the cases trading silence and occasionally a name. Widow Hessler's sons, both of them, gone in the same hour on the bridge. The cooper's apprentice, fourteen. A woman named Odile Ferrant whose only crime, per the witnesses, had been carrying water.

Somewhere past two in the morning, my hands slowed and stopped pretending to work.

"Sebastian."

"Hm." She was squinting at a line of eight-point, checking it against the copy.

"When I said part of me wanted it — I need you to understand the shape of that part. It wasn't weakness. It was arithmetic. Eight years, Sebastian. Eight years of being nobody on paper, and he walks in with a single sheet and offers to make it all undone. One signature. That's all I am worth to them — one signature. And that's all it would take to make two hundred and six people into a footnote." I looked at my hands, stained to the knuckle. "A man should cost more than one signature."

"He costs exactly what he refuses to sign," she said.

I turned that over. It had the ring of something she'd learned young and carried a long way, like everything else she'd inherited from her father along with the type.

"Do you ever think about it?" I asked. "Signing your own name. On something."

"Constantly." The word came out quick, involuntary, and she heard herself and went quiet. Then, more slowly: "Every broadside that leaves this shop unsigned. Every funeral notice cut in a face nobody knows is mine. Someday — I used to think someday I'd get to sign things. Now I mostly think I'll die unsigned, and the press will go on, and some future compositor will wonder whose hands wore the cases smooth like this." She blew a fleck of dust off the stone. "But that's my bargain, not yours. You can have your name back anytime you like. Mine has to be taken."

"Not tonight it doesn't."

"No." Almost a smile. "Not tonight."

We worked on. And near three o'clock, with thirty-three names remaining and my shoulders aching in the shape of the composing stick, there came a knock at the street door. Three knocks, soft, spaced — the signal Mrs. Hessler used, and after her the cooper's widow, and after them half the street by now.

Sebastian blew out the lamp before opening to the crack. I heard voices, low and urgent, more than one. When she turned back to me her face was strange in the dark — lit from below by the banked coals of the trimming stove, and older than her years.

"The garrison posted new orders at the Custom House," she said. "Effective dawn. All printing presses in the district to be registered and inspected. Unregistered output to be treated as seditious." She paused. "They're coming for the presses, Quentin. All of them. Ours first — we're the loudest."

I stood there in the dark with ink to my elbows and thirty-three names unset, and I understood that Merrick's letter had not been an ending. It had been a courtesy. The historian had given me one last chance to join the victors before the victors arrived.

"They'll find the forme," I said. "Even if we hide it. Even if we melt the type. They'll inspect every case in this shop, and they'll know mirror-set type when they see it."

"Then we don't hide it," Sebastian said, and her voice had gone very quiet and very certain, the voice she must have used at her father's bench when she was nine years old and already knew more than the customers guessed. "We finish it. Tonight. Before dawn. And then we decide what can be carried."

She relit the lamp.
