## 14. The Informer

The knock came at half past six in the morning, which was wrong on two counts.

First, because the shop did not open until eight, and everyone in Copperlane knew it. Second, because the person knocking knocked the way people knock when they are afraid of being heard — quick, soft, twice, then nothing, like a bird testing a branch.

Sebastian put down her graver mid-cut and stood very still in the workroom, listening. The type cases around her held the night's labor: two hundred names set mirror-wise, waiting for the press to turn them right. Two hundred names that had no existence anywhere in the world except in walnut and steel and her own tired hands.

The knock came again. Three times now, with a rhythm she almost recognized.

She crossed the shop floor past the press — old Aldous, her father had called him, though he'd been built decades before any Aldous owned him — and lifted the latch without opening the door. "We're closed."

"It's me." A boy's voice, cracked at the edges. "It's Wren. Toby Wren."

Toby Wren was nineteen years old and had been delivering paper stock to the shop since he was eleven, back when Sebastian's father still answered the door himself, back before there were two Vanes or one or none, depending on who was doing the counting. He'd carried reams of rag paper up the hill from the wharf through every weather Aldenmoor could devise. She knew the exact weight of his shoulders under a bale of stock. She knew his mother made a soup with barley and too much pepper that Sebastian had eaten at their table a dozen times during the worst of the grain winter.

She opened the door.

He looked like a boy who had been unmade and hastily reassembled. His coat was buttoned wrong. One eye was swollen half-shut, going yellow-green at the rim, the color of a bruise deciding what it would become. His hands wouldn't settle — they went to his cap, to his pockets, to the doorframe, nowhere twice.

"Toby." She stepped back to let him in. "Who did this?"

"Nobody." He said it fast, the way you say a word you've been told to say. Then his face did something complicated and he said, "Soldiers. At the garrison house. They took me Tuesday night."

Tuesday was four days ago. Sebastian felt the cold arrive in her chest, patient and certain, the way it always did, the way it had when Quentin hadn't come to the bridge. "Four days."

"They asked questions." He was inside now, standing on the shop floor among the cases of type as if he'd never stood there a hundred times, as if the room had turned strange for him overnight. "They asked about the pamphlets. About the broadsides. Who brings paper, who carries finished work, whose names are on the—"

"Stop." She closed the door. Turned the lock, though a lock meant nothing against soldiers, and they both knew it. "What did you tell them?"

* * *

Here is what Toby Wren told her, standing in the middle of the shop his family had helped keep alive for twenty years, with his ruined eye aimed at the floorboards:

They had taken him off Wharf Street near his mother's door. No charges read, no reason given beyond a sergeant's hand on his collar and the phrase *known associate*. They held him in a cellar with forty other boys from Copperlane and asked each one the same questions in different orders, so that no two answers could be matched up into a lie. He'd held out for a day. He was proud of that — she could see the pride trying to survive in him like a plant under a dropped plank.

Then they brought bread. Army bread, white, with actual wheat in it, not the sawdust barley loaf his mother stretched across three days. And they said the bread was for cooperators only. And his stomach — nineteen years old, four days in a cellar — his stomach voted before his conscience could convene.

"I told them about the deliveries," he said. "I told them the shop prints at night sometimes. I told them—" His voice broke and reformed lower. "I told them about the ledger. That you were setting something long. I didn't give them names, Miss Vane, I swear on my mother I didn't, but the sergeant, he already knew some. He said a name and I couldn't keep my face still, and he saw it, he *saw* it—"

"Whose name?"

Toby's mouth worked. He looked, for one unbearable moment, exactly like the eleven-year-old who had once cried over a dropped ream because Pemberton himself had told him accidents were just the world proofreading.

"Hollis," he said. "Old man Hollis at the chandler's. They took him yesterday morning."

Hollis. Seventy-one years old, deaf in one ear, who left a scrap of paper at the shop's door three weeks ago bearing the name of his grandson in a handwriting gone shaky, with the words *he was fourteen* beneath it. Hollis, whose name was the ninth line of the ledger, set in twelve-point, waiting.

Sebastian sat down on the composing stone because her legs informed her they were no longer negotiating.

* * *

When Quentin had been taken at the bridge — when the soldiers found the plates in his case and he'd said, loudly, in front of witnesses, *these are mine, the work is mine, the shop knew nothing* — Sebastian had understood it as arithmetic. One man traded for many. An erased man volunteering for erasure, buying with his body the hours the neighborhood needed to finish the run. She had hated the arithmetic and done it anyway, every day since, because grief that stops working is just grief.

But this was different. This wasn't sacrifice. This was Toby, who ate her father's memory in the form of barley soup at his mother's table. This was the found family she had spent months assembling out of neighbors and scraps of paper and shared risk, and one thread of it had sold the pattern to the men who wanted to burn the loom.

She should have hated him. She reached for hate the way you reach for a familiar tool in a dark case, by feel, by habit — and found the compartment empty. What she found instead was worse. She found herself remembering the white army bread. Remembering her own hunger, the lean years after her father died, when she'd presented as a grieving son to keep the bench and eaten less than an apprentice should and told no one why. Hunger makes liars of us all, her father used to say. It's the one censor we can't outprint.

"How much time do we have?" she asked.

"A sergeant came by our street last night asking if Wren's boy was home. Ma lied for me — first lie of her life, I think, she was terrible at it." Toby almost smiled and then couldn't. "They're coming back. Today, maybe. Tomorrow. They want to search the shop proper. They let me go so they could follow me here, Miss Vane. I know that's why. I'm not stupid. I walked three streets wrong first, doubled back twice, I tried—" His voice finally failed altogether. "I came to tell you instead of leading them. That has to count. Tell me that counts."

Sebastian looked at him for a long moment. Outside, Copperlane was waking: the chandlers' shutters going up, the smell of the bakery on Fen Street, someone arguing cheerfully about the price of tallow. A street full of people who had left scraps of paper at her door because they trusted the shop more than they trusted God, or the government, or their own memories, to keep their dead.

"That counts," she said. "Now get out of here before anyone sees you came. Go home by the tannery lane. And Toby—" He stopped at the door, hand on the latch, braced. "Your mother is to say you never came here. Not to protect me. To protect you. Do you understand? Whatever happens next, you told them everything you told them, and then you stopped. You owe them nothing further and neither does she."

He nodded, wet-eyed, and slipped out, and the door clicked behind him like the last page of something turning.

* * *

She stood alone in the shop with the ledger.

Two hundred names. Six weeks of night work, hers and Quentin's, set in a mirrored language only the press could speak. The forme lay locked in its chase beside Aldous, ready, waiting on paper that was due tomorrow from the wharf — paper that would now arrive, she realized, into the hands of whoever watched the wharf, because Toby had known about the deliveries, Toby had *been* the deliveries.

Run or stay.

She laid it out the way her father had taught her to lay out a page, weighing every element against the others, hunting the balance.

Running meant the plates. Two cases, split between her and Quentin according to the plan they'd made before the bridge — each carrying half, each able to reprint the whole from their half plus whatever the other smuggled out or memorized. Running meant survival, meant the truth living on somewhere else, meant Quentin's sacrifice staying worth what it had cost. Running also meant the shop empty when the soldiers came, which confirmed everything, which gave them the story they wanted: a seditious print shop, flushed, scattered, its operator fled like a criminal. Which made every neighbor who'd left a scrap of paper a suspect. Which made Toby's mother a suspect. Which made the whole street answerable for the flight of one woman who had spent her whole life learning how to disappear.

Staying meant printing. Tonight, if she could find paper; the shop still had perhaps three hundred sheets of good stock in the loft, enough for a first run, not the ten thousand the plan demanded but enough to start, enough that the record would exist outside these walls before the soldiers' boots reached the door. Staying meant she'd be here when they came. Staying meant — she was honest with herself, sitting on the composing stone in the gray morning light — it might mean the same thing it had meant for old Hollis, taken yesterday morning over a grandson's name. It might mean arrest, or worse, and the second half of the plates locked in a case upstairs, useless, orphaned.

Her father's voice, in memory, dry as new type: *A printer who runs leaves no edition. A printer who stays and fails leaves a martyrdom, which sells better but reads worse.*

And underneath both voices, a third one, quieter, that sounded disconcertingly like Quentin: *The archive burns because they are afraid of what's written in it. Fear is information. Ask what they're afraid of, and do precisely that.*

What were they afraid of? Names. Two hundred names in the margins where their clean official history wanted white space. They weren't afraid of the shop. Shops burned every week in this city lately. They were afraid of the *record* — of a thousand hands holding the same true page, which was the one thing a victor's narrative cannot survive.

So the record had to leave these walls tonight. And the record needed a compositor to set its final line, and the final line needed her standing at the stone.

Decision arrived not as thunder but as a sort of settling, the way a forme locks into its chase — pressure applied evenly until suddenly nothing can move.

She would stay. She would print tonight with every sheet in the building. And she would send word, through the network of neighbors who owed the shop their dead, that the second half of the plates must go to the river district printers by dawn — that the plan held even if she didn't.

* * *

By noon she had moved through Copperlane the way she'd learned to move as a boy nobody looked at twice: unhurried, bored, carrying nothing worth stealing. Three messages, delivered in three different registers. To Frau Ostrow at the bakery, a request for "the usual flour order sent round tonight instead of Thursday," which meant: bring every hand you trust to the shop after dark. To the lamplighter Pell, a complaint that "the upstairs window sticks and needs seeing to," which meant: watch the street and warn of soldiers. To Widow Marchetti, whose son's name was line forty-one of the ledger, she said nothing coded at all. She simply stood in the widow's doorway and said, "Tonight we print them all," and the old woman gripped her wrist hard enough to bruise and said, "Then God will finally have accurate records," and laughed, terribly, wonderfully.

Back at the shop, she opened the loft and counted paper like a miser counting breath. Three hundred and forty sheets. She pulled the first forme from its bed and began breaking it into quarter-formes — four names to a plate, small enough that any borrowed press in the district could run them — and the work took her down into itself, into the click of the composing stick and the smell of ink, and for a while there was nothing in the world but the names and her hands.

She was setting the last quarter-forme — lines ninety-seven through one hundred, including Hollis's grandson, *aged fourteen*, in the space where the authorities' ledger would have said *no fixed abode* — when the light in the doorway changed.

Not the lamplighter. Not Frau Ostrow with flour. A shape in the door, breathing hard from running, and when she turned with the composing stick still in her fist like a weapon, she saw it was Toby again — Toby, white-faced, one shoe missing, who had walked three streets wrong and doubled back twice and was apparently incapable of doing anything but tell the truth badly.

"They moved it up," he gasped. "The search. I heard the sergeant at our door — they're not coming tomorrow, Miss Vane. There's a patrol leaving the garrison house now. For Copperlane. For your street." He swallowed. "You've got maybe an hour."

Sebastian looked at the forme in front of her — one hundred names locked in steel, half the truth — and then at the stairs leading up to the case that held the rest, and began, calmly, with hands that did not shake until she noticed them shaking, to calculate what can be carried.
