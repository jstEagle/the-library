## 15. What Can Be Carried

**Sebastian**

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when I finally said it aloud.

"They're coming tomorrow night. Maybe sooner."

Quentin didn't look up from the forme he was locking. The quoins clicked under his key — he'd learned that much well enough, though his lockups were still crooked in a way that would have made my father weep. "You've said so twice."

"Because you keep pretending I haven't."

He set the key down. In the candlelight his face had the gray look of paper left too long in the sun. Three days since the informer's mother had come to the shop wringing her apron, and three days of listening for boots on the cobbles, and it had worn us both to tissue.

"Then we stop pretending," he said.

We stood in the middle of the composing room, surrounded by everything my father had built and I had kept alive by becoming someone else: two iron presses, forty cases of type, the stone, the galley racks, the great wheel of the press-bench worn smooth by sixty years of palms. None of it could be carried. That was the arithmetic of flight, and I hated it the way I hated all simple sums.

But the plates could be carried. The ledger plates — two hundred names, causes, dates, cast in stereotype metal over four nights of work that had aged me a decade. Forty-one plates, each one a page of the true record, each one worth more than the building they'd burn down to reach.

"We split them," Quentin said. He'd been thinking it too; I could see the shape of the plan already assembled behind his eyes, the archivist sorting facts into columns. "Two cases. Half the plates each. If one of us is taken—"

"The other prints." My voice came out flat. "From half a book."

"From half a book," he agreed. "The names are set in order but the pages are independent. You taught me that. Any page can stand alone if the composition is clean. A reader who finds page twelve doesn't need page one to know that Marguerite Ostler died of a bayonet wound on the ninth, in her own doorway."

I hadn't taught him that. He'd learned it from watching me, which was better.

"And where do you take your half?"

"The river district. There are three jobbing printers on Tanner's Row who owe me favors from before — before I was nobody. One of them owes me more than a favor. He'll run the pages without asking what they are." He paused. "And you?"

"I don't leave." I said it before I'd decided to say it, and once it was out I couldn't take it back without it sounding like cowardice. "The neighborhood knows this shop. If the broadsides appear tomorrow morning stamped with Pemberton & Vane, people will believe them. If they appear from nowhere, they'll wonder whose hand made them, and wonder is a door the government walks through."

"You'd stay in the building when they come for it."

"I'd be gone before they arrived. Out through the paper cellar, into the Kessler's yard, and back again when the street clears." I'd walked that route in my head a hundred times since the informer's name surfaced. It was a good route. It was also a route designed by a woman who could not bear to abandon her father's presses to a soldier's crowbar, and we both knew it.

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. Then, instead of arguing — instead of the speech about risk and prudence I could see him drafting behind his teeth — he nodded once, slowly, like a man signing something.

"All right," he said. "Then we pack."

* * *

**Quentin**

There is a particular silence that falls between two people dividing up the evidence of a crime, and it is not an uncomfortable silence. It is the silence of a crew at the oars. Sebastian worked the near end of the stone and I worked the far end, lifting plates one by one from their bed of tissue paper, and neither of us spoke because there was nothing left to decide and speaking would only have been fear looking for a shape.

I counted as we went. Twenty-one plates into the case she would keep — the first half of the ledger, names beginning with A through L, the early deaths, the ones from the first week when everyone still believed the fighting would be brief. Twenty into mine. M through W, the later weeks, the worse weeks. I did not choose them and neither did she; the division fell where it fell, alphabetical, arbitrary, fair.

The cases themselves were old ones, compositors' traveling cases with hinged lids and false bottoms, bought secondhand years ago for delivering small jobs across town. They looked like what they were: furniture. A man carrying a type case is a tradesman going to a job. No soldier looks twice at a tradesman. This was either true or it was the last comfortable lie we told ourselves, and I have never since been able to determine which.

Sebastian wrapped the plates herself. She wouldn't let me touch the wrapping — said I packed like a man moving house rather than a man fleeing one, that the plates must lie flat, tissue between every face, no pressure on the raised letters or the whole page would print with bruises. She handled each one the way other people handle infants. Watching her, I understood something I should have understood weeks ago: that these were not documents to her. They were bodies. Two hundred bodies that the provisional government had declared unworthy of interment, and she was the sexton, and the plates were the coffins, and God help anyone who jostled them.

"There," she said at last, closing the lid of my case. Her palm stayed flat on it a moment longer than the task required. "Twenty pages. M through W. Don't read them on the way. Don't stop anywhere. And Quentin—" She looked up, and the candle caught the ink stains on her fingers, the ones she'd stopped hiding from me months ago. "If they stop you at a checkpoint, you drop the case. You hear me? You let it go. Metal sinks. Metal can be recast. Names can't be recast out of a hanging man."

"That's not how recasting works," I said, because my mouth does that when I'm terrified, "but I understand the sentiment."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. I'm being accurate, which is how I joke." I picked up the case. It weighed perhaps thirty pounds, dense and cold, the weight of the dead distributed evenly across M through W. "Sebastian."

"What."

"When you reprint your half — when the pages come off your press with the Vane mark on them — put my name in the colophon. Compositor, second bench. Whatever the formula is."

She frowned. "Why? If you're taken—"

"If I'm taken, then my name appears in exactly one document in the entire city, and it's the right one." I shrugged, and found the shrug cost more than I expected. "I spent eleven years struck from every roll in Aldenmoor. I'd like my erasure reversed somewhere that matters. Even if I'm not alive to see the proof pulled."

For a moment she was silent, and I thought I'd broken something — the plan, the evening, the strange hard thing we'd built between us out of type metal and secrets. Then she came around the stone and took my hand, pressed it flat against the lid of her own case, hers with A through L inside, and held it there.

"A through L," she said. "You hold M through W. Between us we've got the whole alphabet." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, just enough. "That's the whole truth, Quentin. Don't lose your half of it."

"I won't," I said. And because faith is not a thing you can prove, only a thing you can spend, I added: "Tanner's Row by midnight. Back by dawn with printed sheets. If I'm not back by dawn—"

"Then I finish it alone," she said. "That was always the plan. Everything else is hope."

* * *

**Sebastian**

He left by the paper cellar door, the case slung under his coat like any tradesman's burden, and I watched him until the fog of the alley took him, and then I stood in the dark of the cellar with my hand against the closed door and listened to the city breathe.

This is the part no ballad ever gets right. The ballads give you trumpets at the parting, some grand vow exchanged at a gate. What actually happens is smaller and worse: you watch a man become a shape, and then a shadow, and then nothing, and you realize you have just handed him twenty pages of the dead and half of your heart's remaining credit, and there is no ceremony in it at all. There is only the door, and the cold coming through the seams, and the long walk back upstairs to the empty composing room where the presses waited with their iron patience.

I did not cry. There wasn't time, and besides, tears blur type.

I lit a fresh candle and unlocked her case — mine, the A-through-L case — and checked the plates one final time, though I had checked them twice already and knew every name by heart. Abeline Croft. Bram Ostler. The child called Fitch, age unknown, cause listed in my hand as *struck by cavalry in the square*, because *trampled* felt too honest to set at midnight and *murdered* would have gotten the whole edition burned on principle. Each plate lay flat in its tissue. Each name slept.

Upstairs, I began preparing the press for morning. Ink slab wiped, rollers dressed, a fresh stock of dampened paper stacked within reach of the tympan. If Quentin made Tanner's Row, his half would begin printing by two o'clock, and mine would follow at dawn, and by noon two halves of one true book would be loose in a city that had been told there was nothing to record.

I was oiling the spindle when I heard it — not boots this time, not yet, but something almost worse: a knock. Soft. Three times. The rhythm the neighbors used.

I blew out the candle and crossed the dark shop, and put my eye to the crack in the door, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards.

It was the informer's mother. Standing alone on the step with a bundle under her arm and her face wet, and behind her, far up the street, lanterns — more than two, more than four — swinging toward the bridge.

"Open the door, child," she whispered through the wood. "They gave my boy back his bread, but they didn't tell him everything. They didn't tell him it was tonight."
