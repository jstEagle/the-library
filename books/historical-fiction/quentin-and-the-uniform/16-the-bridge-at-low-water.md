## 16. The Bridge at Low Water

The river was down to its winter bones.

Quentin had crossed the Alden Bridge a thousand times in the years he'd worked the census — first as a man with an official satchel and a stamp of authority, later, after the fall, as a man with nothing but his legs and his shame. He knew every plank of it, every patch where the ice gripped the pilings and groaned like something alive. He knew that at low water you could see the foundations, the great black stones the city preferred to forget it stood on.

He was thinking about stones now. About what holds a thing up when everything above it is burning or lying.

The case weighed forty pounds if it weighed an ounce, and it sat against his hip under a fishmonger's canvas, and every step from the shop to the bridgehead felt like walking with a bell strapped to his chest. Half the ledger. One hundred and four names, cast in metal, mirror-wise, waiting for whatever press Sebastian could borrow on the far side of the river. His half. His burden. His to carry across the one crossing in Aldenmoor where every soul passing was looked at twice.

*You take the river road,* she'd said, packing the second case with hands that didn't shake, which he knew cost her something enormous. *I'll go through the tannery district. We meet at Mother Corvane's laundry by dusk. If either of us doesn't come—*

*I'll come.*

*If either of us doesn't come, the other prints anyway. Promise me.*

He had promised. It was the easiest promise of his life and he had known, even as he made it, in some cold archival corner of himself that kept records no one asked for, that he might not keep it. Not because he would break faith. Because he had spent nine years learning exactly how men like Captain Hollis's soldiers saw the world: they saw a man carrying anything, and they wanted it.

He walked. The morning was gray and hard, breath smoking, the gutters rimed with frost that wouldn't last past noon. A dray horse stamped at the foot of the bridge. Somewhere behind him, toward the square, a drum was beating — the garrison changing watch, or mustering, or simply reminding the city it existed. For six weeks the drums had been the weather. You stopped hearing them until suddenly you couldn't hear anything else.

At the bridgehead there were four soldiers and a table.

That was new. Two days ago there had been two soldiers and no table, and they had waved through anyone who wasn't driving a cart. Now there was a table, a ledger open on it — a ledger, God help them all, everyone in this war was keeping ledgers — and a sergeant with a red face and a clipboard manner who was making people open their bundles one by one while a corporal with a young, unhappy face patted them down.

Quentin considered turning around.

Turning around was worse. A man who turned around at a checkpoint had a reason, and reasons invited pursuit, and pursuit meant they'd find the case on his hip instead of finding it politely, stupidly, in front of witnesses who might remember a different story. No. There was only forward. There had only ever been forward; every other direction was a door back into a life someone else had already written for him and then struck out.

He joined the line. A woman ahead of him clutched a basket of salted herring and answered questions about her husband, her parish, her intentions. The corporal wrote things down. The sergeant waved her through without looking at the herring, because herring were not dangerous, and because this was still, technically, peacetime — the provisional government's word for the interval between one kind of violence and another.

Then it was Quentin's turn.

"Name," said the sergeant.

"Ashe. Quentin Ashe."

Something happened in the sergeant's face. It was small — a flicker, a recalculation, the way a clerk's face changes when a number doesn't match its column. But Quentin had spent nine years reading registers, and he knew what a mismatch looked like on a human being, and he felt the morning tilt beneath his feet.

"Ashe." The sergeant said it again, tasting it. "Archivist. That Ashe."

"I'm a printer," Quentin said. "Pemberton and Vane, Cooper's Lane."

"Oh, I know where you work." The sergeant smiled without any warmth arriving in his eyes. "Everyone knows where you work. That's rather the point of you lately, isn't it? Open the bundle."

"It's fish."

"Open it."

There were perhaps eight people in the line behind him. A lamplighter off shift. Two women with a handcart of turnips. A boy of maybe twelve, wide-eyed, memorizing everything, the way boys did in this season — the whole city had become a school of children learning which adults stood up straight and which ones folded. Witnesses. Quentin thought: *witnesses*, and put his hand on the canvas, and understood that he had maybe ten seconds to decide what the next nine years of his life would contain.

He unwrapped the canvas.

The type case was just a type case to any eye but a printer's — a wooden tray of small compartments, and inside the compartments, rows of dull metal slugs. To the corporal it looked like junk. To the sergeant, whose face said he'd been briefed, it did not look like junk, because the briefing had told him to look for a print shop's property moving across the river, and here it was, and the sergeant knew it the way a dog knows a rat under floorboards.

"Well," the sergeant said softly. "What have we here."

"Type," Quentin said. "Broken sorts. I'm taking them to be recast at the foundry on Mill Street."

"At low water in February," the sergeant said, "you are personally carrying broken pieces of lead across the only bridge in the city, to a foundry." He lifted one slug out of its compartment and held it up to the gray light, squinting at the mirror-letter pressed into its face. "Recast."

"The foundry buys scrap by weight."

"And you're the man to carry it." The sergeant set the slug down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a card he intends to win with later. "You know, Archivist, we had a description. Of a man carrying type out of your shop. My captain is very interested in what the print shop on Cooper's Lane has been casting these past two nights. Very interested. He used the word *sedition*. He also used the word *treason*, but I got the impression he was showing off."

Behind Quentin, the line murmured. The boy was leaning to see.

Here is what Quentin Ashe knew about himself, knowledge bought at the price of nine years: he was a man who had been erased once, thoroughly, administratively, by people who never once raised their voices. Struck from the university rolls. Struck from the tax rolls. Struck so cleanly that when the purge came last month and burned the last file that proved he had ever matriculated, there was a black comedy in it — they were burning proof he'd existed, and he'd stood in the crowd watching his own name smoke, and part of him had thought, *well, at least they've confirmed I was real enough to burn.* He knew what erasure weighed. He knew it was not nothing; he knew it was everything, that a man without a record could be done anything to, because done-to required paperwork and he was paperless as a ghost.

And he knew — this was the thought that arrived now, calm as tide — that Sebastian Vane was not paperless. She had a father dead and mourned publicly, a trade licensed in her dead name's shadow, a mother buried in consecrated ground, a shop full of neighbors who loved her. She was erasable, but she was expensive to erase. It would take inquiry, scandal, a trial, a spectacle. Whereas he —

Whereas he was free.

It was the strangest gift his ruin had ever given him: he was the cheapest person in Aldenmoor to destroy. No family to interrogate. No register entry to corroborate. A disgraced archivist caught with seditious plates — the story wrote itself, needed no co-author, implicated no one. They would believe it because it flattered every suspicion they already held, and belief requires no evidence when the story is convenient.

He could give them a finished narrative. He was, after all, a professional.

"Those plates," Quentin said, "are mine."

The sergeant's eyebrows went up, pleased. "Are they."

"All of them. Cast, composed, and carried by me alone. Vane had nothing to do with it — Vane doesn't know I've been cutting type after hours. Check the shop if you like; you won't find the mate to this case. This is my work. My composition. My authorship." He heard his own voice steady itself into the tone he'd used for nine years over municipal registers: dry, exact, faintly bored, the voice of a man discussing inventory. "You want a seditionist. I'm giving you one. Why complicate it?"

"Why indeed." The sergeant turned a slug over between finger and thumb. "Whose names are in it?"

"Dead people," Quentin said. "People your historians have decided weren't killed by anybody. Read it. It's all in there. Dates, streets, causes. It's better documented than anything your captain's office has produced, and I say that as a professional."

Someone in the line laughed — a short, shocked bark, instantly smothered. The corporal's young face went pink. And Quentin felt it happen, the thing he'd gambled on: the story taking hold of its tellers. The sergeant had come looking for a conspiracy, and conspiracies require many men, and here was one man offering himself up complete, self-authored, no loose threads — and a soldier's deepest longing, Quentin knew from watching armies eat a city, is for simplicity. One traitor. One case of type. One arrest that closes the matter before supper.

"You understand," the sergeant said, and now the warmthlessness had spread to his whole voice, "that claiming sole authorship of treason is not a clever legal maneuver. There's no cleverness available to you. There's a rope, or there's the cells, depending on the captain's breakfast."

"I'm aware."

"And the Vane fellow walks away clean."

"Vane knows nothing. Vane is a craftsman who minds his bench. You've had men watching the lane for a month — tell me you've seen otherwise."

He hadn't, of course. Quentin knew the watchers' schedule better than they did; he'd mapped it weeks ago out of archivist's habit, noting times and rotations in the margin of a hymnal. The sergeant's silence confirmed it, and confirmation settled over the scene like frost.

The sergeant closed the case. Snapped the hasp. Nodded to the corporal, and the corporal — the young one, the unhappy one, the one who had a mother somewhere and a conscience not yet fully requisitioned by the state — took Quentin's arm, gently, apologetically, which was somehow worse than violence.

"Walk," the sergeant said.

They walked him across the bridge at low water, and Quentin looked down once, between the planks, at the black foundation stones standing in the shallows, holding up everything, seen by almost no one, and he thought: *there. That's the whole trade. That's what Sebastian cuts into metal every night. The stone doesn't sign its work.*

*But I can. Let me be the stone that signs.*

The drum was louder on the far side. The garrison yard opened off the quay like a mouth, and beyond it he could see the windows of the provost's building, and beyond that, in imagination, a cell, an interrogation, a captain's breakfast. Fear arrived then, finally, fashionably late, and Quentin let it sit beside him without argument. He was not brave. He had never been brave. What he was, was accurate: he had assessed the two available outcomes — Sebastian taken, or Quentin taken — and recorded the true figure, and acted on it, and that was all courage had ever been in any age: arithmetic performed honestly under pressure.

At the gate, the corporal hesitated. Close enough now that Quentin could see the chapped skin on his knuckles, the ink stain on his fingers — the corporal wrote reports, then, wrote and rewrote them, a little clerk-soldier, a mirror.

"They'll ask who taught you," the corporal said quietly. "To set type like that. The quality of the casting, sir, it's — it's masterwork. Whoever cut those letters spent years learning."

"Years," Quentin agreed.

"And you'll tell them it was all you."

"Every letter," Quentin said. "I cut them myself. I learned from no one living."

It was, he reflected, as they took the case from his hip and the door swung inward on darkness and the smell of straw and iron, the first lie he had ever entered into an official record. He found he minded it far less than any truth he'd ever filed.

The door shut. The bolt went home with a sound like a full stop.

Somewhere south of the river, in a tannery district gone quiet under curfew, a woman with sawdust in her hair and half a ledger's worth of names sewn into her coat lining was walking toward a rendezvous that would not fill, and Quentin Ashe pressed his forehead briefly against the cold wall and gave her, silently, the only thing left in his possession:

*Dusk, love. Count to a hundred. Then print anyway.*
