## 17. The Empty Bench

He was late.

That was the first thought, the small domestic thought, the thought that belonged to a woman who had spent three weeks sharing a workbench with a man and had learned his rhythms the way she knew her own pulse. Quentin Ashe was punctual the way only a former archivist could be punctual — not merely on time but early, arriving with the anxious energy of someone who believed lateness was a moral failing. He would stand outside the door of the cooper's yard by the river, checking the position of the church clock against his pocket watch, and when Sebastian arrived he would already have counted the barrels twice.

So when she came down Cooper's Lane at half past four in the morning, the plates wrapped in oilcloth under her coat, and the yard stood empty, she told herself: he is late. He is delayed. The checkpoints were worse since the garrison's second mutiny; soldiers were stopping everyone, turning out pockets, reading letters over people's shoulders for the pleasure of it. He would come. He always came.

She waited by the third barrel, the one with the loose stave that whistled in the wind, and she watched the water run low and brown over the pilings, and she did the thing she had trained herself never to do in a city gone mad, which was to imagine.

She imagined him stopped at a checkpoint. She imagined him talking his way through it — Quentin, who could talk, who had spent an hour once explaining to a drunken sergeant why the census rolls of 1841 were systematically unreliable, until the sergeant forgot what he'd been asking. She imagined him crossing the bridge with the first case of plates held against his chest like a child, like a relic, like the truth itself made leaden and portable. She imagined the rendezvous at the tannery on the far bank, and the printer there — old Hessler, who owed her father a debt from '39 — warming his press for the night run.

She imagined all of it because the alternative was to imagine nothing, and nothing had a shape too. Nothing had the shape of an empty yard at dawn, and a whistle in a barrel stave, and the light coming up gray over the river like a page nobody had set type for yet.

At five, the church clock struck and the wind changed and she smelled the tannery across the water, which meant the wind had turned from the east, which meant — she made herself note these things, craft things, bench things, because a compositor's mind that stops measuring goes to pieces — the fog would burn off by six and a woman standing alone in a cooper's yard with two hundred names' worth of stereotype plates under her coat would begin to be visible.

At quarter past five, a figure came down the lane, and Sebastian's heart lurched so hard she had to put a hand on the barrel.

It wasn't Quentin. It was Mrs. Corvane.

* * *

Widow Corvane lived two doors from the shop, in the house with the blue door that had been green before the smoke of the barricade week stained everything on their street the same tired color. Her son Tam had been arrested in the first wave of the grain riots and released nine days later, thinner and quieter, and everyone in the neighborhood understood without saying it what the quietness meant, because the provosts had ways of making men useful and bread was still a shilling a loaf.

Sebastian had known, in some chamber of herself she kept locked, that Tam had traded something. You didn't get out of the Marshalsea cells in nine days unless you carried something out with you that they wanted more than your body. And she had said nothing, done nothing, because what could you do — denounce a boy of nineteen to the same provosts? Turn him out into a winter street to starve honestly?

So when Widow Corvane came hurrying down the lane at a quarter past five in the morning, in her shawl, with her breath smoking, Sebastian knew before the woman spoke. Some knowledge arrives whole, the way a line of type arrives whole in the composing stick, and you cannot unsee its sense.

"Mr. Ashe." The widow's voice cracked on the name. "They took Mr. Ashe at the bridge."

The plates under Sebastian's coat weighed suddenly what a person weighs.

"When?" she said. Her voice came out flat. Bench-flat. She heard it and hated it.

"An hour past. Maybe more. I couldn't come sooner, I had to wait for the streets—" Mrs. Corvane wrung her hands inside the shawl. "Tam saw it. He was carrying fish up from the wharf and he saw them stop a man at the tollhouse and turn out his case, and he knew the case, he'd seen it in your shop, he—"

"He told you." Sebastian heard her own voice do something terrible then, go soft and cold as a slug plate. "He ran home and told his mother."

"He's my son," Mrs. Corvane said, and lifted her chin, and Sebastian saw that this small round grieving woman had spine enough to stand in a cold lane at dawn and take whatever the print shop's keeper meant to give her. "Whatever Tam did or didn't do, he's my son, and I'm not going to let you learn it from strangers. They say the man claimed the plates were his alone. Claimed he'd written it himself, stolen the names himself, that no one else in your shop had any part. He gave them a false name for the shop. Gave them mine, ma'am. Gave them Corvane, and a street that doesn't exist."

The lane was very quiet. Somewhere across the river the tannery hammers had started up, the sound Quentin had joked about, the sound he'd said was the city's own press beating out its slow editions of leather and blood.

"They'll know it's false," Sebastian said.

"Yes," said the widow simply. "And then they'll know where to come. That's why I'm here. That and—" her voice broke properly now, and she pressed her apron to her mouth, and when she took it away her eyes were fierce through the tears. "That and because I'm sorry. Because my son sold bread for your secrets and I raised him hungry and I don't ask you to forgive either of us. But I'm sorry, and I wanted you to hear it from me, and I wanted—" she steadied herself with visible effort, a woman setting her own type, letter by letter, "—I wanted to help. Whatever comes next. I wanted to help."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. Under the grief and the fury — and both were there, both rising in her like ink climbing a blotter — under all of it, she registered the absurd arithmetic of it: the informer's mother standing in the cold to warn the informed against, giving a false address to the provosts because her boy had given the true one, buying back with one act of courage what he'd sold for a loaf. The neighborhood was like that. It had always been like that. Everyone owed everyone; everyone betrayed everyone a little; and somehow the ledger balanced.

"Come inside," Sebastian said. "Off the street. If they're coming, we've work to do before they do."

* * *

The shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and the ghost of last night's candle, and it looked wrong to her the moment she stepped in, though it took her a moment to see why. Then she understood: Quentin's coat was gone from its peg. His apron, folded on the second stool, sat there like a shed skin. The stone where he'd practiced his spacing for hours — badly, doggedly, his big archivist's hands fumbling the em quads while he narrated to himself the history of every size — the stone sat empty.

She hung her own coat, unwrapped the plates, and stood looking at them a moment. Two hundred names. Two hundred lives reduced to cast metal, mirror-wise, unreadable to any eye that hadn't learned the trade. Only the press could read them. She'd built it that way on purpose, weeks ago, in another life.

Then she unlocked the door, went out into the gray morning, and did the thing she had promised herself she wouldn't do, the thing her father had warned her about in the only letter of advice he ever wrote her: *A shopkeeper who closes her shutters tells the street she has something to hide.*

She opened the shutters. All of them. Let the lamplight spill onto the wet cobbles, let the press show through the glass, black and patient as a pulpit. Let them see.

Word traveled the way it always did in Aldenmoor — not by post, not by crier, but by the mysterious arterial system of women with errands and men with opinions. By eight o'clock, Mrs. Okafor from the chandlery was at the door with a basket of candles and no explanation offered or needed. By nine, the cooper's apprentice appeared with a handcart of fresh-cut kindling, stacked it by the stove, and left without a word. By ten, Hessler's boy came across the bridge — *across the bridge*, past the checkpoint, past the soldiers, because boys could move and grown men with cases could not — with a scrap of paper in Hessler's cramped hand: *Press is warm. Send word when. We can run nights if you split the forme. —H.*

By noon there were eleven people in the shop, and Sebastian stood at the bench with her sleeves rolled and realized she was crying without having decided to, tears dropping onto the imposing stone among the sorts, and no one remarked on it, which was its own mercy.

They brought names.

That was the thing she hadn't foreseen — that she had thought she and Quentin between them had gathered the ledger whole, two hundred souls pried out of parish registers and grave diggers' memories and the mouths of the displaced — but the neighborhood knew better than either of them. They came to the counter one and two and three at a time, hatless in the cold, holding scraps.

A woman whose brother had drowned in the canal in October, pushed, though the coroner wrote *misadventure*. A dockhand with the name of a boy shot on Tanner's Row, twelve years old, carrying stones for the barricade. An old man who'd kept count all winter in the back of his prayer book, forty-one names in pencil, and he laid the book on her counter open like a man laying down a sword. A girl of maybe sixteen who didn't speak at all, just put down a strip of wallpaper with a name written on it in charcoal, curtseyed, and fled.

By evening the scraps covered the imposing stone, the counter, the floor of the back room. Two hundred and sixty-three names now. Sixty-three more than the ledger she and Quentin had built together. Sixty-three more dead the victors' historians would fold into the phrase *isolated disturbances* — and she thought of that phrase, which she shouldn't have known, which she knew because Quentin had once recited it with bitter mimicry, describing the language of official reports, and her throat closed.

Quentin was in a cell somewhere, and they were already writing him into a phrase.

"Ma'am." Mrs. Corvane stood at the counter, sorting scraps into piles with the methodical competence of a woman who had washed for other households for thirty years. "What do you want done with these?"

Sebastian looked around the shop. The lamplight, the neighbors, the scraps of paper like fallen leaves, the press waiting in the corner with its iron arm raised — patient, hungry, faithful. Her father's press. Her press. Their press, now, though none of them had signed anything.

She wiped her eyes with her wrist and picked up her composing stick.

"We set them," she said. "All of them. Tonight. Every name, every date, every cause, exactly as they were given to us — we change nothing, we soften nothing, we omit nothing. Hessler runs half the forme across the river. I run the other half here, and if the soldiers come through that door, well." She heard her father's cadence come into her voice, or maybe it had been there all along, or maybe it was Quentin's, borrowed and kept. "Then the type will tell them what we think of them, mirror-wise, and good luck to any censor trying to read it."

A murmur went around the room, and Mrs. Corvane bowed her head over her piles of paper and began, in her careful washday hand, to copy the scraps onto clean sheets, one name to a line, the way a compositor would want them.

Sebastian turned to the case. Her fingers found the compartments by memory, by love, by twenty years of this exact motion, and she began to set the first name of the new dead.

The bench beside her was empty. It would stay empty. But it was not, she found, an empty bench — it was surrounded, crowded, ringed with neighbors and lamplight and sixty-three new names, and the shop that had been hers alone and then theirs and then hers again in grief was fuller tonight than it had ever been.

They had taken Quentin. They had not taken the ledger.

And far off across the dark city, in a cell she could not picture and refused to imagine, she set the first letter of the first new name — and swore, in the silence where his voice should have been, that whatever bargain they offered him, whatever they threatened or promised, when the presses ran tomorrow night, ten thousand copies would carry his sacrifice and her signature both.

She just had to decide what name to sign.

Her hand hovered over the case. Outside, boots rang on the cobbles — a patrol, passing, fading. Not yet. But soon.

Sebastian Vane reached into the type case, and began to compose.
