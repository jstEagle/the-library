## 19. Ten Thousand Copies

The message came by way of a baker's boy, folded inside the paper wrapper of two loaves that neither of us had ordered.

I knew the hand before I unfolded it. Not Quentin's — he was beyond our reach now — but his sister-in-law's, a woman I'd met once at a wedding I attended under my father's name, back when attending weddings as anyone at all still felt possible. The note said only: *Marrow Lane, cellar, midnight. Bring your half. Bring ink. Bring every name you have.*

I read it twice, then held it over the lamp flame and watched it curl to nothing. Somewhere across the district, nine other notes were burning in nine other hands. That was the whole design. No order could be traced because no order existed. There was only a time, a place, and the assumption — which turned out, for once in this city's miserable winter, to be correct — that everyone who mattered would understand.

* * *

The cellar beneath the Marrow Lane bakery had been dug to store coal and now stored hope instead, which took up less room but weighed more. By midnight there were eleven of us pressed among the sacks and the low black beams: three compositors from the Gazette's night shift who had walked out on their employer without ceremony; a widow named Frau Hessler who ran a card press no bigger than a bread box; two brothers whose father had printed hymnals until the provisional government decided hymnals were insufficiently patriotic; a boy of maybe sixteen with ink already under his nails, which meant he'd been apprenticed somewhere and lied about leaving; and old Tomas Brandt, who had set type for forty years and lost three fingers to a press accident and could still out-compose any man in Aldenmoor one-handed.

Nobody spoke above a murmur. Somebody had brought a lantern with a shuttered hood, and the light moved around the room like something alive and cautious.

I opened the case I'd carried through four checkpoints' worth of streets strapped beneath my coat, against my ribs like a second heartbeat. The type sat in its galley exactly as I'd set it in the shop on those last nights — mirror-wise, reversed, unreadable to any eye that hadn't spent its life learning to read backwards. Two hundred names. Causes. Dates. Streets. The true ledger of what the victors were calling isolated disturbances.

Frau Hessler lifted her lamp close to the forme and read it anyway. She'd been setting type since before I was born.

"Well," she said. "It's clean work."

Coming from her, it was a knighthood. I heard my own breath go out of me.

"Here is how it must be," said one of the Gazette men — Kessler, his name was, though I didn't learn that until later. He spoke quietly and fast, a compositor's economy, no wasted letters. "No single press runs the whole sheet. If the garrison seizes one shop, they seize nothing. Each of us takes a portion. A quarter page, a third — however we can divide it. We compose our portions tonight, duplicate where we can, and distribute matrices so no two shops hold the same complete thing. At dawn tomorrow — not today, tomorrow — every press in the district pulls copies simultaneously. Ten thousand sheets if the gods are kind. More if they're generous."

"And if they come before dawn?" the boy asked.

Kessler looked at him. "Then some of us stop printing and others don't. That is the entire strategy. That is why there are eleven of us."

* * *

We worked until the coal dust in the air turned grey with the first suggestion of morning and then worked past it. I won't pretend I remember all of it. What I remember is the sound — eleven people setting type in a buried room, the small music of it, sort clicking into sort, the mallet taps on the planer, someone humming an old grain-riot song under his breath and someone else telling him to hush and no one meaning it. What I remember is passing a composing stick down the line to the boy when his hands started shaking, and taking over his portion without comment, and him watching me work with an expression I recognized from the inside: the look of someone discovering that a craft can be a refuge, that competence is a kind of armor you forge yourself, link by link, at a bench, alone.

What I remember most is the division.

We broke the ledger into eleven parts along natural seams — the deaths of the first week, the second, the disappeared, the displaced, the burned streets, each section ending mid-sentence so that no single fragment told a whole story. Each printer memorized their portion's first and last lines and then destroyed nothing, because there was nothing to destroy; each of us carried only a piece, and pieces are not evidence. Pieces are grief. You cannot arrest a man for carrying grief.

But the final line — the colophon, the signature line at the foot of the last page — that I kept myself.

Kessler noticed, near the end. He saw me setting a short line apart from my portion, wrapped in tissue, tucked into my breast pocket rather than the case. He raised an eyebrow, which in the lamplight looked like a whole conversation.

"The closing line should be set by whoever finishes last," I said. "That's tradition."

"It isn't tradition," he said. "You've invented it just now."

"Yes," I said. "That's how traditions start."

He almost smiled. Then he went back to his sorts, and let me have my invention.

Because here is what I had decided, somewhere between the checkpoint streets and the coal-dark cellar, somewhere between Quentin's empty chair and the informer's mother weeping on my doorstep: I was done being unrecordable. My father had cut type under his own name and died for opinions printed plainly. I had spent seven years becoming a ghost in my own shop, signing *S. Vane* to work while neighbors praised the mysterious young master, letting them believe whatever shape the shadow suggested. It had kept me alive. It had also kept me small.

Quentin Ashe had given up his name twice — once to disgrace he hadn't earned, once to soldiers he hadn't deserved — and both times to protect something true. The least I owed him was the opposite trade. If the ledger was going out into ten thousand hands, then the hand that set it would stand behind it, spelled out, letter by letter, in type my own father might have cut.

* * *

Dawn came the next day the way artillery comes: everywhere at once.

I will never know the full choreography of it, and that was the point. But I know my part. I know that Frau Hessler pulled her little card press in a laundry behind Cooper's Row and hung the wet sheets inside shirt collars on washing lines, so that by eight o'clock the neighborhood was literally wearing the names of its dead. I know the hymnal brothers ran off six hundred copies in a chapel basement and passed them out through the confessional, priest and printer collaborating for the first time in either of their careers. I know the Gazette deserters used their own former workplace's presses after hours, which Kessler described afterward as the single most satisfying act of his professional life.

And I know that at the corner of Pemberton & Vane's own street — my street, my father's street — the baker's boy stood on a barrel at first light and nailed the finished broadside to the notice post with four strokes of a hammer, one for each corner, loud enough to wake the lane.

I had gone there myself before light. I couldn't help it. I stood in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery across the way with my collar up and my heart going like a drum, and I watched my own work appear on the wall of the world.

The sheet was simple. Black ink on cheap stock, because beauty is expensive and urgency is free. At the top, in the face my father had cut thirty years ago and I had maintained ever since — the Vane face, though no one alive but me called it that — the title: **A TRUE LEDGER OF THE DEAD AND DISPLACED OF ALDENMOOR, SET BY THOSE WHO KNEW THEM.** Below it, column after column. Names. Ages, where anyone knew them. Streets. How they died, or how they vanished, in language too plain to be spun and too specific to be denied. Two hundred entries, give or take the ones that arrived too late for the formes, the ones that would come later, the ones that always come later.

And at the bottom, the final line. The line I had set apart and carried against my chest through the dark:

*Composed and printed by Sebastian Vane, at the sign of the Pemberton & Vane Press.*

My name. Not S. Vane. Not my father's name standing in for mine. Sebastian. The name my mother whispered over my cradle and my father taught me to set at age six, letter by letter, backwards, until the mirror of it became the truth of it.

The boy hammered the fourth nail and stepped down, and people were already gathering — the baker first, naturally, then the chandler's wife, then a knot of dockworkers coming off night shift who stopped as one body and began to read. I watched a woman I did not know reach up and touch one of the names, just touch it, the way you touch a gravestone. I watched an old man read aloud, slowly, moving his finger down the columns, and other people fall silent to hear him, and someone begin to copy the sheet onto paper of their own, by hand, letter by letter, because one copy is never enough and everyone understands that now.

By noon, the government's criers were calling the broadside a forgery. By evening, there were broadsides denouncing the broadside, printed on official presses with official eagles in the corners. And by the following morning there were so many copies of the true ledger on so many doors, in so many windows, sewn into so many coats, recited in so many markets, that the official denials had begun to look like what they were: the forgeries.

You cannot arrest ten thousand hands. You cannot burn a memory that has been copied into a thousand different houses, a thousand different mouths. Kessler said it best, that night in the cellar, wiping ink from his fingers: "They control the archives. We control the multiplication table."

* * *

On the third day, a girl came to the ruined door of my shop — the door still hanging crooked where the soldiers had been — carrying a scrap of paper. On it, in a child's careful hand, was another name. Her brother's. He had died in the second week, in a street I knew, and no record of him existed anywhere official. She had seen the ledger. She had counted the names. His wasn't there.

"He should be in it," she said. "Can you put him in?"

I looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, through the broken window, the street was full of people walking past my shop with new purpose, and more than one of them nodded to me — to me, standing in the doorway in my dead father's apron, unhidden, unnamed by anyone but myself.

"We'll run a second edition," I said. "There will be corrections. There will always be corrections. That's what makes it a true record — it can admit it was incomplete."

She nodded solemnly, the way children accept the terms of adults, and left the scrap in my palm like a coin.

I closed the door as much as it would close and turned to the bench. The shop was gutted, the big press smashed, the cases overturned, sorts scattered across the floorboards like a spilled alphabet, like the city itself shaken out and needing to be gathered again letter by letter. But they had missed the small case. They had missed the drawer under the floor where my father hid the pamphlets, and in that drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, was enough type to begin again.

I knelt and lifted it out, and my knees hit the floorboards, and I stayed there a moment with the case in my lap, saying nothing to anyone, because there was no one there and because everything that needed saying was already in type somewhere, on a thousand walls, signed.

Then I heard footsteps on the broken glass outside. Deliberate ones. Unhurried. And a voice I knew — thin, worn, unmistakable, the voice of a man who had once torn a flag from my file because he recognized in me the erasure written on himself — calling through the ruined doorway:

"Sebastian? The guard post on the bridge stands empty. They released everyone. I walked out of that building and past the checkpoint and nobody stopped me, and the first thing I wanted—" a pause, a breath, "—the first thing I wanted was to see whether my name was in your ledger."

I stood up, holding the case, ink under my fingernails, my own name drying on ten thousand walls.

"It's there," I called back. "Come in and read it."
