## 22. The First Proof

The graver sings against the steel.

That's the word for it — not scrapes, not bites, though I've used both in my time when the metal fought me. Sings. A clean note under the wrist, felt more than heard, the way a tuning fork hums through bone. I've been cutting this punch for eleven days and the letter is nearly born: an *S*, the first of the alphabet, because you always cut the capital *S* first. My father taught me that. *The S tells you everything,* he said, turning his own first punch in the lamplight like a jeweler with a stone. *If your S is honest, the rest will follow.*

His S was honest. Every letter he ever cut was honest, which is why half of them were confiscated and the man himself was watched for the last decade of his life by clerks who couldn't set a line of type between them.

My S is honest too. It has to be. It carries two names now.

* * *

Spring came late to Aldenmoor that year, the way spring does after a winter when a city bleeds. The river ran high with melt off the hills and something else besides — the garrison had dumped crates of burned documents at the bend below the tannery, and for a week in March the water carried charred paper past the docks like black moths drowning. Nobody fished them out. Everybody knew what they were. The victors' historians had done their work, and the river did its small counter-work, carrying the ash out to sea where no one could claim it.

The shop reopened on the fourth of April. Not by plan. By pressure — the kind that accumulates in a neighborhood the way water accumulates behind a door.

They started coming before we'd even swept the floor. The soldiers who looted us in February had taken the press's iron frame apart looking for the plates, thrown the cases across the room, pissed on the floorboards out of what I can only assume was professional principle. What they didn't take — what they evidently couldn't be bothered to weigh — was the type itself. Eleven hundredweight of lead alloy scattered through the wreckage like a hoard nobody recognized as treasure. Soldiers want gold. They have never once wanted letters.

So on the fourth of April, while Quentin and I were still hauling the frame upright with block and tackle borrowed from the ropewalk, Mrs. Calloway from the chandlery appeared in the doorway with her daughters and said, "We've come to sort," as if she'd been invited to a quilting. By noon there were nine people in the shop sorting sorts. By evening the cases stood full again, every letter home, and old Ferro — who'd lost two sons in the street fighting and whose name I had set myself, mirror-wise, in the ledger — pressed a coin into my hand and refused to take it back until I used it to buy ink.

"We'll need a new sign," Quentin said that night. We were sitting on the loading step with a bottle of plum brandy Ferro had also left, watching the lamplighter work up Copper Lane. "Pemberton & Vane burned. Well — half-burned. The *Pemberton* survived; the *Vane* got scorched."

"Symbolic," I said.

"Everything's symbolic now. That's the trouble." He turned the bottle in his hands. "I thought perhaps just Vane."

"You're mad. You're the one who pulled the press through the censor's visit. You took the checkpoint so I could run. If anyone's name goes over that door alone—"

"It isn't about deserving." He said it mildly, the way he says everything, the archivist's patience worn into him like a groove. "It's about accuracy. Pemberton & Vane was your father's shop. This one should be yours."

I looked at him a long moment. Six months ago this man had stood in my shop with a census ledger and one question too many, and I had measured the distance to the back door in my head. Now he was arguing himself out of top billing.

"And yet," I said, "the records show two compositors on the true ledger. Two names. Ten thousand copies, each signed twice." I held out my hand for the bottle. "Ashe & Vane. Alphabetical. Don't argue with the alphabet, Quentin; it always wins."

He laughed — really laughed, the unguarded sound I'd only heard a handful of times, mostly at night over the composing stone when the work went well and neither of us wanted to admit how tired we were. Then he said, quietly, "Nobody's called me Ashe in years. Before you. It was always the disgraced one, or the archivist, or nothing at all."

"Well," I said, "get used to it. It's going to be on the door, the colophon, the tax rolls, and God help us, eventually the census."

"I'll lie to the census taker," he said solemnly. "I know exactly which questions not to answer."

* * *

The sign went up a week later, painted by Ferro's youngest, who had a steady hand and a future in lettering if printing ever failed us both: **ASHE & VANE, PRINTERS** — and beneath, smaller, in the old style my father favored, *Compositors · Type-founders · Booksellers*. Under that, because Ferro insisted and because I discovered I wanted it too: *Established 1848.* Let the official record say whatever it liked about when the shop began. From that day forward, the shop dated itself from the ledger. From the dead. It seemed right that a business built on memory should count its age the way a person does — from the moment it became undeniable.

Business, I'm pleased to report, was immediate and impossible. Everyone in Aldenmoor needed something printed, and everyone wanted it printed by *us* specifically, which meant wedding invitations sat in the queue behind memorial cards and land claims behind broadside ballads about the uprising — some accurate, some gloriously not. We printed all of it. That was part of the point. A press that prints only truth starves; a press that prints everything can afford to print truth when it matters. My father understood that. It took me a bankruptcy scare and a revolution to understand it myself.

But underneath the trade work — the bills and ballads, the chandler's labels and the schoolmaster's primers — the real book was growing. It had been growing since February, in the evenings, after the last customer left. Quentin wrote; I corrected; we argued; he rewrote. *A True Account of the Aldenmoor Rising,* with the whole ledger bound in as an appendix: two hundred and six names, ages, trades, causes of death, dates, streets. Not *isolated disturbances.* Not *regrettable excesses.* Names. All of them, including the ones the provisional government's official history had folded into a single sentence about "collateral disorder in the riverside districts."

Collateral disorder. Quentin read that phrase aloud when the government's volume finally appeared in April, bound in respectable cloth, priced beyond any laborer's purse. He read it and then he set the phrase in twenty-four-point type and printed fifty copies of just those two words and nailed them to the door of the archive with a nail and his own bare thumb, and then walked away before anyone could arrest him for it. By morning someone had written beneath it in chalk: *WE WERE NOT DISORDER. WE HAD NAMES.*

That chalk line went into our book too. On the title page of the appendix, reproduced in facsimile. History is made of stranger things than ink.

* * *

Which brings me back to the graver, and the S, and the eleven days.

A book needs a face. Every printer knows it. The type in which a story is set is the first thing a reader touches before touching a single word — the shoulders of the letters, the weight of the strokes, the color of the page. Our book deserved better than the tired Caslon faces we'd inherited or the government stock we'd bought at auction from a bankrupted loyalist firm. Our book needed its own letter.

I'd been drawing it since March, on the backs of spoiled proofs, in the margins of invoices. A text face, sturdy but not severe. Serifs with a slight flare, like something grown rather than machined. An *a* with a generous bowl, because generosity belonged in it. A *g* with an open lower loop, because closed loops were for ledgers and this was not a ledger, whatever the appendix contained. And throughout — I couldn't help it, my father's training ran too deep — a faint asymmetry, a human unevenness, the mark of a hand rather than a matrix machine. Perfect type lies about its origins. Ours would tell the truth: cut by a person, in a city, in a specific year, for a specific purpose.

Quentin watched me work the first week without comment. In the second week he started bringing me things unprompted — coffee gone lukewarm, the good lamp moved closer, his coat draped over my shoulders when the April damp came through the walls. In the third week he asked, carefully, the way you ask about a wound:

"What are you going to call it?"

I hadn't let myself think the name yet. Naming is a commitment; naming is a signature. But he asked, and the shop was empty, and the S lay finished on the bench between us catching the lamplight like a small silver moon.

"The Vane face," I said.

He nodded slowly. "Not Pemberton?"

"My father cut the Pemberton face forty years ago and spent the rest of his life watching other men get credit for his pamphlets. He'd have cut this one himself if—" I stopped. Started again. "The Vane name spent thirty years hiding. Mine spent five. I'm done hiding either of them. The face goes out under Vane. Both of us, if you count that I wouldn't have dared without you."

"I count," Quentin said. "For the record, I count everything."

"That," I told him, "is what I'm afraid of."

* * *

By the second week of May, I had cut the lowercase. Twenty-six capitals, twenty-six minuscules, the numerals, the points, the ampersand — the ampersand gave me three days of grief and one midnight of triumph, its spine curling like the river itself around the bend below the tannery. Quentin learned to cast matrices beside me, badly at first and then adequately and then, one afternoon, beautifully, pulling a casting that made me go quiet and hand it back to him without a word because some praise doesn't fit in speech.

On the fourteenth of May, the foundry delivered the first full case of Vane type. Real cast type, hundreds of pounds of it, every letter descended from the punches I'd cut at this same bench where my father had bent over his own steel a lifetime ago. I stood with my hands flat on the case lid for a long minute before opening it. Quentin, wisely, went to make tea and left me alone with it.

When I lifted the lid, the smell rose up — lead and tin and antimony, the smell of my entire childhood — and underneath it, faintly, the oil from the punches, my oil, this year's linseed. Old smell and new smell in the same breath. I don't cry easily. I cried a little then, standing over a box of letters, and I'm not ashamed to put it in the record, because this book is nothing if not the record.

* * *

We composed the first page that night.

Title page. Of course the title page — you pull a proof of the title page first; it's tradition, it's superstition, it's the page every reader meets. Quentin set the running head while I set the imprint, and we worked without talking, the way you do when the rhythm is right, the stick passing, the case singing, two pairs of hands moving around one composing stone like partners in a dance neither of us had been taught.

The forme locked up at half past eleven. I carried it to the proof press myself — the little galley press we'd rescued from the cellar, the one that survived everything, the one that had pulled proofs of the ledger's final pages in those last hours before the soldiers came. I inked the ball. I laid the dampened proof sheet. I pulled the bar.

And there it was.

* *A True Account of the Aldenmoor Rising* — set in Vane. *Being a complete record of the dead, the displaced, and the deeds of the riverside districts, with the names of two hundred and six persons, faithfully collected.* Below that, the imprint, and I made myself read it twice because reading it was the point of the entire exercise:

*Printed by Ashe & Vane, Copper Lane, Aldenmoor. The types cut by Sebastian Vane, compositor, in the shop of her father, Gideon Pemberton Vane. MDCCCXLIX.*

My name. Not Sebastian-the-nephew, not the young man everyone assumed ran the shop for some absent uncle. Sebastian Vane, compositor, daughter of the house, cutter of the type, keeper of the ledger. Set in letters I had made with my own hands, in a face that would outlive every clerk who ever tried to strike me from a roll. When this book reached the university libraries — and it would; Quentin had already corresponded with three of them, and their librarians, hungry for anything the government histories lacked, had promised shelf space sight unseen — readers would run their thumbs over pages printed from my letters and never know, and always know, that a woman had made them.

"Pull another," Quentin said softly behind me. "Before the ink dries. One for the wall."

One for the wall. Every print shop has its wall — the proofs that matter, pinned up where the light hits them. My father's wall had held his first Pemberton specimen and a child's drawing of a press that I now understood he'd kept because I'd made it. Our wall had held nothing yet. There'd been no time for anything so gentle as pride.

I pulled the second proof and Quentin pinned it up while I watched, and then he stepped back and we looked at it together, the way you look at a thing you made that is also, somehow, a thing that was always waiting to exist.

"There's a passage on page ninety-one," he said at last, in the tone he used when approaching the archive shelves of his own feelings. "Where I write about the census. About the household rolls, and the discrepancies, and the question I shouldn't have asked." He paused. "I've rewritten it eleven times. In ten versions I protect you. In one version I protect myself. In none of them does it come out right."

"Read me the eleventh."

He read it. It was good — honest, spare, admitting what he'd seen and what he'd chosen and what it cost him to choose it. But the last line circled its own tail, apologetic, and I heard my father's voice in my head saying *if your S is honest, the rest will follow.*

"It ends wrong," I said. "You keep ending it with doubt. *Whether I did right, the reader may judge.* No. Strike that. End it with what happened. *I tore the flag from her file and asked for work at the press. It was the only entry I ever made that improved the record.*"

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed, once, and took out his pencil, and wrote it down exactly as I'd said it.

"That's the dedication," he said. "Page one. Before everything. *To the only entry that improved the record.*"

"That's absurd sentimentality," I said.

"Yes," he agreed. "Keep it anyway."

* * *

We finished the last corrections in June. The edition would be five hundred copies — modest, expensive per copy, printed on the rebuilt press and the loaned one from the Wesleyan chapel — and already there were subscribers enough to cover it twice over, from the chandlery to the dockworkers' fund to, I am told, a certain librarian at the university who paid double under a false name and was fooling no one.

On the last evening of composition, after the final forme was locked and queued, after Quentin had gone up the outside stair to the room he'd rented above the chandlery — he still kept his own rooms; some habits of independence die hard, and I confess I liked knowing the door was there if either of us needed it — I stayed alone in the shop.

Lamplight. The smell of ink and lead. My father's bench, my punches, his portrait on the wall that Ferro's daughter had reframed for us after the looting. Outside, Copper Lane was quiet the way it was quiet now — not the held-breath quiet of the occupation but the ordinary quiet of a city learning to sleep without counting soldiers. Somewhere a dog, somewhere the river, somewhere Mrs. Calloway scolding a son who'd stayed out past the lamps. The neighborhood, alive. Recorded. Unownable.

I opened the case drawer where I keep the things that matter: my mother's thimble. The coin Ferro forced on me. The chalk-written words from the archive door, copied onto paper before rain took them. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, the original flag Quentin tore from my household file, kept all these months like a trophy or a scar. *Discrepancy noted. Investigation pending.* Pending forever now. I touched it once and closed the drawer.

Then I sat down at the bench, took up the graver, and began the next punch.

Because that's the thing no one tells you about endings: in a print shop, there aren't any. The ledger closes and the account opens. The book is bound and the next manuscript arrives. The face is cut and someone, someday, will cut a better one, and thank God for it, because a craft that stops is a craft that died. The soldiers breached the door and the truth was already in a thousand hands, and in the morning we swept the floor, and in the summer we printed a book, and tomorrow — tomorrow there is a wedding invitation due, and a memorial card for old Marta Hollis who died peaceful in her bed at eighty-one, thank heaven, one death in this district that needs no explanation — and after that, Quentin says, a newspaper. *The Aldenmoor Record,* he wants to call it. Weekly. Independent. "Someone has to watch the watchers," he said, "and I have references."

So I cut. The graver sang, and the letter grew under my hands, and the lamp burned, and the press waited the way presses do — patiently, hungrily, ready for whatever truth came next down Copper Lane asking to be set in type.

The *T* went into the case at dawn. The rest of the alphabet would follow.

It always does.
