## 1. The Question That Wasn't on the Form

The census came to Vessany's border towns the way frost came to the harbor—early, quietly, and with the certainty that something green would not survive it. Isandro had lived through eleven of them in Kellin's Reach, and she had learned their grammar: the knock at midmorning, never early enough to catch a household undressed, never late enough to seem an imposition; the clerk's ink-blackened fingers; the form itself, cream-colored and smelling faintly of the government press in Coravel, four hundred miles away and a lifetime from mattering.

She answered the door on the twelfth census morning with her hands still dusted from the mapmaker's pigments, because a widowed mapmaker's daughter should have pigment on her hands. It was part of the costume, like the widow's black she no longer wore but kept folded in the cedar chest, like the limp Ovid had taught her to affect on census days only—a small dishonesty, worn briefly, then set aside like festival clothes.

"You're early this year," she said.

The man on her step was not what the republic sent to border towns. The census takers of previous years had been soft men near retirement, or nervous boys counting days until transfer. This one stood as though the step were a stage he'd been asked to share, neither large nor small, gray-eyed, perhaps forty, with the particular stillness of someone who had trained himself out of fidgeting. His coat was plain wool, government-issue by the cut, but his boots were better than government-issue, and they were clean. In Kellin's Reach, where the mud held everything the river gave it, clean boots meant a man who watched where he stepped.

"Rafael," he said, producing the ledger with a small bow. "I'm taking the eastern quarter this year. The usual clerk has influenza."

"Influenza," Isandro repeated, in the tone border people used for official explanations: acknowledging receipt, conceding nothing.

"He sends regrets." Rafael smiled—it reached his eyes, which she noted, and which she distrusted for exactly that reason; most liars' smiles stopped at the mouth. "May I come in? The form requires a household inventory, and the light is better indoors."

It was a reasonable request, reasonably made. That was the first wrong thing. Census men did not need good light. They needed numbers, names, occupations, livestock if you had it, and a signature at the bottom. She had watched them work through eleven springs, standing in doorways with their coats buttoned against the river damp, wanting nothing so much as to be finished and gone before noon.

She stepped aside anyway. Refusal was a color you did not wear twice.

* * *

The house performed itself around him while he wrote.

Ovid was at the long table in the back room, cataloguing. He worked without looking at anything, which was the point—the hymnals of the old congregations had been contraband for nine years, and Ovid had spent those nine years learning to read them by touch alone, his fingers walking the spines like a blind man's though his eyes were fine. When strangers came, his hands went still and became merely a deaf archivist's idle hands, resting on a stack of legitimate ledgers. Today they rested on shipping manifests, Sef's work, innocently blank.

Sef himself had vanished into the loft above the map room, which was where nineteen-year-olds went when authority crossed the threshold, and where Isandro let him go, because a runaway heir who presented himself voluntarily was worth ten times more dangerous than a boy hiding in a loft. Marta sat by the stove with her knitting, ancient and half-present, muttering to the fire in the dialect of the old coast villages that no census clerk could follow even if he tried.

Rafael moved through the house the way water moves through a hull—finding every low place without hurrying toward any of them. He paused at the map room door. Isandro watched him take in the charts pinned to the walls, the half-finished survey of the river delta her father's name was still attached to, the smell of linseed and iron-gall ink.

"Your father did fine work," he said. "The delta survey. I've seen it cited."

"My father is dead six years," Isandro said. "The citations outlive us all."

"They do." He made a small mark in his ledger—not writing an answer, she noticed. Writing a note. Census men did not take notes about maps.

He recorded the household in the order the form demanded. Hartwell, Isandro—mapmaker's daughter, widowed status confirmed, age entered as twenty-nine, which flattered her by two years and insulted her by none. Ovid Lenz, archivist, deaf, sixty-one, resident eleven years. Marta Voss, no occupation listed, eighty-three, born in the republic's first spring—which made her, technically, the same age as the country, a coincidence Isandro had heard Marta deny with great bitterness on more than one anniversary. Sef was entered last, as Sef Arden, apprentice, ward, seventeen—shaved two years off like a beard, because minors drew less scrutiny than young men of military age, and because the true surname would have drawn worse than scrutiny.

Rafael wrote all of it down in a hand that was startlingly beautiful, each letter formed with the care of a man who had been taught penmanship by someone who believed it mattered. Then he did not close the ledger.

He looked up instead, and the gray eyes settled on her with a weight that had nothing administrative in it.

"One question that isn't on the form," he said. "If you'll indulge me. The department allows some latitude at the clerk's discretion—for accuracy."

"The department is generous," Isandro said.

"It asks whether anyone in this household"—and here his voice did not change at all, which she would think about later, the way you think about a floorboard that didn't creak—"has ever held a commission. Military, civil, diplomatic. Of the republic, or of any state that preceded it. Anyone at all. Yourself included."

Outside, the river made its long sound against the pilings. Somewhere down the street a cart complained over cobbles. Marta's needles clicked twice and went quiet, and Isandro understood that Marta had heard, in whatever way Marta heard everything, and was listening through the wall with her whole knitted body.

"No," Isandro said.

She let the word stand alone for a moment, unadorned. Then she added the texture that lies require to pass as truth—the small human friction, the detail volunteered rather than extracted.

"My father held a harbor survey commission, years ago. Before my mother died. But that ended when the funding did, and he used to say the republic paid him in paper and apologized in the same ink." She smiled, a daughter's fond, tired smile. "No commissions since. We're mapmakers and archivists and old women here, sir. We hold opinions, not offices."

Rafael laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised out of him—and something in Isandro's chest went cold while her face stayed warm, because the laugh was genuine and that meant the question had been genuine too, and genuine questions from census clerks were how lists got started.

"Opinions, not offices," he repeated, closing the ledger at last. "That should be the town motto." He signed the page, blotted it, and rose. At the door he paused, coat already buttoned, and said—lightly, almost as an afterthought:

"The delta survey. The final plate was never published. There's a blank space in the atlas where it should be." His eyes touched hers, courteous, unreadable. "I've always wondered what filled it."

And he was gone, his clean boots finding the clean stones down the walk, leaving behind the faint government-press smell of the ledger and a silence that had changed shape.

* * *

Isandro waited until the lamplighting hour to go up to her room.

This was discipline, not calm. The house needed its evening performed—Ovid's tea, Sef summoned down from the loft and fed, Marta's fire banked, the ordinary machinery of a family that had nothing to hide running audibly through its gears. Sef asked, once, with his eyes, *what did he want*, and Isandro answered in the smallest gesture, two fingers flat, pressing down: *later*. Ovid caught her wrist in passing and pressed a question into her palm in their private sign—*the new one, what does he count?*—and she pressed back: *us.* She felt Ovid's hand go very still inside hers, and then release, and neither of them looked at the other, because the first rule of their household had always been that fear was carried separately so that no one person ever held all of it.

Only when the house was dark did she lock her door and lift her shirt.

The cipher sat against her left ribs, where it had sat for fifteen years: a seal the size of a coin, grown into her, implanted in a hospital corridor that smelled of vinegar and iodine, by a surgeon who never met her eyes. They had told her everything about it in those hours before the oath. How it slept. How it could not be removed without killing the carrier. How it would keep its secret through interrogation, through fever, through torture, through the simple erosion of a life, and would release its contents only when the republic had need of it—by some signal, some trigger, some mechanism the handler had declined to specify, smiling the way officials smile when they are withholding something they consider beneath your clearance.

And they had told her one thing more, with the emphasis men reserve for physics: *it will not itch. There are no nerves in the implant. You will forget it is there, because it will give you no reason to remember.*

For fifteen years, it had kept that promise. Through two relocations, three assumed names, her father's death, her mother's grave left behind in another province, the slow accumulation of a family of strays who believed she was a mapmaker's daughter and nothing else. She had bathed around it, slept around it, let lovers' hands pass over it without a flicker. It was not there, in the way that a vow is not there—invisible, load-bearing, absolute.

Tonight, under the single candle, she pressed two fingers to her left ribs, the ritual touch she permitted herself once a year on the anniversary of the oath, and felt—

Nothing. And then—

Something.

Not pain. Not heat. An itch. A small, maddening, impossible itch, deep under the skin, at the seal's edge, like the first tickle of a word rising toward the tongue. She pressed harder. The itch retreated and returned, retreated and returned, patient as tide.

Isandro sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, shirt still lifted, one hand flat against her own ribs as though holding a wound closed.

Fifteen years, the surgeons had said, and no nerves, and no sensation, and no reason to remember. Fifteen years of physics.

But the census taker had come with clean boots and a beautiful hand and one question that wasn't on the form, and now the thing beneath her skin was waking up—as if it had heard him ask, as if somewhere in its sleeping architecture a condition had finally been met, and the code her handler promised would name itself when the republic needed it had begun, politely, dreadfully, to scratch at the door.

Downstairs, faintly, a floorboard shifted. Someone else in the house was awake too, listening to the dark, doing their own arithmetic.

Isandro blew out the candle and sat in the black, hand against her ribs, counting the itch like a pulse.
