## 2. A Household of Borrowed Names

The morning after the census, the house woke the way it always did: one body at a time, each pretending the others were still asleep.

I lay in my narrow room above the stairwell and listened to the architecture of us assemble itself. First Ovid's door, which never opened quietly because he believed a silent door was a dishonest one—he wanted the house to know where he was at all times, wanted no one startled by his passage, and so he let the hinge sing its one dry note into the dark. Then the creak of the third stair from the bottom, which Sef had learned to skip as a boy and now skipped out of habit even though he weighed twice what he had at twelve. Then Marta's kettle, which she filled before she lit the fire so that the water's small sound would cover whatever else she was doing in the kitchen before anyone came down.

I knew all their sounds the way I knew the cipher against my ribs: not by looking, but by living with them long enough that they had become a second pulse.

I dressed in the gray wool I wore for mapmaking—wool takes ink stains without complaint—and went down to inventory my family.

That is what I did every morning, though I would never have used the word *inventory* aloud. A courier learns early that love and surveillance are made of the same material; only the intention differs. So while the kettle muttered and the stove ticked, I stood in the kitchen doorway and counted what I had.

Ovid first, because Ovid was always first. He sat at the long table with his back to the window—not for warmth but because light meant nothing to him and shadow meant nothing either, and he preferred to face the door where he could read the tremor of footsteps through the floorboards. His fingers moved over the object in front of him the way a blind man reads a face: patiently, completely, with an attention the sighted rarely survive learning.

It was a hymnal. I recognized it by its swollen spine, the way it refused to lie flat—the fourth printing of the *Vessany Vespers*, banned eleven years ago when the provincial governor decided that songs with too many minor keys were a form of sedition. There were forty-one copies in this town, Ovid had told me once, in their private language of hands. He had found nine. He could find the rest by feel alone, he claimed, because the paper stock of the forbidden printing had been milled in the south and held a dampness the local papers never did, a faint coldness like holding a stone from a river.

He catalogued by touch. Author, printer, year of the binding, reason for the ban—all of it pressed into wax tablets he kept in a drawer lined with felt, because wax records nothing unless you mean it to, and Ovid meant everything. At night he transcribed the tablets into braille boards, though I didn't yet know how many boards, or where they lived. That knowledge was still folded away in him like a letter never posted.

"Good morning," I said aloud, because he liked voices in the morning even if he caught only their shape.

His answer was a hand lifted from the hymnal, two fingers touching his chin and flicking outward—*the house is quiet, you are late, I have already worked an hour.* Then, softer, the old sign we'd invented in my first winter here, the one that meant *you slept badly*: he watched my shoulders when I thought no one watched, and deaf men see everything the hearing miss.

"I slept fine," I lied, in words and hands at once, and put the kettle on for him because he liked tea hotter than any of us dared drink it.

* * *

Sef came down at half past seven wearing yesterday's shirt and today's arrogance, which on him looked nearly identical. He was nineteen and had been the runaway heir to the Aldery fortune for four years now, though the fortune had been seized, the name struck from the rolls, and the boy who owned them both reduced to forging shipping manifests in the back room of a harbor office that paid him almost nothing and suspected him of exactly enough.

"You're doing the Ferrow account again," I said. It wasn't a question. The Ferrow manifests had a particular smell—cheap iron-gall ink over cheaper paper—and Sef carried the smell home on his cuffs like a confession.

"The Ferrow account pays." He dropped into his chair across from Ovid and stole a heel of bread from the board, tearing it with the violence of the young. "The honest accounts pay in thanks, and thanks don't buy coal."

"And the forged ones pay in?"

He grinned at me around the bread. My mother's son would not have approved of the grin. My mother, whoever she had been—I had been given her name as cover and nothing else—would have seen it for what it was: a boy practicing being unafraid in a country that punished the practice.

Sef forged beautifully. That was the trouble with him. Give him a shipping manifest and he wouldn't just copy the hand of some harbor clerk; he'd improve it, add the little fatigue tremor to the last line, the coffee ring at the corner, the smudge where a thumb had dragged. His forgeries were better than the originals because he understood that documents aren't written—they're *lived in*, and a forgery without wear is a corpse without wounds. Someday, I thought not for the first time, that gift would save him or hang him, and there would be very little warning about which.

This morning he was working on something at the table, half-hidden under the bread board, and I let him keep it hidden. A household runs on agreed fictions the way a lamp runs on oil. We did not ask Sef exactly what he copied at the harbor office; he did not ask me exactly why a widowed mapmaker's daughter kept locked drawers and woke at strange hours. We had built our silences like load-bearing walls. You do not knock on those to test them.

But I noted, the way I noted everything, that his eyes were brighter than usual, and that he ate standing up, and that he left for the harbor ten minutes earlier than he needed to. A boy with a secret walks faster than a boy with none.

* * *

Marta burned letters on Thursdays.

She did it in the yard, in the dented tin basin she'd carried across two borders and one marriage, kneeling beside it with her shawl pinned at the throat like a woman in mourning—which, at eighty-nine, she arguably was, permanently, professionally. The letters arrived irregularly, wrapped in brown paper, postmarked from towns whose names changed every few years as the maps around us were redrawn. She claimed not to be able to read them.

"They're in script," she told me the first time I asked, three years ago, holding one up to the light as if the handwriting itself offended her. "Nobody writes script anymore. Nobody can read it. They send them to me because I'm old and old people receive things."

Then she fed it to the flames unread, page by page, holding each corner until the fire took it from her fingers.

I had watched her do it perhaps thirty times before I understood. You cannot fake reading by firelight. Your eyes must move the way eyes move across lines, catching, returning, slowing at the difficult passages. Marta's eyes moved exactly so—too exactly, a performance of illiteracy polished over decades. And once, when a corner of a page curled before burning, I saw, upside down and backwards, the shape of a date. A date from forty years ago. From the purge year, when lists like the census taker's forms had gone out across the province and come back stained.

Marta remembered too much. That was her burden and her craft. She had been born in the republic's first year—she was the republic's age, its living ledger—and she had learned what every survivor of that decade learned: that memory is contraband, and the only safe place to store it is inside an old woman everyone has stopped taking seriously.

So she burned letters she read fluently, and I let her believe I hadn't noticed, and the household's great economy of silence absorbed another entry without a ripple.

"Sit," she said this Thursday morning, without turning from the basin. "You hover like smoke."

I sat on the step. The fire cracked and spat. Up close, burning ink smells almost sweet, like a promise being kept.

"The census man," she said.

"Marta."

"I'm old, not stupid, and those are different professions." She fed another page to the flame. "He stood in our doorway writing things down, and you smiled the way soldiers smile before a retreat. What did he ask you?"

"Whether anyone in the house ever held a commission."

Her hands stopped. For one breath the whole yard was only fire-sound and gull-cries from the harbor below, and then her hands resumed their work, steady as tide.

"And you said?"

"No one has."

"Good girl." She said it the way she said *pass the salt*. "In the purge year, they asked a different question. Whether anyone had ever *carried* anything. Papers, mostly. Everyone said no. Half the town had carried something." She turned and looked at me then, and her eyes in the firelight were pale and terrible and kind. "The ones who said no loudest died quietest. Remember that arithmetic, Isandro. Lies aren't weight. They're ballast. You need them to stay upright in weather."

She turned back to her letters. I sat a while longer, watching the pages curl, and thought about Rafael's pen moving down his form, unhurried, thorough, pausing one line too long at our household entry.

* * *

The rest of the day passed in the small trades that kept us alive. I worked on the atlas in the front room—the mapbook that was my cover and had become, treacherously, a pleasure—inking the coastline with its false inlets and true depths. Ovid catalogued two more hymnals brought to him by a fisherman who owed him for something no one discussed. Sef came home smelling of iron gall and triumph, said nothing about his day, ate everything on the table, and went up to bed humming, which he only did when something had gone *right*, which frightened me more than if he'd come home bleeding.

And in the evening, after the lamps were trimmed and Marta's kettle had gone cold, I climbed to my room and did what I did every night since Tuesday.

I checked the seal.

It sat against my seventh rib, where it had sat for fifteen years—a wafer of foreign matter no larger than a thumbnail, mute, inert, a debt I had stopped counting interest on. The handler in the vinegar-smelling corridor had told me what every courier is told: *You will feel nothing. That is the design. A cipher you can feel is a cipher your enemies can find.*

Tonight, pressing my palm flat over it, breathing slow the way they taught me, I felt it itch.

Not pain. Not heat. An itch—small, patient, obscene, like a word forming on the tip of the tongue. I pressed harder. The itch deepened, moved a fraction inward, as if the thing beneath my skin had shifted in its sleep and dreamed of waking.

Impossible. Fifteen years impossible.

Below me, the house settled into its sounds—Ovid's tablet scraping softly in wax, Sef's uneven breathing through the floor, Marta's stairs announcing her final round of doors. Four lives stacked in a building worth nothing on any map I'd ever drawn.

I lay in the dark with my hand over my ribs and understood, with the cold clarity the oath had trained into me, that the question Rafael had asked off-form had not been idle curiosity. And that whatever was itching beneath my skin had begun answering something.
