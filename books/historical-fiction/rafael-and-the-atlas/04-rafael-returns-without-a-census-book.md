## 4. Rafael Returns Without a Census Book

The error was real. That was the first thing Rafael had made certain of before he allowed himself the errand: that there existed, in the household entry for Number Nine Lantern Row, an actual mistake he could point to without invention. The daughter of the mapmaker—Widow Hartwell's household, entered under the name Isandro Ferro—had been recorded as born in Vessany's southern districts, but her handwriting had slipped once on the line for birth-year, and a careless clerk downstream had transposed two numerals. A small thing. A nothing. The kind of nothing that no census taker worth his salt would cross town twice to fix.

Which was precisely why it would work.

Rafael dressed for the visit the way other men dressed for court appearances: slowly, with attention to what each layer announced. Not the gray field coat—he'd worn it Monday, and men did not wear the same coat twice to the same house unless they were courting or auditing, and either reading cost him something. Instead: a plain wool jacket gone soft at the elbows, ink stains at the cuff that were genuine and therefore beyond suspicion. He carried no census book. That absence was itself a message, though only one kind of reader would receive it. A census man returning without his book was either finished with the household or too comfortable in it to bother with pretense. He intended to be read as the second, while remaining entirely the first.

He had spent the intervening days doing what he always did between contacts: nothing visible. He walked the harbor road at the hour the fishing boats came in, because crowds of tired men make poor witnesses. He drank coffee at the Kettle & Anchor at the hour the harbormaster's clerks took their break, because clerks repeat what they hear to anyone who buys the second round. He learned three new things about Lantern Row and discarded two of them as planted. The third: old Marta Voss had stood in her doorway Tuesday night for the better part of an hour, facing the street, not moving, not speaking, in weather that would freeze the spit in a younger throat. People said she was simple now, her mind gone soft as overwintered fruit. Rafael had seen minds go soft before. He had never once seen them do it in a doorway, facing the street, at the exact hour when footsteps carry farthest.

* * *

The house smelled of cedar shavings and yesterday's bread when Marta opened the door before he knocked.

"Census man," she said. Not a question. Her eyes went to his hands first—the way a beggar checks for coins—and then to his empty arms, and something moved across her face like wind over standing water.

"Madam Voss. I've come about an error in the record. Ink work only. I won't take ten minutes of your household's time."

"Time is the one thing this house has never owned." She stepped back from the door without inviting him in, which in Vessany border etiquette was an invitation of the most deliberate kind—you entered past her at your own risk, unescorted, witnessed by no one but her. "The girl's in the kitchen. She's always in the kitchen. You'd think a widow's daughter would have somewhere else to be."

He heard the kitchen before he saw it: the particular silence of a room where someone has stopped moving. Then, as his boots found the threshold, movement resumed—a drawer closing, water set to heat, the small honest theater of a woman caught mid-morning. She was at the stove with her back to him, pouring from the kettle into a brown teapot, and Rafael stopped just inside the doorway and gave himself permission to look.

Her hands.

There is a grammar to hands that no border town teaches and every war does. Most people's hands speak the language of their work: the butcher's grip, the scribe's callus, the laundress's burn scars along the inner forearm where steam finds skin through cloth. He had catalogued hands for eleven years, first for the Republic's Bureau of Transit and now—though his papers said otherwise—for the administration that was coming, the one whose letterhead he already kept folded in his breast pocket like a stone against his ribs. And the hands of Isandro Ferro, widow's daughter, mapmaker's child, were saying something their owner very much did not want said.

She poured left-handed.

Not awkwardly—that was the thing that made his spine prickle, the thing that separated signal from noise. An ambidextrous person pours left-handed naturally; a right-hander forced left pours clumsily, all elbow and apology. This was neither. This was a right-dominant hand choosing, fluidly, deliberately, to be left-dominant for the duration of the pour, the way a man changes his gait in a market he suspects is watched. The teapot tilted at a precise angle. No tremor. No thought. And then she set the pot down and reached for the cups with her right hand, and the transition happened inside a single breath, seamless as a liar's smile.

Couriers trained for that. He knew because he had been trained for it, in a corridor in the capital that smelled of carbolic and fear, by instructors who taught you to redistribute your body's habits so that no single observation could ever be confirmed twice. Write with your off hand for documents that might be seized. Eat with your wrong hand in public houses. Pour with whichever hand keeps your dominant side free. It was called load-bearing deception, and it was drilled until it lived below decision, below thought, in the marrow.

Nobody poured tea like that who had anything to lose but time.

"Tea?" she said, turning, and her face was arranged into mild courtesy so well that he felt almost tender toward it. Almost. There was a flicker at the left corner of her mouth—not a smile, not quite its suppression—something held in check the way you hold a door against wind. "You've come back. I thought we were done being counted."

"Not done. Corrected." He took the chair she indicated—the one with its back to the wall and sightline to both the kitchen door and the window, and he noted that she had given him that chair without hesitation, which meant she had thought about it beforehand, which meant she had assigned the seating the way a commander assigns ground. "A numeral transposed in your year of birth. The Bureau apologizes for the inconvenience."

"The Bureau apologizes," she repeated, setting his cup down, "the way rain apologizes to a roof."

He laughed before he could stop himself—a short, real sound that surprised them both—and covered it by opening his satchel and producing the correction slip, the blotter, the pen. All genuine instruments. He laid them out with the ceremony of a surgeon laying out steel.

* * *

While she signed, he watched her write.

This was the true errand. The slip required her signature—standard procedure, the Bureau's insistence that corrections be acknowledged by the recorded party—and a signature under observation tells you more than a face ever will. Faces are edited hourly. Hands only know how to tell the truth in the language they were taught.

Isandro Ferro wrote like a clerk. Neat, upright, slightly compressed letters, the hand of someone who has copied maps for a living—legible, disciplined, dead. But Rafael had spent years reading forged documents for a living before he'd been promoted to making them, and dead handwriting is a genre with rules, and she broke every one of them in ways invisible to a casual eye. Her loops closed at exactly the same angle each time. Too exactly. Natural writers vary by two, three degrees; hers varied by none, which meant the variation had been trained out, replaced with consistency, the way soldiers replace a civilian walk with a march. And at the final stroke of the final letter, her pen lifted clean off the paper—no tail-off, no fatigue-flicker—because she ended signatures the way fencers end lunges, on balance, ready to begin again.

He had known four couriers in his life. He had buried two of them. Every one of them signed like that.

"You're staring at my penmanship," she said pleasantly, not looking up. "I warn you, it's the most interesting thing about me."

"I doubt that very much."

"I'm a mapmaker's daughter who copies coastlines and names towns after dead birds. I promise you the penmanship wins."

"Your father taught you?"

"My father taught me everything I know." She blotted the signature and slid the slip back across the table, and her eyes came up and met his, and for a moment—one held breath, no longer—they simply looked at each other the way two people look when both of them have decided simultaneously to stop pretending and are not yet ready to say so. Her eyes were dark and level and patient as deep water. In them he saw the thing he had seen in mirrors during his own courier years: the flat readiness of a person keeping count. Of exits. Of seconds. Of everything you said and everything you didn't.

He looked away first. He noted that he looked away first, and filed it, and hated filing it.

"The correction is registered," he said, gathering his papers. "You'll receive confirmation by post within the month."

"By post," she said. "How official. And here I hoped you'd come back just for the tea."

It was a trap laid in sugar, and they both knew it, and he stepped on it anyway because declining cleanly was its own confession. "The tea was excellent."

"It was ordinary. The water here is ordinary. Everything on this street is ordinary." She walked him to the door, and at the threshold she stopped, and her voice dropped half a register into something private and unhurried. "Census taker. Do you find many errors in your rounds?"

"More every year."

"And what happens to the errors?"

"They get corrected."

"Mm." She smiled then, fully, and it was the worst thing he'd seen all week, because it was warm. "Give my regards to the Bureau."

* * *

He made himself walk past the house twice more before leaving the street—once at a normal pace, once pausing at Ovid's gate to admire the archivist's arrangement of potted herbs, which was either decorative or a sightline, and Rafael suspected it was both. The deaf man tracked him the whole time with a stillness that wasn't blindness but selection: Ovid watched the way deaf people watch, whole-body, reading vibration and silhouette and the grammar of doors, and Rafael had the distinct impression of being transcribed.

At the corner he stopped and let himself think the sentence he had been refusing since Monday:

*She is a courier.*

Not was. Couriers retired the way rivers retire. And if Isandro Ferro was carrying—if she was active, seeded in this town like a spore waiting for conditions—then everything he had reported about this household was already wrong, and everything the incoming administration wanted from Lantern Row was sitting behind that kitchen door drinking ordinary tea from ordinary water.

And here was the arithmetic that kept him standing at the corner long past reason: the cipher the Bureau wanted recovered was sealed to a courier's body. Sealed, in the old method, under the skin—usually at the ribs, usually stitched in a hospital corridor smelling of vinegar, and the seal itched when it woke, and carriers were told the itch was psychosomatic, and every carrier who ever lived learned eventually that it was not.

His superiors wanted the document intact. They had not specified, in any instruction he had received, whether the document required the woman attached to it.

Rafael turned up the hill toward the harbor office with his collar up against the wind, and began, in the privacy of his own skull, the first small forgery of what he suspected would become a large one: tomorrow's report would say that the follow-up visit had concerned a routine ink correction, that the household presented no anomalies, that the census taker recommended no further contact.

One day's delay. One day, bought cheap.

Behind him, at Number Nine, a kettle was being lifted by the wrong hand, and the steam rose straight up through the cold air like something trying to leave a message.
