## 6. The Extraction Lists

The harbor office smelled of tallow, wet rope, and the particular staleness of paper that had been argued over. Sef had learned to love that smell the way other people loved rain—because it meant work, and work meant he was allowed to be exactly where he was supposed to appear to be: hunched at the corner desk by the window, third from the ink stove, a boy of no consequence correcting shipping manifests for men of no patience.

He had been Veyren Kost for eleven days now. Before that he had been Tam Ellery for most of a season, and before that a name he'd already forgotten, belonging to a clerk's apprentice who had drowned—on paper—in a ferry accident so tidy that even Sef, reading the forged death record he'd built around it, had felt a flicker of professional admiration. Names were coats. You wore them until they wore out. The trick was never to mourn them.

His hands knew the work better than his mind did. That was the strange mercy of forgery: after enough hours, the pen moved without consultation. He could produce a customs stamp in four seconds flat, the little crowned gull of the Republic of Vessany pressing into wax with its wings half-folded, tired-looking, like everything else in the country lately. He could age paper with tea and lamp smoke. He could make a manifest say that forty crates of salted cod had left the pier on Tuesday when in truth the crates had never existed, or had existed as something else entirely—rifles, once, though that shipment still made him sweat to remember—and the harbor master would sign it because the harbor master signed whatever the pen presented to him, provided the penmanship was correct.

Correctness was the whole religion of this place. Sef had understood that within a week of arriving. The empire that was coming—the one everyone spoke about in the lowered voices reserved for weather and death—would not arrive as fire. It would arrive as forms. It was already arriving as forms. Every document Sef forged was a small act of treason committed in perfect bureaucratic grammar, and no one had ever once looked up from their desk.

Which was why, on the morning the extraction list came through, he almost missed it.

* * *

It arrived the way all dangerous things arrived: carried by an unremarkable man in an unremarkable coat, in a folder the color of weak tea.

Sef noticed the folder before he noticed the man, because folders were his trade and he read them the way Ovid read spines—by feel, by weight, by the sound they made when opened. This one made a heavier sound than its size justified. Thick stock. Government issue, but not harbor issue; the watermark, when the man set it down on the harbor master's desk three meters away, caught the light wrong. Too fine. Capital paper, in a border town that hadn't seen capital paper since the last inspection, two years gone.

The man was youngish, gray-coated, with the complexion of someone who worked indoors near lamps. He said something Sef didn't catch—Sef had spent years learning to read lips at distance, a skill inherited from Ovid, who had taught him without ever meaning to, simply by existing so competently in silence—but the harbor master's response was audible enough. A grunt. A chair creaking under his considerable settling.

"Leave it. I'll get to it after the *Meridian* clears."

"It requires countersignature today," the gray man said. "By noon."

"Everything requires everything by noon." The harbor master pulled the folder toward him with two fingers, the way one handles something suspected of being damp. "Sit if you want. There's tea somewhere."

There was not tea anywhere. Sef knew this because he had been quietly investigating the office's tea situation for eleven days and had concluded it was a rumor maintained purely for cruelty.

The gray man did not sit. He stood at the window instead, which put his back to Sef, which put Sef directly in the geography of the room that mattered: the desk, the folder, the harbor master already reaching for the first manifest of the morning and thus for Sef's own forged handiwork, laid out in a neat queue awaiting signature.

"Kost!" the harbor master barked. "These tide corrections. Whose arithmetic?"

"Mine, sir."

"It's wrong."

"It's corrected against last month's almanac, sir. The almanac's wrong. The moon has been unreliable since the solstice floods."

A pause. The harbor master looked at him over the rims of his spectacles with the expression of a man deciding whether to be angry or impressed, and choosing, as always, the cheaper option. "Fix the almanac," he said, and stamped the first page.

Sef bent back over his work. His heart was doing something complicated that he declined to acknowledge. Eleven days of invisibility, holding. He copied out a new tide table with hands that did not shake, because shaking was a luxury for boys whose names were real.

* * *

The harbor master read slowly. This was well known. It was the single reliable fact of his administration, celebrated throughout the waterfront: give Aldous Bremm anything requiring a signature and he would read every line twice, moving his lips, before applying the stamp. Merchants planned their departures around it. Smugglers built entire schedules on it. Sef had once watched a captain of thirty years' experience stand in that office visibly aging while Bremm worked through a bill of lading.

So when the folder came open, Sef had time.

Not much. But the gray man by the window was watching the harbor, not the desk—he had the fixed, professional stare of someone counting boats—and Bremm read the way he always read, lips moving, one thick finger tracing down each column.

Sef did not look directly at the folder. Looking directly at forbidden documents was how amateurs died. Instead he let his eyes do what eyes did naturally in a long morning: drift, rest, drift again. Each drift bought him a few lines. He stitched them together between penstrokes, the way he'd stitch together a manifest from fragments overheard across a tavern table.

The header first. He got it on the second drift, in capital letters, embossed with a seal he didn't recognize—a tower? a fist? some new heraldry being rehearsed for a country that didn't officially exist yet:

**PRIORITY REGISTER — PERSONS FOR DIRECTED RESETTLEMENT**
*Annexation District Nine — Coastal Zone*
*Classification: Administrative. Circulation: Restricted.*

Resettlement. Sef turned the word over. Everyone used it now, in the rumors that came up the coast ahead of the troop ships: resettlement, relocation, registration, processing. Words that had all been invented, he suspected, by the same committee of very tidy men, words designed to have no smell. Nobody said deportation anymore. Nobody said purge. Those words smelled like the last century, and the coming century intended to be remembered as clean.

The columns next. Name. Trade. Address. Category. Priority. And a final column headed *Disposition*, mostly filled with abbreviations he couldn't parse—*RT-2, RT-3, HL, OBS*—each one presumably a different flavor of what happened to you after the form was finished with you.

Then the names began, and the morning changed shape.

He wasn't looking for anyone. That has to be understood—that must be written down somewhere, someday, if any of this is ever written down: he was not looking. He was a forger doing a forger's drift, cataloguing the document's architecture rather than its contents, the way you'd appraise a lock by its mechanism and not by what it guarded. The first several names meant nothing to him. Dockworkers. A chandler. Someone called Pirelli whose trade was listed as *rope & tar* and whose priority was marked low, as though even the coming empire found rope merchants boring.

Then his eye caught on an address, and the address was a street, and the street was his street.

*Vosk Lane.*

He kept his pen moving. Downstroke, crossbar, the little crowned gull waiting in wax. The harbor master turned a page.

*Ovid Renn.* Archivist (ret.). Trade listed with a small notation Sef needed two more drifts to fully read: *hearing impairment noted.* Category: C-4. And beside it, in the Disposition column, letters that Sef copied onto the inside of his wrist with a glance later that night and only decoded days after, when it was far too late for the knowledge to be anything but a stone in his chest: *HL — held locally, records access.*

They weren't going to take Ovid away. They were going to keep him, the way you keep a library—because he knew where things were filed, because his memory was an index, because a deaf archivist was useful precisely in proportion to how thoroughly he could be made to forget that he had ever been anything else.

*Sef closed his eyes for half a second. Half a second was affordable. He saw the archive room, the smell of dust and binding glue, Ovid's hands moving along the shelves in the dark like a man greeting old friends by the shoulders.*

*Marta Ostrevic.* Laundress. Age entered in a column that seemed designed to embarrass: eighty-nine. Category D-1. Disposition: *RT-2 — reassessment pending.* Reassessment pending. As if her existence were a manuscript sent back for revision. As if ninety years could be returned to sender.

And then—

The third name. Their street. Their actual street, their row of leaning houses where the gutters sang in the rain and Marta grew onions in a barrel and nobody asked anybody anything, because asking questions was a debt the whole lane had agreed never to incur.

*Isandro Vale.* Mapmaker's daughter. Widow (noted). Category: B-1.

B-1. Sef didn't know what B-1 meant yet. He would spend the following weeks learning what B-1 meant, learning it the way you learn the depth of water—by falling into it. But even that morning, even ignorant, he understood from the density of the ink, from the fact that her entry occupied more of the clerk's attention than any other on the page, from the small annotation in a different hand—*verify household composition*—that B-1 was a word with a future in it. A word with a ship in it, or a train, or a ditch. In this century those were the options, and the forms decided which.

*Verify household composition.*

The census taker had come to their house. Sef remembered him now with the particular vividness of retroactive dread: the tall, quiet man with the careful voice who had lingered over Isandro's answers, who had asked about Ovid's hearing and Marta's age and whether anyone in the house had ever held a commission, and Isandro lying so smoothly that Sef had actually admired her, had thought, *she's good, she's better than me,* and had gone back to his forged manifests feeling safe, feeling that the house's disguises were holding, feeling—

Feeling what a fool feels, which is warm.

*Verify household composition.* The blank space beneath Isandro's entry was where the verification would go. Four persons at the address, according to the census. Three names on the register. Sef counted them again, drifting his eyes one final time, committing the page to the memory he'd trained for exactly this: Ovid Renn. Marta Ostrevic. Isandro Vale.

And a fourth line. Ruled, empty, waiting. The clerk's pen had hovered there—or so Sef imagined; he hadn't actually seen the hovering, but he felt it, the way Ovid felt footsteps through floorboards—and passed on. An unfinished sentence. A name to be supplied.

Whose?

His own, obviously. Some part of him answered immediately, coolly, the professional part, the part that had been dying on paper since he was fourteen: *it's your line, boy, you're the unregistered heir, you're the loose thread, of course it's yours.* But another part, the part that had eaten breakfast that morning at Isandro's table while she pretended not to watch him eat, whispered something worse: *or hers. Or it stays blank forever, which is its own kind of verdict.*

The harbor master turned the last page. Stamped. Signed. Said, "Tell your people the coastal zone files need updating before annexation, not after. I'm not doing this twice."

"No one is asking you to do anything twice, Master Bremm," the gray man said, and took his folder, and left, and the door closed behind him with the softness of all doors that have already won.

* * *

Sef worked until noon. This is important too. He sat at his desk and he corrected tide tables and he aged a bill of lading with the tea-staining sponge and he forged, with steady hands, the departure of three hundred nonexistent crates of salted cod, and not once did his pen falter, because his pen was the best-trained part of him and pens do not grieve.

But underneath the work, in the cellar of himself where he kept everything he couldn't afford to carry upstairs, a machine had started running and would not stop.

Here is what no one tells you about survival: it is administrative. Sef had imagined, as a boy, that history arrived as soldiers—boots, banners, the crash of doors. What he had learned since, working the harbor, watching the paperwork move like tide through every office on the coast, was that history arrived as clerks. That the difference between living and dying, in the century now closing its accounts, might come down to a category letter. A disposition code. A blank line, ruled and patient, waiting to be told whose life it was.

Three names from Vosk Lane. He walked home that evening through streets he suddenly saw as a document—every doorway a field to be completed, every neighbor an entry awaiting its abbreviation—and the lamplighter was making his rounds, and the light came up pool by pool along the cobbles, and Sef thought: *they'll process us in order of the list. They'll process the whole town in order of the list. And none of us will hear our own names read out; we'll just be a mark moving down a column, RT or HL or OBS, until one day the column ends.*

Marta would know. The thought arrived plainly, the way true thoughts do—not reasoned, just present. Marta was born in the republic's first year; she was older than the country itself, nearly, older than its promises, and she had survived things that Sef had only ever seen referenced in the banned hymnals Ovid catalogued by touch. She recognized formats. She read a government form the way Sef read a manifest—by what it was trying to hide in its margins.

She was burning letters again when he came in. He could smell the paper smoke from the gate, thin and bitter, and see her silhouette in the kitchen doorway feeding pages to the stove with the serene efficiency of a woman who had done this in other decades, other purges, and expected to do it again. She didn't turn around.

"You're late," she said. "And you've been reading something you shouldn't."

"How—"

"You walk like a ledger when you're frightened," Marta said, and fed another page to the flames. "Come in, boy. Tell me what the columns said."

Behind her, at the big table, Ovid's hands had stopped moving over his braille boards, and Isandro had looked up from the mapbook she was never quite drawing, and the whole house went quiet in that listening way it had—as if the walls themselves were waiting to learn what they were worth.

Sef stood in the doorway with the smoke curling past him, and understood that he was about to change the arithmetic of everyone he loved, with nothing but three names and a blank line.

"The list," he said. "There's a list. And we're on it."
