## 7. Marta's Century

Marta Vey had been born in the republic's first spring, in a room above a dyer's shop that smelled of madder root and rain, and her mother had named her for the season rather than the saints because the founders had said, in their broadsheets and their speeches, that there would be no more saints—only citizens. She was ninety-nine years old now, or ninety-eight; the arithmetic of her birth had been adjusted once by a clerk who misread an ink-blotted register, and Marta had never corrected him, because a woman who is older than she claims is granted a small extra country of patience that no one can annex.

She remembered the founding. Not the speeches—speeches are weather, they pass—but the texture of those years. The way the new republic smelled: lamp oil and cut grass, because every public square was planted with lindens as a promise that shade would belong to everyone. The way children were taught to write their names twice, once in their own hand and once in the state's standardized script, so that the self and the record would learn to agree with each other. The promise had been simple enough to fit on a coin: *No one counts alone.*

She remembered, too, the first betrayal, which came before she could read but not before she could count. It arrived the year the lindens were felled in the eastern squares to build barracks for a war nobody had voted for, and the wood was sold back to the citizens as commemorative boxes at three times its worth. Her father bought one. He kept his ledger in it. On the inside of the lid he had written, in pencil so it could be erased if anyone looked: *The shade was the point.* That sentence was the first thing Marta ever memorized, and it had outlived two constitutions, one occupation, and every man who had ever shaken her father's hand.

So when Sef came home from the harbor office on an evening in late autumn with a folded paper pressed against his chest like a stolen loaf, and when Isandro spread it on the kitchen table under the good lamp—the one with the green glass shade they only used when something needed to be seen truly—Marta did not need to put on her spectacles to know what it was.

She knew the format the way you know your own heartbeat: by rhythm.

* * *

The paper was headed with a border of small printed stars—not stars, actually, she saw when she leaned close, but eight-pointed rosettes, the kind the Chancellery used in the years before the last war—and beneath them ran columns ruled in gray ink, gray because gray does not shout. Names. Dates of registration. A column headed *Disposition*, which is what bureaucracies call a person's future when they intend to decide it without asking.

Three names from their street sat in those columns like stones in a shoe. And near the bottom, a blank line where a fourth should be—a ruled space, patient as a grave waiting for its tenant.

"Where did you copy this?" Marta asked Sef, though she already knew the answer by the way he wouldn't look at her hands. Young men always thought old women's hands were finished with the world. Hers had been in more registers than he had been alive.

"The harbor office. It was in the tray for the morning post." Sef's voice had the flatness he used when frightened, which Marta had learned to hear as a kind of honesty. "There were four copies. I took—" He stopped. Recounted. "I took one."

"You took one," Marta repeated, "and left three."

"I couldn't take all four without—"

"Without them knowing someone wanted it." She nodded slowly. It was correct. It was also terrible, both things at once, which is how most true things arrive. "Sit down, child. All of you. If we are going to look at this thing, we will look at it together, and no one will touch it with damp hands."

Ovid came to the table by feel, trailing his fingertips along the chair backs—he read rooms through surfaces the way other people read them through light—and sat where he could feel Isandro's sleeve near his wrist, which was his way of sitting next to someone. Isandro pulled the lamp closer. The green glass made all their faces into something underwater, something preserved.

"This format," Marta said, and touched the rosettes with one finger, not quite touching the paper. "I have seen it before."

Isandro looked up. She had a courier's face, that girl—no, woman; Marta refused to think of her as anything younger than what she'd earned—which is to say a face that showed exactly what it chose to show. But her eyes went still the way water goes still before ice.

"Forty-one years ago," Marta said. "The Purge of the Registrars, some called it afterward. At the time it had no name, because naming it would have required admitting it happened." She smoothed the tablecloth beside the paper rather than the paper itself; you didn't touch such documents idly, even decades dead ones. "I was fifty-eight. I kept the parish register then, unofficially, the way women keep everything unofficially. And one autumn the census forms came back changed. New columns. Gray ink. Rosettes instead of the plain seal. And a heading word I had never seen on any form before."

"*Disposition*," Isandro said quietly.

"*Disposition*," Marta agreed. "And below the columns, a blank ruled line. Just one. Do you know why just one?"

No one answered. Ovid turned his face toward her voice, and she felt his attention the way you feel sun through glass.

"Because a list with many blanks admits uncertainty. A list with one blank admits design. One blank means the form expects a specific absence. Someone is meant to be written there—or meant to be erased from somewhere else first." She drew breath, and it whistled faintly, the way her breathing had whistled since the winter of the barricades. "That autumn, forty-one years ago, families on my street learned what that blank was for. And some of them learned something else besides. Something that isn't written down anywhere official, because it was never supposed to survive. But I am old, and my memory is the last archive that doesn't require a key, and tonight I am going to spend some of it."

"Marta." Isandro's voice was careful. "What do you mean, trade places?"

* * *

The kettle was on. Marta insisted on the kettle; instruction goes better with steam rising between people, giving everyone something to watch that isn't each other's fear.

"When the lists come," she began, pouring, "the first instinct is to hide. This is wrong. Hiding is for fugitives, and a fugitive is a shape the machinery already knows—it has a drawer for that shape, it has practiced with that shape. No. When the lists come, the families who survived did not hide. They *exchanged*."

Sef frowned. "Exchanged what?"

"Identities. Places on the paper. Listen." She set down the pot and held up fingers, counting, because she had taught schoolchildren once, briefly, in the year the schools were open to girls, and some habits of the hand outlive their institutions. "A list is a document, yes, but it is also a *flow*. Names go in at the top, dispositions come out at the bottom. Everyone imagines the danger is being *on* it. The real danger is being *movable* on it. So. Three methods, and I knew the women who used all three, and I drank tea with two of their ghosts for twenty years after."

"First method: substitution." She laid one finger flat. "Two households, similar in size, similar in ages. One name high on the list—one marked for removal—and one low. The families swap surnames at the registry level. Not the persons—the *papers*. My neighbor Ana did this with the Ferreol family across the lane. The Ferreols were slated; Anas were not. So the Ferreol mother became Mrs. Ana Ferreol, and the Ana mother became the widow Ferreol, and when the wagons came for the Ferreol household, they collected a woman who waved from the window and got into the wagon voluntarily, smiling, because the alternative was worse, and the family she saved watched her go and lived forty more years and never once spoke above a whisper in their own kitchen."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Nobody drank.

"She got off the train at Kessel Junction," Marta added, softer. "That part they don't tell. She walked home in eleven days and knocked on her own door as a stranger, and her own daughter gave her bread through the fence for two winters before it was safe to let her in. Substitution works. But it costs exactly what it saves. Remember that arithmetic. Write it nowhere."

"Second method: dilution." A second finger. "A name on a list can be buried under a name that must not be touched. Every purge has its protected categories—clerks too useful, informants too productive, the ill whose deaths would raise questions of contagion rather than mercy. You wrap the endangered name inside the protected one. The widow Halloran registered herself as the household's *laundress employed by the state garrison*, and the garrison clerk who owed her money signed the category without reading it, and when the lists were reconciled, the reconciliation clerks found a contradiction they hadn't the staff to resolve—and an unresolved contradiction is a door that stays shut. Bureaucracies do not destroy what confuses them. They shelve it. You want to be shelved, children. Shelved is alive."

"And the third?" Isandro asked. Her hands were wrapped around a cup she had not sipped from, and Marta noted that without saying so: the girl watched everything and consumed nothing, even tea. Fifteen years of carrying something secret will teach a body to keep its hands full and its mouth empty.

Marta looked at her for a long moment over the rim of her own cup.

"The third method," she said, "is the blank line."

* * *

She rose, slowly, joints reporting in their usual order—hips, knees, the old fracture in her ankle from the year of the ice—and went to the dresser where she kept the things that were not keepsakes, because keepsakes invite searching, and took from behind the stack of linens a tin that had once held throat lozenges and now held a life's contraband of the small kind: buttons cut from uniforms, a child's milk tooth not hers, and a strip of paper gone amber with age.

She brought it back and unrolled it on the table beside Sef's stolen copy.

The two formats matched. Forty-one years apart, and the rosettes identical, the ruling identical, the gray ink the same gray—as if the empire that was coming to swallow them had learned paperwork from the same teachers and changed nothing but the flag.

"There," Marta said, and touched the amber strip where, faded almost past reading, a fourth entry existed where a blank line should have been. "This is the Halloran list. The original. The one the street never saw. See the fourth name? *M. Vey.*"

Silence. Even Ovid went very still, and Ovid's stillness was louder than most men's shouting.

"My name was on it," Marta said. "Fourth position. And the list was submitted with a blank fourth line, because I wrote myself out of it before submission. I was the registrar's assistant that autumn—I copied the fair copies, which is how I learned the format, which is how I recognized it tonight, which is why I am telling you this and not some safer story about my weddings." She smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth as river stone. "I entered the fourth line blank, and I entered my own name into the *protected* column of another district's list, using a laundress's classification and a clerk's debt, and I mailed the corrupted original to a cousin in the mountains who burned it unread, because burning unread is a skill and she had it. Then I waited to see if the machinery would notice."

"Did it?" Sef whispered.

"No. Because here is the secret, and lean in, all of you, because this is the inheritance and I will only say it once: **a list is only as strong as the weakest hand it passes through.** Not the weakest mind—the weakest *hand*. Somewhere between the ministry and the wagon, every name passes through ordinary fingers: a copying clerk, a filing assistant, a tired sub-official with bad eyesight and a sick child at home. Those fingers can smudge. Those fingers can transpose. Those fingers can leave a line blank." She tapped the amber paper once, sharply. "The state believes its lists are fate. Fate cannot be edited. But lists are *paper*, and paper has always belonged to the people who handle it quietly. Your young man there"—a nod at Sef—"already knows this in his bones; it's why he forges manifests instead of running messages. Ink is a weapon that requires no courage until afterward."

"But it required courage afterward for Ana," Isandro said. It wasn't a question. It was a cost being counted.

"It required everything for Ana," Marta said. "And she paid it, and her descendants live in Vessany still, and they do not know why they exist. That is the shape of it. Survival purchased by exchange leaves a debt that can never be presented for payment—the person who took your place cannot be thanked, only *remembered wrongly*." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the last word, and she let it catch; at ninety-eight you earn the right to a cracked note. "I have spent forty-one years being remembered wrongly. I recommend it over the alternative. The alternative is being remembered accurately in a disposition column."

Ovid's hand moved then—not sign-language, not quite; his private grammar of flat palm and curved finger that he and Isandro shared, and Marta had learned enough of it across the years to follow its larger shapes. *Ask her,* he indicated toward Isandro. *Ask her what she carries.*

Marta watched the question land on Isandro's face like weather.

"I know what you carry, girl," Marta said aloud, gently. "You think an old woman doesn't see. You press your side when you think the house is asleep. You sleep on your left, always, like a soldier guarding a wound that never was a wound. Whatever is sealed to your ribs, it has begun to matter again, hasn't it—the itch started recently." Isandro's cup, finally, trembled. "Don't be astonished. I was married thirty-one years to a customs officer; I can smell a sealed thing through a wall. The question is not whether you carry it. The question is whether what it contains can be *edited*. Because if it can—if it is a list, or a roster, or a grant of passage, anything with lines and spaces—then everything I have just taught you applies to it. Paper is paper. The state prints fate, and we hold the pen at the moment of copying. Always. There is always a moment of copying."

The lamp hissed softly in its green glass. Outside, the town was performing its evening forgetting—shutters closing at the same hour, the same creak, house by house down the lane, the town's oldest prayer: *nothing here, nothing here, nothing here.*

"Now," Marta said, and gathered both papers—the amber strip and Sef's theft—and slid them toward the center of the table where the lamplight pooled like yolk. "Here is what we do tomorrow. Sef returns to the harbor office and memorizes which clerks touch the resettlement lists, and which of them have sick children or debts or bad eyesight. Isandro writes nothing down, ever, but tells me everything she remembers of the cipher's behavior, because an itching seal is a seal approaching release, and a released cipher is a document, and a document—" she paused, and her old eyes went around the table, gathering them the way you gather scattered sheep before dark— "a document has a moment of copying. And I—" she placed her hand, flat and certain, on the blank line of the stolen list, on the space where a fourth name was expected— "have done this before, and I am the only person at this table who has ever gotten away with it, which makes me, children, the closest thing this house has to an expert in survival."

She withdrew her hand. The blank line remained, empty, expectant, ruled in gray.

"But understand me," she said, and her voice dropped to the register she had used, forty-one years ago, reading names aloud to a registrar who never suspected the fourth line, "understand what I am teaching you is not safety. There is no safety. What I am teaching you is *authorship*. The difference between dying as an entry and living as an edit. Ana died eventually—all of us die eventually—but she died as the author of her own line, and I intend—"

The knock came at the front door.

Three knocks, evenly spaced, unhurried. Not the knock of a neighbor, which stutters. Not the knock of a bill collector, which apologizes. The knock of someone who knows the door will be opened regardless, and is measuring, perhaps, how long courtesy takes in this particular house.

Everyone froze. The kettle ticked. The lamp hissed.

Marta looked at Isandro, and Isandro looked at the blank line, and outside the window, passing under the streetlamp with a leather case and no census book, walked a man they had seen once before—except this time he did not stop at their door. He continued past. The knocking had come from the direction of Ovid's archive, two houses down.

Marta stood, unhurried, and began, deliberately, to hum—an old founding-era hymn, banned these twenty years, chosen precisely because a deaf man's house was the one place in Vessany where a forbidden melody could be sung at full volume without consequence—and as she moved toward the door she murmured to the others, without turning her head:

"Set the table for four guests, and boil more water. Whatever happens tonight, we will meet it the way paper meets ink: *quietly, and on our own terms.*"

And she opened the door onto the cold, onto the lamplight, onto whatever the century intended to hand them next.
