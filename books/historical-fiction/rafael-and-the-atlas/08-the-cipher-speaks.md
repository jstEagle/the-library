## 8. The Cipher Speaks

The itch began at the market.

Isandro had learned to read her own body the way Ovid read a room—by vibration, by pressure, by the small grammars of sensation that ordinary people ignored because they had never been taught that a body could lie to them or, worse, tell the truth too late. For fifteen years the seal beneath her left breast had been a stillness, a coin of heat sunk in her flesh, mute as a swallowed stone. The handler had been explicit about this in the vinegar-smelling corridor where they'd put it into her: *It will not speak until it speaks. You will feel nothing before that. If you feel anything—anything at all—you come to me.*

She had felt nothing for fifteen years. She had come to no one, because there was no one left to come to; her handler's name was on a list now herself, though Isandro had only ever known her as Vess and had last seen her walking north through a rain that made the whole republic look like something being washed off a window.

Now, standing at Marta's stall with a cabbage in each hand and the census taker's question from three nights ago still lodged under her ribs like a fishbone—*has anyone in this household ever held a commission*—the seal itched.

It was not pain. That was what unsettled her most. It was almost pleasant, the way a healing wound is pleasant, the body congratulating itself on some repair she hadn't authorized. She set down the cabbages, paid Marta with coins she counted twice, and smiled at the old woman with the smile she kept for public use, the one that said *nothing is wrong here, nothing has ever been wrong here, we are a town that forgets on command.*

Marta looked at her a moment too long. Marta always did. Then she wrapped the cabbages in brown paper and said, in the tone she used for weather, "You're holding your side like a woman with a stitch. Walk slower."

"I walked too fast," Isandro said, which was true, and also a lie, and also exactly the kind of sentence this town ran on.

She took the long way home anyway. Not slower—longer. There was a difference, and the difference was that a longer route gave her time to decide whether she was going to do what she already knew she was going to do.

* * *

The house smelled of ink and tallow when she came in. Sef was at the kitchen table with his manifests spread out like a card game he was losing beautifully, his pen moving in the quick confident strokes of a boy who had discovered that paper could be persuaded. Ovid sat by the window with a banned hymnal open on his knees, reading it with his fingers, his lips moving around words he would never hear. The afternoon light came through the glass thick as honey and lay across everything like a benediction neither of them had asked for.

"Market?" Sef said without looking up.

"Market," she confirmed.

"Cabbage?"

"Cabbage."

He grinned at the page he was forging. She loved him so suddenly and so completely that it frightened her—the way love had begun to frighten her lately, arriving less like a feeling than like a debt coming due.

She climbed the stairs to her room. She closed the shutters. She latched them, which she almost never did, because latched shutters told a watcher you were doing something worth watching.

Then she sat on the edge of her bed, in the slatted dark, and pressed her palm flat against her left breast, over the place where the seal slept, and she said aloud, to no one, to the room, to whatever patient machine Vess had sewn into her fifteen years ago:

"All right. Speak."

Nothing. Of course nothing. Seals did not answer invitations; that was the entire theology of them. She laughed once, short and ugly, and reached for the lamp—

—and the itch flared.

Not pain. Recognition. It was the sensation of a lock hearing its own key somewhere in the house, a vibration too deep for the ear, traveling up through the bed frame, through her spine, into the roots of her teeth. She sat very still. Outside, a cart went by, and its wheels wrote their usual sentence in the ruts of the street, and beneath that sentence, underneath it, like a voice under a voice, something else was writing.

*Under stress,* Vess had said. She'd meant battlefield stress. Shellfire. The moments when a carrier might die with the code unrecovered and the seal needed to make itself findable—by an ally with the right instruments, digging it out of a corpse if necessary. She had never imagined stress like this: not explosions but arithmetic. Not shellfire but a blank line on a census form and a man who poured tea like someone taking dictation from your hands.

The seal was waking because she was afraid. It had taken her fear for the thing it was calibrated to recognize: the end approaching.

* * *

She worked until the candles guttered and then lit more and worked past those.

The extraction was a thing she knew how to do the way she knew how to pour tea without spilling—with hands trained so long ago they belonged to a different girl. The seal sat just under the skin above her fourth rib, a disc the size of a thumbnail, and it had two states: sealed and spent. Vess had shown her the spending of it once, on a pig carcass in that corridor that smelled of vinegar, and Isandro had vomited into a basin afterward and been told, without unkindness, that this too would become professional.

It had not become professional. It had simply become hers.

She heated the needle in the lamp flame until it glowed dull orange. She cleaned the patch of skin above her ribs with spirits that burned cold and then hot. And she thought—not for the first time, but for the first time with the itch still singing under her fingers—about the fact that she was about to cut open the one part of her mission that had never once troubled her conscience, precisely because it had never once done anything.

Fifteen years of carrying a treaty text. That was what she'd believed: a cipher of state, terms of surrender or alliance, the republic's last word to whatever powers might outlive it. A document, not a decision. She had carried it the way you carry a stone in your shoe—uncomfortable, familiar, finally invisible—and she had built a whole life in the border town on top of it, a family of strays, a trade in maps, a self named after a dead mapmaker's daughter who had never existed. All of it resting on the assumption that what slept in her ribs was paperwork.

The needle touched skin.

The pain was bright and clean and strangely clarifying, and through it she felt the seal respond—a softening, a yielding, the way wax yields to a signet ring. Vess's instruments had included a lens and a chemical bath and a great deal of ceremony. Isandro had none of that. What she had was lamplight, a shaving mirror propped against the water pitcher, and the steady hands of a woman who had spent fifteen years pretending to be calm and had, somewhere along the way, actually become it.

The disc came free with a sound she felt rather than heard, a wet click, and lay in the saucer of spirits she'd prepared: a wafer of grey material, no bigger than a thumbnail, featureless as a fish scale.

And then, in the spirit solution, it began to develop.

* * *

She had expected columns of figures. Treaty language reduced to cipher-blocks, dense and dead, needing weeks of work and a keybook she'd have to reconstruct from memory. She had braced herself for months of nights like this one.

What rose instead out of the wafer, blooming in the spirit like frost forming on glass, was legibility.

Names.

The first name surfaced whole: **Adelmar Voss, Archivist-General, Northern Repository** — and beside it a string of numerals she understood instinctively, the way you understand a face, as a *where*. A place. Coordinates folded into numbers, or numbers folded into coordinates; either way, an address for a person.

She waited, hands flat on the table, breathing through her mouth.

The second name surfaced: **Cettine Marlowe, Keeper of the Civil Registries**. A *where* again.

Third: **Ilse Brandt**, and Isandro's breath caught, because Brandt was a harbor name, a name from four streets away, though the given name meant nothing yet—coincidence was cheap in a small town, and she refused to spend belief on it. A *where*, again.

Fourth, fifth, sixth. Names rising through the spirit-solution in order, each with its coordinates, each with a designation she slowly parsed from the marginal glyphs: not rank, not office. *Priority.* These were people the republic had decided, in its last lucid hour, could not be allowed to drown with it. Keepers. Archivists. Registrars. The people who remembered where things were written down.

It was not a treaty. It had never been a treaty. It was a lifeboat manifest, and it had been sleeping in her chest while she fed cabbage to a family of strays and told herself she was a courier, a vessel, a neutral thing, a woman carrying paper.

She counted the names as they finished developing. Eleven. Eleven protected persons scattered across the dying republic, each with an address, each with a claim on whatever evacuation machinery still existed.

And then the twelfth entry began to surface, and it was different.

There was a name-line. And the name-line was blank.

Not undeveloped—blank. Deliberately, structurally blank, the way a form is blank, the way a berth is blank. Around it the glyphs assembled themselves with terrible patience, and Isandro read them twice because the first reading seemed impossible and the second merely unbearable:

*Final entry assignable by carrier alone. One (1) guaranteed passage, last vessel, any destination. Carrier's designation supersedes all list priority. Carrier may assign to any single named individual. Assignment irrevocable upon inscription. Carrier retains right of passage only if unassigned.*

She sat back. The chair complained. Somewhere downstairs a floorboard spoke—Ovid's tread, heavy and specific, checking the doors the way he checked them every night, counting the house safe—and she listened to him move through the grammar of the rooms and thought: *the ship will take eleven strangers whose names I now know, plus one name of my choosing, plus me if I choose no one.*

One berth.

She had spent fifteen years believing she carried the republic's testament. She had imagined herself a messenger, and messengers are innocent; they deliver, they do not decide. But there had never been a message. There had been a *choice*, packed in wax and spirit and her own living tissue, waiting fifteen years for her fear to ripen enough to open it. The mission had always been this: not to deliver the cipher but to *spend* it—to stand at a gangplank before the century turned and write one name on a line while everyone she loved stood behind her on the dock, doing the same arithmetic in silence.

Vess had known. Vess had chosen her partly for the steady hands, yes, but mostly, Isandro understood now, for the heart—the kind of heart that would take fifteen years to learn it was a loaded weapon.

She looked at the blank line for a long time.

The candle burned. The spirit evaporated slowly off the wafer, and as it dried, the names began to fade—she saw it happening, the first entries dimming like breath off a window, and she understood with a lurch that the medium was mortal, that the roster lived only minutes in air. She snatched paper and copied fast, her hand flying, transcribing names and coordinates in the compressed shorthand of her courier training while the wafer died by degrees on the saucer. Ten names secured. Eleven.

The eleventh—Ilse Brandt, or whoever she was—half-faded before Isandro pinned its shape down. She would need the harbor records to confirm it. She would need Sef's access to the shipping office, which meant telling Sef something, which meant—

Which meant everything downstream of this night changed.

The wafer went blank. She held it up to the candle and turned it over: grey, smooth, spent, an eggshell after the hatching. Fifteen years of cargo, delivered in twenty minutes, into a notebook that now weighed more than the house.

* * *

She blew out the candles except one and sat in the small ring of its light, pressing a cloth to her bandaged ribs, and let the truth arrive the way dread arrives—politely, hat in hand, asking to be shown in.

The census taker had asked about commissions. The extraction lists existed; Sef had seen the machinery of them with his own eyes, though he didn't know she knew. Rafael walked like a man counting exits. And now she held a document that said, in effect: *of everyone on this street, one gets out.*

Tomorrow—or soon, soon—she would have to sit her family down at the kitchen table among the cooling teacups and the forgery ink and the hymnals read by touch, and tell four people who had become her reason for existing that existing was about to be rationed. One berth. One name on the line. And every one of them, she already knew—Ovid with his terrible gentleness, Marta with her century of practice at other people's endings, Sef with his fury at being pitied—every one of them would try to give it back to her.

She looked at the blank line one more time.

Her pen was already in her hand. She hadn't decided to pick it up; her hand had simply done what hands trained by Vess do, reaching for the moment of inscription the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

The nib hovered above the line. Below her bandage, the spent seal ached with the clean dumb pain of any honest wound, and upstairs a floorboard creaked—Sef this time, sleepless, padding toward the water closet—and Isandro Hartwell, widowed mapmaker's daughter, carrier of the republic's last choice, sat in the dark with a name trembling on the tip of her pen and understood that whatever she wrote next would be the truest sentence of her life.

She did not write it.

Not yet. First, tomorrow, there would be tea in the kitchen, and faces around the table, and the arithmetic laid out plain. Because a choice made alone wasn't inheritance. It was only escape.

She capped the ink, hid the notebook inside the false spine of an atlas of northern coastlines, and lay down with her hand pressed over her ribs, listening to the house breathe around her—four sleepers, one blank line, and outside, far off toward the harbor, the first foghorn of the season sounding low and long, like something enormous clearing its throat to say: *soon.*
