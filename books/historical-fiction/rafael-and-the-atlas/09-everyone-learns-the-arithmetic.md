## 9. Everyone Learns the Arithmetic

I told them on a Tuesday, because Tuesday was the day the bread came, and I wanted the news to arrive in a house that already smelled of something good.

It was cowardice dressed as strategy. I had rehearsed the telling for three days—on the walk to the harbor office where Sef worked his forged manifests, on the stairs past Ovid's archive room with its smell of dust and lamp oil and the faint vinegar ghost of old binding glue, at Marta's window while she shelled beans into a bowl that had outlived two governments. Every rehearsal ended the same way: with my mouth opening and nothing coming out, because there is no way to say *one of us* to people who have made themselves into one thing.

So I let the bread come first. I set it on the table still warm, tore the heel off for Marta because she liked the crust before anyone else could soften it, and then I stood at the head of the table with my hands flat on the wood like a woman about to say grace, which in a way I was.

"There's a ship," I said. "The last one. It leaves on annexation day, before the harbor closes."

Ovid felt my voice through the table—he always sat closest to the wood for exactly this reason—and he went still in that particular way of his, the way that meant he was listening with his whole body, his palms flat on the grain like mine, reading me through vibration the way other men read print.

Sef kept working. That was his answer to everything: keep the pen moving, keep the ink flowing, pretend the world is just another manifest to be doctored until it says what you need. But I saw his nib stop mid-stroke, saw the small bloom of ink spread where it touched too long.

Marta cracked a bean pod with her thumb and said, "How many berths?"

"One."

The word sat on the table between the bread and the ink bottle like something dropped from a great height.

And here is the horror, the thing I will carry longer than any cipher: nobody argued.

Nobody said *take mine*. Nobody said *we'll find another way*. Nobody even looked up with that quick animal arithmetic people do when they hear a number that might include them. They simply absorbed it—the way our town absorbs everything, the way Vessany itself had learned to absorb invasions, decrees, disappearances—with a terrible practiced quiet. We had all been trained by fifteen years of forgetting to receive bad numbers without flinching. The census forms had done this. The lists had done this. Every year of our lives under the dying republic had been a lesson in hearing an impossible figure and nodding as though it were reasonable.

Sef looked at the ink bloom on his page. Ovid looked at his hands. Marta looked at me, and her face was the face of a woman doing sums she'd done before.

"No one's going to say anything?" My voice came out sharper than I intended. "One berth. Four of us. Someone should—"

"Someone should what?" Sef said, and now he did look up, and his eyes were wet with fury, not grief. "Fight over it? Draw lots? Isandro, don't you dare stand there and make us audition for our own lives."

"Sef—"

"No." He pushed back from the table hard enough that his chair legs shrieked against the floor, and Ovid flinched—not at the sound, he couldn't hear the sound, but at the tremor of it coming up through the boards. "You don't get to hand down a number like scripture and then watch us squabble for your entertainment. You don't get to make this a competition we have to lose gracefully."

"It isn't entertainment," I said. "It's arithmetic."

"Then do the arithmetic!" He was standing now, both palms on the table, leaning across the bread toward me, twenty-two years old and shaking. "Do it out loud. Say why you've already decided, because you have—I can see it, everyone in this house can see it, you walked in here tonight having already chosen and you're hoping one of us will be noble enough to save you from saying it."

He was right. That was the worst of it. He was right the way only the young are right, with no mercy in it.

* * *

Let me tell you how each of them took it, because they took it differently, and the differences were the truest portraits of them I will ever possess.

Ovid took it first, and fastest, and most quietly. While Sef was still shouting, Ovid raised one hand—not for attention, since attention was never something sound gave him, but for the room—and began signing.

His sign-language was ours, invented across ten years of shared walls: a grammar of the kitchen, of doors, of weather. *Me*, he signed, tapping his own chest twice. *Go*. Then he pointed at the door, then made the shape we used for *ship*—a flat hand rocking on invisible water—and then, the part that undid me: he signed *yes* the way he signed everything, with total economy, no flourish, no drama, as if volunteering to die were a matter of filing.

"You're sixty-eight," I said aloud, uselessly, because speaking was how I panicked.

He shrugged, and the shrug said everything speech would have cluttered: *sixty-eight years is a complete sentence.*

But then—and this was Ovid, this was entirely Ovid—he stopped. His hand hung in the air between *me* and *go*, and I watched him reconsider, not the offer but the offering. I watched him understand, in that slow deep way of his, that a gift freely given could become a debt cruelly imposed; that if he volunteered first, loudly enough, sincerely enough, then whoever was chosen after him would carry his sacrifice like a stone in their pocket for the rest of their life. He would not be saving someone. He would be sentencing someone.

So he withdrew it. Deliberately. He turned his palm over, the sign we used for *erased*, *struck from the record*, and then he folded his hands on the table and looked at the bread as though none of it had happened, as though the offer had never existed, so that no one could accept it, so that no one could be burdened by it. He erased his own generosity to protect us from it.

I have seen courage in many shapes. I have never seen anything braver than a man un-offering his life so that his family wouldn't have to refuse it.

* * *

Sef took it worst, or best—I still haven't decided. Anger was his native language and he spoke it fluently that night, but underneath the anger was something that frightened me more: he wasn't angry at being asked to leave. He was angry at being considered at all.

"You've already written my name," he said. "Don't lie to me, Isandro. You've had it written since you found out. Youngest. Healthiest. Most future. That's the formula, isn't it? That's what the lists say, that's what the ships want, that's—" his voice cracked, and he hated it cracking, I watched him hate it, "—that's what *you* think love looks like. Sending the young ones ahead. Spending the old ones like coin."

"Would you rather I spent you?" Marta asked mildly, from behind her bowl of beans.

"I'd rather," Sef said, rounding on her, "someone ask me. Once. In my whole life in this house, somebody *ask me* instead of deciding for me."

The silence after that had texture. It had weight and grain. Ovid's hands lay still on the table; even the fire seemed to hold its crackle. And I understood that Sef had put his finger on the wound beneath the whole conversation, the wound beneath the whole household, maybe the whole republic: every institution that had ever claimed to care for us—the census, the extraction lists, the resettlement bureau, the evacuation itself—had decided for us. Had sorted us. Had moved us around like freight according to formulas of value. And here I was, the person who loved him, doing the same thing with a kinder face.

"You're right," I said.

He blinked. He had prepared himself for argument; the agreement disarmed him worse than any defense.

"You're right that I'd already chosen. You're right that I didn't ask. So I'm asking now." I looked at him across the table, across the bread, across fifteen years of borrowed names. "Sef Okonkwo-Vessany. Do you want the berth?"

And he stood there, furious and young and cornered by his own demand, and couldn't answer. Because wanting it meant abandoning us. Refusing it meant wasting the thing four people were breaking themselves open over. There was no clean answer; I had known there was no clean answer; I had asked anyway, because he deserved to feel the shape of the trap with his own hands instead of being told about it.

"That's not fair," he whispered.

"No," I agreed. "None of it is. That's the first honest thing anyone's said at this table all night."

* * *

Marta took it last, and took it the way she took everything—as history repeating, which is to say as something she had survived before and intended to survive again, but not necessarily intact.

She finished shelling the bean she was holding. She set the empty pod aside with the others, aligned them, a small tidy row. Then she folded her hands and looked down the length of the table at all of us—at Sef still standing, at Ovid still silent, at me still standing guard over bread I no longer expected anyone to eat—and she said:

"Forty-one years ago there was a list."

We knew. We always knew, when Marta started with a year, that we were about to be taught something no archive held, because Marta's memory was the archive the republic kept burning and she kept rebuilding inside her skull.

"My mother's street. Eleven families. The format was different then—hand-inked, no carbon copies—but the logic was identical. Priority persons. Resettlement. One transport." She picked up another bean pod, considered it, didn't shell it. "Two families solved it the way you're imagining. They traded places. A healthy daughter went in place of a sick grandmother, a clerk went in place of a laborer, names swapped on the line because the list-checkers never looked at faces, only paper. Both families thought they'd beaten it."

"What happened?" Sef asked, though his voice said he'd guessed.

"The trade worked. The list worked. The ship sank." She set the pod down. "Storm, or mine, or incompetence—the records disagree, which is its own lesson. Eleven families' worth of cleverness, and the sea didn't read the paperwork." She looked at me then, and her eyes were clear and ancient and absolutely without self-pity. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you away from trying. I'm telling you so you know what you're choosing between. There is no version of this where everyone is safe. There is only the version where someone is saved, and versions where no one is."

"Marta," I started.

"Decide before you love us more," she said.

It landed in the room like a stone into still water. Sef sat down. Ovid's head lifted slightly, reading the shift in the floorboards, the change in my breathing, whatever tremor Marta's words had sent through the house's bones.

"Every hour you wait," Marta said, "you'll find a new reason. Every kindness we do you will make it harder. That's how it works—that's how it worked for the woman who gave up her seat in '34 because her neighbor's child had fever, and then the neighbor's child recovered and the woman was still on the dock when the ship pulled out. Love doesn't clarify these things, girl. Love obscures them. Decide now, cold, while you can still bear to be wrong. Because if you wait until you can't bear it, you won't decide at all, and the harbor will decide for you, and the harbor has no imagination."

Nobody spoke. The bread had gone cold. Somewhere outside, a dog was barking at the census taker's footsteps, or at nothing, or at the century turning over in its sleep.

* * *

Later—much later, when Sef had gone up to his attic and I could hear him not sleeping through the floor, when Marta's candle had gone out behind her door—Ovid caught my sleeve in the hallway.

He drew me to the kitchen table, the table whose wood was our telephone, and pressed my palm flat against it. Then he signed slowly, deliberately, making sure I caught every word through the grain:

*You. Go.*

I shook my head. *No.*

*Yes.* His jaw set. *Ship. You.*

*I'm the carrier,* I signed back. *The seal dies with me if I run. And who seals the wall? Who keeps the archive? Who—*

He cut me off with the flat-hand gesture that meant *stop*, and then he did something he almost never did: he took my face in his two hands, the way you'd hold something breakable, and he looked at me from six inches away, close enough that I could see the map of every year in his skin, and he mouthed the words without sound, shaping them with exaggerated care so I could read them off his lips:

"Not. Your. Job. To. Die. For. Us."

"It's exactly my job," I mouthed back.

He smiled then—a real smile, sad and crooked—and released my face, and signed one final thing before he took his candle up to bed:

*Ask Rafael.*

I stood alone in the dark kitchen for a long time after that, with my hand still flat on the table, feeling the house breathe around me. Ask Rafael. Ask the man who came without a census book, who watches hands, who walks like someone counting exits. As if Ovid knew something I hadn't told him. As if the deaf archivist had heard, through the soles of his feet and the grain of the doorframes, a second set of footsteps keeping time with mine.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a ship's horn sounded—one long note, low, like the republic clearing its throat before it spoke its last sentence.

Three days, the harbormaster's schedule said. Three days until the manifest closed.

And somewhere between here and the docks, a man with a ledger and a past was deciding, question by question, which side of the form he stood on.
