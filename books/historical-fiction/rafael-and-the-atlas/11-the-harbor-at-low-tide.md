## 11. The Harbor at Low Tide

The harbor at low tide smells like an apology it never intends to keep.

Sef had learned that in his first month in Vessany's Edge, when the water withdrew past the pilings and left the mud exposed like a ledger someone had opened and abandoned — old rope, broken crockery, a child's shoe, the rib of a boat nobody claimed. The tide went out and showed everyone exactly what the town had thrown away, and then it came back and covered it all again, and by morning the harbor looked respectable, and everyone agreed to have seen nothing. He had thought it was the most honest place in the republic. He had been fifteen and wrong about most things.

Now he was nineteen and wrong about fewer things, and he crouched behind the salt-stained breakwater with a manifest folded against his chest like a stolen letter, watching berth nine do its slow morning arithmetic.

He had come for the ship.

Everyone in the house knew something was coming — you could smell annexation the way you smelled rain on dust, a metallic patience in the air, in the way the harbor office had stopped accepting corrections to its records, in the way the new forms arrived already printed in two languages though only one language was legal yet. Sef forged shipping manifests six days a week in the back room of the harbor office, close enough to the clerks to hear them clear their throats before saying words like *resettlement*, and he had copied things he shouldn't have, and he had learned this much: there was a ship scheduled, and there were berths on it, and berths could be counted the way coins were counted, and counting was the one skill he had never had to fake.

So he counted. Berth nine sat low in the water at slack tide, a coastal freighter called the *Merciful Hour* — some clerk's joke, surely, or some clerk's prayer — flying no flag he recognized, which meant it flew the flag that hadn't been raised yet. Cargo cranes stood idle along its deck like herons waiting for a fish. And on the gangplank, checking papers, stood a girl.

Not a woman. A girl, maybe his age, wearing the harbormaster's coat three sizes too large for her, the sleeves rolled twice and still swallowing her wrists. She checked papers the way Ovid read hymnals: by touch, quickly, with an attention that had nothing to do with the eyes. She would look at a document for two seconds, hand it back, wave the man through. Then the next one she would hold for ten seconds, turn it toward the light, and shake her head once, and the man carrying it would go gray and walk away without arguing, which told Sef everything: she wasn't official. Officials got argued with. She had some other authority, quieter and heavier, and the men who approached her seemed to know it before they reached the plank.

Sef watched her turn away four people in an hour. He counted that too.

* * *

He should have left. This was the discipline Marta had tried to teach him — *look once, remember everything, don't look twice; looking twice is how they learn your face.* But the fourth man she turned away didn't walk off cleanly. He lingered at the foot of the gangplank, talking low and fast, hands moving, and Sef saw the girl's shoulders change — not fear, exactly, but the particular stillness of someone deciding how much of a scene she can afford. The man reached into his coat. The girl stepped back. Two dockhands materialized from nowhere with the smooth simultaneity of people who had rehearsed this, and the man reconsidered whatever lived inside his coat, and left.

And Sef understood, with the cold clarity of a forger reading another forger's work, what he had just seen. She was running a rescue. Small, private, rationed — turning away most, passing a few, keeping the whole operation invisible inside the machinery of the evacuation itself. Someone else's arithmetic, smaller than the lists, crueler in its way because she had built it herself.

He came out from behind the breakwater because his legs decided before he did.

She heard him — of course she heard him, the mud sucked at every footstep like a witness — and turned with a paper half-raised as if documents could be weapons, which, in Sef's experience, they could.

"You're not on my list," she said.

"I'm not on anyone's list," Sef said, which was true in more directions than she could know. "That's rather the point of me."

Her eyes did a quick inventory of him — boots, hands, the ink stain on his right middle finger that no amount of pumice ever fully lifted. He saw her see it. He saw her file it.

"You're the one who works in the office," she said. "Third window. You copy manifests."

"I transcribe shipping data," Sef said. "With occasional interpretive liberties."

"Your liberties are good," she said. "I've seen three of your manifests come through here. The Kessel grain shipment that never existed. The timber consignment with the extra tonnage."

"The extra tonnage was a rounding error," Sef said.

"It was eleven tons of flour bound for a village that isn't on any map anymore." She said it without accusation, which unsettled him more than accusation would have. She folded her hands over the papers. "Ilse," she said. "My father thinks I'm inventorying rope."

"Sef." He hesitated over the surname, out of fifteen years of habit, then gave the borrowed one. It felt different saying it to her. Lighter. Almost like trying on a coat that might fit.

"Sef," she repeated, tasting it, and he had the distinct impression she was deciding whether the name was real and concluding that it didn't matter, which was, he would later understand, the highest compliment available in Vessany's Edge.

"What happens to the ones you turn away?" he asked.

"They go home and wait for the lists to find them." Her voice stayed level but something underneath it moved, the way the water moved under the mud. "What happens to the ones your lists find first?"

* * *

They walked the length of the seawall because standing still invited attention, and walking gave their conversation the shape of accident. Ilse talked the way she checked papers — fast, by feel, trusting him to keep up. Her father held the harbormaster's office and had held it through three administrations by the simple method of being indispensable to each one and loyal to none. She had grown up in the paperwork. She knew which clerk took bribes and which took pity and which took both from the same pocket. When the evacuation schedules had crossed her desk two months ago, she had done what her father taught her to do with anything valuable: she had copied it, hidden the original, and started spending the copies.

"There are forty-two berths on the *Merciful Hour*," she said. "Thirty-one are spoken for by names I can't touch — officials, collaborators, men whose money will still be money after the flag changes. That leaves eleven. Eleven berths for a street with three hundred people on it." She kicked a gull feather off the seawall with unnecessary force. "So I choose. Every name I pass is a name I've failed to pass for someone else. Do you know what that does to a person?"

"No," Sef said honestly. "But I know what it does to a household."

She glanced at him. He hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"Tell me," she said.

And because the sea was loud and the wind was coming off the water and no one in the history of Vessany's Edge had ever successfully eavesdropped on a conversation conducted at low tide, he told her — not the cipher, not the mission, not Isandro's sealed skin or Rafael's census questions, but the shape of it. A house of strays. An old woman born the year the republic was. An archivist who read with his fingers. A boy with a forged pedigree and a talent for other people's signatures. One berth, eventually, somewhere, for someone, and no fair way to count it out.

Ilse listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of skill, and when he finished she was quiet for the length of a full wave.

"You're going to need papers," she said. "Real ones. Not your liberties — real ones, with real seals, the kind that survive being held up to the light by someone who hates forgery professionally." She pulled a folded oilcloth packet from inside the enormous coat and opened it just enough for him to see paper edges, dozens of them, blank forms and half-finished identities, a whole drawer of unborn people. "I have a contact inland. Ex-registry clerk. She owes my family a debt from the last time the world ended, which was 1819, and yes, I know how that sounds, debts outlast regimes here."

"Why show me this?" Sef asked. "You've known me for twenty minutes. For all you know I report to the census office."

"For all you know *I* do," she said. "We're both contraband, Sef. Contraband recognizes contraband." She closed the packet. "Besides. You said *one berth*. Singular. Which means somebody in your house has already counted, and somebody in your house is planning to stay behind so the count comes out right. Am I wrong?"

He couldn't answer. The tide chose that moment to turn — he felt it before he saw it, a change in the harbor's breathing, the mud beginning to drink the water back — and Ilse watched his face while the sea undid its morning honesty.

"I thought so," she said softly. "My mother did the same thing. Stayed behind in '19 so the rest of us fit on the cart." She said it the way Marta said things — flat, factual, a stone placed on a pile. "So listen. Eleven berths. If one of yours goes on my list, it goes on clean — real papers, real seal, a name that checks out under interrogation. But I need something in return."

"Money we don't have."

"Not money." She stopped walking. Below them, the water was climbing the pilings, erasing, forgiving, lying again. "The harbor office keeps duplicate manifests. Everything your hand has touched, there's a second copy of. When the annexation auditors come — and they'll come within the season — they'll reconcile those duplicates against the originals, and every liberty you've taken becomes evidence, and evidence becomes lists, and lists become—" She made a small gesture toward the mudflats, toward everything the tide covered.

"You want me to steal the duplicates."

"I want you to *correct* the duplicates," Ilse said. "Make them match the originals. Make your forgeries retroactively true. Can you do that? Forge the past?"

Sef looked at her for a long moment. Nineteen years of being the youngest, the borrowed, the heir to nothing — and here was a stranger handing him the largest commission of his career: not a future, but a past, rewritten.

"Ilse," he said, "forging the past is the only thing I've ever wanted to do."

Something happened to her face then — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might build later, if either of them survived long enough for foundations. She shook his hand once, hard, businesslike, and pressed the oilcloth packet into his coat pocket while doing it, so smoothly he didn't register the weight until her hand was gone.

"First papers will be ready in six days," she said. "Bring me the name you want them made for. The real name." Her eyes held his. "Whoever rides my list rides as themselves. That's the whole point. They've spent their lives as other people. Let the ship at least know who it's carrying."

* * *

He walked home along the top of the seawall because the long way let him think, and thinking, lately, had become a form of grief he needed to pace out carefully.

Six days. A name. *The real name.*

He turned it over as he walked. Ovid's name, restored after decades of cataloguing other people's — no, Ovid wouldn't take it, Ovid had already begun arranging himself around staying, Sef could feel it in the vibration of him through the floorboards. Marta — Marta would sooner board the ship of a government she'd watched betray its own founding. Isandro — Isandro carried a country sealed to her ribs and would carry it into the water before she abandoned it.

Which left, by elimination, the arithmetic nobody in that house would speak aloud: the youngest, the healthiest, the one with the least invested and the most remaining. Him. They were going to hand him the berth the way they handed him everything — gently, guiltily, as if survival were a chore they hated to assign.

And now Ilse wanted the real name. His real name. The one from before, the one attached to a house with gates, a title, a father whose signature he could still reproduce perfectly from memory — the first forgery he'd ever learned, practicing it in candlelight at fourteen, hating how well it came.

By the time he reached the street where forgetting was practiced daily, the tide was fully in, the harbor respectable again, berth nine riding high with its cargo of counted souls. In his pocket, the oilcloth packet shifted against his hip like a sleeping animal.

Inside the house, he could hear Marta burning something and Ovid's stylus ticking against its boards, and Isandro's voice low, asking Rafael something in the tone of two professionals negotiating a border neither trusted. Sef stood in the doorway with a stranger's name still in his mouth and a real one rising in his throat like a tide of its own.

Isandro looked up. Read his face the way she read everything — completely, immediately, without mercy.

"What did you find at the harbor?" she asked.

Sef put his hand in his pocket, around the packet, around the six days, around the name he hadn't said aloud in five years.

"A ship," he said. "And a problem."
