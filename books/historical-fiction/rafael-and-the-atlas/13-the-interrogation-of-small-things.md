## 13. The Interrogation of Small Things

The telegram arrived on a Tuesday, which Rafael would later understand was the first cruelty of it. Tuesdays were market days in Vessany's border towns; the streets filled with carts and shouting and the smell of smoked fish, and no one noticed an extra man stepping off the mail coach in a gray coat cut to imperial specifications. Colonel Aurel Voss did not travel to be unnoticed. He traveled because notice was his instrument.

Rafael read the wire twice at the harbor office, standing very still while the ink dried under his thumb. *ARRIVING THURSDAY. REPORTS IRREGULAR. WILL CONDUCT VERIFICATION IN PERSON.*

Irregular. Three days late, then four, then a week — each delay dressed up as weather, as ink shortages, as a household too large to census quickly. He had told himself he was being careful. He had told himself that careful men write slow reports. But Voss had spent thirty years reading other men's slowness, and he could smell a stalled ledger the way old horses smell storms.

Rafael set the telegram facedown on his desk and looked out the window at the town. Low roofs, salt-bleached shutters, laundry strung like signal flags between buildings that had learned to keep their opinions to themselves. Somewhere down those lanes Isandro Hartwell was pouring tea or sharpening a pen or doing whatever she did with those hands of hers when no one was watching — courier's hands, steady as a surgeon's, and he had known it from the second cup.

He had one day and a night to decide what kind of report he would have ready when Voss walked through her door.

* * *

Voss arrived not Thursday but Wednesday evening, ahead of his own announced schedule, because predictability was for men who trusted the world. He was smaller than Rafael remembered — the empire promoted tall men to its frontiers and kept its compact ones close, where their patience could do the most damage — and he carried no luggage except a leather case that clinked faintly when it shifted, like bottles, like instruments.

"You look well," Voss said, in place of greeting. He said it the way a man reads a gauge.

"Colonel."

"The reports." He held out his hand without sitting down, in Rafael's own rented room above the harbor office, and Rafael gave him the folder he had prepared: three weeks of census work compressed into honest-looking pages. Household counts. Occupation entries. A widow mapmaker's daughter, two lodgers, an elderly aunt. Nothing flagged. Nothing circled.

Voss read standing, which was itself a performance; he wanted Rafael to watch him find whatever flaw he'd already decided was there. His finger stopped on the fourth page.

"You visited this house twice."

"To correct an ink error."

"Mm." Voss turned the page. "And the error?"

"A smudged date. Entry seven."

"And you corrected it yourself rather than sending the household clerk a form?" Voss looked up. He had pale eyes, the color of water over chalk, and they did not blink so much as pause. "You were always thorough, Rafael. Thoroughness is why I sent you. But thorough men correct errors by post unless the error interests them."

"It was near," Rafael said. "I was walking past."

"Were you."

It was not a question. It was a door left open, and behind it Rafael could hear the shape of everything Voss suspected and nothing Voss knew. He kept his face in the register he had practiced for fifteen years — mild, competent, faintly bored. "The household is unremarkable, Colonel. A deaf archivist, a young man who works manifests, an old woman. The daughter runs the map shop since her father died. If any of them are worth your attention, I've missed it."

"That," said Voss pleasantly, "is what tomorrow will establish."

* * *

He began, of course, with Marta.

Not because she mattered — Voss's notes listed her as ancillary, aged eighty-something, no occupation — but because she was the softest-looking target and Voss liked to open with an easy kill. It steadied his hand. It also, Rafael understood with a cold turn in his stomach, established the tone for the whole household before Isandro ever entered the room.

They used the kitchen of the house on Tanner's Lane. Voss sat at the scrubbed table with his leather case beside him, and Rafael stood by the window in the role assigned to him: silent escort, neutral witness. Outside, rain had started, fine and sideways, ticking against the glass.

Marta came in leaning on Ovid's arm, and Rafael watched her take the room in one pass — the table, the case, the window, himself — and then arrange her face into something soft and wandering, a woman arriving late to a conversation decades long.

"Sit, sit," she said, before anyone invited her, and sat. She peered at Voss with the frank squint of the very old. "Are you from the gas company? Because we paid. I have the receipt somewhere. Ovid, tell him we paid."

"We are conducting a census verification," Voss said.

"A what?"

"A count. Of persons."

"Well, I'm one," Marta said promptly. "There might be more of me some days, but I'm one today."

Rafael bit down on nothing. Beside him, rain ticked.

Voss began gently, name and age and birthplace, and Marta answered each question with an answer adjacent to the truth — born in the republic's first year, yes, but she gave the year wrong by three, then corrected herself wrong again; her maiden name offered in two versions, hyphenated differently; her husband a ferryman, no wait, a cooper, no, the ferryman was her father, wasn't it, Ovid? And Ovid, summoned from the doorway, signed something small and noncommittal, and Marta nodded as though this settled it.

But Rafael had interviewed enough liars to know the difference between confusion and craft, and by the third question he saw the machinery running under the fog. Her misrememberings were never random. When Voss asked whether anyone had ever held a commission — government, military, diplomatic — Marta launched into a ten-minute story about a cousin who'd once delivered a letter to a mayor, a story with no information in it whatsoever, shaped precisely to waste time. When Voss asked about visitors to the house, she described the milk delivery, the priest, a dog. Never a courier. Never a coach at night. Her senility had walls in it, and every wall was load-bearing.

Voss felt it too. Rafael watched him feel it — the small stillness, the recalculation — and then Voss did the thing Rafael dreaded: he stopped asking big questions and started asking small ones.

"How many cups," Voss said, "do you use when the whole household takes tea?"

Marta blinked. "How many people live here?"

"Five."

"Then five cups. Unless someone's cross with someone. Then fewer."

"And when guests come?"

"What guests?" Marta laughed, a dry rustle. "Nobody visits us. We're nobody."

She said it cheerfully, and it should have been nothing, and it was almost perfect — but Rafael saw Voss's pen stop moving, because *we're nobody* was the one sentence in twenty minutes that Marta had delivered without a single hesitation, clean as a coin from a launderer's cloth. Truth, worn smooth by repetition. Or by rehearsal.

Voss closed his notebook. "Thank you. You've been very helpful."

"I helped?" Marta asked, genuinely pleased, and let Ovid lead her out, and only at the threshold — only where Voss couldn't see, where only Rafael could — did her hand tighten once on Ovid's sleeve, a signal passed between them like contraband, and Rafael understood she knew exactly what she had done and exactly how close the blade had come.

* * *

Sef nearly broke inside of six minutes.

It was not his fault, or it was entirely his fault, depending on how you weighted the arithmetic of being nineteen. Sef came in bristling — Rafael could see him deciding, in the doorway, whether to be insolent or invisible, and choosing insolence because invisibility required a stillness he hadn't yet grown — and Voss took one look at the anger and began to farm it.

"You work at the harbor office."

"Manifests."

"You copy documents."

"I *file* documents." The correction came too fast, too hot. Rafael saw Voss note it the way a fisherman notes where the line jumps.

"Have you ever copied a document you weren't authorized to copy?"

"No." Fast again. Sef's ears had gone red. His hands, folded on the table, had gone white-knuckled, and Rafael thought: *he's thinking about the list, he's thinking about the priority roster with the blank line, he's thinking about it right now with his whole face.* Sef had been carrying that knowledge for weeks like a coal in his shirt, and Voss was circling him with a bellows.

"Your handwriting is very good," Voss observed, turning a sample page from the earlier census. "Better than a manifest clerk's usually is."

"My mother made me practice."

"Where is your mother?"

Sef's mouth opened. Closed. The silence stretched one second, two — and Rafael stepped in before the third, because the third was where boys like Sef drowned. "Orphaned," Rafael said mildly. "It's in the entry. Lodger taken in by the household four years ago. The aunt raised him."

Voss glanced at him — a flick, quick as a card sharp — and returned to Sef. "Four years. And before that?"

"Before that I lived places." Sef had found his feet, barely. "Places don't interest me anymore."

"But paper does." Voss smiled. It was a courteous smile, a professional smile, and it was worse than any frown he'd produced all morning. "A young man who loves paper. In a harbor office. During annexation preparations." He let it hang. He wrote nothing, which was worse than writing. "We may need to verify your employment records."

"They're filed," Sef said. "Alphabetically."

It was such an absurdly defiant thing to say that Rafael nearly admired it, and Voss — who recognized bravado when it was all that stood between a boy and collapse — let him go with a nod that promised nothing good. Sef walked out with his spine rigid and his terror visible in the set of his shoulders, and Rafael heard him take the stairs two at a time, and thought: *the next time Voss comes, the boy won't survive the interview.* Whatever happened next had to happen before Voss's patience ran its natural course, and Voss's patience, Rafael knew from fifteen years ago, ran about four days.

* * *

Isandro was last, and she was different.

Rafael had watched her pour tea on his second visit specifically to confirm what his hands already knew — the economy of her wrists, the way she never reached across her body, the way a cup was set down at the exact edge of a saucer as if calibrated. Couriers' habits fossilize early. You can bury them under fifteen years of mapmaking, but pressure brings them up like bones after rain.

Now she sat across from Voss with her own hands folded, and they were perfectly ordinary hands, a widow's hands, and Rafael stood at the window and watched her lie with the fluency of a woman discussing weather.

Name: Hartwell, Isandro. Father deceased, map trade. Mother — "gone since I was small." Residence: lifelong, though Rafael knew the entry was fabricated down to the church record. She answered in full sentences but never more than needed. She let silences sit without filling them, which most civilians can't abide and most trained operatives refuse to do. She met Voss's eyes comfortably and looked away naturally, at intervals no pattern could hold.

And she watched Rafael.

That was the thing. Every answer she gave Voss, some fraction of her attention sat with Rafael at the window, weighing him — and Rafael realized, with a sensation like a floor tilting half a degree, that she was not checking whether he would betray her. She had already priced that risk. She was watching him *decide*, question by question, and cataloguing the decisions, the way he had once catalogued hers over tea.

*Has anyone in this household ever held a commission?* No. Smooth.

*Do you receive correspondence from outside the province?* Maps orders, mostly. Vienna, mostly dead letters now. Smooth.

*If the border changes, will your household remain?* A pause — genuine, human, the only true pause she'd allowed — and then: "People remain where their dead are buried, Colonel. Ask any army that's tried to move a town."

Voss actually inclined his head a centimeter. He respected craft even when he hunted it; it was the one decent thing about him. "Your father's maps. Are they originals?"

"Some. Copies of copies, mostly. Borders redrawn so often the originals are lies anyway."

"May I see the shop?"

Something crossed her face then — fast, controlled, but Rafael caught it, the fractional tightening around the eyes — and he understood before she spoke that the shop contained something. Papers. A wall. Ovid's archive, maybe, or the cipher's shadow in a drawer, or simply evidence of competence beyond a widow's means. "The shop is inventory and dust," she said. "You're welcome to both."

"I will be," Voss said, "tomorrow morning."

* * *

Rain all evening. Rafael sat in his room above the harbor office with Voss downstairs drinking the office's brandy and reading the town's shipping schedules like scripture, and Rafael took out his report forms and uncapped his ink.

This was the moment, he understood. Not tomorrow at the gangplank of some decision, not in some dramatic corridor — here, now, at a desk, with a pen. Reports were how the empire thought. A report that said *household unremarkable, recommend closure of file* would close the file. A report that said *anomalies pending further observation* would bring more observers, and observers bred arrests, and arrests bred lists, and he had seen where lists went — he had written names on them once, for the other republic, and half of those names were graves now and the other half hated him in cities he'd never revisit.

He dipped the pen.

*Household of five,* he wrote. *All entries verified. The elderly woman is senile but consistent. The young clerk is ambitious and frightened, which is common at his age. The archivist is deaf and harmless. The daughter manages a declining trade with declining prospects. Recommend:* —

He stopped. The nib hovered. Rain ticked the glass.

*Recommend,* the form wanted. Closure? Observation? Extraction review? Every word was a stone thrown into a family's pond, and he could hear, absurdly, the sound each would make — Marta's dry laugh, Sef's red ears, Ovid's hands signing in the grammar of doors, Isandro at the window of her own interrogation watching him choose.

He thought about his superior's actual instructions, the ones given in a capital that smelled of wax and confidence: recover the courier's seal intact. The person is irrelevant. The person had never once been relevant. Fifteen years ago Rafael had believed that sentence, had built a life on it, had survived his own republic's fall by repeating it to himself in the dark like a prayer that didn't work. Half his names had died anyway. The sentence had never saved anyone. It had only organized the dying.

He wrote: *Recommend continued routine monitoring through quarter-end. No action warranted. Household poses no administrative significance.*

No action warranted. Four days of cover, bought with four bland words. By quarter-end the border would be redrawn, the harbor closed, the last ship gone or going, and whatever he and Isandro had begun negotiating in glances across her kitchen table would either have become a plan or a grave marker.

Below him, a chair scraped. Voss, retiring early. Tomorrow he would walk through the map shop with his pale eyes and his clinking case, and Isandro would perform dust and inventory, and somewhere behind a wall or under a floor the true weight of that house would hold its breath.

Rafael blotted the page, signed it, and set it aside to dry.

Then he took out a fresh sheet — blank, unofficial, for no file — and wrote a single line in a cipher both of them would recognize, because some professionals, sooner or later, stop counting exits and start counting allies:

*Tomorrow he opens the drawers. Tell me what he must not find, and I will make sure he finds it boring.*

He sealed it. In the morning, before Voss woke, it would reach Tanner's Lane inside a rolled survey map, harmless as weather.

Outside, the rain thickened toward the coast, where the last ship's berth waited at low tide like a held breath.
