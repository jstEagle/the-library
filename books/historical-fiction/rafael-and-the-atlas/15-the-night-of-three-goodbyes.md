## 15. The Night of Three Goodbyes

The last night of the republic began, as all its nights had begun for a year now, with the sound of boots on the harbor road—distant, unhurried, the tread of men who knew they would not need to run. I sat at the kitchen table with the cipher's final page spread before me, though I no longer needed to read it. One berth. One blank line. One name.

I had told them at supper. I had laid it out with the flat arithmetic of it, the way Marta had taught me: one berth, four of us, and the exemption line assignable by the carrier alone—which was me, which meant the choice was mine, which meant every kindness they had ever shown me was about to be spent trying to buy my life back from me.

No one argued. That was the horror of it. In a house built on borrowed names, where argument was our nightly music, there was only silence, and then Ovid clearing his throat in that way he did when he wanted the room to know he was about to speak even though he could not hear whether anyone else was.

I washed the dishes. I banked the fire. And then, one by one, they came to me—not together, never together, because grief is a shy animal and each of them had learned its habits separately—and tried, in their own grammar, to give me away.

* * *

Ovid came first, as he always did when something needed doing before fear could talk him out of it. I heard his door across the hall open at the hour when the house settles into its own weight—the tick of cooling beams, the sigh of the chimney—and then his footsteps, soft in the way of a man who has learned to listen through his soles.

He found me in my room with the lamp still burning, pretending to mend a coat sleeve I had no intention of mending. He stood in the doorway a moment, a tall grey shape filling it, and I watched him read the light in the room the way he read everything: by its warmth, by the way the flame leaned when I shifted, by whatever small currents a body makes moving through air.

"You can't sleep either," I said, uselessly, and he smiled at the vibration of it against the floorboards—he felt my voice through his feet sometimes, he'd told me once, low tones especially, and so I spoke low for him, always, without deciding to.

He crossed the room and took my hands.

This was not unusual between us. In fifteen years we had built a language out of palms, out of pressure and tracing, a script older than any alphabet because it asked nothing of ears or eyes. But tonight his hands were cold and deliberate, and he turned my right hand over as if it were a document he had been cataloguing all his life and only now permitted himself to open.

He signed into my palm, slowly, pressing each word like a seal into wax:

*You. The berth. Yours.*

"Ovid." I started to pull back and he held on, gentle and immovable, the way he held a banned hymnal he was transcribing—as though the thing in his hands were already sacred and he merely its temporary custodian.

He pressed on. *Listen.* His finger drew the word's full weight into my skin. *I am sixty-one years. My hands are going. Last winter I misread eleven pages of the Ferrenze hymnal and did not discover it until spring. A man who cannot trust his hands cannot carry a record. But you—* and here he stopped, and I felt the tremor pass through his fingers, the one thing about himself he could not edit, *—you have hands that lie beautifully. You have lied with your whole body for fifteen years and never once broken. The archive needs a liar. I am only honest. Honest men die first in annexations; it's practically a law.*

"I won't take it," I said. "I've already—"

*Already decided,* he signed, cutting me off, and his mouth did something I had rarely seen it do: it went hard. *So have I. We are two people who have both decided. Do you know what that means, girl? It means tomorrow one of us loses. That is what deciding is.*

He released my hand then, but only to take up the other, and what he wrote into that palm I will keep until my own hands fail:

*Fifteen years ago you walked into my archive soaked to the bone and carrying a false name, and I let you in because your hands shook exactly the way honest liars' hands shake. I have watched you become the daughter I didn't get to choose. Choosing you is not sacrifice. It is the only competent decision left in this house.*

Then he did the cruelest, kindest thing. He folded my fingers closed over the words, sealing them, the way one seals a letter, and he pressed his lips briefly to my knuckles—an old scholar's gesture, the blessing given to a completed manuscript—and left before I could answer. He knew I would try to answer. He arranged his exit so that answering would require chasing an old deaf man down a dark hallway, and he knew I would not disgrace either of us by running.

I sat with my closed fist in my lap for a long time. When I finally opened it, the words were gone, of course. Skin keeps nothing. That is either mercy or design.

* * *

Marta did not come to me at all. That was her method. Marta had been born in the republic's first year; she had buried its promises and its betrayers and half its census takers, and she had learned that the most powerful things arrive unannounced and are discovered rather than received.

I found her evidence near midnight, when I went down to bank the stove a second time. She was asleep—or performing sleep, which for Marta was indistinguishable, she claimed, after ninety-odd years of practice—in her chair by the window, wrapped in the shawl Sef had stolen for her two birthdays ago. On the table beside her, weighted under the salt cellar where no one could mistake it for litter, lay her wedding ring.

I stood very still. The ring was iron, old work, the kind forged in the republic's first optimistic decade when they believed iron was more honest than gold. She had worn it for seventy years. I had seen her hands a thousand times—kneading bread, burning letters, signing the trading-places trick into my palm during the long lessons—and the ring was simply part of them, the way a knot is part of a tree. I had never once imagined the bare finger beneath.

There was a scrap of paper under the salt cellar too. Her handwriting, which had gone thin and spidery but refused entirely to surrender: *For passage. Don't argue with an old woman, it wastes the night. Iron buys quiet on the black ships—they respect what doesn't shine. Sell it, wear it, throw it in the harbor, but go.*

I looked at her. Her breathing was even, theatrical, perfect. I said, quietly, "Marta."

Nothing. Not a flicker. Ninety years of remembering too much had made her memory a fortress and her forgetting an art, and she could deploy either at will.

"Marta, I'm not leaving."

Her mouth moved, barely, against the shawl. I had to lean close to catch it. "Decide before you love us more," she whispered, the same words from the council, the ones that had gone through the room like a blade through water. "That's the whole instruction, girl. Everything else is commentary."

"And your ring?"

"My husband traded places on a list in the purge year," she said, still not opening her eyes, still wearing sleep like a mask worn slightly crooked. "A man named Aldous went north in his coat and lived forty years because of it. My husband went south in his own name and lasted nine days. I have thought about that trade every day since, and I have concluded—" here her voice did something ancient and amused, "—that the coat was wasted on Aldous. Take the ring. Iron remembers whose hand it warms next. Let it be yours a while."

She turned her face to the window then, decisively, and I understood the audience was over. I picked up the ring. It was heavier than it looked; iron always lies about its weight until you lift it. I did not put it on. I put it in my pocket, against my palm, where Ovid's words had been, and climbed the stairs carrying two inheritances I had not agreed to receive.

* * *

Sef was waiting in my room.

He was sitting on the floor with his back against my bed, knees drawn up, and beside him—this was how I knew, before he said anything, this was the confession entire—sat my bag. Packed. Not hastily: packed the way Sef forged manifests, with obsessive care, every fold squared, every strap buckled, my spare stockings rolled, the cipher's decoy ledger tucked where I kept it, even the tin of vinegar-sweets I bought from the harbor woman and pretended not to love.

"How long have you been in my room?"

"An hour." He didn't apologize. Sef had run away from a house with servants at fourteen and had been apologizing for nothing since. "It took that long to pack properly. You own almost nothing, did you know that? Fifteen years and you own almost nothing. I had room left over. I put in the atlas pages. The ones you work on at night when you think we're asleep."

My chest did something complicated. "Sef—"

"Don't." He said it fast, before I could spend anything. "Don't give me the speech. I've heard everyone's speech all day. Ovid's speech has Latin in it. Marta's speech has a dead husband in it. Mine doesn't have a speech. Mine has a bag."

"Sef, you're nineteen."

"I'm aware. I count." He got up, and I saw that he had dressed for the harbor already, in the dark clothes he wore for forging runs, and my stomach dropped before my mind caught up. "Here's what I figured out today, while you were all deciding for each other. Nobody in this house will take the berth if it means watching you stay. So we'll stand at that gangplank tomorrow and we'll have a beautiful stalemate, and the ship will leave empty, and the exemption will die with the republic, and we'll all have been noble together, which is just another word for dead together with better posture."

"So what's your plan? Put me on the ship by force? Ovid tried lifting me onto things when I was nine. It worked once."

"No." He picked up the bag and held it out, and his hands were shaking—I had never seen Sef's hands shake, not over a forgery, not over a harbor raid, not once in six years, and the sight of it frightened me more than the boots on the harbor road had. "My plan is that I'm not going to be at the gangplank."

"What?"

"Ilse's network moves papers at dawn. Someone has to carry the duplicate manifests to the north quay before the harbor closes. Ilse can't—her father watches her. It has to be someone the guards don't catalog yet. That's me. That's been me for months." He pushed the bag into my arms. "By noon I'll be on the north side of the breakwater. By the time the manifest is final, I'll be a rumor. You'll write my name in the blank line because there will be no one there to refuse it, and by the time I come back—if I come back—it'll be done, and you'll be at sea, and furious, which is how I prefer you. Furious people survive. It's the tender ones the lists eat."

"Sef, if you're not on that ship and I'm not on that ship—"

"Then the berth goes to waste and Ovid's archive burns and Marta's ring buys nothing, and everything we did was decoration." His voice cracked on *decoration*, just barely, a boy's crack inside a man's certainty. "You told us the mission was always a choice. Fine. This is mine. I'm choosing the errand. I'm very good at errands. Ask anyone I've robbed."

I wanted to hold him. I wanted it the way the drowning want air—entirely, stupidly, without technique. But he stepped back before I could, quick as he'd stepped out of his father's house at fourteen, and he tapped the bag once, twice, the way he tapped finished forgeries before sliding them across a counter.

"It's packed," he said. "Everything you need. I checked twice." And then, softer, at the door, not turning around: "You packed mine too, once. First night I came here. I found my old bag by the stairs in the morning with bread in it and a map with the safe roads marked. I never said thank you. Six years. Terrible manners."

"Sef—"

"The bread was terrible, by the way. Marta's baking has improved enormously since. Tell her I noticed." And he was gone, down the stairs and out into the dark, toward the harbor, toward dawn, toward the errand that was really a goodbye wearing a courier's coat.

* * *

Afterward I stood alone in my room with the packed bag at my feet and the ring in my pocket and Ovid's sealed words fading from my palms, and I finally let myself understand what the whole night had been.

They had rehearsed it. All three of them, separately, in the private workshops of their own hearts, they had rehearsed giving me away—Ovid with his liturgy of the hands, Marta with her iron and her dead husband's arithmetic, Sef with his bag and his errand and his unbearable competence. They had each arrived at the same conclusion by different roads, the way separate surveyors will independently fix the same summit. They had decided I was the one who should live.

And the terrible thing—the thing I carried down the dark stairs and into the kitchen where the cipher's page waited with its patient blank line—was not that they were wrong. It was that I could feel, rising in me like water finding its level, the beginning of agreement. Because they had each argued from strength, not weakness. Ovid had argued from his failing hands. Marta from her ninety years and her finished account. Sef from his speed and his anonymity and his whole unwritten future, which he was suddenly, furiously willing to spend as collateral.

They were not sacrificing themselves. They were investing me.

I sat down at the table. I took out the ring and set it beside the blank line, iron against paper, the republic's first metal beside the empire's first form. Outside, the boots on the harbor road had stopped for the night, but I could hear the sea working at the pilings, and somewhere beyond the breakwater, faint as a thought, the last ship riding at anchor with one warm berth and one empty name.

Tomorrow the troops would come with the new flag and the new forms. Tomorrow the harbor would close at dusk.

Tonight, I dipped my pen.

And that was when I heard the front gate—soft, practiced, counted—and through the window saw a lantern moving up our path with the particular rhythm of a man who walks counting exits, and I understood that Rafael had chosen this night, of all nights, to stop being a census taker.
