## 16. Annexation Day

The bells began wrong.

Isandro heard them from the kitchen, where she had been standing since before dawn with her hands wrapped around a cup that had long gone cold. The town's bells had always rung in thirds—call, answer, call again—the way they'd rung for as long as anyone could remember, and everyone in Vessany knew their grammar the way they knew the grammar of rain. But today the bells rang in fours. Four strokes, a pause, four more. A foreign arithmetic. Somewhere in the tower, hands she did not know were counting hours she did not own.

She set down the cup and went to the window.

The street below was already dressed for its own funeral. Bunting in the annexation colors—slate grey over gold, the empire's idea of mercy—had been hung overnight from the lintels of the chandlery and the dye-house, and someone had swept the cobblestones so thoroughly that the stones showed their old bones, pale and clean as teeth. A town scrubbed for inspection. A town pretending it had always wanted to be looked at.

Down the road, past the well, the first trucks were coming. She counted them out of habit: six, seven, eight, each one riding low with soldiers, each one trailing a smell she could catch even from here—canvas, machine oil, and under it something sweeter, like vinegar left too long in the sun. New flag on the lead truck, furled tight as a wound. They would unfurl it at noon, when the magistrate read the dissolution decree from the steps of the customs house, when the Republic of Vessany formally ceased to have ever been anything at all.

Noon. And the ship's manifest final by then. The harbor closing at dusk. Isandro pressed her palm flat against the cool glass and felt, beneath the skin of her wrist, the faint heat of the seal—the cipher's last living ember, still carrying its single blank line toward the only place it could be spent.

Behind her, Marta moved through the kitchen without light, laying out bread and salt pork on the table as if this were any morning, as if the household were any household. She moved slowly now, but nothing about her was slow. Isandro had learned to read the difference years ago: the difference between an old woman's body and an old woman's attention. Marta's attention was a blade laid flat on the table, waiting.

"They've swept the streets," Marta said, not turning. "You sweep before a wedding or before an execution. Today it's both."

"I know."

"You don't eat."

"Later." Isandro turned from the window. "Where's Sef?"

"Gone before the bells. Took his bag." Marta's hands paused over the bread knife. "He didn't say goodbye. He said *see you at the harbor*. There's a difference in a boy who's decided something. He walks differently when he's decided. Like you do."

Isandro looked at the woman who remembered too much and found, as always, that there was nowhere safe to rest her eyes. "And Ovid?"

"In the archive since yesterday's dark. Sealing the last boards." Marta finally turned, and her face was the face she wore for hard weather: plain, dry, unsurprised. "He asked me to tell you the wall is finished. All forty-one histories. He says whoever opens it will find the town whole inside, even if the town outside is not." She set the knife down with a small deliberate click. "Eat something, girl. You can't write a name into a manifest with shaking hands."

* * *

Rafael stood at the customs house steps among the other census men, holding his ledger against his chest the way the soldiers held their rifles, and understood that he had become a kind of armament himself—an instrument pointed at a town, loaded with paper.

The square filled through the morning. Troops in slate-grey formed two lines from the harbor road to the customs house, their boots finding a rhythm that made the cobbles hum. Civilians pressed behind rope barriers, quiet in the particular way of people who had practiced being quiet. Rafael scanned faces he had spent three months learning: the harbormaster with his daughter Ilse beside him, her eyes doing the quick inventory work Rafael recognized because he had once done it himself—counting boats, counting guards, counting minutes. The dyer's widow. the ferryman. And at the edge of the crowd, half-hidden behind the well, Ovid—a deaf man standing alone in a roaring square, reading the annexation through the soles of his feet.

The magistrate took the steps at noon exactly. He was a local man, Rafael knew; his brother had sat on the republic's first council. He read the decree in a voice sanded smooth by rehearsal:

*"By order of the Imperial Commission, the Republic of Vessany, having failed to maintain sovereign administration of its territories, is hereby dissolved. Its records pass to the Empire. Its persons pass to the Empire. From this hour, the word 'republic' is removed from official usage."*

Removed from official usage. As though a country were a misspelling. As though fifty years of laws and hymns and harbor ledgers were ink that could be scraped. Around Rafael, the crowd absorbed the sentence the way ground absorbs rain—nothing visible, everything changed.

Then the forms came out. That was the part Rafael had been dreading, the part his superiors had designed with their usual genius for cruelty disguised as procedure: every adult in the square would sign a declaration of subjecthood, line by line, name by name, while the new flag rose over the harbor and the ship that stood in the roads—*the last ship*, though officially it was merely a mail packet—loaded cargo no one would describe aloud.

He watched Isandro come forward in the queue. She wore her widow's grey, her hands folded, her face arranged into the mild patience of a mapmaker's daughter with nothing to hide. When her turn came she signed the form in a steady hand—her false name, her false trade, her false grief—and Rafael, watching her fingers, saw what no one else in the square could see: the smallest tremor as she lifted the pen. Not fear. Restraint. The tremor of a hand holding back everything it wanted to write instead.

Their eyes met for half a second across the table. In that half-second Rafael performed the calculation he had been performing for weeks, the one that ate him from the inside: the report due tonight would say the courier's seal was recovered intact, roster corrupted beyond recovery, exemption untraceable—or it would say nothing at all, because tonight he might be arrested before he wrote anything. Either way, the blank line on the manifest belonged now entirely to her. He had burned his bridges one falsified day at a time until all that remained was this: a gangplank, a deadline, and a name.

The flag went up. The crowd did not cheer, and did not weep, and the silence was the loudest thing Rafael had ever heard.

* * *

Sef reached the harbor gate at half past eleven with a forged manifest in his coat and his heart going like a trapped bird.

He had told himself all night that he wouldn't run. That he'd stand at the gangplank and refuse, properly, loudly, the way he'd refused at the kitchen table—that he'd give the berth back to her the way you return something borrowed. But walking those last streets, past the swept cobbles and the grey-and-gold bunting, past soldiers checking papers with gloved hands, he had discovered something shameful in himself: his feet kept choosing forward. His body, it turned out, wanted to live, and wanted it so badly it was willing to make a liar of everything he'd said.

At the gate, a queue. At the head of the queue, a table, and at the table a clerk with the empire's stamp and a manifest book open to its final page. Behind the clerk, the gangplank, and beyond it the ship—ugly, rust-streaked, the most beautiful thing Sef had ever seen.

Ilse was there, working the queue's edge with her father's authority draped over her like a borrowed coat, whispering to families, sliding folded papers between hands. When she saw Sef she did not smile. She caught his sleeve and drew him into the shadow of a net-drying frame.

"You're the fourth line," she said quietly.

"What?"

"The priority list. Your street. Three names printed, and a blank where the fourth should be." Her eyes were fierce and frightened. "I've been thinking about that blank all week. Lists don't have blanks unless someone means to fill them by hand. Someone with authority meant to choose, at the last minute, and I don't think it was going to be mercy." She pressed something into his palm—a folded transit card, real stock, forged countersign. "Whatever happens at that table, walk, don't run. Running reads as guilt."

"I'm not getting on the ship," Sef said, and heard how thin his voice came out.

Ilse looked at him the way Ovid looked at a damaged book—not with pity, but with assessment, deciding whether the thing could be mended. "Yes, you are," she said. "Because a girl I've never met spent her whole life keeping a promise for you, and because I've watched enough ships leave to know that staying isn't always the brave choice. Sometimes it's just the one with better views." She squeezed his hand once, hard, and stepped back into the crowd, already someone else's rescue.

The queue moved. Twelve ahead of him. Nine. Six. Sef stood in the salt wind and thought about the atlas he'd seen on Isandro's desk once, half-finished, coastlines drawn in her careful hand, and he thought: *she maps hiding places, and I am about to become one.*

Three ahead. Two.

From the customs house side of the harbor, faint under the gull-cries and the boot-rhythm, he heard the bells begin again—their wrong, foreign count of four—and understood that noon had passed somewhere, that the republic had died on schedule, and that the only piece of it still breathing was standing in a queue behind him wearing his face.

* * *

Ovid felt the ship before he saw it.

He had taken his position by the well not to watch but to listen with his body—to stand where the ground carried everything. Through the soles of his feet came the tramp of the honor guard, the groan of the gangplank taking weight, the deep irregular shudder of the ship's engine warming in its hull, a vibration like some enormous animal deciding whether to wake. The crowd around him was a field of small tremors: shuffling shoes, held breath released, the collective lean of two hundred bodies swaying toward the water.

He read the square the way he read all rooms. The soldiers' footsteps came in metronome pairs—confidence. The clerk's pen scratched in bursts—bureaucracy, which was slow, which was beatable. And cutting through both, arriving late and moving fast along the harbor road: one runner's stride, urgent, uneven. Then voices, too many, overlapping; then a whistle.

Ovid turned toward the commotion with the rest of the crowd and saw the thing that made his chest go cold: an imperial officer at the manifest table, tall, grey-gloved, holding up one hand—and the queue stopping. The clerk standing. The stamp put down.

The officer was speaking. Ovid couldn't hear a word, but he could see the shape of the announcement in the crowd's body, the way panic moves through people like wind through wheat: heads turning toward the harbor mouth, toward the roadstead, where—Ovid followed their eyes—a second ship had appeared. Grey hull. Gun mounts. A navy cutter, flying the imperial ensign, dropping anchor directly across the lane the mail packet would need to leave by.

Beside him, Isandro arrived as if summoned by the vibration itself, breathless, her hand finding his sleeve. She leaned close and spoke near his jaw where he could feel the words:

"The cutter's here to inspect outbound manifests. Rafael warned me an hour ago—it wasn't in his reports, it was ordered from the provincial capital. They're checking for the seal." Her fingers were digging into his arm. "The officer wants the final manifest now. Before the packet can move. Every name verified against the extraction list."

Ovid looked at her face and saw the future arrive in it: noon had come and gone, dusk was a handful of hours away, the harbor would close on schedule, and between the family and the sea now stood a table, a stamp, a gun-grey ship, and one blank line that had suddenly become the most examined piece of paper on the coast.

He raised his hands and signed to her, fast, the private language of their kitchen: *How much time?*

Isandro looked at the cutter, at the queue, at the sky measuring out its hours. When she answered, her signs were very small and very steady.

*Until dusk. One berth. And every eye in the empire about to look at it.*

Out in the roads, the packet's horn sounded—one long note, impatient, alive—and Ovid felt it come up through the cobbles and into his bones like a heart insisting on itself. The day was not finished with them. The day had barely begun.
