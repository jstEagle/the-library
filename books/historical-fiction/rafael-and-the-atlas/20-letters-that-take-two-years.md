## 20. Letters That Take Two Years

The first letter arrived in the pocket of a fishmonger's coat, folded into a square the size of a communion wafer and wrapped in waxed paper that had once, judging by its smell, held herring.

Ilse's network worked like that. Nothing traveled by post—post was annexation's bloodstream now, every envelope opened, read, filed, remembered against you. Instead letters moved the way contraband had always moved through Vessany: inside coat linings, beneath false bottoms of flour tins, sewn into the hems of dresses worn by women with honest faces traveling to honest markets. A letter might take eight months to cross the water. It might take two years. It arrived when it arrived, and the waiting was part of its weight.

Isandro found it on a Tuesday in the fish market, tucked into her basket between two mackerel, and she knew before she touched it what it was because no one else in Vessany folded paper into squares anymore. The census men folded their forms into thirds. The occupation authorities folded theirs lengthwise. But Sef had learned his folding from forging shipping manifests for six years, and he folded paper the way he did everything—compactly, defiantly, as if daring the world to make him waste space.

She didn't open it at the market. She didn't open it walking home. She walked home at her usual pace, past the harbor where the annexation flag hung limp in windless air, past the archive with its sealed wall, up the stairs of the house where Ovid was cataloguing by touch and Marta was dozing in the chair that faced the sea, and only then, with the door closed behind her and both of them looking up at whatever her face was doing, did she unfold it.

*Dear everyone,* it began, in handwriting that had not improved since he'd left. *I have been trying to write this letter for four months but every time I start I sound like a eulogy so I am starting over and this time I will just tell you things.*

*Things:*

*1. The sea is bigger than any map. I know how that sounds coming from me, who has drawn coastlines since I was nine, but there is drawing a coastline and then there is standing on the deck of a ship for nineteen days watching nothing happen to the horizon and understanding that all my maps were lies of scale.*

*2. I got seasick in a way I will be describing to my grandchildren. Ilse says hers is worse. Ilse came too. She said someone had to keep an eye on me which is a lie, she came because her own list was finished and there was nothing left to forge in that town worth staying for.*

*3. The exiled archivists are real. I found them. Or they found us, which is more accurate, because within a week of docking a man appeared at our boarding house asking for "the young man who carries old paper" and I nearly died of fright until Ovid's duplicate fell out of my bag and the man went white and sat down on the stairs.*

Isandro read the first three aloud, her voice doing that thing it did now whenever Sef's name came up—the careful steadiness, the overcontrol—and Ovid turned his face toward the sound of her reading the way other people turn toward light. When she paused, he tapped twice on the table: *continue.*

*4. They wept over the braille boards. I mean actually wept, grown men and women, archivists who spent forty years memorizing banned hymnals so they could recite them in prison, and they put their fingers on those boards and read them like people reading letters from the dead. One woman, Dr. Amaya Voss—she signed her name for me three times because I kept forgetting it, she says names matter, she says the republic's whole crime was deciding some names mattered less—Dr. Voss says your boards are the most complete record of the eastern provinces' suppression anywhere in exile. Ovid. You hear me? Most complete. Anywhere. She wants you to know that if you ever come, there is a chair here with your name on it and students who would learn from you.*

Ovid's hands were still for a long moment. Then, slowly, he made the sign Isandro had taught him years ago and almost never used: the one that meant something too large for the small vocabulary of their kitchen. He pressed his flat palm over his heart, then turned it outward toward the room.

*Given,* it meant. *Handed forward.*

*5. I have a job. It is boring and I love it. I copy maps for a trading concern, real ones, legal ones, and the foreman says I have unusual precision for someone so young which is the funniest sentence anyone has ever said to me and I laughed for a full minute and had to explain to the foreman that in my last position precision was a capital offense.*

*6. Marta: the bread here is terrible. I say this with authority. Your bread was better than anything on this continent and I include the bakery that won the prize at the autumn fair, whose rye loaf I ate in protest.*

Marta laughed—a short, cracked sound—but her eyes were wet, and she pulled her shawl around her shoulders the way she did when feeling arrived faster than dignity could manage it.

*7. Isandro. This is the hard paragraph. I have started it eleven times.*

*You gave me the berth. I know why. I argued about it for the whole first year and then somewhere in the second year I stopped arguing and started trying to deserve it, and I want you to know those are different projects and the second one is harder. Every day I try to be worth the arithmetic. Some days I think I am. Some days I think no one is, and that the whole point was never whether I deserved it but whether you could live with the choice, and so I am writing today mostly to tell you: I saw the harbor from the deck. I saw you standing there. Rafael next to you. And I want you to know that from the water, the two of you looked like a map legend—like the little symbols that tell you what the lines mean. That's what you are. You're the key to the map of that place, and I'm carrying the territory now, out here, where nobody can burn it.*

*Don't answer this. There's no way to answer it that doesn't cost money or risk. Just know that I eat well, I sleep well, I work with my hands, and every morning I fold my papers the way you fold yours, badly, defiantly, wasting space on purpose because we can afford to now.*

*Your stray,
Sef*

*P.S. Ilse says hello and also that she is teaching me to sail, which means she shouts and I pretend not to hear, which Ovid will understand makes us family already.*

*P.P.S. Tell the fishmonger his herring wrapper kept my letter dry across an ocean and he should know he's part of history now, though don't tell him that, he'd charge extra.*

* * *

The second letter took fourteen months and arrived inside a hollowed-out cheese wheel, which Marta declared wasteful and then refused to throw away.

By then the household had learned the ritual. Letters were read aloud, always, at the table, with tea, because Ovid could not hear them otherwise unless Isandro spoke slowly enough for him to follow the vibration of her voice in the floorboards, and because Marta claimed she couldn't hear quiet reading anymore, and because saying the words out loud made Sef present in a way silent reading never could. He became, during those readings, a member of the household again—not the absent son but the boy at the end of the table, talking with his mouth full, making the archivists laugh in a language they were still learning.

*Dear everyone, again,*

*I have news of the large and small varieties.*

*Large: they have printed the first volume. OUR first volume. Dr. Voss let me set the type myself for the title page even though my letters came out crooked, and she said crooked letters prove human hands made it, which is either wisdom or charity, possibly both. It is called "A Register of What Was Almost Lost, Volume One: The Eastern Provinces." Inside it are forty-one hymnals, the harbor ledgers of three towns that officially never existed, and the testimony of a woman named Marta Okonkwo-Vessany, born the republic's first year, recorded from memory by a man named Ovid who heard her tell it for thirty years without hearing a word of it.*

Wait—Isandro stopped. Read it again silently. Read it aloud again.

*Marta,* she said. *He put you in the book. He transcribed your stories—he must have written them down before he left, from memory, everything you told us at the table—*

Marta had gone very still. Her hands lay flat on the table, palms down, the posture of a woman steadying herself against weather. Born the republic's first year. She had been born in its first year; she had told them so a hundred times, and they had listened the way you listen to old people, politely, assuming the past was decoration. Somewhere in his last months, Sef had been taking minutes.

*I asked her permission afterward,* the letter continued, *by letter, through the network, and her answer took ten months to reach me and consisted of one line: "About time someone wrote it down before I forget the good parts." So I assume consent.*

Marta made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and covered her mouth with her hand.

*Small news: Ilse and I are to be married in the spring. I am telling you in a letter because I cannot watch anyone's face while they react, which is cowardice, but also because I wanted the announcement to arrive the way everything important arrives in our family—slowly, smuggled, wrapped in something that smells faintly of fish.*

*Ovid: I am learning to sign. Badly. There is a deaf typesetter at the press named Corin and he is patient with me and merciless about my grammar. I told him about you and he learned your name-sign first thing—you're the one with the flat palm turning outward, given, handed forward—and now when I mess up a sign he does your sign back at me like a rebuke. You are disciplining me from across an ocean. I hope you're pleased.*

Ovid was pleased. His pleasure filled the room like heat from a stove. He stood, crossed to Isandro, and took her hand—their private channel—and signed into it, deliberately, so she could relay: *Tell him my grammar was bad for thirty years and I turned out fine.*

She laughed and promised to send it back through Ilse's channels, knowing it would take a year, knowing that was the price and the shape of their life now: love moving at the speed of smuggled paper, arriving late, arriving anyway.

*Last thing. Dr. Voss asks me constantly about the wall. Whether the histories stayed sealed. Whether anyone remembers where. I tell her yes, and I tell her copies are scattered through the paper network, and she nods and says, "Then the republic isn't dead, it's distributed." I liked that so much I wrote it on the shop wall where the foreman pretends not to see it.*

*The republic isn't dead. It's distributed.*

*I miss you. I miss the terrible bread. I miss being someone's stray. Write when you can. Fold badly.*

*Sef*

* * *

There were seven letters in all, over five years. Isandro kept them in the drawer where the cipher used to live, back when she carried a country under her ribs. Each one arrived smelling of whatever had hidden it—herring, cheese, lamp oil, once, inexplicably, cloves—and each one was read aloud at the table with tea going cold because no one remembered to drink it.

They learned Sef's life in installments, the way you learn a serial: the marriage (a description of Ilse's vows that made Marta cry), the stillbirth of their first child (a letter that arrived in oilcloth, that Isandro read alone first, that she read aloud anyway because he had trusted them with it and silence would have been its own kind of loss), the birth of their daughter two years later, a girl named Vessa, after a country that no longer existed on any map except the one Isandro was drawing.

Because Isandro was drawing one. Had been, for years—the atlas, slow, obsessive, each hiding place marked with who waited there. And lately she had begun copying passages from Sef's letters into its margins, so that the atlas was becoming something else: not a record of concealment but a correspondence, the family's whole conversation with itself, mapped.

It was on an evening in the sixth year—Rafael in the kitchen repairing a kettle he had no intention of finishing repairing, Ovid sorting duplicates of the braille histories into travel cases for the exiled archivists' second request, Marta asleep with her chin on her chest—that the eighth letter came.

It came early. That was the first strange thing. Ilse's letters took a year minimum; this one had crossed in seven months. It came in oilcloth, which meant importance or grief. And when Isandro unfolded it at the table, alone, before the others woke, she saw that the hand was not Sef's.

It was Ilse's. Precise, unhurried, the writing of a woman who forged documents for a living and never wasted a stroke.

*Mrs. Okonkwo, Ovid, Marta, and Isandro,*

*Forgive me writing in his place. Sef's hands are occupied.*

*Vessa was born on Tuesday. Mother and daughter are loud and healthy and already arguing. Sef has not put her down long enough to write, so I am writing to tell you what he would take three pages to say: she has his stubbornness and my handwriting.*

*Also this. Dr. Voss came to the house yesterday with the final page proofs of Volume Four. The last section is called "The Network." It documents every route, every hiding place, every household in the old republic that sheltered forbidden records—including yours. All of you. By name. The names are printed in the book now, in a library, in a free country, where no census can touch them. Sef insisted your street address go in exactly as it was. He said, and I quote him at length because he quoted himself at length: "They hid in plain sight. Let the record show the plain sight."*

*The book ships next month. A copy is coming to you inside a bale of wool via the usual route. Expect it around midwinter.*

*One more thing, and then I'll stop, because Sef is hovering behind me making faces.*

*He asked me to tell you that the atlas you sent—the one with all your markings, who waited where—he has shown it to Dr. Voss, and Dr. Voss wept, and then Dr. Voss did something unprecedented: she had it copied, plate by plate, and the copies now sit beside Ovid's braille boards in the archive reading room, under glass, labeled "The Route." Not the treasure. The route.*

*Inheritance is the route. He told me you wrote that. He tells everyone. Our daughter will grow up thinking it's a nursery rhyme.*

*Come see us. Any of you. All of you. The chair with Ovid's name on it is still there. So is mine, next to Sef's, though he hogs it.*

*With love and salt air,
Ilse*

Isandro sat with the letter in the grey beginning of morning, and the house breathed around her—Rafael's kettle-clinking, Ovid's careful sorting, Marta's sleep-rattle—and she understood that the distance she had measured for six years in months and smuggling routes had quietly changed units. It was measured now in volumes printed, in daughters born, in names set in type beyond the reach of any census taker who would ever knock.

She put the kettle on. She woke the house the way she always did, gently, door by door, and when they were assembled and the tea poured, she began to read.

But halfway through Ilse's letter, a knock came at the door.

Not the census knock—she knew that knock, had catalogued its variations across two regimes. This was lighter. Two raps, apologetic, the knock of someone who counted exits.

She opened it to find a courier girl of perhaps sixteen, sea-browned, breathless, holding out a parcel wrapped in wool bale-cloth with a customs tag dated midwinter—a year ago, lost, rerouted, finally delivered.

Inside, under the wool, was the book.

*A Register of What Was Almost Lost, Volume Four.* Their names on the third page. Their street. Their plain sight.

And tucked into the binding, in handwriting Isandro knew better than her own—a folded scrap, creased badly, defiantly, wasting space:

*For the margins of the atlas. The route continues. — S.*
