## 21. The New Census

The knock came on the first morning of the thaw, when the eaves were dripping their long silver sentences into the street and Marta had opened the window over the sink for the first time since autumn, letting the cold air carry away the last of the winter's burnt-letter smell.

Three knocks. Measured. The knock of a man who has been trained to knock and has never quite unlearned it.

I was at the table with my atlas pages spread before me—the harbor section, the ink still tacky where I'd drawn Ilse's father's warehouse with its false wall and its true heart—and I looked up at that knock the way one looks up at weather. Not with fear. With recognition. A year is not so long that the body forgets what a census taker sounds like against a door.

"Isandro," Rafael said from the kitchen doorway, without turning from the stove where he was warming milk the way Ovid liked it—scalded almost past bearing, sweetened past sense. "Do you want me to go upstairs?"

It was a real question. That was the miracle of it, still, after everything: that he asked as if either answer were possible, as if my household were a country whose borders he respected rather than a room he had once burned down and rebuilt with his own hands.

"No," I said. "I think I'm done hiding you."

"I wasn't asking about me."

"I know." I closed the harbor page and weighted it with the brass letter-opener Sef had sent from across the sea, wrapped in oilcloth, stamped with a port I had never seen except through his descriptions of it. "But I'm not answering for you anymore. Or for Ovid. Or for anyone but myself. One question at a time. That's all I can promise anyone now—one honest question, honestly answered."

Marta appeared in the hall with her shawl already pinned, her hands folded, wearing the expression she wore for funerals and for the arrival of officials, which she had long ago determined were nearly the same thing. Ovid stood behind her, feeling the doorframe with two fingers, reading the vibration of the third knock before it even landed. His face did something I had learned to read across years of shared silence: *one man, alone, no escort, boots worn soft.* He signed it small against his thigh, where only I could see.

*One man,* I signed back. *Alone.*

*Counting exits?* Ovid asked, and there was humor in it now, the old wound become an old joke.

*No,* I signed. *This one isn't counting anything.*

I went to the door.

* * *

He was young. That was the first thing. The empire sent them young because youth had not yet learned what questions cost, and because a young man's notebook looked like innocence even when it was a net.

He wore the gray of the new administration—not the black of the annexation year, which had frightened people properly, but gray, the color of ledgers and of rain, the color they had decided meant *ordinary*. His satchel was regulation. His boots, though—Ovid had been right; the boots were soft, civilian-soft, secondhand or inherited, and I thought: this one was not born to the empire either. This one had been conscripted by paperwork the way we all had been, once, in our various republics.

"Good morning," he said. His accent was northern, the coast beyond the straits, the vowels rounded the way Sef's letters were beginning to round after a year among exiles. "Census of the Imperial Domain, fourth revision. I'll need to speak with the head of household."

"You're speaking with her."

Something flickered across his face—surprise, quickly filed away. He opened his book. The book was new, the paper crisp, the forms printed in both scripts now, the old republican alphabet and the imperial hand side by side like two languages pretending to be neighbors.

"Name of household head?"

And here is what I want you to understand, whoever reads this, whoever Ovid's braille boards someday teach: for fifteen years I had answered that question with a dead woman's name. *Hartwell.* The mapmaker's daughter, widowed, quiet, no commission, no history, no debts worth collecting. I had worn Hartwell the way one wears a coat in rain—so long that the shoulders remembered its weight better than my own name's lightness.

The young man waited. His pen hovered. Behind him the thaw dripped its silver sentences, and inside the house Rafael was warming milk he would not drink, and Marta was standing guard in a hallway she had defended with nothing but senility performed so well it had half become true, and Ovid was reading the grammar of the open door.

"My name," I said, and heard my own voice arrive in my own ears as if from a distance, "is Isandro Vey."

Not Hartwell. Not the courier designation they had burned into my ribs along with the seal. *Vey*—my mother's name, the one from before the oath, before the vinegar smell of that hospital corridor, before the handler with his kind voice telling me the code would name itself when the republic needed it. The republic had needed it. It had named itself too late, and it had named Sef, and I had put him on a ship with a horn that sounded like grief made mechanical, and none of that changed what my mother had called me across a kitchen smaller than this one, in a country that no longer existed on any map except the one I was drawing.

The pen scratched. "Vey," he repeated. "First name?"

"Isandro."

"Occupation?"

"Mapmaker."

"That's—" he checked his form, and I watched him find the line, the little printed box that had been designed by someone in the capital who believed towns like ours contained only farmers and fishermen and the occasional smith. "There's no box for that."

"Draw one," I said. "You're allowed. The forms have margins."

He looked up at me then, really looked, and I saw the moment the net in his hands became, briefly, just paper. "The instructions say deviations require notation."

"Notate it, then. Mapmaker. Marginal occupation. Marginal woman." I smiled, and the smile surprised me with its ease. Fifteen years of practiced smiles had been currency; this one was simply weather coming off my face. "Ask your questions. I'll answer all of them. Truthfully."

That word—*truthfully*—did more damage to him than any lie would have. He shifted his weight. Young men are taught that truth is the thing citizens conceal and the state extracts; they are not equipped for citizens who hand it over like bread.

* * *

He came inside, because the form required verification of dwellings, and I let him, because I had decided the night before—lying awake listening to the eaves—that this census would be the first document in fifteen years that described my actual life.

He counted the rooms wrong, because the house had been built by a smuggler with a talent for walls, and I corrected him, and he noted the correction in the margin next to my marginal occupation. He recorded Ovid as *archivist, deaf since childhood, resides*—and here I gave him Ovid's full name, the one from the banned hymnals' colophons, the one that had gotten three archivists shot in the purges Marta could recite like lullabies. The young man's pen stopped over that line for a long moment.

"This name appears in a restricted registry," he said carefully.

"It would. He catalogued banned books by touch for thirty years. If your registry doesn't include him, it's incomplete."

"I'm supposed to flag restricted names for review."

"You're supposed to record what's true. Flag whatever you like afterward. But write it down first." I poured him tea, because refusing hospitality to the empire in one's own kitchen is a luxury for people with stronger walls than mine, and also because Rafael was standing at the stove with his back to us, and I wanted the boy to see a household that had nothing left to hide and therefore nothing left to lose, which is the most dangerous thing a household can be.

He wrote: *Ovid Renner. Archivist. Restricted registry, flagged.* And then, unprompted, in the margin, in his own small hand: *deaf since childhood.*

"Marta Sorrel," I said next. "Born the first spring of the republic. She is older than the country that replaced it and will outlast yours too, if she decides to."

"That's sedition," he said, but softly, the way one notes the weather.

"It's arithmetic," I said. "Write it however you want."

* * *

He saved the hardest question for last, because they train them for that even when the training fails everywhere else. He closed his book partway, the way Rafael used to, the gesture of a man about to leave the form behind.

"There's a man in your kitchen," he said.

"Yes."

"He wasn't on last year's rolls."

"No. He arrived during the annexation administration, under a name that no longer exists, in a capacity that was never official, and he stayed." I set down the teapot. Through the doorway I could see Rafael's shoulders, unhurried, the milk steaming. "His name is Rafael Ilves. Formerly a courier for the administration that preceded yours, which is to say the one that won, which is to say the one that lost, depending on how far back you draw the line. He was arrested fourteen months ago for negligence in the handling of documents. He served his sentence. He was released eight weeks ago. He walked here from the interior because he had nowhere else, and because—" I paused, and found I did not want to soften it for the boy's sake, nor for mine. "Because I asked him to come home."

The pen did not move. The thaw dripped. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame, and I felt Ovid reading that sound too, cataloguing it among the day's vibrations: *wind, harmless, wind.*

"The forms don't have a category for that," the young man said finally.

"I know. Draw another margin."

He drew it. I watched him do it—watched his ruler-straight hesitation, then the freehand line, slightly crooked, the first crooked thing in that whole crisp book. In the space he wrote, slowly, checking each syllable against my pronunciation: *Rafael Ilves. Courier, former. Convicted, released. Resides.*

Then, in the margin beside it, without being asked, he wrote: *home.*

He didn't look up when he wrote it. I didn't thank him. Some things curdle when acknowledged.

* * *

When he was gone—I walked him to the door, and he shook my hand, and his grip was a boy's grip, uncalloused, and he said "thank you for your cooperation" in the tone of a man who means something entirely different and cannot say it—I stood in the doorway a while, watching him go down the street past Ilse's paper shop, where she was already in the doorway watching him too, and past the harbor road, where the cranes stood idle against a sky the color of the empire's uniforms.

Behind me, the kitchen breathed.

Rafael had set four cups out, though we were three. The fourth was Sef's, kept filled with nothing, kept facing the sea-facing window, the way we kept it every morning—a superstition of absence, a chair held at the world's table. Marta was already seated, working a knot out of her shawl fringe. Ovid had come in from the hall and taken his place with his palms flat on the table, feeling for the conversation the way he felt for hymnals in the dark.

"Well?" Rafael said.

"He drew margins," I said. "Two of them."

Marta snorted. "The empire will recall him for that. They always recall the ones who learn to draw."

"They'll recall him for the name I gave," I said. "Mine. Ovid's. Yours." I sat down and took the cup Rafael poured, and the tea was scalded past bearing and sweetened past sense, because he had made it the way Ovid liked it and handed it to me instead, and neither of us remarked on it. "They'll send someone to review the restricted registries. Maybe within the season. Maybe sooner."

"So we run again," Marta said. It was not a question and not a complaint. It was the oldest sentence in her repertoire, older than the republic, older than the purge lists, probably older than writing itself: *when they come, move.*

"No," I said.

Ovid's palms lifted a centimeter off the wood. *No?*

"We spent fifteen years learning how to disappear," I said. "Every one of us. You in the archive wall. Marta in her performed forgetting. Sef in forged manifests. Me in a dead girl's coat. And do you know what survived? What actually crossed the border, what reached Sef, what the exiled archivists are reading by lamplight right now?" I touched the table, near Ovid's hand, and he turned his palm up to meet my fingers, our private telegraph, the one language the empire had never managed to intercept. "The truth did. The names did. Ovid's boards. Sef's letters. Every honest line we ever hid inside the lying ones. Forgetting has a backup now—but only because we wrote things down. Only because somebody, eventually, said the name out loud."

I looked at Rafael. He was watching me with the expression I had learned to read across interrogation tables and kitchens alike: the ledger-face, the man tallying costs. But the ledger had changed currencies. I could see it. He was no longer counting what truth would cost us. He was counting what hiding it already had.

"So when they send their reviewer," I said, "we will be home. All of us. Under our own names. With the archive wall exactly where it is and duplicates scattered from here to the straits, in Ilse's paper and Sef's letters and forty braille boards that can be rebuilt by anyone who learns to read them. Let them come with their gray coats and their margins. We are the most documented household in this town, and none of it is hidden anymore, and that—" I laughed, short and disbelieving, at my own arithmetic, "—that is the safest we have ever been."

Rafael reached across the table and turned my cup a quarter-turn, an old courier's habit, aligning handles to some compass only he could see. "The reviewer they send," he said quietly, "will be someone like the boy. Someone who draws margins. They always are, the second time around. Empires get careless with their clerks." A pause. "Or someone like me, the first time around. And those ones—" he met my eyes, "—those ones can be turned. I should know. It took me twenty years and a kitchen."

Marta raised her cup in a toast to absolutely nothing. "To visibility," she said dryly. "Chosen, not imposed. May it bore them to death."

Ovid laughed—he felt our laughter through the table and joined it blind, which was somehow the truest sound in the room—and then his hands began to move, quick and bright, addressing me directly.

*What will you do now,* he signed, *now that no one needs you to be silent?*

I looked at the table where my atlas pages waited under the brass letter-opener from across the sea. The harbor section. The safehouse sections. Forty-one hiding places, each marked with who had waited there, each waiting to be finished, bound, and sent to a young man with rounded northern vowels who had inherited a country that existed nowhere but in the people still carrying it.

"Now," I said aloud, so the whole room could hear, so the house could hear, so the thaw outside could hear, "I draw the map."

I uncapped the ink.
