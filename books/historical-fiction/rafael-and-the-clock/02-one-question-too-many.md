## 2. One Question Too Many

Mara dressed in the dark because the light would take too long and because she wanted, for a few seconds more, to be no one at all — just a shape moving through a cold house, uncounted, unwritten. She could hear the gentleman below through the floorboards: the small civilized sounds of a man making himself comfortable. A chair drawn out. The tap of a walking stick set against a table leg. Sera's voice, pitched low and pleasant, offering tea that the household could not afford to offer.

She braided her hair quickly, fingers working without her attention. In the looking glass she caught only the ghost of herself — a pale smudge, ink-stained at the cuticles, the way she always was. Good. Ink stains were honest. Ink stains said *clerk*, said *copyist*, said *a woman who works with her hands and has nothing else to declare*. She had learned years ago that the best disguise was not a false face but a true one, worn at an angle.

Downstairs, the front room smelled of yesterday's fire and Sera's lavender water. And there he was, standing by the window as though he owned the view of the gray harbor below — a tall man in a good coat that had been made for someone slightly broader, hat in hand, his stick leaning against the chair Sera had offered him and which he had, notably, declined to sit in until invited twice.

He turned when Mara entered, and something happened in the space between them that she would spend the rest of the day refusing to name. Not recognition — nothing so dramatic. It was more like the moment on a chart when two contour lines nearly touch: nothing crosses, nothing joins, but the cartographer's eye notes the proximity forever.

"Miss Coldharbour," he said, and bowed exactly the correct amount.

"Mr. Vane," Sera supplied smoothly, from her post near the mantel. "Of the district census office."

"Vane," Mara repeated, and heard her own voice come out flat and careful, like a hand laid over a page so it cannot turn itself. "How official it all sounds."

"Official is the whole of my trade, I'm afraid." He smiled. He had a long face, patient, with eyes the gray of the sea before weather — the color of water deciding what to become. "Elias Vane. I'm told the household numbers four persons."

"You're told correctly," Sera said.

"And yet," he went on, mildly, as though remarking on the weather, "the last enumeration recorded three."

The silence lasted one full second. Mara felt it the way a helmsman feels a current take the keel — nothing visible changes, but everything underneath has shifted.

"The last enumeration," Sera said, "was taken by a man who counted our cook twice and forgot the house entirely. We assumed the office preferred accuracy now."

"We do prefer accuracy." He produced a ledger from inside his coat — a slim one, bound in green cloth, its corners soft from handling. "That's rather the point of me. May I?"

He did not wait for permission; he simply opened the ledger against his forearm, standing, and Mara understood that this was deliberate. A seated man settles in. A standing man can leave — or stay as long as he likes. Either way, the household remains slightly off balance, waiting.

"Head of household first. Mrs. Odile Coldharbour, widow. Age?"

"Fifty-eight," Sera said.

"Born where?"

Sera's teacup made a small sound against its saucer. Mara crossed to the window, ostensibly to adjust the curtain, actually to put distance between her face and his questions, and looked down at the harbor while Sera answered.

"She was born abroad, Mr. Vane. Her family were in trade. Mediterranean shipping, mostly. They settled here when she was a girl."

"Mediterranean shipping." He wrote. Mara watched his pen move in the window glass's reflection — quick, neat strokes, no hesitation. "And her maiden name? The schedule requires place of birth and family name at birth for every person enumerated. It's how we track migration, you understand. The movement of families. Where they come from. What they were called before they were called what they are called."

*What they were called before they were called what they are called.* The sentence turned over in Mara's mind like a coin showing both faces.

"Aldersey," Sera said.

Mara did not let her breath change. Aldersey was a name they had prepared years ago, rehearsed in the kitchen like lines for a play no audience would ever see, and Sera delivered it now with the flawless dullness of a woman reciting her own address. But Mara watched Elias Vane write it, and saw him pause — not at the word, but *after* it, his pen hovering above the paper for the length of a heartbeat, as though the ink itself required inspection.

"Aldersey," he repeated pleasantly. "A northern name, I'd have said."

"Her mother's people were northern. Her father's were southern. Families are rarely one thing." Sera smiled her careful smile. "More tea?"

"No, thank you." He closed that line of inquiry with a small tick of the pen, and moved on, and Mara understood she was watching a man work the coastline of their lives the way she worked a chart — sounding here, skirting there, noting depths. "Occupations. You keep the books, Miss Coldharbour?" This to Sera.

"I keep the house."

"And your brother? Mr. Tam Coldharbour."

"He works the docks."

"Which employer?"

"Various."

"Various," he echoed, writing it, letting the word sit in his ledger like a specimen pinned slightly wrong. "And the young lady?"

Mara turned from the window. "I'm a copyist. Maps and documents. For the late Mr. Hollis, the surveyor — you may know the name; his atlas of the coast is being finished posthumously. By me."

Something crossed his face then, quickly, gone before it resolved into any expression she could have sworn to. Interest. Not surprise — interest, the way a man leans toward a sound he has been listening for.

"Hollis," he said. "The atlas. Yes — I believe I've seen notices. A considerable work."

"It will be."

"And you finish it alone?"

"I finish it. Alone is a condition, not a boast."

His mouth curved — approval, or amusement at being parried, impossible to say. "A valuable skill," he said. "Making other men's knowledge permanent." Then, before the words had even cooled: "Your grandmother will be at home, I assume. The schedule requires each person present be seen, where practicable."

*There it is.* Mara felt the question arrive the way one feels the first drop of rain through a roof — not the storm, only the proof of the hole. Sera's smile remained perfectly fixed, but Mara knew her aunt's face the way she knew her own charts, and beneath the pleasantness the lines were drawn taut as rigging in a blow.

"My grandmother keeps to her room," Mara said. "Her health is poor. She sees few strangers."

"I'm hardly a stranger. I'm an instrument of the Crown." He said it lightly, self-deprecatingly, a joke about his own dull importance — and it was precisely the right note, disarming, reasonable, the kind of reasonableness that makes refusal look like guilt. "One glimpse. I needn't trouble her. A formality of presence — that the person enumerated exists. It takes ten seconds."

"She is asleep," Sera said.

"Then I'll return when she wakes."

"She sleeps most days now," Mara said. "It isn't a clockwork matter, Mr. Vane. Dementia does not keep office hours."

For the first time, something genuine moved through his composure — not sympathy exactly; she was too practiced at reading faces to mistake it for that. More like a man finding the depth he expected and hating that he'd been right. "I'm sorry," he said, and sounded it. "My own grandfather — no. Forgive me. That isn't why I've come."

"Why *have* you come?" Mara asked. It came out more direct than she intended, and she let it stand, because directness was also a costume, and sometimes the boldest lie was to stop lying for a moment.

"To count you," he said simply. "Four persons, one dwelling, occupations, names at birth—" the phrase again, smooth as water over stone, "*—and ages.* Ages are the hardest, do you know? People misstate them constantly. Vanity. Or the opposite." His gray eyes rested on her, mild, unhurried. "Some people make themselves younger. Some make themselves plainer than they are. Some give themselves names that fit the life they've built better than the ones they were born to."

"That must make your arithmetic very difficult," Mara said.

"It makes my work interesting." He tucked the ledger away and retrieved his stick, and Mara thought — hoped — he was done. He moved toward the door, paused at the threshold with his hand on the frame, and turned back with the air of a man remembering something trivial. "One more thing. A small one. The schedule requires it, and I'd hate to leave the entry incomplete."

"Another question," Sera murmured, "from a man who only had a few."

"Just the one." He smiled at her, unbothered. "Mrs. Coldharbour's family — the Alderseys, in trade, Mediterranean shipping. Would that be the same Alderseys who held berths at Vane & Sons' wharf, some thirty years back? The firm kept records. I've been reading them. One develops a taste for the older ledgers — the handwriting alone is worth the trouble."

Mara heard her own pulse. Once. Twice. The harbor light lay across the floorboards between them, and she stepped deliberately into it, deliberately casual, deliberately a copyist with ink on her hands and no history worth a ledger's attention.

"I wouldn't know," she said. "My grandmother doesn't speak of her girlhood. The sea took everyone she loved. She calls it a ledger, sometimes — did you know that? Says the sea keeps accounts. Perhaps you should ask it about the Alderseys. It seems to know everyone's name."

Something flickered behind his eyes — that near-touch of contour lines again, closer now, close enough that she could almost hear it, two depths sounding the same water.

"Perhaps I shall," Elias Vane said quietly. "Good morning, Miss Coldharbour. Miss Coldharbour." A bow to each of them, identical, impartial. At the door he added, without turning fully around: "The atlas. When it's published — I should very much like to see it. There are places on this coast I've wondered about all my life."

Then he was gone, his footsteps measured and unhurried down the path, and the gate clicked shut with the precision of a well-made instrument closing.

Sera stood at the window watching him go, arms folded, lavender and dread rising off her in equal measure. Mara counted his steps — she couldn't help it; she counted everything — forty-two paces to the gate, then the street, then nothing.

"Well," Sera said at last. "That was thorough."

"That was not a census taker."

"That is exactly what a census taker looks like, Mara. That's what frightens me. They sent us a man who looks like the questions he asks." Sera turned from the window. Her hands had found each other and were holding on. "The maiden name. He asked for it straight out, first pass. Last year's man didn't ask at all — barely looked up from his book."

"People get more diligent."

"Sera-like," Mara said gently, using the old childhood teasing, and her aunt almost smiled. "No. He asked the way a surveyor asks — he already had his fix, and he wanted confirmation. Did you see him pause after 'Aldersey'? He wasn't writing it down to remember it. He was writing it down to compare it."

"To compare it with what?"

"With whatever he brought in that coat pocket under the ledger. Did you notice the coat? Too heavy for the season. Men carry papers in coats like that. Contracts, warrants, old letters." Mara moved to the window. The street was empty. The morning was ordinary, boats going out, gulls arguing over the tide line, the world performing normalcy with total conviction. "He asked to see Grandmother. Nobody asks to see Odile. Nobody has asked to see Odile in nineteen years. That isn't diligence, Aunt. That's intent."

Sera sat down slowly, the careful woman suddenly looking her age and several beyond. "Then we move her."

"Where? On whose legs? She hasn't walked past the garden gate since spring, and if we carry her out of the house like cargo, every neighbor on this street becomes a witness to a mystery they've spent nineteen years politely ignoring. No." Mara pressed her fingertips to the cool glass. Below, far out past the breakwater, a ship was standing in toward the harbor roads, sails half-drawn, patient as a signature. "We don't run. Running confirms. We stay exactly as boring as we have always been, and we find out who he is."

"How?"

"He told us how. He wants to see the atlas." She said it and felt the strangeness of it settle over her — that the one thing in her life that was entirely hers, entirely honest, the master's great work and her grief-work both, might also be the hook he'd baited. "When a man tells you what he wants, Sera, the only question left is what he's willing to trade for it."

"And if what he wants isn't the atlas at all? If the atlas is only the door he thinks leads to us?"

Mara was quiet. Out on the water, the incoming ship altered course a degree, unhurried, certain, as though the harbor had already agreed to receive it.

"Then we learn what he knows before he learns what we are," she said. "And we lie beautifully. We've had practice."

"You lie beautifully," Sera said, with something that was not quite admiration and not quite grief. "You lied to that man's face this morning and never blinked. Sometimes, Mara, that frightens me more than he does."

Mara didn't answer, because there was no honest answer that wasn't also a confession: that she had lied fluently, easily, with pleasure almost, and that some sealed room in her had noted the ease and filed it away as evidence — evidence of what, she refused to examine. Her grandmother's generation had buried a name. Her aunt's generation had guarded the grave. Hers had grown up on top of it, building maps over the buried thing, and perhaps the talent for concealment was simply another inheritance, passed down the female line like the gray eyes, like the steady hands.

She climbed back up to the map room. The atlas lay open where she had left it at dawn, the final coastline drying in its long black sentence, and she stood over it a while without touching anything, listening to the house below — Sera moving cups, Tam's boots hammering up the area stairs with the particular violence of a young man arriving late and unrepentant, and from behind her grandmother's closed door, faintly, the sound of Odile Coldharbour talking to someone.

There was no one in the room with her. There never was.

Mara bent back to the coast she was inking, and found that her hand — the famous steady hand, the hand the master had praised in front of the whole survey office once, saying *this girl draws truth like a priest gives absolution, cleanly and completely* — found that the hand was trembling.

Not from fear. From the other thing. The thing she had felt in the space between herself and Elias Vane when their eyes met across the harbor light, the thing shaped like recognition's poor cousin:

The terrible, vertiginous suspicion that she had just lied, flawlessly, to the one person in the world who might have known her real name on sight — and that some part of her had wanted, desperately, shamefully, to be caught.

Below, her grandmother laughed at her invisible companion — a young girl's laugh escaping an old woman's throat — and Mara dipped her pen, and waited for the tremor to pass, and it did not.
