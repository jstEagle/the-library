## 6. What Odile Remembers

The fog came off the harbor on a Thursday, and Odile Coldharbour came back from wherever she had been living for the past year.

Mara knew it before she reached the kitchen. There was a quality to the silence of the house when her grandmother surfaced — a listening quality, as though the walls themselves leaned in — and she heard it through the floorboards while she was still grinding ink at her desk upstairs. The mortar stopped mid-turn in her hand. She listened. Downstairs, a chair scraped, and then Sera's voice, low and startled: *"Mama? Mama, look at me."*

Mara set down the pestle and went down two steps at a time.

Odile was sitting at the kitchen table with her hands folded on the oilcloth, upright as a figurehead, and she was looking directly at Sera with eyes that had not been direct in eleven months. Her hair was braided — someone must have braided it that morning without knowing why they bothered — and her nightcap sat straight, and her face held an expression Mara had only ever seen in the portrait that hung turned to the wall in the back parlor: the young Odile Vane, painted before the name became poison, wearing the particular patience of a woman who is used to being obeyed.

"Is the tide book balanced?" Odile asked.

Sera's hands were trembling around the teapot. "Mama, it's—"

"Is *the tide book balanced*," Odile repeated, with the mild steel of someone who does not ask twice. "We sail on the spring tide or we don't sail at all, and I will not have the manifests short."

"There's no ship, Mama," Sera said gently. "There's no sailing today."

Something flickered across the old woman's face — disappointment, or its cousin — and then she turned her head and saw Mara standing in the doorway, and the whole room changed.

Odile's face went white. Not the milk-pale of confusion but the bloodless white of shock, of a woman seeing a ghost, and Mara felt the ghostliness of it enter her own body, cold along the spine, because she knew — she had always known, from fragments dropped by Sera over the years like coins into deep water — who she was about to be named as.

"Renata," Odile whispered.

"Mama—" Sera began.

"Renata, you're wet." Odile was pushing back her chair, rising on legs that hadn't carried her firmly since autumn, crossing the kitchen with her arms out. "You're soaked through, child, come here, come here, you'll take your death — you're always in the water, you never listen, you never—" Her hands found Mara's shoulders, then her face, cupping it, thumbs pressing against Mara's cheekbones as if checking whether the flesh was real. Her palms smelled of camphor and lavender and something older, the ghost of salt. "They said you went under off the point. They said the current took you past the light. But here you are. Here you are, and you're cold as ballast, Renata, why didn't you say—"

"I'm not—" Mara started.

Sera caught her eye over Odile's shoulder and shook her head, once, small and fierce. *Don't.*

So Mara didn't.

She let her grandmother hold her face between her hands and did not correct her, and hated herself for how easily that came too — the not-correcting, another fluent lie in a house built of them — and at the same time felt something crack open in her chest that had nothing to do with shame at all. Because Odile's eyes were bright and present and utterly certain, and there was more love in them than Mara had seen directed at her own actual name in fifteen years. Whoever this Renata had been, she had been loved with a ferocity that had outlasted memory itself, outlasted the mind that held everything else, and now it poured out onto Mara like water finding a channel cut long ago.

"Sit down," Odile commanded. "Sit. Sera, dry towels. And the ledger — where's the ledger? If we're taking on water I want it entered."

"Mama, there is no ledger," Sera said, but she was already moving to fetch towels, already crying without any change in her voice, which was a skill Mara recognized suddenly as inherited — the careful silences, the steady hands over shaking ones.

"There is *always* a ledger." Odile drew Mara down into the chair beside her own and kept one hand on her wrist, gripping it the way a woman grips a rope. "Everything is a ledger. That's the first thing your father never understood and the last thing I will ever know. The sea keeps accounts. Every hull that floats, every hull that goes down, every man who signs articles and every man who doesn't come home — entered, entered, entered. You think the water forgets?" She laughed, a dry sound like canvas in wind. "The water is the most patient clerk in God's creation. It waits. It balances its books at the end of every season, and woe to the house whose columns don't match."

"What columns don't match, Grandmother?" Mara asked softly.

It was the wrong thing. Or the right thing; she couldn't tell. Odile's grip tightened on her wrist and her gaze drifted, just slightly, toward the window and the gray wall of fog beyond it, and for a moment Mara thought she had lost her again — the tide going out of those eyes, the person receding.

But no. Odile came back. She leaned close, and her voice dropped to the register of conspiracy, of cargo moved at night, and what she said next made the hairs rise along Mara's arms.

"The Vane columns," she said. "Someone has been writing in the Vane columns, Renata. Entries that were struck through thirty years ago. Debts we paid in full — paid in *blood*, girl, we paid until there was nothing left to pay with — and now some clerk somewhere has inked them back in. Do you understand? Someone is calling in old paper. Someone wants the account reopened."

"Who?" Mara breathed.

"If I knew who, I'd know what to do, wouldn't I." Odile released her wrist and sat back, and her hands resumed their fold upon the table, prim as a schoolmistress's. "I know what was owed. I was the one who counted it. My father taught me the books before he taught me my letters — said a Vane who couldn't read a manifest wasn't worth the berth she slept in. I counted the ships out and I counted them in and for forty years the numbers sang. Forty years, and then one season they didn't." Her jaw worked. "One season the sea took more than it gave, and my father's partners said the difference was theft, and the magistrate believed them because their purses were deeper than our honor, and that—" she tapped the table once, hard, "—that was the entry that sank us. Not the storm. The *entry*."

Sera stood frozen at the dresser with the towels still folded in her arms, not coming closer, tears running freely now, making no sound at all.

"They took the ships," Odile went on, conversational now, terrible in her clarity. "They took the house. They took the name — you can't put a name in your mouth anymore without it costing a shilling fine and a night in gaol, did you know that, Renata? Did they teach you that in whatever port you've washed up from? We are *Coldharbour* now. A made-up thing, a shell, a false bottom in the hull. Twenty-two years I have answered to a name like a smuggler answering to a flag of convenience, and I have kept my true colors furled, and I will keep them furled until I am nailed into my box, do you hear me? Until they nail me in. Because the moment a Vane surfaces, the moment one of us puts our mark to paper where the authorities can read it—" She stopped. Her breath went ragged. "The last time a Vane signed anything, your mother signed it. And six weeks later she was in the water off the point, and they called it an accident, and I have spent thirty years not believing them and being unable to prove otherwise, and that, Renata, that unbalanced column, that is the debt that will follow me into the ground."

The kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere outside a gull cried once and gave up.

"Sera," Odile said, without turning. "Stop crying where the girl can see you. You'll frighten her."

Sera wiped her face with terrible efficiency and set the towels on the table and sat down, and for a moment the three of them sat in a row at the oilcloth like figures in a painting of something that could never be exhibited.

"You think I'm Renata," Mara said carefully. "Grandmother. I'm Mara. Mara Coldharbour. Your granddaughter."

"I know exactly who you are." Odile turned and looked at her, and the clarity in her face did not waver, which was the frightening part — that she could say the impossible thing with a sane woman's steady eyes. "You are Mara Coldharbour, born in the winter of '31, who bites her thumbnail when she's working and hides sweets in her desk drawer and has her grandfather's left-handed slant. And you are also Renata Vane, drowned off Harrow Point on the ninth of October, 1822, aged nineteen, entered in the ledger of the sea." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Mara had ever been given. "A good clerk learns to hold two entries in mind at once, child. The truth is rarely one line. It's the sum of the column."

"But she died," Mara whispered. "Before I was born."

"Yes." The word fell like an anchor. "Yes, she died. And yet here you sit, wet-haired and salt-eyed and stubborn as she ever was, and I am an old woman whose ledger is nearly closed, and I find I can afford both entries now. There's no auditor coming. Let me have the double entry. Let me hold my drowned sister's hand while my granddaughter's pulse beats in it. The books will bear it. I promise you the books will bear it."

Mara looked at Sera. Sera looked back, and behind the tears there was a warning — or perhaps a plea; Mara couldn't separate them — *let her, let her, don't take even this from her.* And beneath the warning, fainter, something else. Something that looked almost like fear of Mara herself. As if Mara, sitting there being loved under a dead woman's name, had become dangerous in some way Sera understood and Mara did not.

*The last time a Vane signed anything, your mother signed it.*

Mara turned the sentence over in her mind as Odile began, gradually, to drift — the tide going out by inches, her gaze softening, her grip on Mara's hand loosening from rope to ribbon. The sentences shortened. The shipping terms dissolved into murmurs about tide-times and harbor lights and a dog named Binnacle nobody else remembered. Within a quarter hour she was asking Sera whether supper would be herring again, and complaining mildly that the herring was always bones, and she was gone — not gone-gone, but gone to the near shore of herself, where she lived now, waving from a distance.

But the lucid hour stayed in Mara like dye stays in cloth.

She climbed back to her desk that evening and could not work. The atlas lay open under the lamp — her dead master's great unfinished coasting atlas, three years of his life and the last thing he touched before they found him face-down in his own ink — and she looked at the final coastline she'd been inking that morning and saw, for the first time since childhood, the place-name she had flinched from.

Harrow Point.

Off which a girl named Renata Vane had gone into the water on the ninth of October, 1822, and not come out. Off which, if Odile was to be believed, no accident had occurred at all.

Her master had charted that stretch of coast with unusual care. Unusual — she had always thought it devotion to detail, the old man's famous exactitude. Now, with Odile's voice still in her ears — *someone is calling in old paper, someone has inked the entries back in* — she found herself wondering, with a chill that had nothing to do with the fog pressing against the windows, why a cartographer with no connection to the Vanes had surveyed that particular mile of shoreline four times, each sounding more precise than the last.

As if he had been looking for something buried there.

As if he had known exactly whose daughter was holding his pens.
