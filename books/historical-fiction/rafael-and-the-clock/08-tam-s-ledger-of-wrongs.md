## 8. Tam's Ledger of Wrongs

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded inside a copy of the *Shipping Gazette* that no one in the Coldharbour house had any reason to read. Tam bought it at the stationer's on Cordwainer Lane, paid his penny, and walked home with it rolled under his arm like a man carrying nothing more dangerous than news of freight rates.

In the alley behind the cooperage, where the smell of oak staves and tar covered everything, he stopped and drew out the sheet hidden between pages four and five.

*Dear Mr. Colton,* it began. *Your inquiry is received and under consideration. Be advised that claims of this nature require documentary proof of descent — a register, a testament, a christening record, or sworn testimony from a living principal. Without such proof, the Commission will not entertain even the most plausible narrative. I would further advise discretion. The Vane interests are watched by parties who do not welcome competition for the estate.*

Mr. Colton. That was him now. Tam Colton, clerk to a timber merchant, twenty-four years old, with ink-stained cuffs and a face nobody remembered twice. He read the letter three times, then fed it to the cooper's scrap fire, watching the edges curl black before the words could be accused of existing.

*Sworn testimony from a living principal.*

There was exactly one living principal. She sat upstairs in a chair by the window with her hands folded in her lap, and most days she did not know his name.

Tam stood in the alley a while longer, listening to the coopers hammer hoops, and told himself — as he had told himself every day since the notice went up at the Custom House — that what he was doing was not betrayal.

It was arithmetic.

* * *

He kept a ledger. Not the merchant's ledger he kept for Mr. Prewitt, all neat columns of board-feet and shillings, but another one, private, bound in grey cardboard and kept beneath a loose floorboard under his bed. In it he recorded not money but wrongs. He had begun it years ago, when he was fourteen and had first understood that his family's poverty was not an act of God but an act of men.

*1854, March.* He wrote by candlelight after everyone slept, the way he did everything that mattered.

*Wrong the first: they took the name off us before I was born. I have been Colton my whole life and I never agreed to it.*

That was true. Sera had chosen the name — he'd pried that much out of her once, one bottle of cheap gin into a rare evening of talk. Colton, she said, because it was common as dirt, because there were forty Coltons in the parish registers and none of them were watched. His aunt, who could not lie about the weather without blushing, had built their whole lives on a lie and called it love.

*Wrong the second: whatever was done to Grandfather Vane, it was done with paper. Contracts, seizures, a bankruptcy signed by lawyers while he drowned himself in brandy. Paper took the ships, the warehouses, the house on the Terrace. And paper can give them back.*

*Wrong the third:* — here his pen had hesitated, weeks ago, the first night he wrote it — *they decided we would be poor quietly, forever, and never asked me if I consented to be poor.*

Odile had not asked. Odile, who remembered the sea better than she remembered her own grandchildren, who spoke in tides and tonnage when she surfaced at all. Sera had not asked; Sera only pressed her lips together and burned things. And Mara —

Mara spent every waking hour bent over a dead man's atlas, inking coastlines for a master who had died in debt, as though drawing the world faithfully enough might excuse her from living in it. Mara believed history happened somewhere else, in ports and capitals, to people whose names got printed. Tam knew better. History happened in alleys, in ledgers, in letters posted under false names. History happened to whoever was willing to pick up the pen.

He blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the house breathe. Through the wall he could hear Odile's voice, thin and looping, talking to someone who wasn't there — the drowned sister, probably, or the sea itself. The sound should have made him gentle. Instead it hardened something in him, the way frost hardens mud into a road you can walk on.

One berth. One verified heir. The port authority's notice had been pasted up for eleven days now, and the whole harbor talked of nothing else. The last emigrant ship of the line — the *Meridian*, they were calling her, which struck Tam as either poetry or a joke — would carry away one heir of the Vane fortune, verified, honored, set up in the colony with a grant of land and a purse.

His family looked at that notice and saw a trap, or a grief, or a moral test. Tam looked at it and saw a door.

And doors, in his experience, did not open for people who waited politely to be deserving.

* * *

The lawyer's name was Quill — Mr. Ambrose Quill, chambers on Fetter Lane, a name so perfectly suited to his trade that Tam suspected he had invented it along with his respectability. They had corresponded four times now, always through the *Gazette*, always under the name Colton, though the letters had grown warmer as Quill smelled fee.

On Thursday, Quill requested a meeting.

*Tam Colton, Esq.,* the note began, flattering him with the honorific. *Certain matters are best discussed in person. I am engaged to dine at the Ship Tavern, Limehouse, Friday next, at seven. Come alone. Bring nothing written.*

Bring nothing written. As though Tam were the sort of man who needed to be warned against evidence. He almost laughed. He had spent his whole life being the invisible member of a family of ghosts; caution was the one inheritance he had actually received.

Friday evening he told Sera he was working late at Prewitt's, counting timber by lamplight, and watched her accept the lie with the tired relief of a woman who preferred not to know where her nephew went at night. Guilt flickered and was put away, the way he put away everything — neatly, in the dark, under the floorboard.

Limehouse at night was a country of its own. Gaslight turned the fog the color of weak tea; sailors of six nations shouted in six languages outside the public houses; the river breathed its slow rot-and-salt breath over everything. Tam walked through it feeling, for once, not like a ghost but like a spy — and finding that he preferred it.

Quill was small and dry and precise, a man assembled entirely of angles, with spectacles that caught the tavern light and gave back nothing. He shook Tam's hand with two fingers, ordered him a porter without asking, and opened the conversation the way surgeons open a body.

"You understand," he said, "that I cannot represent a claim that does not exist. So let us begin with whether it exists. You wrote me that you are descended from Josiah Vane, the shipowner. Prove it."

"I can't," Tam said. "Not yet."

Quill's eyebrows performed a small ironic pilgrimage upward. "Then our business is concluded before it begins."

"Not concluded. Postponed." Tam leaned forward. "You said testimony from a living principal. There is one. My grandmother. Born Odile Vane, 1789, daughter of Josiah. She's alive, she's in London, and she is the last soul on this earth who can swear to the line."

"And her condition?"

"She's failing." The word came out flat. He had practiced it being flat. "Her mind goes in and out like a tide. But she has lucid hours. Whole hours, sometimes, when she remembers everything — the ships' names, the routes, the year the *Providence* was lost. A magistrate could take her deposition in a good hour. I've seen her good hours."

Quill removed his spectacles, polished them on his cuff, and replaced them, and during the whole operation he studied Tam with the unhurried interest of a man appraising cargo.

"There is a difficulty," he said, "which you may not have considered. If your grandmother testifies to the line, she authenticates *all* the heirs. Not only you."

The porter suddenly tasted bitter. "I'm the eldest grandchild."

"Are you? You've told me nothing of your cousins."

"My sister." The correction came too fast and Quill heard it.

"Ah. A sister." The lawyer smiled without warmth. "Then hear me plainly, young man, because I shall say this only once. Claims of this kind are won not by blood but by preparation. Whoever stands before the magistrate with documents, witnesses, and a coherent narrative — whoever has done the work in advance — will carry the day. Sentiment distributes nothing. Estates go to the ready." He tapped the table once, sharply, like a gavel. "Is your sister ready?"

Tam thought of Mara hunched over the atlas, inking a dead man's coastlines, believing that accuracy was a form of virtue. No. Mara wasn't ready. Mara didn't even want it — he'd seen her face when she read the notice, that quick flinch, that look of dread sharpening into something she wouldn't let herself call wanting. Mara would stand in front of the magistrate and tell the truth, and the truth, unprepared, was worth precisely nothing.

"No," Tam said. "She isn't."

"Then there is work to be done, and little time. The *Meridian* sails within the month." Quill drew a card from his breast pocket — plain, unprinted, with only an address in pencil — and slid it across the table. "A sworn deposition requires a commissioner of oaths, a fee, and discretion. I can arrange all three. What I cannot arrange is a principal who cannot remember. So. Your task is to catch your grandmother in a good hour and bring her to me — or bring her statement to me, properly witnessed, within a fortnight. After that, the Commission's verification sits, and the window closes."

"And the rest of the estate?" Tam asked. "The berth is one thing. The ships, the property—"

"The berth," Quill said, rising, "is the key that opens the lock. Verify the heir and the rest follows like water finding its level." He put on his hat. "One more piece of advice, gratis. These inquiries attract attention. There are parties — I'll not name them — who profit from the Vane estate remaining extinguished. If they learn a claim is stirring, they will move to smother it. Trust no one who asks questions you haven't paid them to ask."

He left coins on the table, precise as everything else about him, and was gone into the fog.

Tam sat a long while with the blank card in his hand. *Trust no one who asks questions.* He thought of the census taker, Vane — the irony of the name still made his jaw tighten — with his polite voice and his pen that never stopped moving. He thought of how the man's eyes had gone around their parlor like a surveyor's, measuring.

Then he thought of Sera, who had burned the papers. Of Odile, who was drowning hour by hour in her own past. Of Mara, who would sooner die than claim anything.

They would hate him. He understood that the way you understand weather. Sera would call it betrayal and mean it; Mara would simply look at him, and that would be worse. But hatred, Tam had learned, was a cost like any other, and he had spent his whole life poor. He could afford to be hated for a few years. What he could not afford — what none of them could afford, though none of them would admit it — was another decade of this: the rented rooms, the mended cuffs, the grandmother dying by inches on charity bread while a fortune sat in Chancery waiting for someone with the stomach to reach for it.

Ambition, he told himself, walking home through the fog, was just abandonment wearing good clothes — no. That wasn't right. He rejected the sentence the way he rejected a mis-inked figure. Ambition was survival, extended forward in time. He wasn't abandoning them. He was abandoning *poverty*, and if they chose to stand on the dock of it waving, that was their sentimentality, not his crime.

But the thought followed him anyway, all the way down Cable Street, patient as a tide: *you are not cutting yourself out of the wreck. You are cutting them out of the rescue.*

At home, the house was dark except for one candle in the front room. Mara sat at the table with the atlas open, asleep over her work, her cheek on her forearm, an ink brush still loose in her fingers. The candle had burned down to a stub; wax had pooled and hardened around the base like something trying to hold the light in place.

Tam stood in the doorway and looked at his sister — really looked, for the first time in months. At the tremor in her sleeping hand. At the page beneath her: a coastline, half-inked, and beside it, faintly, some annotation in another hand that he couldn't read at this distance. Her master's, probably. The dead man got more of her attention than the living ever had.

She stirred, murmured something — a place name, he thought, or a person's — and did not wake.

Tam went upstairs, lifted the floorboard, and added a line to the ledger by feel, in the dark, in handwriting he hoped was legible:

*Wrong the fourth, pending: myself.*

Then he closed the book on it, because a man could not run a ledger at a loss, and tomorrow he would need a clear page.

He was just pushing the board back into place when he heard it: footsteps in the street below, stopping. Not passing — stopping, directly outside the house, the way a man stops when he is checking an address against a paper. Then silence, long enough to count as a decision. Then the footsteps moved on, unhurried, toward the harbor.

Tam knelt in the dark with his heart going like a pump in a leaking hull, and understood that whatever came next had already begun coming.
