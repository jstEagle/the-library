## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

The bread line outside the Rue Vasseur depot began forming before dawn, and by the time Elodie Ashgrove passed it at half past seven, the queue had already learned to hate him.

He could feel the hatred without seeing it — a shift in the quality of the silence, the way conversation thinned as he came near and thickened again behind him. He wore the grey coat of the Courier Service, unadorned but for the small brass seal at the collar, and that seal was enough. It meant he ate from the ministry kitchens. It meant his boots were new leather while theirs were cardboard stitched with string. He did not look left or right. Looking was a form of acknowledgment, and acknowledgment was a debt, and debts in this city were being called in with increasing frequency.

*Be overlooked,* his first instructor had told him, twelve years ago, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and old paper. *Not invisible — invisible is impossible, and trying for it makes people notice you. Overlooked. Be the man whose face slides off memory like water off wax.*

Elodie had made an art of it. He kept his features arranged in an expression of mild administrative boredom, the face of someone perpetually on his way to deliver something tedious. He walked at the pace of a man who would not run even if ordered to. He tipped his hat to no one and everyone. When soldiers stopped him at checkpoints — and they stopped him often now; there were more checkpoints than streetlamps these days — he produced his papers with the weary competence of a man doing this for the thousandth time, which he was, and their eyes glazed over before he finished speaking.

The city itself had stopped pretending. That was the strangest part, Elodie thought as he crossed the Place des Martyrs, where last spring they had raised a statue of the Provisional Consul and this morning someone had hung a butcher's apron over its bronze shoulders. The republic was dying the way a great house dies: not all at once, but room by room, service by service. The trams ran only until noon now. The gaslights along the boulevard stayed dark because the men who lit them had been conscripted. In the window of the bookshop on Corvel Lane, where once they had displayed new novels in gilt-lettered rows, there stood instead a hand-painted card reading **CLOSED FOR THE DURATION**, and beneath it, smaller, in someone's bitter pencil: *What duration?*

Rumor moved through the capital faster than any dispatch Elodie carried, and it carried worse news. The Third Army had turned its coat at Sarnholt — or hadn't, depending on whom you asked. The Chancellor had fled to the coast — or was barricaded in the palace with two regiments and a priest. The victors were already among them, some said, walking the streets in plain coats, counting windows, making lists. Elodie listened to all of it the way a ferryman listens to weather: attentively, expressionlessly, without believing any single account enough to bet his life on it.

His pouch held eleven letters that morning. Eleven seals, eleven names of officials whose signatures he had come to know better than his own family's handwriting — though, he reflected grimly, that comparison had grown less damning than it once was. He had not received a letter from the Ashgroves in four years. He had sent none either. This was not a quarrel, exactly. Quarrels require two parties willing to speak. It was something quieter and more permanent: a slow mutual withdrawal, like water finding its level, until what remained between them was a flat, featureless expanse that neither crossed.

His father had wanted him in the family trade. Ledger-keeping, estate management, the patient accumulation of obligations — the Ashgrove business, three generations deep, built on the principle that everything owed eventually comes due, and that the man who holds the record of what is owed holds power no army can confiscate. *Paper endures,* his father liked to say, tapping the ledgers with one blunt finger. *Men don't.* Elodie had chosen different paper. Dispatches instead of debts. And his father had looked at him across the supper table with an expression Elodie still could not name — disappointment folded inside something like fear — and said only, "Then you'll be useful to whoever wins."

He had not gone home since.

The Ministry of Correspondence occupied a building that had once been a duke's residence and still bore the marks of grander intentions: a staircase of veined marble, ceilings painted with allegories that had faded to ghosts, rooms where clerks worked at desks looted from better days. Elodie signed in at the courier desk under the eye of Sergeant Brannoc, a heavy, gray-faced man who had served since before the war and treated the collapse of the republic primarily as an inconvenience to filing systems.

"Eleven," Elodie said, setting the pouch on the counter.

"Nine out, two in." Brannoc slid two sealed packets across. "And the Director wants the Vasseur run done today. Today, Ashgrove. Not tomorrow."

"The Vasseur run takes me past the east checkpoints."

"Then take the river road." Brannoc lowered his voice without lowering his gaze from the ledger. "There are men asking questions at the east gates. Not our men."

That word — *our* — had begun to mean less every week. Elodie nodded, took the new dispatches, and went down the corridor to the sealing room, a narrow chamber with a long table, a rack of wax sticks, and a window that looked out onto a courtyard where nothing had grown in years.

Sealing was the last quiet act of his craft. He laid the eleven letters in their order, checked each against the manifest, warmed the wax over the spirit lamp, and pressed the Service seal into each puddle with the same measured pressure, so that every impression came out identical — identical impressions meant no letter could be singled out afterward as having been tampered with, or so the theory went. It was work that demanded exactly enough attention to keep the mind tethered and no more. On ordinary days it was almost restful.

He was on the ninth letter when he saw the name.

It was not addressed to him. It was addressed, per the manifest, to Commissioner Halvard at the Office of Public Safety, and it bore the usual heading — *Report on Persons Under Investigation for Acts Against the Security of the Republic* — and the usual dense, bureaucratic opening that Elodie had read a hundred times without ever truly reading. But the second paragraph listed names, and the third name down, rendered in the clerk's careful copperplate, was **Ashgrove**.

Not his own name. His father's. And beneath it, in the column marked *Associates and Household*, his sister Marguerite's, and the names of the estate staff, and — Elodie's breath went very carefully shallow — the name of his cousin Tomas, who had been dead six years and whose inclusion could only mean the investigators were scraping the ledger itself, going through the family's records of who owed what to whom, and finding in those old debts a pattern that someone in the government had decided to call conspiracy.

He read it twice. Then, because the craft held him the way a drowning man is held by the thing pulling him under, he read it a third time, noting the details with the part of his mind that could not stop working: the date of the report, ten days old; the routing mark indicating a copy had already gone to the military governor; the annotation in red ink at the margin — *detention recommended upon confirmation*.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the corridor a door slammed, and Elodie realized how long he had been standing there, the letter open in his hands, the wax cooling unused beside him. Any moment now someone would walk in. Any moment his face would become memorable.

He thought about the fireplace in the corner of the sealing room. It was summer-grate small, meant for chill mornings, but it would take a letter easily. Ten seconds. No manifest discrepancy worth noticing — papers went astray in wartime; entire ministries had gone astray. He could burn it, finish the other seals, walk out into the ration lines and the rumor and the falling light of the republic, and be nobody again. The overlooked man, carrying his tedium, delivering nothing that mattered.

His father's voice, unbidden: *Then you'll be useful to whoever wins.*

And beneath it, quieter, a voice he trusted more because he had heard it less: Marguerite, years ago, laughing at him across the orchard wall — *You always did think distance was a kind of safety, Elodie. Distance is just somewhere else for the fire to reach.*

Down the corridor, boots. Coming closer.

Elodie set the letter down on the table, face up, and reached for the wax.

* * *

He told himself, pressing the seal into the cooling pool, that he was simply following procedure. A courier delivers what he is given; the alternative is theft of state correspondence, punishable by methods the Office of Public Safety had recently expanded. He told himself that the report was one document among thousands, that accusations in wartime were cheap as coal smoke, that the Ashgroves had survived three generations of governments rising and falling by keeping their heads down and their ledgers balanced. He told himself that his family had made their arrangements long ago, as such families always did, that his father's paper would endure as it always endured, that whatever came for the estate would find it prepared.

He told himself all of it in the flat tone of a man reciting a manifest, and none of it took hold, because underneath the recitation ran a single sentence he could not stop hearing, the sentence every courier learns in his first year of training and prays never to need:

*A courier who knows what he carries is no longer a courier. He is a witness. And witnesses must choose.*

By noon he had delivered nine of the eleven letters. By one o'clock, standing in the shadow of the Vasseur depot with the tenth packet — the Ashgrove report — burning a hole through the leather against his hip, he had abandoned his route entirely and was walking west, toward the river road, toward the country, toward the house he had sworn four years ago never to enter again.

Behind him, the capital rang its broken noon bell. Ahead, the roads were filling with soldiers.
