## 2. What the Ashgroves Kept

The road to Vellancourt was nine hours by the slow coach, and Elodie spent the first six of them learning how to sit like a man who was not carrying the death of his family in a canvas pouch against his ribs.

He had done this before, of course — the sitting, the being-no-one. It was the whole of his craft. A courier's genius was not speed or nerve; it was forgettability. He wore a coat the color of wet ash and carried a face that made clerks look past him mid-sentence. He had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being overlooked, and he had believed, until three days ago, that it was a kind of freedom.

Now every trick he knew felt like a confession. To be invisible on this particular road, with this particular letter, was to choose it.

*To the Commission for the Suppression of Conspiracies: it having been established that the household of Ashgrove, of Vellancourt, did conspire with agents of the Coalition to subvert the provisioning of the Eastern Departments...*

He had read it eleven times too. He could recite it the way other men recited prayers.

The coach stopped at a ration station outside Ferrières, where a woman wept quietly over a chit that would no longer be honored by Thursday, if there was a Thursday, and Elodie bought bread he did not want because men who weren't running didn't skip meals. Through the window he watched the capital's rumor arrive ahead of him, traveling faster than any courier: the Third Column had mutinied; no, it hadn't; the Coalition was at the river; the historians had already been appointed, four gentlemen and a engraver, commissioned to write down what had happened as though it were already over.

As though the ending could be drafted while people were still alive inside it.

He ate his bread. He kept his face forgettable. And all the while his thoughts went ahead of him, up the long drive to Vellancourt, to a house he had not entered in six years, to a sister who signed her letters *M. Ashgrove* as if even her own name were a formality she'd rather not extend to him.

*Marguerite will know what it means,* he told himself. *She keeps everything. She always has.*

That was the trouble. That was exactly the trouble.

* * *

Marguerite heard the soldiers before she saw them — not the boots but the dogs, a thin complaining sound from the beech avenue that the wind sometimes brought up from the north road when it wanted to.

She was in the stillroom, cataloguing. That was her word for it and she offered it to no one: *cataloguing*. In truth she was reading the ledger again, the old one, bound in cracked green calf that had sat behind the false drawer of their grandfather's desk since before either of the surviving Ashgroves could walk. Their mother had shown it to her at nineteen, one candle, one warning: *This is what we are. Not the house. This.*

It was not a ledger of money. Or rather, it was, but the sums had gone strange decades ago — payments recorded in two hands, some entries debited from accounts that belonged to ministries, credits entered against names that had since been hanged, exiled, or quietly promoted. Grain that had never existed, sold to a quartermaster who had never asked why. A loan to a deputy whose vote arrived punctually each spring like a migratory bird. Page after page of small, patient corruptions, the family's true inheritance, compounding interest across three generations.

Their father had called it *the family's memory*. Marguerite had come to understand that memory, in a family like theirs, was indistinguishable from evidence.

The dogs were closer now. She closed the ledger, slid it home into the false drawer, and stood for a moment with her palm flat against the wood, listening to her own heartbeat the way you listen to weather.

Six years of silence between her and Elodie, and now, of course, now the world chose to end. She had written to him twice since the autumn — short letters, cold ones, the sort you write so that later you can say you wrote. No answer. She had told herself she preferred it that way. She had almost believed it.

Then the knock came, and it was not soldiers. It was her brother, gray-faced, hatless, his courier's pouch strapped to him like a bandage, standing in the doorway of her own house as though he'd never left it and as though he might bolt back into the rain at any moment.

"They're coming," he said. "Half a mile, maybe less. The dogs gave them away."

"How do you—"

"I've been watching roads my whole life, Marguerite." He stepped inside and shut the door softly, which was somehow worse than slamming it. "We have minutes."

"Minutes for what?" But she knew. Her hand was already drifting toward the study, toward the desk, and he saw the gesture and went pale in a way that told her everything before he said it.

"It's in the letter," he said. "Your name. Our name. The whole ledger, or enough of it. Someone talked, and they've dressed it up as conspiracy, and I'm the courier carrying the warrant for our destruction." He laughed once, a dry terrible sound. "I sealed the pouch myself. I checked the seals myself. I did everything correctly, and doing things correctly is going to kill us."

The dogs were silent now. That was worse.

* * *

They stood in the study with the ledger open on the desk between them, and for the first time in six years the siblings looked at the same thing at the same time.

"Burn it," Elodie said.

"No."

"Marge—"

"Don't call me that. You lost the right to shorten my name somewhere around the funeral you didn't attend." She said it without heat, which was its own cruelty. "Burn it, and then what? They don't need the book to convict us. The book is the only proof of anything — including the parts that would acquit us. Do you see? Half these entries are payments *we received*. Coercion, Elodie. Look at the margins. Grandfather wrote *under instruction* beside the grain contracts. We're not just guilty in this book. We're *victims* in it, if anyone ever reads it honestly."

"And who will read it honestly?" His voice cracked low, urgent. "Not the commission. Not the victors' historians. They'll read it the way they've already decided to read it, and then it goes in the archive with their seal on it, and in fifty years the Ashgroves are a footnote about treachery, and our grandchildren—" He stopped. "If there are grandchildren."

Outside, a voice called an order, unhurried, administrative. The sound of a state doing paperwork.

Marguerite put both hands flat on the ledger. "Then we don't burn it and we don't surrender it. We move it. You're the courier — you know routes, you know seals, you know how documents travel. You told me once you could make anything disappear into the archives of this republic, or out of them."

"I said that when I was twenty-two and showing off."

"You also said it was true."

Boots on gravel now. Elodie crossed to the window and looked out through the gap in the shutter: six soldiers, a sergeant consulting a paper, a lieutenant with the bored posture of a man following instructions he hadn't written. They would knock politely. That was the horror of the new republic-in-ruins — it had learned manners.

"There's a courier's annex under the Grand Archive," he said slowly, thinking aloud, hating himself for thinking aloud. "Misfiles go there. Everything gets shelved eventually, even the things no one wants found. If the book were... misfiled. Correctly catalogued under something dull. It could survive both sides."

"And the letter? Your dispatch?"

He touched the pouch at his ribs. "That's the problem, Marge. That's the whole problem." He looked at her then — really looked, for the first time since the doorway — and saw that she was afraid, and that she was going to do it anyway, whatever it was, and that he had missed her more than six years of correct behavior had allowed him to admit.

"The ledger stays," she said. "You take me with it. And Elodie—" the dogs barked once, at the door this time, "—you decide, before they open that door, whether you're delivering that letter or saving your family. Because I don't think you can carry it both ways anymore."

The knocker fell, twice, courteous as a clerk.

Elodie's hand closed over the pouch, and through the window, far down the darkening avenue, he could see the lamps of a second carriage coming — faster, unmarked, and flying no colors he knew.
