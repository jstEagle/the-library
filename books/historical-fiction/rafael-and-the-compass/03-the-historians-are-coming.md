## 3. The Historians Are Coming

The republic died the way a lamp dies: not all at once, but in a slow dimming that no one wanted to name until the dark was total.

Elodie felt it in the streets first. The ration line outside the granary on Rue Vasseur had doubled overnight, and doubled again, and now the people standing in it did not speak. Silence in a queue was worse than grumbling. Grumbling meant hope; silence meant arithmetic, each person privately calculating whether they would eat before the city fell.

He moved through it all as he had taught himself to move through everything — at the pace of a man with nowhere particular to be, wearing the gray coat of the courier service with its brass button unpicked so no one could tell at a glance whose authority it carried. Under his arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle, were two things: the sealed dispatch pouch, and beneath it, flat as a hymnal, the Ashgrove ledger.

Three days since Marguerite had pressed it into his hands on the steps of the burning east wing. Three days since the soldiers came up the drive and the siblings who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not writing to each other had stood shoulder to shoulder in the smoke and decided, without a word being spoken, what to protect. She had kept them talking at the gate — *yes, officers, the estate is listed, yes, we are the Ashgroves* — while Elodie went through the window with the ledger against his chest like a second heart.

Now the capital itself was the estate, and everyone in it was an Ashgrove.

At the checkpoint on the Pont des Orfèvres, a boy of perhaps seventeen held up a hand that shook only slightly. "Papers."

"Courier service." Elodie produced the folio with the bored efficiency of a man who had done this four hundred times. "Dispatch run to the Ministry quarter. You can open the pouch if you want to explain to the Commission why the seals are broken."

The boy looked at the seal — the republic's owl, pressed in black wax — and something moved behind his eyes. Not respect. Recognition. His brother, maybe, or his father, was a name in one of these pouches. Everyone's someone was a name in one of these pouches now.

"Go on, then," the boy said, and did not meet his eyes again.

The Ministry quarter, when Elodie reached it, was already half-emptied. Desks stood on the pavements, their drawers pulled out and their contents sorted into piles: burn, keep, sell. Men in civilian coats with the particular posture of men who intended to be civilians again very soon carried armfuls of files toward a brazier, and the ash came down like gray snow, settling on Elodie's shoulders as he passed.

And there, at the corner of Rue Delorme, behind a barricade of crates that had once held ministry stationery, he saw them.

The historians.

They had come before the fighting was finished — that was the genius of it, and the obscenity. Six tables under an awning, six clerks with clean paper and ink that was not rationed, and at the head of them a man Elodie recognized from engravings: Corvel, the commission's chairman, famous even before the collapse for his histories of other people's wars. He sat with his coat off despite the cold, sleeves banded at the forearm, writing in a hand so confident it barely seemed to touch the page.

They were drafting the epitaph while the body still breathed. By winter there would be a printed account, authoritative, indexed, citing documents that Corvel's clerks would select from everything the ministries had failed to burn. And somewhere in that account — Elodie knew it the way you know weather coming — the Ashgrove name would appear in a chapter titled something like *Traitors Within*, sourced to a dispatch pouch that was currently riding under his own arm.

He turned away before any clerk could mark his face, and made for the archive.

* * *

Verrin was waiting for him in the doorway of the chandlery on Rue aux Chats, exactly where the signal said, and that should have been Elodie's first warning. Handlers who were where they said they'd be, on time, in a falling city, were handlers who wanted something.

"You have it." Verrin's eyes went to the oilcloth bundle. In the chandlery's dimness he looked older than his forty years, grayer, like a man photographed by bad light. "Both pieces."

"The letter and the ledger. Yes."

"Then it's finished." Verrin smiled, and it was the smile of a man setting down a weight. "You've done extraordinary work, Elodie. Better than I ever—"

"Where is it going?"

"That's no longer your concern." Verrin extended his hand for the bundle. "Give it to me, and walk away. New name, new coast. There's money—"

"Who receives it?"

The pause lasted half a breath too long. Half a breath was a confession. Elodie had spent nine years reading pauses; it was the whole of his craft.

"No," he said quietly. "It isn't Corvel's commission."

"Corvel writes what he's told," Verrin said, and now the weariness in him was total, a man admitting the shape of his own cage. "There are people who want the ledger *found*, Elodie. Found properly, with seals intact, traceable to your family's hands. A conspiracy needs evidence more than it needs conspirators. Your name makes the whole account credible. Don't you see? You didn't smuggle the truth out of the estate. You smuggled the verdict in."

The drizzle had stopped. Somewhere north, artillery spoke twice, unhurried, like a man clearing his throat.

Elodie thought of Marguerite on the steps, soot in her hair, holding the door of the burning wing with her whole body because the lock had failed and the fire wanted through. *Take it,* she'd said. *Whatever it costs, take it.* She had not said take it to safety. She had said take it, full stop, trusting him to understand the difference.

He understood it now.

"I'm sorry," Verrin said, and meant it, which was the worst part. "Truly. But you were always the instrument, not the hand."

From the alley mouth came footsteps — two sets, heavy, deliberate. Elodie did not wait for the third thing he knew would follow, which was Verrin calling his real name aloud for the first time in nine years, the name that would unmake the gray coat and the unpicked button and every careful invisibility he had ever built.

Instead he took the lantern from the chandlery step and hurled it through the open doorway behind Verrin, into the storeroom where lamp oil stood in crates marked for the ministry braziers, and the world went orange.

He was running before the shout went up, bundle clamped under his arm, and behind him his cover identity burned faster than the building did. Verrin knew his name. Verrin's men would have it to the checkpoints within the hour. The courier Elodie Vasseur — the gray coat, the folio, the bored face at seventeen barricades — was now a hunted man, which meant the coat had to go, the folio had to go, everything that made doors open had to go, and what remained was only a man with two forbidden documents and one place left in the city where paper could outlive politics.

* * *

The Grand Archive occupied a block of its own on the Île, a palace built by a king for the purpose of proving kings mattered, seized by a republic for the purpose of proving otherwise. Its reading room had glass in the roof and dust in the air that turned the afternoon light into something almost liquid. Elodie had delivered dispatches here a dozen times and had learned its rhythms the way he'd learned checkpoint rhythms: when the porters took their bread, when the under-librarians dozed over their catalogs, which corridors smelled of mildew and which of vellum and glue.

Tonight it was nearly empty. The director had dismissed the staff at noon, telling them the collection would be "secured by the incoming authorities" — a sentence every librarian had understood to mean whatever the men with the lists decided it meant. One old cataloguer remained, Maître Ombreux, who had served three regimes and intended to serve a fourth by the simple method of knowing where everything was and telling no one.

Elodie found him shelving folios in the west gallery, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man arranging furniture in a house he owned.

"They're sealing the doors tomorrow," Ombreux said without turning. "Commission clerks came at four. They inventoried the political collections. They'll be back with carts."

"I need to leave something here that they won't find."

"Everyone does, near the end." Ombreux turned then, and studied Elodie — the coatless man, the sweat, the oilcloth bundle. "What is it?"

"A record. Two records. If they find them, they become evidence. If they don't—" Elodie unwrapped the bundle, and the ledger's cracked leather caught the gallery light. "If they don't find them, they stay true."

Ombreux looked at the ledger a long moment. Then at the sealed letter with the owl in black wax. Then, lastly, at Elodie, with the expression of a man reading a catalog entry that hadn't existed that morning.

"There are ninety thousand volumes in this building," the old man said. "Misfiled things live forever. It's the well-catalogued things that die."

They worked until the light through the glass roof went from gold to iron. Elodie broke the letter's dispatch binding himself — sacrilege, treason, the crime his whole career had been built to avoid — and separated the pages, interleaving them among the minutes of a defunct irrigation committee from a province no historian would ever choose on purpose. Pages 1 and 4 went into a box labeled *Drainage Assessments, Southern Reclamation, Year XI*. Pages 2 and 3, and the Ashgrove ledger itself, he bound in plain blue board with a false spine: *Parish Tithes, Unindexed, Misc.* — a title so dull it was practically camouflage. He wrote nothing on the flyleaf. No name. No date. Only, inside the back board, where only a hand searching specifically would think to look, a single line in pencil, small as a margin note:

*Ashgrove debts, honestly kept. Ask the tithes box.*

Ombreux watched him work and asked no questions, which was its own kind of oath.

When it was done, Elodie reshelved the boxes himself, high in the west gallery, among ten thousand equally anonymous spines. From the street below came the sound of carts arriving early — the historians could not wait for morning either, it seemed; everyone in this story was racing everyone else — and voices, and the rattle of the great doors being tested.

"They'll change the locks tonight," Ombreux said. "After that, no one goes in who isn't on their list."

"Then it's done."

"It's done," Ombreus agreed — no: Ombreux, Elodie corrected himself, memorizing the old man's face, the shelf number, the color of the binding, everything a verification might someday require. "Unless you intend to be here when they open it again."

Below, the first cart rolled into the courtyard. Above, in the dark of the west gallery, the truth sat misfiled among the tithes and drainage assessments of a dead province, patient as sediment.

Elodie descended the service stair as the locks began to turn, and stepped out into a city that no longer contained a single person who knew his name — which was, he understood, the only identity that could not be burned.

Behind him, the doors closed. Ahead, somewhere in the smoke, a young woman was filing papers she did not yet know would matter — and would not matter for thirty years, until the day she pulled a plain blue box from a shelf no one visited, and read a penciled line that would bring her looking for a man the record insisted had never existed.
