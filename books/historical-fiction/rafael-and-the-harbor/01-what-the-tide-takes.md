## 1. What the Tide Takes

The harbor of Vessamare smelled the way grief smelled, if grief had a smell: salt and tar and the sweet rot of fish heads thrown back to the gulls. Lior Thornbury had breathed it every day of her nineteen years, and she had long ago stopped noticing it — except on mornings like this one, when the fog came off the water thick as wool and made the whole city feel submerged, as though she were diving again into the cold green silence where her father lived now, at the bottom of things.

She counted what she owned the way other people said their prayers. It took less than a minute. Her mother's diving knife, bone-handled, its blade worn thin as a moon sliver from years of prying oysters off the rocks. Three coins — a copper, a copper, and a battered silver piece with a king's face rubbed to a ghost, kept in a knotted scrap of oilcloth inside her shirt where it lay against her breastbone like a second heart. And the ledger.

The ledger was not hers. It was Harrow & Vale's, stamped with their anchor-and-crown device in faded blue ink, and it held her name in a clerk's careful hand: *Thornbury, Liora — indentured, term of nine years, three expired, six remaining.* Six years. She could recite the whole account without opening the book. She could recite most of them.

*Thornbury, Liora. Original debt, twelve marks, four shillings — funeral costs for D. Thornbury, diver, recovered by company boat. Interest compounded quarterly at one part in twenty. Deductions: lodging, two shillings monthly; rations, eighteen pence; lamp oil; mending of company netting damaged through diver's daughter's negligence, four shillings, assessed once, disputed never.* That last entry had been Aldous Fen's work, or Fen's master's — a net torn in a storm, blamed on a fourteen-year-old girl because someone had to pay for it and her father's death had already eaten his collateral. She had been fourteen. She had not argued. Arguing cost more than four shillings, in Vessamare.

She knew the numbers the way divers knew the pull of a current: intimately, involuntarily, in the body before the mind. It was a habit she'd fallen into without meaning to, the way you fall into water. The clerks at Harrow & Vale thought she was dull — a diver's daughter, quiet, competent, good with a pen when they needed extra hands at copying season, good with nothing else worth noting. They let her stand near the ledgers because standing near ledgers was like standing near furniture. No one guards a thing they believe is invisible.

So she memorized them. All of them. Not out of any plan she could have named — plans came later — but because numbers were the only part of her father's world that still moved, that still *did* something. He had drowned owing eleven marks to the company, and the company had charged his daughter for the rope that hauled him up. The rope had cost sixpence. She could see the entry. She could see all of them, lined up in the dark behind her eyes like lanterns down an endless quay.

* * *

The compound woke around her in its usual order: the bell at half past five, cracked so it sounded like a coin dropped down a well; then Brannick's boots on the gravel walk, even before the bell finished dying; then the divers, moving down toward the sheds with their shoulders already braced against the cold. October cold. The kind that got into the joints and stayed there until spring.

"Thornbury." Brannick didn't slow. Overseer Brannick walked the way tide did — relentless, unhurried, certain of arrival. "Count house. Fen wants the autumn manifests recopied before the bridge closes. All of them."

"All of them," Lior repeated. It wasn't a question, but he answered anyway, because men like Brannick answered questions no one asked; it was how they reminded you who held your years in their fist.

"The season doesn't wait for your penmanship." He was already ten feet gone, his voice carrying flat across the yard. "Fen does. Try to be quick about both."

The count house sat at the compound's landward edge, a squat stone building with windows fogged from the inside, where the clerks' breath and the ink pots' fumes mingled into a permanent gray haze. Aldous Fen was already there when she came in, bent over his desk like a question mark that had given up asking. He was older than her father would have been now — fifty, maybe, or sixty; it was hard to tell with clerks, who aged like paper, all at once and along the folds. His cough had gotten worse since summer. Everyone heard it through the wall at night, that wet tearing sound, and everyone pretended they hadn't.

"You're late." He didn't look up. His pen scratched on, steady as rain.

"The bell just rang."

"The bell rang at half past five. It is now four minutes past." He tapped the page before him. "Four minutes is four lines, at your speed. Sit."

Lior sat. There was a stool by the window that wobbled on a folded scrap of leather, and a stack of manifests taller than her arm, and a pot of ink gone thick with cold that she thinned with a few drops from the kettle. The work was simple and endless: copy each manifest fair, column by column, weight by weight, consignment by consignment. Pearl, dried cod, seal oil, whalebone, timber, salt. The names of ships. The names of buyers in the colonial ports across the water. The sums.

She copied without reading, mostly. Reading was dangerous in the count house; Fen had a way of looking up at exactly the wrong moment, as if he kept a clock running somewhere behind his own eyes. But some numbers snagged on her the way a hook snags cloth. Weights that didn't reconcile. A shipment of pearl listed at forty pounds gross that the dive captains' tallies put nearer thirty. Dockage fees charged twice. A diver named Oskell, dead these two years, whose equipment charges continued to accrue interest on his widow's account — she'd seen that name before, in another book, another season, and told herself she'd misremembered.

She didn't ask. Asking was how girls in the compound ended up reassigned to the fish-gutting sheds, where the numbers were simpler and the smell never washed out.

Outside the count house window, the harbor bridge stretched across the water on its old stone legs, and the traffic on it had turned urgent. Carts loaded to their bows. Fishwives running with bundles. A man leading three goats and swearing at them. By nightfall the storms would come up from the south the way they always did, and the bridge would close — chains across both ends, wardens posted, the whole city split in two for the winter. Harbor side and town side. Inside and outside. Everything Harrow & Vale wanted kept separate, kept *in*, would be kept in until the tides relented sometime after the new year.

Everyone in Vessamare planned their lives around the closing. Weddings were rushed. Debts were settled or fled from. Letters were written in haste and regretted at leisure. Lior had meant to be on the last cart over — Maren Osei had promised to hold her a place beside the charcoal sacks, warm ones — because once the bridge shut, she wouldn't see the town market or the shrine to the drowned or anyone who didn't wear a company badge until the ice broke. Six months inside the walls. Six years left on her paper. Sometimes those two numbers felt like the same number.

"Fen," she said, not looking up from her copying. "If I finish the manifests by midday, can I cross before the chains go up?"

"No."

It came fast and flat, and something in its flatness made her look up. Fen had stopped writing. He was staring at the window, at the bridge, with an expression she couldn't read — or could read too many ways at once. His handkerchief was in his fist, crushed small.

"The manifests must be done tonight," he said. "Tonight, do you understand me? Every page, every figure, checked twice. I don't care about the cart. I don't care about the bridge." A cough took him then, doubling him over the desk, and when he straightened he pressed the handkerchief briefly to his mouth and Lior saw — before he folded it away, quick as a card trick — the red bloom in the white linen.

"Sir," she said carefully, "are you—"

"Copy." His voice had found its dryness again, but his hands hadn't; they trembled slightly as he squared the stack of pages and pushed it toward her. "There will be time for everything else after. There is always less time than people think, Miss Thornbury. Remember that, if you remember nothing else I say." He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were fever-bright and very tired, and for a moment — a strange, unaccountable moment — he looked less like a man guarding a secret than like a man deciding whether to set it down before his arms gave out.

Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and he bent back over his own desk, and the pens resumed their scratching, two small sounds in a large cold room while outside the last carts rattled over the bridge like bones down a throat.

Lior copied. Weight by weight, sum by sum. And when she reached the third manifest of the afternoon — the autumn consolidation, the one that gathered the year's accounts into a single clean summary for the owners across the sea — her pen slowed without her permission, the way her eye always slowed at numbers that didn't want to be understood.

A column headed *Recoveries & Adjustments*. Under it, in Fen's own small precise hand, entries she had never seen in six years of copying: deductions she couldn't match to anything, transfers to an account numbered rather than named, and a final line, initialed twice, that read simply — *per instruction, balance carried forward.*

Carried forward to whom? Carried forward from what?

She glanced up. Fen was watching her. Had been watching, she realized, for some time — not with the clerk's habitual suspicion but with something rawer, something almost like hope, which frightened her far more than suspicion ever had.

The bell began to ring. Not the morning bell. The evening one, early, ragged — the wardens' signal, rung from the bridge tower, that meant the storm front had come faster than the almanacs promised and the chains were going up within the hour.

Fen closed his eyes. "Too late," he said quietly, and Lior couldn't tell whether he meant the bridge, or the ship, or something else entirely — something that had been too late for years, and was only now being spoken aloud.
