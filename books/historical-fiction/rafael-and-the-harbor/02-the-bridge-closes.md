## 2. The Bridge Closes

The bridge closed at the turn of the tide, and the tide turned at noon, and Aldous Fen made me copy a manifest until my fingers went numb.

That was how I would remember it afterward — not as fate, not as weather, but as an order given in a dry voice by a man who smelled of ink and camphor and something underneath both that I would later learn to name as sickness. *Re-copy it, Thornbury. The third column is smudged.* The third column was always smudged. Fen had eyes like a magnifying glass held over everything, and he knew the manifest was clean, and he made me write it again anyway because it was the last day and he could.

I heard the cart before I understood what I was hearing. Wheels on planking have a particular music — a hollow rolling thunder that travels the length of the harbor bridge like a pulse through a wrist. All autumn I'd listened for it without meaning to, the way you listen for your own heartbeat when you're frightened. That morning it came twice, three times, and then, near midday, one long final passage: a groaning freight of barrels and burlap and the last families who would cross until spring.

I put down my pen. I remember that precisely — I set the pen across the inkwell like laying down a weapon, and I stood, and Fen said, without looking up from his own ledger, "I didn't dismiss you."

"The cart," I said. "The last cart."

"The cart," said Fen, "is not your concern. The manifest is." He turned a page with one finger, delicately, as if the paper might bruise. "Sit."

I sat. Of course I sat. A girl indentured to Harrow & Vale does not argue with a clerk over a pen she doesn't own, copying figures she isn't permitted to understand, in a compound she cannot leave. I sat and I wrote numbers while the wheels faded off the bridge and into the far shore, and when I finished the final column and looked up, the light through the counting-house window had gone the color of old pewter, and the first gust of the season hit the shutters hard enough to rattle them on their hooks.

Fen examined my work, found nothing wrong with it, and dismissed me with a flick of two fingers, the way you'd shoo a gull off a fish crate.

I want to say I hated him then. The truth is worse: I thanked him. I actually said, "Thank you, Mr. Fen," because that was the liturgy of the place, the small daily surrender dressed up as courtesy, and I had performed it so many hundreds of times that it came out of me smooth as water. Then I walked out into the wind and watched the bridge empty itself of the world.

* * *

The Vessamare harbor bridge was a mile of timber and iron spanning the mouth of the bay, and every year when the winter storms came up from the south, the harbormaster ordered it drawn open and chained, so that the seas running through the strait wouldn't tear it apart plank by plank. For four months, maybe five, there were exactly two ways out of the compound: the sea, which belonged to the company's ships and to God, or the land road north along the cliff, which belonged to the mud and the wind and anyone fool enough to try it. Divers had tried the road before. There was a cairn halfway to the headland where the workers left tokens for them — a bent nail, a button, once a child's shoe. Nobody talked about it directly. You just added your token when you passed and kept walking.

I stood at the compound gate as they drew the bridge, because some animal part of me needed to see it happen, needed to watch the sections swing up on their great rusted hinges like a jaw opening to swallow the season. The chains went on with a sound like church bells falling down a well. Beyond them, the water between the pilings had already gone white and furious, the color of milk boiled too long, and the spray came up over the seawall and salted my lips where I stood.

"First time?" said Maren beside me. She had a way of arriving without arriving; I never saw her come, only found her there, arms crossed against the cold, her forearms mapped with rope scars. She was the closest thing the dockworkers had to a forewoman, though no one paid her to be it. "The closing?"

"I've seen it before." Four times. Once as a child pressed to my father's side, once the year after he drowned, twice more since. But I'd seen it from the divers' quarters, through glass fogged with other people's breath. I'd never stood at the gate itself, close enough to hear the chains.

"And it still gets under your skin."

"It's not—" I started to say it wasn't anything, which is what I said about most things, but Maren was looking at me with those flat grey eyes that didn't accept nothing, and the word died somewhere in my chest. "It's the sound," I said instead. "Like a door."

"It is a door," Maren said. "Just not the kind we get to use." She pulled her collar up. "Come on. They're doing stew tonight. Real meat in it, if Brannick hasn't skimmed the ration again. You'll want to be early or you'll be scraping pot-glue."

I should have gone back to the counting house. There was always more copying; Fen's manifests bred overnight like mice. Instead I followed Maren toward the cook-shed, and behind us the wind picked up its long winter argument with the roof tiles, and the gatekeeper threw the bar across the compound door with a boom I felt in my back teeth.

A door closing. That was the whole of it. From November until the tides relented, Harrow & Vale owned not just my years but the air inside its walls.

* * *

You learn the dimensions of a prison quickly, even one you've lived in all your life, once the door shuts. The compound was a rough rectangle of buildings inside a palisade that hadn't been needed since the pirate raids ended thirty years ago and now served mostly to hold up drying nets: the counting house, the warehouse, the barracks for the dockworkers, the low stone divers' dormitory where I slept among women whose fathers and husbands the sea had taken at a rate the company called acceptable attrition. The company men had their own quarters near the gate, warmer, with real glass in the windows. Between the two halves of the compound ran a lane of packed mud that turned to soup with the first rain and stayed soup until April.

By evening the soup had frozen at the edges. Winter had arrived in a single day, the way it does in Vessamare — no polite decline, just a border crossed. In the cook-shed the steam rose so thick you could barely see the rafters, and forty people pressed together shoulder to shoulder, and the noise was enormous after the silence of the counting house. Someone had smuggled in a fiddle, which was against regulations, which meant it traveled wrapped in oilcloth like contraband, which meant everyone treasured it doubly. When the overseers' supper ended, the fiddle came out, and a man named Osk began a song about a girl and a lighthouse keeper that had eleven verses and got filthier in each.

I sat on the bench with my bowl and did what I always do in crowds: I counted. Forty-one people in the shed. Three pots on the fire, each holding perhaps twelve portions. Nineteen bowls visible, which meant the serving line had run twenty-two deep already, which meant the meat would be gone before the last ten reached the front. I knew this without wanting to know it. Numbers simply settled into me the way silt settles in a harbor — my father used to joke that I'd been born with a ledger where other girls had lullabies. He'd taught me tally-marks on the beach with a stick, back when the stick was mine and the sand was mine and nothing in my life was written down by another hand.

"Lior." Maren dropped onto the bench beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched. She'd gotten two bowls somehow — she had a talent for being owed things — and pushed the fuller one into my hands. "Eat. You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone totaling the room." She tore bread and handed me half. "My mother did that. Counted everything. Said if you know the numbers, nobody can surprise you." She chewed, watching the fire. "She also said it's a poor substitute for having someone watching your back."

"I watch my own back fine."

"Mm." Maren let it go, which was one of the things I was beginning to notice about her: she planted things and didn't demand they grow. Around us the song reached its ninth verse and the whole shed was singing the refrain now, dockworkers and divers together, and even two of the younger company clerks who pretended not to know the words but knew them anyway. For a moment the steam and the noise and the warmth made the walls seem almost friendly, almost ours, and I felt something loosen in my chest that I hadn't known was knotted.

Then the door opened and the cold walked in wearing Brannick's coat.

He didn't shout. Brannick never shouted; shouting was for men who needed volume to be obeyed. He simply stood in the doorway with the wind at his back, and the fiddle faltered, and Osk's tenth verse died un sung, and forty-one people became very interested in their stew. Brannick counted heads. I watched him do it — his eyes moving along the benches with the unhurried thoroughness of a man checking cargo — and I understood, with a small cold click, that I was doing the same thing he was doing, and that the difference between us was only what we intended to do with the totals.

"Winter rules begin tomorrow," he said, in the voice of a man reading a list he'd written himself. "Compound stays locked. Rations are issued at dawn bell. Anyone leaving the palisade without written leave answers to me." His gaze crossed mine and paused there, half a heartbeat longer than it needed to. Half a heartbeat. "Enjoy your evening."

The door closed. After a moment the fiddle started again, quieter, and the room reassembled itself around the music like water around a dropped stone. But the warmth had changed texture. It was borrowed now. We were all of us aware, in the way animals are aware, that we had just been counted.

* * *

I couldn't sleep. The dormitory was a stone box of snores and wet wool, and the storm had arrived in earnest, throwing spray against the shuttered window in handfuls, and beneath all of it I could feel the compound around me the way you feel a splint on a broken arm — tight, enclosing, holding. Somewhere beyond the walls the sea was rearranging the harbor bottom, and my father lay under it past the second shoal, and in the morning there would be manifests to copy, columns of names and weights and debts, and I would copy them without understanding them, because that was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

Except.

Except that tonight, walking back from the cook-shed, I had passed the counting house and seen lamplight burning in Fen's window at an hour when even Fen slept, and through the frost-furred glass I had seen him bent over his desk, coughing into his handkerchief — coughing the way men cough when the thing inside them has stopped being a nuisance and become an occupant. And when he'd caught me looking, he hadn't waved me away or drawn the curtain. He had stared back at me through the glass for a long moment, this man who owned my daylight hours and treated them as a resource, and his face had worn an expression I had no column for.

It wasn't anger. It wasn't the usual magnifying-glass contempt.

It looked, as nearly as I could tell through the frost, like a man deciding something.

I lay in the dark listening to the storm work the walls, and I thought about doors. The bridge was chained. The gate was barred. Every exit I had ever mapped in this place was shut for the winter, and I had memorized the numbers of my captivity the way I memorized everything — three coins, six years remaining, one knife — and none of those numbers had ever included a sick clerk keeping late hours over papers I wasn't allowed to read.

In the morning, I told myself, I would ask him for extra work. Extra copying. I was good at it; he knew I was good at it; there would be nothing strange in a girl asking to shorten her debt with honest labor.

There would be nothing strange in it at all. That was what made it a good plan, and that was what, much later, made it a dangerous one.

Outside, the tide came up under the frozen bridge supports and boomed against them like something knocking, patiently, to be let in.
