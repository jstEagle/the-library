## 3. A Ledger of Drowned Men

The counting room smelled of tallow and old paper, a smell Lior had once thought of as the company's breath — dry, patient, indifferent to what it consumed. She had been sent there at dusk to carry out the ash from the grate before Clerk Aldous Fen locked up for the night, a task so small it barely counted as work, which meant no one watched her do it.

No one watching was the most dangerous thing in the world.

The fire had burned low, leaving the room in that amber half-light where shadows pooled like ink spilled across the floorboards. Fen's desk sat beneath the one salt-fogged window, its surface a country of stacked ledgers, each spine stamped with the year and the words H&V — VESSAMARE — DIVER ACCOUNTS. Lior set down the ash pail. She told herself she was only straightening the stack so it wouldn't topple when she bumped the desk. She told herself a great many things.

Her father had been number forty-one in her memory. Not a ledger number — she had never seen his name written in any book but her own mind — but she kept him the way she kept everything: precisely. Josiah Thornbury, drowned the autumn she was nine, off the oyster beds past the breakwater. The company had given her mother a receipt for his diving coat, returned waterlogged, and a bill for the rope that had come back with nothing tied to either end. Her mother had paid it. Her mother had paid everything until paying killed her too, slow as rust.

Lior pulled the nearest ledger toward her and opened it.

The handwriting was beautiful. That was the first horror — how lovely it looked, the copperplate flourishes on names she knew. *Okonwe, T.* *Sandoval, R.* *Bray, E. (widow).* Each entry began with equipment issued: coat, helmet, air hose, weights, knife. Each entry ended with the same phrase, repeated down the years in different hands but always the same words:

*Equipment lost at sea. Balance outstanding.*

Lost at sea. As if the sea had picked their pockets. As if the men themselves had been an afterthought, a footnote to the missing gear.

She turned pages with fingers gone suddenly cold. Tomas Okonwe, drowned three winters past — his account passed to his daughter, aged eleven, indentured against the debt. Elias Bray, drowned two summers ago — his widow billed for the helmet, billed again for retrieval efforts, *efforts discontinued due to cost*. And near the bottom of the page, in ink still sharp enough to bite: *Thornbury, J. Coat recovered. Rope recovered. Weights not recovered. Balance outstanding: 4 marks 12 shillings.*

Four marks and twelve shillings. Her father's life, itemized. The weights that had dragged him under were the only thing the sea had kept, because they were the only thing worth keeping, and the company had put the loss on a nine-year-old girl's account like a stain she was expected to scrub out with her whole life.

Lior stood very still. The ash pail sat forgotten by the grate. Somewhere below, the tide knocked against the pilings, patient as arithmetic.

She should close the book. She had survived seven years of indenture by being invisible, by knowing exactly what she was permitted to see — the inside of a washbasin, the bottom of a coal scuttle, the middle distance. But her hands didn't move. Her hands turned the page instead, and another, and another, and the dead accumulated around her like silt: men whose faces she knew from the docks, men whose children she shared bread with, all of them converted into balances outstanding, debts that did not die when the debtor did but sank downward through the generations like anchors on a line.

*They inherit,* she thought. *We inherit.* She had always known this in the body — known it in the hunger of lean winters, in the way her mother counted coins three times before spending one. But knowing in the body and seeing it in copperplate were different hungers entirely. Written down, it became real the way a drowned man becomes real when they finally bring him up.

The door latch clicked.

Lior's blood went to ice. She had heard Clerk Fen's step a thousand times across the compound yard — the drag of his left heel, the small cough he carried like luggage. She had perhaps four seconds. She used them badly. Instead of closing the ledger she found herself gripping it, as though it were something that could be defended, and so she was standing there clutching the evidence of her crime when Aldous Fen came into his own lamplight and saw her.

He was thinner than he'd been at harvest, she noticed. Thinner even than last week. His collar sat loose on a neck going gray, and his eyes — pale, watery things — went from her face to the open ledger and stayed there a long moment.

"Page," he said quietly. "Which page."

"I—" Her voice came out cracked. "I was taking out the ash. I wasn't—"

"I know what you weren't doing." He crossed the room slowly, one hand pressed to his side as if holding something in. "Which page."

"Tomas Okonwe," she said, because lying to a dying man seemed suddenly worse than dying itself. "And my father."

Something moved through his face — not surprise. Something more like confirmation, the look of a man who has bet on a horse and watches it come in. He lowered himself into his chair with a sound like a rope creaking, and then he coughed.

It was not the small dry cough he carried around the yard. This one bent him double, handkerchief already raised, and when he drew it away from his mouth Lior saw the dark bloom against the white linen, wet and spreading. Fen folded the handkerchief closed with terrible care, the way a man folds away a letter he cannot answer yet.

"You'll be whipped," he said. "Or bound over another five years. Those are your options if I call Brannick, and he sleeps light these days." He looked up at her, and his eyes were clear in a way his voice wasn't. "Or you can tell me why you were reading."

Lior's heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough to hurt. Every instinct she owned said *run*, said *grovel*, said *be small until the danger passes*. But her eyes went down to the ledger still open between them, to her father's name in its neat and murderous script, and something in her chest that had been clenched for seven years — maybe seventeen — refused to unclench now.

"Because nobody reads it," she said. "That's the whole trick, isn't it? You write us down and nobody ever reads it back."

Fen exhaled — a long, rattling breath that might have been laughter in a healthier man. "Seven years in this room," he said, "and the first person to say something true about it is the ash girl." He reached beneath the stack, unhurried, and drew out a single sheet of paper — torn, she could see, ragged along one edge, as if ripped violently from a binding. He laid it flat on the desk between them, smoothing it once with both palms, the gesture almost tender.

"This morning I copied the spring manifest for the *Merrow's Bell*. Weights, cargo, accounts receivable. Standard practice." His voice dropped. "Except the manifest is false. Has been false every spring for eleven years. The clean copy goes aboard; the true figures stay here, in books like the one you're holding, and the true figures would hang every officer of this company from the harbor crane if a magistrate ever read them."

Lior stared at the torn page. "Why tear it out?"

"Because tonight I couldn't make myself write the lie twice." He coughed again, shorter, and pressed the handkerchief to his lips until it passed. When he spoke again his voice had thinned to thread. "Thirty-one divers lost in eleven years. Do you know what the true recovery rate is on Vessamare beds, girl? Half what the reports claim. The beds are worked past reason because the debts demand it. Men go down sick, exhausted, half-frozen, because their fathers' helmets are still on the company's books and interest doesn't grieve. It just accrues."

"My father," Lior whispered.

"Went down in weather no diver should have been in, because his account was three seasons overdue and the overseer wanted it cleared before audit." Fen said it flatly, without ornament, and somehow that was worse than any flourish of cruelty could have been. "There it is. Fourteen years I've written those entries. I told myself I was keeping records — that someday the record would matter. Then I started telling myself I was too old, too sick, too far in. And today I tore a page out of the manifest of the Harrow & Vale Trading Company, and tomorrow I will likely be dead, and the sum total of my courage fits on one sheet of paper."

The lamp guttered. Outside, wind had begun working at the shutters — the season's first real storm, the one that would close the bridge, though none of them yet knew by how much or for how long. Lior felt the draft move through the room like a hand turning pages.

"Why show me?" she asked. "You could burn it. Burn all of it, and die clean."

"Burn it?" For the first time, anger lit him — a brief, guttering flame. "Burn it and thirty-one drownings become bad luck forever. Burn it and every child carrying a dead man's debt carries it until the company releases them, which is never." He pushed himself upright, swaying, and held the torn page out to her. "Someone should know. That's all. Not justice — I'm past believing I'll see justice. Just — someone should *know*. Know it truly, in the numbers, where it can't be argued into fog. Someone who'll still be alive in the spring."

Lior did not take the page. Not yet. Her hands wouldn't obey; they had turned pages all evening without permission, and now they refused this. So she looked at it instead — at the columns of true weights, true losses, true sums — and understood that whatever she did next, she would never be the ash girl again. Knowledge was a current. You could fight it or ride it, but you could not stand in it unchanged.

"If I take it," she said slowly, "and they find me with it, they won't whip me. They'll drown me and bill my family for the rope."

"Yes," Fen said. "They will."

Maren Kettlefelt had grown up believing the harbor had two voices: the day voice, all gulls and winches and shouting, and the night voice, which was just the water talking to itself under the pilings. Tonight the night voice had a storm in its throat. She stood in the doorway of the net shed, watching the first gusts worry the lantern flames, and counted heads out of habit.

Forty-one workers sealed inside the compound walls until the tides eased. Forty-one mouths, and by midwinter the stores ran thin, and thin stores made men stupid and cruel unless somebody got ahead of it. That was Maren's actual trade, whatever the company rolls said — not hauling nets, but hauling people. Keeping the stew pot honest. Keeping the songs loud enough to drown out the arithmetic of who owed whom.

She saw Lior Thornbury cross the yard from the counting house and stop halfway, just standing in the wind like a woman who'd walked into a door and hadn't finished feeling it. Maren had watched that girl for years the way you watch a wick: waiting to see if she'd catch. Seven years indentured and never a complaint logged, never a friend claimed openly, always three steps outside the circle of the fire — but always listening to it. Maren had seen her give her bread ration, wordless, to the Okonwe girl during the hard week in October, and then go hungry herself without a flicker.

Tonight Lior's fist was closed around something. Even across the yard, even in the wind, Maren could see it — the white-knuckle grip of a woman holding a thing that mattered more than her hand did.

"Well," Maren murmured to the storm, to the harbor, to nobody. "Look at that. Something finally caught."

She started toward the yard to meet her — because whatever the girl was carrying, it would need hiding soon, and hiding things was the other half of Maren's trade — and behind her, in the counting house window, the lamp went out.
