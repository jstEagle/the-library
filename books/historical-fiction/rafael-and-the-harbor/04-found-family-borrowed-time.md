## 4. Found Family, Borrowed Time

The first snow of the season came down over Vessamare like ash from a fire nobody had put out, and Maren Okonkwo stood at the mouth of the long shed where the dockworkers wintered, watching it settle on the tarps and rigging and the shoulders of the men who still insisted on working the outer quay until the ice drove them in. Inside, the shed smelled of pitch and wet wool and the particular sweetness of onions gone soft in a pot too small for forty people. Someone had wedged the stove door shut with a brick. Someone else had stolen that brick from the company's warehouse wall. This was how things worked among them: everything borrowed, everything shared, nothing owned outright except grief.

She had lived through nine of these closed-harbor winters. She knew their shape. The first days were loud — men arguing over bunks, women claiming corners near the stove, children running the aisles between stacked crates like it was a festival. Then the quiet came. The cold got into the bones. The company counted its stores twice a day and counted its workers once, and everyone learned to be smaller than they were.

But this year there was stew.

Not much of it. Barley and salt pork and three fish Old Sefa had pulled from under the ice on a line he wasn't supposed to have, but it was hot, and hot food in the compound was contraband of a kind. Brannick rationed from the company kitchen by head-count, and head-counts were always wrong in the workers' favor because people lied about who belonged to which bunk, and Maren had spent twenty years getting very good at lying about who belonged where.

"Sit," she told Lior Thornbury, pressing a tin bowl into her hands before the girl could do her usual trick of serving everyone else first and eating standing up like a heron. "You've been copying that man's papers since dawn. You'll eat sitting down or you won't eat."

Lior sat. She sat the way she did everything — carefully, as though the floor might present her with an invoice for it later. Her bowl steamed. Around them the shed filled: netmenders, winchmen, the pearl divers with their scarred ears and their permanent half-deafness, all of them crowding close to the stove with bowls balanced on knees. A child — Pipa, six years old, missing two teeth — wedged herself between Lior and the crate wall and stared into the girl's bowl with the frank appraisal of the young.

Lior looked at the child. Looked at her own full bowl. And then, without ceremony, she tipped half her stew into Pipa's, and when Maren opened her mouth to scold her, Lior said, "I wasn't hungry," in the flat voice of someone reciting a number she'd memorized, and Maren understood that the girl would rather go without than owe anyone warmth she couldn't repay.

That was the thing about Lior Thornbury. Everything with her was ledgers. Debts and payments, credits and costs. She'd been raised inside Harrow & Vale's arithmetic until she thought in columns, and Maren had watched enough winters take people apart to know that a person who believed every kindness was a loan was a person the company had already half-won.

"You know what I did before the docks?" Maren said, settling beside her. It wasn't really a question; she'd decided three days ago she was going to tell this story, and she was a woman who finished what she started.

Lior glanced up. "No."

"Neither does anyone here, mostly. I kept accounts." She let that land. Saw the flicker in the girl's eyes — surprise, then something warier. "For a shipping house in Corvane, before it folded. I could add faster than any clerk they had, and I liked it. Numbers don't lie to you. Numbers don't smile at you on Tuesday and dock your wages on Friday. I thought if I just kept perfect books, perfect books would keep me."

"And?"

"And the house folded because the owners were thieves, and the last thing the senior partner said to me was that I'd been a credit to the firm. He meant it kindly." She laughed, short and bitter. "I came here with nothing and found out the difference between keeping accounts and keeping people. One of them you can balance. The other one you just keep."

Pipa, having demolished the stolen stew, had fallen asleep against Lior's arm, her head tipped back, mouth open. Lior went very still, the way people do when something fragile lands on them and they're afraid to move.

"She'll soak your sleeve," Maren said.

"I know." Lior didn't move.

Maren looked at her a long moment — this diver's daughter with ink on her fingers and frost-nipped cheeks, holding a sleeping child like she'd been handed something explosive. *You can't save everyone,* she wanted to say. *And you don't have to earn your place here. It isn't a ledger.* But she'd been doing this long enough to know that some lessons only take when the student is ready, so instead she said, "Eat your half before it goes cold," and began, softly, to hum.

It was one of the smuggled songs. Not smuggled goods — smuggled songs, the ones the company forbade on compound grounds because they were strike songs, older than most of the singers, from the year the divers had walked off the pontoons and the harbor had gone silent for a month and the company had broken it with hunger. Singing them indoors was technically grounds for dismissal. Everyone sang them anyway, low, under the wind, passing verses around the shed like cups.

By the third verse half the room was humming. By the fifth, old Sefa's cracked voice had joined, then the netmenders, then the whole low rumble of it, forty voices barely above breath, the words more felt than heard:

*The tide takes the father, the tide takes the son,*
*but the tide never keeps what the water has won—*

Lior's lips moved. She didn't know the words — Maren saw her mouthing along a half-beat behind, learning, filing it away — but her shoulder had come down from around her ears for the first time since the bridge closed. Her hand rested light on the sleeping child's hair.

*There,* Maren thought. *There it is.* That loosening. That was the whole work of winter, right there in one small unclenching. You couldn't fight Harrow & Vale with fists. You fought them by refusing, one night at a time, to become the thing they'd designed you to be.

The song ended. The stew pot was scraped. Outside, the snow thickened, sealing them in deeper, and for a little while the shed was warm in every way a shed could be.

* * *

Brannick came at dusk three days later, and he came with a lantern and a list.

Maren heard him before she saw him — boots on frozen planking, that unhurried tread of a man who had never in his life needed to hurry because everyone else made way. He appeared in the doorway of the long shed with two company men behind him and the lantern held up, and the light swung across the bunks and caught faces and turned them yellow and still.

"Evening," he said. Conversational. Almost friendly. That was the worst of Brannick; he never performed cruelty, which meant you couldn't dismiss it as performance. "Head-count."

"We were counted Tuesday," Maren said, rising.

"You'll be counted again." He consulted his list. "Forty-one souls assigned to this shed. I make forty-three at supper last night. So either my list is wrong, or two of you are harboring guests, and guests need registering."

Nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere in the back a baby whimpered and was hushed.

Brannick moved down the aisle slowly, lantern high, looking at each face the way he'd look at cargo — assessing weight, damage, worth. He stopped at the far end where the divers bunked, and Maren saw his eyes fix on the space beneath the lowest bunk, and her stomach dropped, because she knew what he was looking for even if he hadn't said it yet.

A page. Torn from Clerk Fen's manifest. Fen himself had torn it — Maren had seen the man do it, gray-faced and shaking, coughing blood into his handkerchief in the records office while Lior stood white as bone beside him. Whatever was on that page, Fen had decided it mattered more than his own safety, and now the company knew a page was missing, and Brannick was hunting it through the compound like a man dragging a river for a body.

"Open search," Brannick said mildly. "Bunks and footlockers. Anyone objects, they can take it up with me personally, tonight, in my office." Nobody objected. There was nowhere to object to.

The company men went down the rows. They were not gentle. They flipped mattresses, dumped sea chests, shook out spare clothes with a violence calibrated to remind everyone whose ground this was. Maren watched a netmender named Col gather his dead wife's shawl out of the dirt with shaking hands and say nothing, absolutely nothing, and she filed the image away where she kept all such images — fuel for a fire she hoped someday to light.

They reached the divers' row. They reached the lowest bunk. Under it, wrapped in oilcloth, was a doll — Pipa's doll, rag-stuffed, one button eye — and Maren's heart went into her throat because the doll had not been there yesterday morning, and what was inside the doll had not been there either, and she had sewn the seam shut with her own thread at two in the morning while the girl slept.

One of the company men crouched. Reached for it.

"That's mine," Maren said, loud and clear, and stepped forward. "My niece's. She cries without it."

The man looked at Brannick. Brannick looked at Maren — a long, level look, the look of a man doing arithmetic of his own — and then he nodded, almost courteously, and the man set the doll down untouched.

Afterward she couldn't say why he'd let it go. Perhaps because searching a child's toy for evidence was the kind of thing even Brannick hesitated at. Perhaps because he already suspected exactly who had the page and wanted her to sweat. Perhaps both. With Brannick you never got the whole column; he showed you figures selectively.

He paused at the door on his way out. The lantern light caught the lines of his face, deep as channels cut by weather.

"Clerk Fen is ill," he said to the room. "Very ill. When a man is dying, he sometimes does strange things with paper. If anyone finds such paper lying about — loose, misplaced, forgotten — the honest thing is to return it." His gaze traveled the room and settled, briefly, deliberately, on Lior Thornbury, sitting quiet at the end with a mending basket in her lap. "The company rewards honesty. It remembers everything else."

Then he was gone, boots fading into the snow-dark, and the shed breathed again, forty-some people exhaling at once.

Maren crossed the room and sat down next to Lior. The girl's hands were steady on the mending — too steady, the steadiness of someone gripping something invisible with all her strength. Up close Maren could see the pulse jumping in her throat.

"He knows it's you," Maren said quietly.

"He suspects." Lior's voice was flat. "There's a difference. Suspicion isn't proof. Proof is what he needs, and proof is what he doesn't have." She threaded a needle without looking at it. "Yet."

Outside, the wind rose off the harbor and rattled the tarps like canvas on a ship, and Maren understood, sitting in the warm noisy dark with a hunted page sewn into a child's doll ten feet away, that the borrowed time had gotten shorter. Winter had only begun. Brannick had all season — and all season was more time than a dying man had left.

Whatever Lior was going to do with what Fen gave her, she would have to decide soon.
