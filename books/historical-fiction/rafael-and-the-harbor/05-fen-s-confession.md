## 5. Fen's Confession

The infirmary smelled of tallow smoke and rot, of camphor rubbed into walls that had absorbed too many winters. Lior had never been inside it before — the company treated divers at a separate shed by the water gate, where they could be counted coming and going — and she was struck by how small it was. Two cots. A stove that gave more shadow than heat. A window glazed with salt-fog through which the harbor bridge showed as a gray suggestion, sealed and snow-laden, its chains iced like something drowned.

Fen lay on the cot nearest the window, propped against a rolled blanket, his chest rising in a rhythm that kept breaking off and starting again, like a man rowing against a current only he could feel.

He had sent for her through the boy who hauled ashes — *tell the Thornbury girl, tell no one else* — and she had come because she was already lost. Because the bridge was shut, and Brannick was counting heads twice daily now, and somewhere beneath her bunk or sewn into Maren's keeping lay a page torn from a manifest that could hang every man in this compound or free them all, depending on whose hands held it when the ice went out.

"You came." Fen's voice was a whisper with gravel in it. He turned his head; the lamplight found the yellow of his skin, the hollows under his cheekbones where flesh had simply gone away, as if the sickness were eating him from the inside out and leaving only the clerk's architecture — the precise jaw, the ink-stained fingers folded on the blanket like two tired birds.

"You sent for me," Lior said.

"I did. Sit." A cough took him then, long and wet, and he pressed the handkerchief to his mouth and held it there, and when he drew it away Lior made herself look at what it carried. She had grown up around blood — her father's hands after the shells bit them, the gaff wounds on the boats — but this was different. This was the sea leaving a man from within.

"The physician comes at dawn," Fen said. "Or he doesn't. It hardly matters which."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because you read my ledger." He said it without accusation, almost with pleasure, the way a man might note that someone else had finally solved a riddle he'd set years ago. "You stood in my office with your thumb on the page and I watched you understand it faster than any magistrate ever has. Do you know what I thought, in that moment?"

Lior said nothing.

"I thought: *she has been memorizing us.*" His eyes were bright and terrible. "You recite debt figures like scripture. The other workers think it's a quirk. But you've been building an account of Harrow & Vale inside your own skull for six years, haven't you? Every weight entered wrong. Every price adjusted downward. Every diver billed for the rope that dragged him under."

Her throat tightened. "I remember numbers," she said. "It isn't a crime."

"It is here," Fen said softly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Outside, wind worried the eaves. Somewhere below, the night watch called the hour — two bells, muffled, the winter count — and the sound seemed to pass through the walls of the infirmary like a hand through water. Fen asked for water and could not keep it down, and after that he asked instead for her to come closer, closer still, until she could smell the sickness on him, sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a closed room.

"I have been dying since autumn," he said. "I've known since the cough started in the manifest season. So I did what clerks do. I put things in order." He lifted one trembling hand toward the narrow table beside the cot, where a leather folio sat squared to the edge with a precision that hurt to look at. "That's not the clean copy. That's mine."

She didn't touch it. Not yet.

"When I first came to Vessamare," Fen said, "I believed the ledgers were true and the world merely untidy. I was nineteen. I copied weights at the pearl-house scale — you've seen it, the great brass balance by the drying racks. And I learned very quickly that the scale told whatever story the man holding the counterweights wished it to tell." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Lior had seen in a year of hard things. "A diver brings up forty marks of shell. The scale reads thirty-one. Where did nine marks go? Into the company's pocket, and then onto the diver's account as a shortfall, and then the shortfall gathers interest, and the interest gathers interest, and a man dives until he dies owing more than his lungs are worth."

"My father," Lior said. Her voice came out flat, strange, as if someone else had borrowed it.

"Yes." Fen didn't flinch from it. "Thornbury. Forty-one marks, spring before last. Entered as twenty-nine." He recited it the way she would have — exact, unsoftened. "I entered that figure myself. My own hand. I want you to know that I know."

The stove ticked. Lior discovered her fists were clenched in her lap and made herself open them, finger by finger, because Brannick's men searched bunks now and a girl with white knuckles walking the corridors at night was a girl who got noticed.

"Why tell me?" she said. "Why not burn it and die clean?"

"Because burning it changes nothing." Fen pushed himself higher against the blanket, and the effort cost him; he had to wait out a shudder before he could speak again. "Burn the folio and the scheme goes on. New clerks, new weights, new widows' debts. Paper is not the sin, girl. Paper is just where we wrote it down. What matters is whether anyone ever *reads* it aloud."

* * *

Then he began to dictate, and she understood at last why he had chosen her, and what he meant to make of her, and how much it would cost.

"The falsified weights begin at the brass scale — counterweights shaved and swapped on the overseer's word, never in writing, always verbal, so the order dies in the air. The shortfall entries are posted quarterly to each diver's account at eight percent compounded — illegal rate, sanctioned by the resident factor, initialled *B.V.* — you'll find his initials in the margin codes, third column, small circles. The magistrates—" he paused, gathered breath like a diver gathering air, "—the colonial magistrate receives an annual gratuity routed through the chandlery invoices, marked as rope-oil. Four hundred crowns, spring shipment. Oyelaran's predecessor took it for eleven years. Whether Oyelaran does—" his mouth twisted, "—that is what your page will find out."

"And the ship," Lior said quietly. "The morning ship."

"The morning ship carries the clean books out each spring, before the ice fully clears. Duplicate ledgers, corrected. Every figure massaged until the colony office in Corvale sees a modest, struggling, honest trade. By the time any inspector reaches Vessamare, the originals are ash and the duplicates are gospel." He looked at her with those bright, ruined eyes. "This spring, the clean books sail on the *Vigilant Mercy*. First fair tide. You have until then."

"That's weeks."

"It's a lifetime, if you use it." He reached for the folio, and his hands shook so badly that Lior finally took it herself, feeling the weight of it, heavier than paper should be, heavy as ballast. "Inside: the weight schedules, the interest codes, the chandlery routing, and sworn statements — mine, witnessed and sealed, taken in October when I still believed I'd live to deliver them myself." A cough bent him double; when he surfaced, there was blood at the corner of his mouth and he wiped it away with the back of his hand, impatiently, like a man annoyed at spilled ink. "And the page. The page you already know."

From beneath his pillow he drew a folded square, worn soft at the creases, and held it out to her. She knew it before she touched it — the torn edge, the column of names, the debts inherited by children too young to sign their own indentures. She had read it once by candle stub in Maren's quarters, and once was enough to carry it forever.

"Take it," Fen said. "Not for me. I'm past saving and mostly past mattering." His breath rattled. "For the record, girl. Do you understand what a record *is*? It's the only weapon the dead have. In fifty years some historian in Corvale will write his tidy volume about the pearl trade, and if all he can consult is the clean books, he'll write that Vessamare was poor and honest and its people unlucky. He'll write that your father was improvident. That the divers died of misfortune." His voice cracked, and he let it. "Don't let the victors write their version unopposed. Someone should know. Someone should *say* it, in daylight, on the record, where ink cannot be burned."

"Brannick will kill me," Lior said. It wasn't fear speaking. It was arithmetic — her own kind of ledger.

"He may," Fen agreed. "Or he may offer you freedom to betray them. He'll be reasonable about it. That's the horror of men like Brannick; they're reasonable all the way down." He caught her wrist with sudden strength, strength that seemed impossible in that wasted body, and pulled her close enough to see the map of veins across his eyelids. "Listen to me. Whatever he offers — and he will offer — remember that freedom given by the man who owns the scales is just another entry in his ledger. You don't take freedom. You *take the record.*"

His grip loosened. Slid away.

"Fen." For the first time since her father drowned, Lior heard her own voice shake. "Who do I give it to?"

"Oyelaran," he whispered. "If he's honest, it ends aboard the *Mercy*. If he's bought—" the ghost of a smile, "—then you'll need to be cleverer than me, which shouldn't be difficult. I was only ever a good clerk." His eyes drifted toward the fogged window, toward the sealed bridge. "Get it on the record, Lior Thornbury. Before the tide takes everything again. Before they write their version."

* * *

She stayed while the fever climbed, fetching water he couldn't drink, watching the lamplight gutter. Near dawn his breathing changed — longer silences between the rows — and when the ash-boy scratched at the door to say the physician was coming, Fen opened his eyes one last time and found hers.

"Memorize it," he murmured. "In case of fire. Memorize everything. Numbers no one asks you to remember."

"I have," she said. "I always have."

Something eased in his face — relief, or release, or simply the end of carrying it alone — and his eyes closed, and this time they stayed closed long enough that Lior stood up and backed toward the door with the folio pressed flat against her ribs beneath her coat, warm as a second heartbeat.

Out in the corridor the cold hit her like a slap, and the bells rang four, and she moved through the blue dark of the compound with her collar up and her mind already working — routes, alibis, the weight of the page against her skin. At the corner by the cooperage she stopped, because a lantern was burning late in the overseer's office, and behind the frosted glass Brannick's shadow sat motionless at his desk, awake, patient, waiting for the morning the way a spider waits.

Lior pulled the folio tighter against her heart and walked on into the dark, toward the dockworkers' quarters, toward Maren — and toward a choice that was already choosing her.
