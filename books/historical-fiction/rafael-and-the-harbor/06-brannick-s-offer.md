## 6. Brannick's Offer

Brannick had learned long ago that a compound in winter was a body that could not stop talking. Every wall had its murmur. Every pipe had its cough. He stood at the window of the counting house with his hands clasped behind his back and listened to the building tell him things it did not know it knew: the scrape of the night watch's boots on the outer stair, the settling groan of the salt-crusted beams, the small industrious sounds of Aldous Fen dying one floor below — the wheeze of his lungs working like bellows full of wet sand.

Fen would be dead by thaw. Everyone knew it except perhaps Fen, who kept copying manifests with ink-stained fingers and the stubborn dignity of a man pretending his body had not already been foreclosed upon. Brannick had seen it before. The company ate men slowly and then all at once, and the ones who went quietly were always the most dangerous, because they had time to think about what they'd swallowed.

The missing page sat in Brannick's mind like a splinter under a nail. One leaf torn from the winter manifest — torn, not lost, not misfiled. Torn meant hands. Torn meant intent. And Fen had been the last to touch it.

Brannick turned from the window. On his desk lay a folder of papers he had spent three evenings preparing, and beneath it, weighted by a brass paperknife shaped like an anchor, a single document that smelled faintly of the seal-press: a certificate of manumission, blank but for the name-line, the wax still soft as a wound.

*Once,* he thought, *that would have been my name on it.*

He remembered the weight of his own indenture the way other men remembered their mothers' voices. Eleven years old, sold against a debt his father had signed drunk and never understood, and the number stamped into the margin of his contract like a brand: seven years, four months, eleven days. He had counted them every morning for thirty years, though the debt died decades ago. Some arithmetic you cannot unlearn. It lived in the body like a scar lives in the skin — painless until the cold came, and then it ached as if freshly cut.

That was why he understood Lior Thornbury better than she would ever believe. That was why he watched her the way a man watches weather.

* * *

She came when summoned, which told him something. A guilty girl runs; this one walked, slow and even, her boots leaving prints in the frost-slick corridor like punctuation marks. She stopped inside the door of his office and did not sit, though he gestured to the chair. Good. Sitting meant settling in. Standing meant keeping your exits.

"Master Brannick."

"Thornbury." He let the silence stretch, because silence was a tool he'd honed for thirty years and it cost him nothing now. Outside, wind dragged itself along the harbor bridge, moaning through the ironwork like something grieving. "How is Clerk Fen?"

"Dying."

The word came flat and quick, no decoration. He noted that. Guilty people decorate.

"So I'm told. Dying men are careless men. They forget where they set things down." He opened the folder. "You copied the winter manifest twice. Once in October, once at Fen's order, two days before the bridge closed. Tell me about the second copy."

Her eyes didn't move. He gave her that much credit; most people's eyes fled toward windows, toward doors, toward anywhere that wasn't the question. Hers stayed on him like a diver's gaze down through dark water, measuring depth.

"It was a copy," she said. "I copied what was given me."

"And what was given you?"

"Papers."

A lesser interrogator would have pressed there, would have heard the stone wall going up brick by brick and thrown himself against it. Brannick poured tea instead. Two cups, because courtesy disarmed more thoroughly than any chain, and slid one across the desk. She looked at it and did not touch it, and he almost smiled. Her mother had been a diver too, he remembered. Pearl boats off the eastern shallows. The whole family had lungs like leather and judgment like iron.

"Do you know what I was doing at your age?" he said. "No — don't guess. No one guesses right." He leaned back. "I was hauling cordage on the north quay for eleven hours a day, and at night I slept in a loft with nine other children, and every morning I woke and counted how many days remained of my seven years, four months, eleven days. Do you know what I learned in that loft?"

"That the company counts everything."

"The opposite." He held her gaze. "That nothing counts unless someone makes it count. Order isn't given, Thornbury. It's built, plank by plank, day by day, by people willing to do the ugly carpentry. This harbor feeds eleven thousand souls because someone keeps the ledgers true and the schedules kept and the storms planned around. Take away the counting and you have Vessamare the way my grandfather described it — bodies in the tide-flats and no one punished, because no one wrote anything down."

"You keep very good count of drowned men's debts," she said quietly.

There it was. The splinter sliding deeper. Brannick felt no surprise, only a grim sort of satisfaction, the way a physician feels confirming a diagnosis he has dreaded. She knew. Or she suspected enough to strike at it, and striking meant caring, and caring meant she had found something worth caring about.

"I keep count of everything," he said. "Including pages that have gone missing from my manifest."

* * *

He rose then, because this next part deserved standing, and crossed to the window where the compound spread out below him in the gray afternoon: the divers' quarters huddled against the seawall, smoke rising crooked from a dozen chimneys, laundry frozen stiff on lines like flags of some small stubborn nation. Maren Oduya's stew-smell drifted even here, onion and salt fish, the smell of people making a home out of a holding pen. He had eaten that stew once, years ago, before he took the overseer's office. He had never let himself eat it again.

"When the spring ship comes," he said, "it carries the books to the colonial magistrate. Clean books. Audited books. And in the autumn it brings back magistrates who sign whatever the auditors put in front of them, because the alternative is governing a port that stops feeding them. That is not corruption, Thornbury. That is architecture. You don't tear down a load-bearing wall because you dislike its color."

"Fen tore a page out of it."

"Fen is a dying man with a grudge against his own mortality, tearing at the wallpaper." He turned. "But pages in the wrong hands become fires. And fires in a sealed compound burn everyone — the guilty and the innocent alike, and mostly the innocent, because the guilty own boats." He came back to the desk and laid his hand flat on the document beneath the anchor-paperknife. "Here is what I am prepared to do, and hear all of it before you answer."

He drew it out. The certificate. Blank name-line. Soft wax. He watched her look at it, watched the hunger move behind her face like a fish under ice — quick, involuntary, instantly suppressed. Seven years, four months, eleven days. He knew exactly what the blank line was worth to her. He had stood where she stood, on the far side of a desk like this one, being offered a smaller mercy by a harder man, and he had taken it, and he had told himself for thirty years that taking it had been survival rather than surrender.

"Your freedom," he said. "Full and legal, sealed and witnessed, effective the moment you hand me the page Fen removed and give me the names of everyone who knows of it. Not punishment. Protection. Because I will be honest with you, since honesty is the one currency I deal in: whoever holds that page intends to use it, and using it will bring the inquiry here, and the inquiry will not ask politely. It will take this compound apart joint by joint, and the joints are people. Oduya. The Okonkwo girl. Old Pietr who can't walk a gangway anymore. Everyone whose name you'd be protecting by staying silent — silence delivers them to the fire faster than speech ever would. Give them to me instead, and I promise you this: I will bury the page myself, and no one will ever know it existed, and your people will see the thaw safe, and you'll see it free."

The wind moaned again through the bridge iron. Somewhere below, a child laughed — one bright note, quickly muffled, the sound of a childhood being conducted indoors for the season.

Lior Thornbury picked up the tea after all. He hadn't expected that. She drank slowly, deliberately, buying herself the time that drinking provided, and he recognized the technique because it was his own, and something in his chest tightened with a feeling uncomfortably adjacent to respect.

"May I ask you a question, Master Brannick?"

"You may."

"When they freed you." Her voice was level, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the sharpest thing anyone had said to him in twenty years. "Did it feel like being saved? Or did it feel like being paid?"

He did not answer. He had no answer he could survive saying aloud. Thirty years of ledgers stood behind him, true and balanced and immaculate, and somewhere in their margins, if he was honest — and honesty was the one currency he dealt in — the same entries Fen had torn out, entered in his own neat hand because the scheme predated him and he had chosen, year after year, to keep the walls standing rather than ask what they were built on. Load-bearing. He had called it load-bearing. He wondered, suddenly and terribly, whether Fen had once stood in this room, on the far side of this desk, being offered a smaller mercy by a harder man.

"Take the night," Brannick said. His voice came out rougher than intended. "Think on it. The page burns as easily in my stove as anywhere."

She set down the cup, aligned it precisely with the desk edge, and left. At the door she paused, half-turned.

"For what it's worth," she said, "you counted your days right. All of them. Nobody ever made you free. You just stopped being owned."

Then she was gone, and her footsteps faded down the corridor, and Brannick stood alone with the blank certificate and the soft wax and the sound of the wind in the bridge iron, and he understood — with the cold clarity of a man reading his own ledger for the first time — that she had just refused him without saying no, and that whatever she decided before morning, the page would move tonight.
