## 7. The Search

Maren had sewn a hundred sails in her life, and she knew the sound of cloth tearing before it finished tearing. That was how she heard Brannick's men going through the workers' quarters — not footsteps, which could be muffled, but the rip and pop of stitching giving way, the wet crack of a straw pallet split open along its seam. The sound came through the frost-furred window of the longhouse like a language she'd been fluent in since childhood: *someone is looking for something, and they do not care what they break to find it.*

She had maybe a minute. Perhaps less.

The page sat against her ribs where she'd kept it for three days, folded small as a prayer, wrapped in oilcloth because Fen had warned her — dying man's foresight — that ink runs when blood or seawater finds it. She had slept with it. Eaten with it. Carried it into the privy at night like a woman hiding an illegitimate child. And now the compound was sealed, the bridge was ice-locked, and Brannick was tearing bunks apart one by one, working his way toward the longhouse where forty dockworkers slept shoulder to shoulder and where a nine-year-old girl named Pip was currently braiding string dolls by the stove.

Pip's mother had drowned two summers past, billed afterward for the loss of her own diving slate. Pip did not cry about it anymore; she made dolls instead, little figures of scrap cloth and harbor-gravel stuffing, and gave them names of people who were still alive. It was, Maren thought, either the sanest or the saddest thing done in Vessamare all winter.

*The doll.* The thought arrived whole, the way the best and worst thoughts do.

"Come here, minnow," Maren said, keeping her voice level as a calm tide. "Bring me that one you're making."

Pip looked up with those quick gray eyes that missed nothing on the docks and nothing in faces either. "Which one?"

"The one for your mother."

The room went quiet around them. Not silent — the longhouse never went silent, there was always someone coughing, someone mending, someone humming the smuggled songs under their breath — but quiet in the way water goes quiet over something sinking.

Pip brought the doll. Half-finished, one arm stuffed, the other hanging empty as a promise. Maren turned it over in her hands once, twice, feeling the weight of what she was about to do settle into her bones like ballast. Then, with a sailmaker's economy, she unpicked the unfinished arm's loose stitches, slid the oilcloth packet deep into the gravel-and-rag belly, and drew the seam closed again with thread pulled from her own cuff hem. Her hands did not shake. Twenty years of needlework held them steady while everything behind her sternum shook enough for both.

"There," she said, handing it back. "Finish her arms. Keep her close."

"Who is she?" Pip asked.

"Someone who's going to hold something safe," Maren said. "You're going to sleep with her tonight. Every night until I say otherwise. Can you do that?"

"I sleep with her anyway." Pip frowned, reading Maren's face the way she read weather. "Is this a secret?"

"It's the biggest kind."

"What kind is that?"

"The kind where you don't know you're keeping it."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a harbormaster assessing tonnage, then nodded once and went back to the stove, cradling the doll in her lap like something hatched.

* * *

They reached the longhouse an hour past dark.

Not Brannick himself first — he sent his readers, two company men with cold-lamp eyes whose job was counting: heads, crates, pages, people. They came through the door without knocking because knocking was for places where you had rights, and everyone in that room understood, without discussion, that they did not. Lantern light swung across the bunks. The tearing sounds resumed.

Maren sat on her bunk with a mending basket in her lap and let her hands work while her mind ran the arithmetic of risk. Lior was across the compound — she'd be safe from this sweep, if *safe* meant anything inside walls built to keep people in. Forty souls in this room, every one of whom had seen Maren take the page from Fen's cold hand, every one of whom could end everything with a sentence. Nobody spoke. The silence itself was a kind of speech.

Old Tamko, half-blind, began singing low — one of the smuggled songs, the one about the pearl diver's daughter, except he'd changed the words years ago so it was really about the company, really about the debt ledgers, really about every diver who went down breathing borrowed air and came up owing more than pearls could cover. One voice joined, then another. The company men hated the singing; it made counting difficult, made the room into something that couldn't be inventoried.

"Quiet," said the taller reader.

Nobody quieted. Tamko sang louder, his cracked voice finding the high note that always sounded like drowning and surfacing at once.

That was when Brannick came in.

He filled the doorway the way bad news fills a letter. Big man, gray at the temples, hands scarred from rope work — and Maren knew, because the docks remembered everything, that those hands had once been indentured hands too, that he had hauled cargo as a boy for the same company whose seal now sat on his coat. He believed in order the way drowning men believe in rope. He had simply forgotten, somewhere along the climb, that rope can also be used to hang.

"We're looking for a page," he said. Conversationally. Almost gently. "Torn from a manifest. Clerk Fen took it before he died. Whoever returns it will be credited. Debts forgiven. First pick of spring berths." He let that sit in the warm air among the stew-smell and the song-echo. "Or we keep searching. My searchers are thorough men. Thoroughness isn't gentle."

Tamko's song wound down. The lanterns hissed.

Brannick's eyes moved along the bunks like fingers along a ledger line, and stopped on Pip.

On the doll in Pip's lap.

Maren felt the moment tilt. Time went thick as cold tar. She watched Brannick look at a child holding a rag doll, watched him weigh it — not with cruelty, she realized; that was the horror of it, there was no cruelty in him, only procedure — weighing whether a child's toy fell within the mandate of a missing document.

"Girl," he said. "Show me the doll."

And Maren stood up.

It wasn't courage. She wanted to be honest about that, even alone inside her own skull. It was arithmetic. A child searched would cry, and crying children draw crowds, and crowds make searches public, and Brannick avoided public. Better: a known troublemaker, a sailmaker with a mouth, a woman already marked for insolence. She stepped between Brannick's sightline and Pip's bunk and heard herself say, in the flat dockyard drawl she saved for company men:

"You want to search someone, overseer, search me. I've got nothing but opinions and a bad knee, but you're welcome to both."

Something flickered behind Brannick's eyes — not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing an old version of himself standing in the wrong place at the wrong time.

"Outside," he said.

* * *

They beat her in the yard by the salt sheds, out of sight of the longhouse windows, though Maren suspected every soul inside could hear it anyway, the way you hear a storm through walls. Two of Brannick's men did the work; Brannick stood watching with his arms crossed, and near the end he waved them off — earlier than he needed to, Maren thought dazedly, spitting blood onto the frozen ground. Earlier than he needed to. As if even his thoroughness had limits, or as if some buried indentured boy in him had flinched.

"Where is the page, Maren?" he asked, crouching to her level. His voice stayed level, almost kind. That was the worst part. "Fen was a dying man. Dying men scatter paper like gulls scatter feathers. You were closest to him. Give it back and walk away clean. I'll even write it so. 'Returned voluntarily.' No mark against you."

Maren laughed. It hurt magnificently to laugh. "You think I'm hiding it for myself?"

"I think everyone hides things for themselves. They just dress it up as principle."

"Then you've been ashore too long, overseer." She lifted her head, tasted copper and frost. "Out there—" she nodded toward the black harbor, ice-locked, silent, holding its drowned like coins in a fist— "out there we learned that some things get carried for other people. Ask any diver's kid."

Brannick studied her for a long moment. Snow started falling, fine and dry, ticking on the salt-shed roof.

"Again tomorrow," he said, rising. "Unless something changes."

* * *

They carried her back on a door taken off its hinges, which struck her, through the ringing haze, as deeply funny — the company providing furniture for its own violence. Lior was there when they laid her down, Lior's hands cold and sure on Maren's face, doing the dockside triage every diver's family knew: fingers checking ribs, breath counting breath, eyes cataloguing damage with the same precision she gave ledgers.

"Fool," Lior whispered, and her voice cracked on the word. "You absolute fool."

"Worth it," Maren managed. "Cheapest beating I ever bought."

But Lior wasn't laughing. Lior was looking past her, around the longhouse — at Tamko rethreading his song like a mended net, at old Sefa boiling willow bark without being asked, at Pip sitting solemn guard by the stove with the doll tucked under her chin, asleep sitting up, guarding a secret she didn't know she kept. At all of them, closing ranks around Maren without a word spoken, without a signal given, the way water closes over a stone.

"They'll come back," Lior said quietly. "Tomorrow. And the next day. And every day until they find it, and they won't stop at you next time." Her jaw tightened. Maren watched her do the terrible math — the same math Maren had done with the doll, only larger, multiplied by forty souls. "If I fail, they'll pay for me. All of them. Pip. Tamko. You."

"So don't fail," Maren said.

Lior looked down at her then, and Maren saw the fear in her face burn off like morning fog, and underneath it find something hard and bright and settled, ore in the rock.

"No," Lior said. "I won't."

Outside, the snow thickened over the compound, softening the fences, filling the footprints of the searchers, laying its white blank page across everything — and beneath it, hidden in a sleeping child's arms, the truth waited, stitched tight, keeping its own counsel until the morning tide came to carry it out.
