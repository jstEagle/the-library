## 8. Love Against Duty

The seawall froze in the last week of the closing tide, and Lior went to it the way she went to all the places her father had loved — quietly, and with a knife at her hip she never quite knew how to put down.

The harbor was becoming something else entirely. The water between the pilings had gone glassy, then grey, then solid enough that gulls walked on it with an air of insulted dignity. Beyond the compound walls the bridge stood dark and empty, its chains furred with ice, and beyond that the sea itself was sealing shut like a ledger being closed by someone else's hand. In three days the morning ship would come through the last open channel, take aboard the spring manifests, the clean books, the company's tidy version of the winter — and then Vessamare would be alone with its frozen harbor until thaw.

Lior sat on the seawall with her boots hanging over stone slick as whalebone, and she did what she always did when fear got too large to hold: she counted.

Three coins sewn into her coat lining. One diving knife, her mother's, bone-handled, salt-scarred. One torn page from a manifest, hidden inside a child's doll named Pip, who slept tonight in the corner of Maren's bunk with one button eye staring at the rafters. And one statement, sworn and sealed in candle wax, folded against her ribs where it rode warm as a second heartbeat.

She was still counting when Maren climbed up beside her.

Maren moved like a woman who'd spent twenty years on wet decking — sure-footed even here, even on ice — and she dropped onto the wall close enough that their shoulders touched, which on the frozen seawall was not nothing. She brought two tin cups and the smell of woodsmoke and stew grease, and she pressed one cup into Lior's hands without asking if she wanted it, because Maren never asked; she simply decided you were cold or hungry or lonely and treated it as fact.

"Brannick doubled the watch," Maren said. "Two men on the gate now. He's not looking for a thief anymore. He's looking for a witness."

"I know."

"He'll find her, too." Maren blew across her cup. "Unless she's already gone."

Lior looked out at the channel — a black seam of open water running between the shorefast ice and the sea ice, narrowing every day, the way everything narrowed in winter. "There's a skiff," she said. "Old Tam's. He owes you from the Kestrel season."

"There is a skiff," Maren agreed. "And there's a gap in the watch at slack water, four hours wide. I could walk you to it. You could be past the point before dawn and clear of the harbor before anyone knows you've gone. By the time Brannick finds the doll empty, you'd be a story. By summer you'd be a rumor. By next winter—" she shrugged, "—a woman somewhere warm, alive, free."

*Free.* The word landed in Lior like a coin dropped into deep water. She watched it sink.

"And the page?"

"The page stays. We keep it. We pass it hand to hand until someday—" Maren's jaw tightened. "Someday someone braver than us gets it where it needs to go."

"Someday is what they're counting on," Lior said quietly. "Fen said so. He said the scheme works because everyone waits. Because *someday* the magistrate will come, *someday* someone will talk, and meanwhile the morning ship sails every spring with clean books and drowned men billed for their own ropes." She turned to look at Maren, really look, and the wind took her breath sideways. "The magistrate is coming tomorrow, Maren. He's coming on that ship. Fen died believing I'd get this in front of him, and I let him believe it."

Maren didn't answer at once. She looked out at the frozen harbor, and Lior watched her do the arithmetic — Maren, who could tally a hold faster than any clerk, who understood exactly what numbers cost.

"You'd be boarding Harrow & Vale's own deck," Maren said. "With Brannick watching. With your name on their indenture rolls and your face known to every man he trusts. If it goes wrong, there's no skiff, no gap in the watch, no *someday*. There's just the hold of a company ship and a magistrate who may or may not be anything but another man paid to read what he's given."

"Yes."

"You'd be gambling your life on paper."

"I'd be gambling my life on the truth," Lior said. "It's the only thing I own that can't be searched out of a doll."

Maren set her cup down on the stone. It made a small ringing sound, absurdly loud in all that white silence.

"Do you know what I was doing," she said, "the night my brother drowned? I wasn't grieving. I was doing sums. Sitting up in the loft with a stub of candle, adding up what he'd owed, because his debt came to me — did you know that? Eighteen years old and inheriting eleven crowns of rope and basket and lamp-oil from a dead man. And I remember thinking, very clearly: *this is how they win. They make the arithmetic bigger than the grief.* You spend your whole life paying them back for loving someone, until you forget there was ever anything to love." Her voice cracked and she steadied it, the way she steadied everything, with work. "So no. Don't ask me if I understand why you want to burn it all down. I understand better than you think."

"Then help me choose."

"No." Maren turned to face her fully, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "That's the one thing I won't do. I'll carry you to the skiff if you ask me. I'll stand on that dock and watch you board that ship if you ask me. But I won't choose for you, Lior Thornbury. I have watched men choose for other men on this harbor my whole life, and it always ends the same way — with somebody drowning in somebody else's arithmetic."

* * *

They stayed on the wall as the light went. The cold came up through the stone and into their bones, and neither of them moved.

It was Lior who spoke first, when the first star came out over the bridge chains, and what she said surprised her, because she hadn't planned it and planning was the whole architecture of her life.

"When my father drowned, they billed my mother for his mask."

Maren went still.

"Four silver marks for a faceplate that came up empty. I know because I memorized it. I memorize everything — every entry, every interest charge, every year added to my term. It's how I survive. I keep the numbers so they can't lie to me about them." She laughed, a small broken sound. "But I never once wrote down what my father looked like. And now I can't— I can't always—"

"Lior."

"Some mornings I have to count backwards from his funeral to remember his hands."

Maren reached over and took Lior's hand off her knee, and held it, and her palm was rough and hot against the cold.

"Then give them something they can't bill you for," Maren said. "Give them tomorrow. Give them the truth, written down in front of a magistrate, in ink, where nobody's ledger ever reaches. That's the only inheritance those divers' children will ever get that isn't a debt."

"That's not choosing for me?"

"That's telling you what I'd do," Maren said, "if I were brave enough to be you."

The tide groaned beneath the ice, a long sound like something turning over in its sleep. Out in the channel, a lantern moved — the pilot boat, checking the seam of open water one last time before the morning ship. Tomorrow. It was real now, tomorrow. Lior felt it the way she felt the cold: in her teeth.

"Maren," she said. "What are we?"

It hung there between them, small and enormous, the question she'd been carrying since the night Maren took a beating rather than give up a name, since the night Lior had stitched her split eyebrow by lamplight with her hands shaking, since every shared stew and smuggled song and ordinary evening when belonging had crept up on her like warmth into numb fingers — unnoticed until it hurt.

Maren didn't flinch from it. She looked at the frozen harbor, and then at Lior, and she answered the way she did everything: plainly, without decoration, as if stating facts about cargo.

"We're the people who stay," she said. "Whatever you choose tomorrow. If you board that ship, I'll be on the dock when it clears the chain. If you take the skiff, I'll be rowing. If it goes wrong—" her grip tightened, "—I'll be the loud problem Brannick has to deal with while you run. That's what we are. We're each other's people, and we don't need papers to prove it."

Lior turned her hand over and laced her fingers through Maren's, and the cold, and the fear, and the counting all went quiet at once, like water going still over a drop-off.

"I'm going to board it," she said. "Not because I'm brave. Because Fen tore that page out with the last of his breath, and because some child on the diver rolls deserves a mother who isn't handed a bill with her grief, and because if I run, I'll spend whatever freedom they give me listening to the numbers recite themselves forever." She exhaled, long and shaking. "I'm going to hand a magistrate the truth and let him do with it what men do. And if it destroys me—"

"It won't."

"You can't promise that."

"I can promise you won't be alone in it," Maren said. "That's the only promise worth making on a frozen seawall."

They sat until the stars were thick and the lantern of the pilot boat swung back toward its mooring. Then Maren stood, pulled Lior up after her, and held on a moment longer than balance required.

"Wear the knife," she said. "Under your coat. And eat something — you don't testify well hungry, I know you."

Lior almost laughed. "You don't know any such thing."

"I know you." Maren's voice was steady, but her thumb brushed once across Lior's knuckles, deliberate, a signature. "Go sleep. I'll wake you at slack water. And Lior—" she paused, half-turned against the dark, "—whatever happens on that deck, say it loud. Say it so the whole harbor hears it. Say it for the ones who couldn't."

Lior walked back across the frozen yard alone, past the dark barracks, past the watch-fire where a sentry stamped and blew on his hands, and stopped once, at the edge of the light, to press her palm flat against her ribs — against the folded statement, warm as a living thing.

In Maren's bunk, the doll called Pip waited with her one button eye, holding the torn page against her rag heart.

Tomorrow the tide would turn.
