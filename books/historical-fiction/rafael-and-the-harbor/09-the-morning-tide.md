## 9. The Morning Tide

The tide would turn an hour before dawn, and Lior had not slept.

She lay in her narrow bunk in the workers' quarters listening to the harbor breathe — the groan of ice against pilings, the slow suck of water beneath the frozen seawall — and counted what she was carrying the way she had once counted coins: Fen's ledger, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with twine; his torn page, hidden since Maren's beating inside a seam of her own coat that she had unpicked and restitched by candlelight; her sworn statement, written out three times until her hand stopped shaking on the third. Three things. Lighter than a diving knife. Heavier than her father's body had ever been.

At some point Maren stirred across the room and crossed to her without a word, sitting on the edge of the bunk, her shoulder a warm weight against Lior's cold one.

"You could still take the skiff," Maren said. "Old Piet owes me more than he'd ever admit. We'd be past the point before Brannick finished his morning coffee."

"And then what?"

"Then you'd be alive."

Lior turned her face toward the dark window, where the company ship rode at anchor, its lanterns strung along the masts like a constellation someone had dragged down and nailed into place. The *Vale's Mercy*, they called it. Even the name was a debt.

"Fen didn't tear that page so I could swim away from it," she said. "He tore it so someone would carry it *forward*."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a dog barked twice and gave up. When she spoke again her voice was rough as tar. "Then I'm coming down to the dock to watch you board. And if you don't come back off that gangway before they cast off, I want you to know—" She stopped. Started again. "I taught you the songs. That has to count for something. You can't just die and leave me the only one who remembers them."

"I won't die," Lior said, and was surprised to find it wasn't a lie. It was something steadier than hope. "I've been drowning in their arithmetic my whole life, Mare. This is the first time I'll be breathing."

* * *

She went aboard at first light carrying a wooden box under one arm and a story under her tongue.

The box held Clerk Aldous Fen's effects — or enough of them to pass inspection at the gangway: his spare spectacles, a tin of cough lozenges gone soft with age, a prayer book with a cracked spine, his winter gloves still smelling faintly of camphor. The story was simpler: *Clerk Fen died owing the company a final duty, and I am indentured to see it delivered.* No one questioned grief in an indentured girl. Grief made them look away. She had learned that at eleven years old, standing in the rain while men measured her father's drowned body for the equipment it still carried.

The gangway guard was young, chapped-lipped, more interested in warming his hands than in a box of dead man's belongings. He waved her through with a grunt, and Lior stepped from solid wood onto the ship's deck and felt the whole world shift beneath her feet — that living sway, the sea taking hold of everything and gently insisting nothing belonged anywhere forever.

*Good,* she thought. *Let it learn my name.*

Magistrate Oyelaran kept quarters in the stern cabin; she knew this because she had spent two nights memorizing the ship's layout from the manifest diagrams Fen had made her copy, back when copying manifests was the worst betrayal asked of her. She also knew the magistrate took breakfast alone, that his clerk retired early, and that between the second bell and the lines cast off there would be perhaps forty minutes in which a woman with a ledger could stand in the right corridor and be either arrested or heard.

She did not go directly to the cabin. That was the first rule of moving through a place where you were prey: never move like you know where you're going. Instead she carried the box openly, nodding to sailors coiling rope, letting them see only a servant on an errand. Her heart beat so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Somewhere aft, a voice was giving orders in the clipped tones of a man preparing for departure, and she recognized it even at distance — Brannick. He had boarded at dawn. Of course he had. He would ride out with the false books himself, hand-deliver the clean version of Vessamare to whoever paid him, and return in spring to a harbor full of divers who owed him their lungs.

Lior descended toward the hold instead, because the hold connected to the aft corridor by a ladder no officer ever used, and because she wanted — needed — one last moment among the cargo. Crates stenciled with Harrow & Vale marks rose around her in the lamplit dark like headstones: pearl-oil, shell, salted fish, and, stacked nearest the bilge where the damp crept in, bales marked *DIVER'S GEAR — RECOVERED*. Gear recovered from drowned men. Gear billed to widows. She put her palm flat against one bale and let herself feel it — not the horror, that had burned down to coal days ago, but the weight of it. Then she climbed the ladder into the corridor that led to the magistrate's door, and knocked.

"Enter," said a voice like dry paper folding.

* * *

Magistrate Oyelaran was smaller than she had imagined, a compact man with iron-grey hair and reading spectacles pushed up on his forehead, seated at a desk bolted to the deck and covered in the tidy, false accounts of the Harrow & Vale Trading Company. He looked up at Lior with the mild patience of a man accustomed to servants appearing with tea.

"You're not the steward."

"No, sir." She set the box on the edge of his desk. Inside it now, beneath Fen's gloves, lay the true ledger and the torn page and her statement, all three, everything she owned that mattered. Her fingers would not entirely stop trembling. "I'm Lior Thornbury. Indentured diver's daughter. I've come to deliver Clerk Aldous Fen's effects." A breath. "And his confession."

Something moved behind the magistrate's eyes — not surprise exactly; recognition, perhaps, of how many times in a long career a door opens and the truth walks in wearing work clothes. He set down his pen. "Fen. The clerk who died at the compound?"

"He died knowing what was done to us. Before he died, he gave me this." She unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that felt suddenly very far away, as though they belonged to someone braver, and laid the ledger open on his desk beside his own immaculate books. "The weights were falsified. The magistrates before you were paid. Every diver lost this season — every diver lost for thirty years — was billed for his own gear, and the debts passed to the children. My father's debt passed to me. That's why I wear an indenture collar instead of my mother's pearls."

The ship shifted. Somewhere above, boots crossed the deck, voices called numbers, and the first line began to groan as men hauled it in. They were casting off. She had maybe minutes.

Oyelaran read. He read the way Fen had told her real readers read — slowly, going back, touching certain entries with one finger as though checking a pulse. The lamplight caught the sweat on his temple. Lior stood absolutely still and watched a stranger's face decide whether the next thirty years of her life meant anything.

"This page here," he said at last, tapping the torn fragment. "It's been repaired. Stitched into cloth."

"A friend sewed it into my coat to keep it safe when they searched our quarters. She took a beating rather than tell them where it was. So did others whose names you won't find in any book, because people like us don't make books." Her voice held. She marveled at it from somewhere outside herself. "My sworn statement is underneath. Everything Fen dictated, everything I witnessed, signed and dated. I'm asking you to hear testimony before you reach the colonial seat. I'm asking you to let the record show we said it ourselves."

Above them, faintly, a whistle blew. The second line came taut.

And then boots on the stairs — not running, which would have been mercy, but deliberate, unhurried, the tread of a man who had counted heads for a living and knew exactly which head was missing. Brannick filled the doorway with the grey morning light behind him, and for one suspended heartbeat the three of them regarded each other across a desk covered in two versions of the same crime.

"Lior Thornbury." Brannick's voice was almost gentle. Almost. She remembered his offer on the seawall, the freedom papers, the terrible sincerity in his eyes — *order requires sacrifice, girl, someone must be the ballast or everyone drowns* — and she saw that sincerity still there, hardened now into something like sorrow. "Step away from the magistrate's effects. Whatever Fen filled your head with, it can be handled quietly. It always could have."

"No," Lior said. "That's the whole point of me standing here. For once, it doesn't get handled quietly."

Oyelaran removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with unhurried calm, and put them back on. "Overseer," he said mildly, "you will remain exactly where you are. As of this moment, you are named in an inquiry, and I would take it as a personal kindness if you did not give me cause to name you in anything worse." He drew Lior's sworn statement toward him, broke its seal, and began to read aloud — softly, but aloud, each word landing on the cabin air like a struck coin: *"I, Lior Thornbury, daughter of Tomas Thornbury, pearl diver of Vessamare, do swear the following..."*

Brannick did not move. His jaw worked. And Lior understood, watching him, that he had built his whole life on the belief that the drowning was necessary — and that no argument could reach him now except the one being read aloud in a dying clerk's handwriting.

The third line snapped free above them. The *Vale's Mercy* leaned away from the dock, and through the stern windows Lior saw the harbor begin to slide — the frozen seawall, the workers' quarters, and one figure at the end of the pier, scarf whipping in the wind, raising one arm high and holding it there, unyielding, the way Maren held everything.

Maren did not wave goodbye. She raised her fist, and Lior pressed her hand flat to the cold glass in answer, and turned back to the magistrate's desk as her own voice went on reading without her — steady, patient, unstoppable as the tide.

Brannick spoke once more, very quietly, as the shore fell away. "You understand what you've cost them. Everyone you left behind."

"Yes," Lior said. "That's why I stayed aboard."

Oyelaran looked up over his spectacles then, and his eyes were not unkind. "Miss Thornbury," he said, "we will be nine days at sea. I suggest you begin at the beginning."
