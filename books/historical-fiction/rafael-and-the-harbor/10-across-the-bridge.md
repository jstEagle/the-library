## 10. Across the Bridge

Spring came to Vessamare the way forgiveness comes — slowly, then all at once, and never entirely earned.

Lior woke in the loft above Maren's aunt's chandlery to the smell of pitch warming in the sun and gulls arguing over something dead on the pilings. For a moment she lay still and counted, as she had always counted: the coins in her tin — three still, though she'd added nothing; the days since the inquiry began — forty-one; the names of divers she had read aloud into the record — twenty-six, and every one of them lodged in her chest like ballast she would carry the rest of her life.

She had spent years measuring her life in portable things. The knife at her hip. The ledger in her skull. The page sewn inside a child's doll for six terrified weeks of winter. She had thought that when freedom came, it would feel like lightness. It did not. It felt like standing up after a long dive too fast, all that pressure releasing at once, her ears ringing with everything they had held down.

The inquiry had ended three days ago. Magistrate Oyelaran had read the true ledger aloud in the customs hall while Brannick stood at the bar with his hands folded like a man waiting out weather. Lior had watched him through all four days of testimony and never once seen him flinch. When Oyelaran asked if he wished to speak, he said only: *The books were kept as I found them.* Which was a lie, but a tidy one, and perhaps he believed by then that tidiness was the same as innocence.

He was not hanged. Lior had expected to want him hanged, and discovered instead that what she wanted was stranger — she wanted him to stand where she had stood, indentured at fourteen to a debt his arithmetic had manufactured, and count what remained of himself. The magistrate gave him something adjacent: stripped of office, bound over to answer civil claims from every family named in the ledger, his own savings frozen until restitution was reckoned. There was poetry in it that even Brannick must have tasted — the man who weighed other people's lives would now spend his remaining years being weighed.

*The company will not simply accept this,* Maren had warned her that first night, when the verdict came down and the dockworkers' quarter lit itself with smuggled lanterns and someone's fiddle. But Harrow & Vale had done the arithmetic men like Brannick had taught it to do. Twenty-six drowned divers, their debts voided, their families owed — against the cost of fighting a colonial magistrate with a sworn statement and an original ledger in hand. The company settled. Companies always settle, Maren said, because companies have no throats to cut and no hearts to break, only columns to balance.

And so, on a gray-gold morning in the fourth week of spring, Lior Thornbury walked to the Harrow & Vale countinghouse for the last time.

* * *

They made her wait in the entry hall among the smell of ink and cold tea, beneath the great painted map of the harbor that had hung there her whole indenture — the bridge drawn as a single confident stroke, the compound rendered neat and small, as if neatness could hold what actually happened inside those walls. A junior clerk she didn't know brought out the paper and read it in a bored voice: *indenture voided, all obligations discharged, compensation awarded per the magistrate's order.*

She signed the receipt. Her hand did not shake, which surprised her. Ten years of her life, closed with the same motion she used to sign for deliveries of rope.

At the door, the old gatekeeper — Tomas, who had looked the other way for a decade when the divers came in late, who had once slipped her an orange at midwinter and never spoken of it — touched his cap. "Your father," he said, gruffly, staring at the harbor rather than at her, "he'd have counted this right."

"He'd have counted it exactly," Lior said, and walked out.

Outside, the bridge stood open.

It had reopened a month ago, after the winter storms finally surrendered and the engineers pronounced the span sound, but she had not crossed it yet. She had told herself she was busy — depositions, statements, the endless small labors of making truth official. But the truth was simpler: for ten years the bridge had been the line between her life and the rest of the world, and every autumn it closed behind her like a door, and every spring it opened onto a world she had no papers to enter. Now she had papers. Now the far shore was hers to walk toward or refuse, and she found she did not know which she wanted, and that not-knowing frightened her more than Brannick ever had.

Maren was waiting at the near abutment, leaning on the rail the way she leaned on everything — as if the world were furniture she'd helped build and could therefore trust. She wore her good coat, the one with the mended shoulder, and her hair was down, which meant she was off shift, which meant she had chosen this hour the way Lior had chosen hers.

"Well," Maren said. "Free woman."

"Don't say it like a joke."

"I'm not joking." She pushed off the rail and took Lior's arm, casual as breathing, and they started across together. "I've been practicing saying it since dawn. *Free woman Thornbury, free woman Thornbury.* Sounds better every time."

The bridge under their boots was the same planking it had always been, groaning its old complaints, salt-swollen and sun-bleached. But the water beneath had changed with the season — winter's iron gone, replaced by that restless green-gold the harbor wore when the herring came back, when the boats went out again, when everything the tide had taken all winter began, in its stingy way, to give some of it back.

Halfway across, Lior stopped.

"What?" Maren said.

"Nothing. Everything." Lior looked back at the compound — the countinghouse roof, the warehouse gables, the dormer window of the loft where she had slept for ten years and memorized numbers no one asked her to remember. From here it looked small enough to pick up and throw into the sea. "I keep waiting to feel different."

"You don't wake up different. You just stop waking up afraid." Maren shrugged. "Takes a few mornings. Ask anyone who's been freed. Well — ask me. You're the first person I know who's been freed by testifying instead of running."

"I almost ran." The memory rose sharp and cold as the seawall in February: the fishing skiff, the black water, Maren's hands on her shoulders saying *choose*, meaning it, loving her enough to mean it. "I stood on that wall ready to let the ship carry the lies away forever, and you were offering me the only exit in Vessamare, and I chose a piece of paper over my own skin."

"You chose everyone's skin over your own," Maren corrected. "There's a difference, and it's the whole point of you."

Lior laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, ringing off the cables. Below them a fishing boat slipped through the channel, and the men aboard waved up at the two figures on the bridge without knowing them, because these days half the harbor knew her name and none of them needed anything from her except the ordinary courtesy of neighbors. That, too, was new. That, too, would take getting used to.

They reached the far shore and stood at the foot of the ramp, where the city proper began — streets climbing away uphill, market awnings snapping in the breeze, the whole unguarded sprawl of a world with no walls around it.

"So," Maren said. "The magistrate's clerk says the colonial office wants statements from you. Official ones. In the capital, eventually. They'll pay passage."

Lior had heard this already, delivered in Oyelaran's dry, careful voice, along with a warning she had turned over every night since: *Miss Thornbury, what you uncovered here is not confined here. The same ledgers exist in every port Harrow & Vale keeps. Names change. Arithmetic does not.* He had not asked her anything. He had simply set the fact before her like a coin on a table, and left it there.

"They want me to go testify again," she said now. "Somewhere bigger. Where nobody knows my mother's name or how to gut a fish or why the diving bell scares children."

"Do you want to?"

Lior looked down at herself. Knife at her hip — her mother's, the only inheritance that couldn't be entered in a ledger. Three coins in her tin, plus the compensation award, which was more money than any diver's daughter in Vessamare had ever held at once. And in her head, recited nightly like a prayer she refused to retire: twenty-six names, every falsified weight, every inherited debt, the whole architecture of the lie, load-bearing in her memory where no fire or flood or bought magistrate could reach it.

For ten years she had carried only what could be carried. She had believed that meant small things — a knife, a few coins, numbers. Standing at the end of the bridge, she understood at last that the heaviest thing she carried had also been the most portable, and that she had no intention of setting it down.

"I want to sleep for a week," she said. "Then I want to eat everything your aunt cooks. Then—" She turned to Maren, and took her hand properly, in front of God and the harbor and the whole ascending street. "Then I think the arithmetic isn't finished. Somewhere else there's a girl re-copying a manifest she shouldn't be reading, and the bridge is closing behind her, and nobody's coming. Unless somebody goes."

Maren was quiet a moment. Gulls cried over the water; the herring boats knocked gently at their moorings, wanting out, wanting the open sea.

"Capital's cold," she said at last. "Bad stew. Terrible songs."

"I know," Lior said. "I've read the trade reports. I remember numbers."

Maren grinned — that fierce, unguarded grin Lior had first seen across a shared pot of stew in a besieged winter, loyalty chosen freely in a place where nothing else was free. "Well then. Somebody has to teach me to read ledgers. Might as well be the woman who memorized one."

They turned together and walked up into the city, past the market, past the mouths of alleys full of morning noise, carrying between them exactly what could be carried: a knife, some coins, twenty-six names — and, folded flat against everything else, light as paper and heavier than the sea, the beginning of whatever came next.
