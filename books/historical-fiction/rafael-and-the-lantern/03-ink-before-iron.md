## 3. Ink Before Iron

Sefa's help turned out to be a name: Corporal Ivo Marsh, third desk from the window in the requisitions office, the one who'd looked away twice while she smuggled dried fish past the checkpoint. "He counts slow," she said. "Counting slow is a kind of mercy."

I found him at dusk doing exactly that — running his finger down an inventory of confiscated rope with the diligence of a man reading scripture in a language he didn't believe in. He was younger than me by a year or two, which meant he'd been a soldier for most of his adult life and had never once been asked what he thought about any of it.

"Corporal."

"Don't call me that out here." He glanced up, then around, then made the mistake of meeting my eyes. People in Saltmere had spent nine years perfecting the art of not seeing me. It was disorienting to be seen. "You're Vane. Ren's boy."

"I'm nobody's boy." The words came out harder than I'd rehearsed. "I'm the man who can tell you that half the rope on your inventory list was sold off last spring, and the company ledger you're holding has already been rewritten twice."

That got him standing. We walked toward the breakwater, two men inspecting the tide, which is what everyone in Saltmere pretends to do when they're committing treason.

"The army historians are compiling a record," I told him. "Loyal service rendered. Debts honestly held. A town grateful for its masters. In ten days they'll sign it into history, and every diver who drowned in Meridian water will have died owing money to the men who killed them."

"And you want me to—"

"Nothing heroic. There's a notary aboard the *Cormorant Queen*, the Vessian grain ship. Neutral registry. Anything certified aboard her stands no matter what the armistice says afterward. I need the true ledger, three witnesses, and one hour of your slowness."

Marsh stared at the water a long time. "I count slow," he said finally, "because the fast counters get promoted, and the promoted ones sign things." He pulled his cap lower. "The ledger moves tomorrow night. Ren's delivering it personally to the historians' barge. There's a quarter hour between the strongroom and the dock when it travels in a single satchel with a single escort."

"Which will be you."

"Which will be me," he said, as if confirming his own sentence. "God help me. I always wanted to be part of something historical."

* * *

We took the ledger at moonset.

It went almost politely — Marsh walking his route, setting the satchel down behind the salt sheds to adjust his bootlaces, Sefa materializing out of the dark like the answer to a prayer neither Ren nor the army had thought to forbid. She handed the satchel back to him weighted with salt bricks, and he shouldered it without breaking stride, a man carrying nothing heavier than his conscience, which by then weighed considerably more.

The ledger itself was smaller than I remembered. Nine years of copying had inflated it in my mind to something scriptural, but there in my hands it was just a book — swollen with damp, its corners soft as bread, every page dense with Aldous Ren's cramped hand. Divers' names. Advances against wages. Fines for storms, for sickness, for dying too far out to bring the body home. My father's entry was on page forty-one. *Vane, Tomas. Balance outstanding at death: 14 crowns, 6 shillings.* They'd charged him for the rope.

Sefa watched me hold it and said nothing, which was its own kind of testimony.

The *Cormorant Queen* rode at anchor past the mole, riding lights burning, her Vessian colors fat and neutral in the dark. Notaries aboard neutral ships were the one institution the war hadn't figured out how to swallow; whatever they sealed stayed sealed, in every court, under every flag. All we had to do was cross four hundred yards of open harbor water in a skiff, before dawn, before Ren discovered the substitution.

We were two hundred yards out when the harbor woke up.

First a bell at the molehead. Then lanterns blooming along the quay like a rash — a dozen, two dozen — and the flat military voice of a sergeant who had been told exactly what was missing and exactly who'd want it back. Ren had gone to check on his ledger at midnight. Of course he had. A man about to sell the truth keeps checking that it's still in the case.

"They'll sweep the water," Sefa said, rowing. She rowed like she did everything, without hurry and without any possibility of being stopped. "Skiff's slower than a funeral."

Behind us, a launch came off the army pier, oars muffled but not silent, a lantern swinging at its bow. I could hear the sergeant's voice carrying over the water, giving directions to my capture with admirable clarity.

Then Marsh stepped out of the dark onto the molehead, directly into the lantern light, and shouted — actually shouted — *"Here! It's here! He went the other way!"* and pointed grandly toward the river mouth, and the launch wheeled after a lie with the whole weight of the army behind it.

I understood, even rowing, even with my heart slamming, what it cost a man to spend himself that way. I would understand it fully later. At the time I just rowed.

The boarding ladder of the *Cormorant Queen* was slick with spray. A mate hauled us up, incurious in the professional way of sailors paid not to be, and led us aft to a cabin lit by a single lamp, where a notary named Verel — small, gray, precise as a scale-weight — waited with ink already mixed.

"Names," he said.

And there it was, the thing I hadn't planned for. Names meant exposure. Names meant that by morning, Aldous Ren would know not just that his ledger was gone but exactly whose hands had taken it, and Sefa would hang beside me, and Marsh — Marsh was already spending himself on the mole, buying minutes with whatever coin a corporal has.

"Quentin," I started, and stopped. Just Quentin. A diver's son, a debt, a first name like a slave's.

Sefa put her hand flat on the ledger. "Sefa Okonjo," she said clearly. "Widow of Emile Okonjo, diver, entered page sixty-three. Witness."

Verel looked at me over the lamp. "The record requires full names, or it requires none."

Outside, faint across the water, a shot cracked. Then another. Then a longer silence than either.

Sefa closed her eyes for half a breath. When she opened them she said, "Write faster."

He wrote. He examined the ledger page by page, comparing Ren's hand to itself, asking me questions in a dry voice — how many divers lost in the '41 season, what fine was levied for a missed tide — questions only someone who'd lived inside those pages could answer, and my answers matched the book, because I had copied every line of it twice over in a locked room while pretending to be illiterate. Verel certified it at the turn of the hour: true copy, original text, witnessed, sealed, inviolate under neutral law.

Which is when the *Queen*'s mate burst in shouting that the harbor patrol had decided neutral registry applied only to ships, not to what climbed aboard them, and that we had perhaps ninety seconds to leave by any means that wasn't the ladder.

We went over the rail into black water.

I had the ledger wrapped in oilcloth lashed to my chest. Sefa had my collar in her fist, and the cold hit like a wall, and the current off the mole — the same current that had drowned better swimmers than both of us — took hold. Somewhere in the chaos of the next minute, the knot on the oilcloth slipped. I felt the ledger tear loose, felt it begin to sink, that whole drowned town of paper going down into the dark, fourteen crowns six shillings and every other theft of nine years sinking toward the mud —

And Sefa's grip failed.

She'd taken the cold worse than me; I saw her go under once, come up coughing, go under again, her shawl spreading around her like ink. The ledger was below me, a pale shape descending. Her hand broke the surface a yard away, grabbing nothing.

There was no choosing, really. That's the thing nobody tells you. You don't weigh truth against a life on some scale in your chest. Your arms decide. I dove for her.

I got her to the shallows by the fish stakes, dragging, swallowing half the harbor, and collapsed with her coughing seawater beside me while the patrol boats crisscrossed the deeper channel hunting a book. Behind us the tide ran out, and somewhere in it lay the original ledger, going soft, going to pulp, every word dissolving back into the water it had been written against.

Sefa spat, laughed — a terrible, gasping laugh — and pressed something into my hands.

Verel, may every god keep him, had thrust a second bundle at us at the last moment. A fair copy. Certified. Sealed. Made in duplicate, because notaries survive wars by assuming the worst.

"It's a copy," I said stupidly.

"It's *true*," Sefa said. "That's the part they can't drown."

I sat in the wet sand holding the only honest account of Saltmere's dead, listening to the searchboats thrash the dark for a book that was already mud. Ten days until the armistice. One copy. Two names signed to it — hers, and mine, written out in full for the first time in my life: *Quentin Vane.*

Now all that remained was to make the army listen to it — and to learn what had become of the man on the mole who'd shouted us our lives.
