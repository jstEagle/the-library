## 4. Signed in His Own Hand

They told us the armistice would be signed on a Tuesday. It was signed on a Friday, and then it was unsigned, because a colonel with soft hands discovered that the harbor of Saltmere contained eleven ships' worth of disputed salvage claims, and men who have spent four years killing each other will happily spend four more arguing about cargo.

So we waited. The town waited. The divers waited in the shade of the net-sheds, and I waited in the room above the chandlery where Sefa had hidden me after the tide nearly took the ledger and very nearly took me with it. My hands still smelled faintly of salt rot. I'd stopped trying to wash it out.

The clerk — Halloran, his name had been — was buried on the hill behind the garrison chapel with a number instead of a name, because the army buries its clerks the way the Meridian Company buried its divers: cheaply, and without ceremony. Sefa went to the grave once, put down a cup of coffee he'd never asked for, and came back saying nothing. Some debts you can't enter in any book. You just carry them.

Then, on the ninth day of the stall, an aide came to the chandlery with the expression of a man delivering a summons he didn't write himself, and said that the Commission on Disputed Claims wished to examine certain documents. Singular document, as it happened. One ledger, notarized aboard the *Vesperine*, flag of no nation anyone could prove, witnessed by a dying man's oath and a widow's thumbprint.

"Bring it yourself," the aide said. "The square. Noon."

"And if I don't?"

He looked at me the way officers look at things they have decided are furniture. "Then the Commission proceeds on the company's certified copy. Master Ren's copy."

I brought it myself.

* * *

They set up a table in the harbor square under the awning of the fish market, which meant the whole proceeding smelled of brine and old blood, which I considered fitting. The Commission sat three: a colonel, a civilian assessor with spectacles thick as portholes, and a naval captain who kept looking at the water like it owed him money. Behind them stood Aldous Ren, cleaned up, collar pressed, wearing the face of a man who has already counted his reward.

I knew that face. I'd grown up under it. He used to wear it at the wage table every quarter-day while he explained to grown men why their shares had shrunk again, and they nodded, because he fed their children, and what else was there? That had been the whole architecture of Saltmere: Ren's face, and everyone nodding beneath it.

The assessor read. That was the thing I hadn't anticipated — not argument, not lawyers, just reading. A voice in the fish market reading out names.

*"Tomas Vane, diver. Entered service aged nineteen. Deceased, year nine of contract. Cause recorded by company: misadventure. Wages owed at death: none recorded. Advance against wages: recorded as sixty crowns, carried to next of kin."*

My father's name, spoken aloud in daylight, in front of soldiers. I had copied those lines by candlelight when I was fourteen years old, my finger under each word, learning letters the way my father had learned the reef — by touch, in the dark, alone. Hearing them read back felt like surfacing. Like the first breath after too long below.

The assessor read on. *"Misadventure"* — and then Sefa stood up in the crowd, uninvited, and said, "The net was rotted. Company record says repaired. It wasn't repaired. I mended nets for that company twenty years; ask anyone." And a dozen voices said yes, yes, we'll say so, and the colonel's quill began moving faster than his dignity liked.

Ren stepped forward. "Gentlemen, this document is a forgery. A diver's boy copying pages he cannot possibly understand—"

"Master Ren," the captain said mildly, "your own certified copy lists Tomas Vane's advance as forty crowns. This one says sixty. Which lie did the company prefer?"

The silence in the square had a texture. I've heard the silence before a dive, before the drop into green dark. It was that silence.

They arrested him there, between the fish stalls, by the officers whose pardon he'd been courting since the requisition began — two lieutenants whose regiments he'd provisioned at a discount, whose dinners he'd hosted, whose boots he'd shined with favors. They were polite about it. That was the worst part. They treated him exactly as gently as he'd taught the company to treat us, and he seemed to understand this, going pale as they took his arm, seeing his whole arithmetic collapse: that usefulness is not loyalty, that a man who buys power buys it from someone willing to sell him out the moment the price changes.

He looked for me in the crowd. I let him find me. I gave him nothing — no hatred, even. Just the ledger in my arms, and the fact of it.

* * *

Afterward, the captain found me sitting on the quay steps, wrung out like line after a storm.

"The Commission can authorize compensation," he said. "For the document. For your trouble. There's a schedule—"

"No."

He blinked. Officers are not built for *no*. "No?"

"I want it archived. The original. Under the divers' names — Vane, Okello, Sefa's husband Marek, all of them. Not 'Meridian Company Records.' Their names. First, on the spine, on the catalog page, everywhere ink goes. Let some historian in a hundred years open the file and learn whose book it was before he learns who owned them."

The captain wrote it down. Bureaucracy, weaponized properly, is a beautiful thing.

"And you," he said. "Your father's entry. The debt of sixty crowns carried to next of kin. Legally, annulled debts leave residue. Claims. You could—"

"I know what I could do." I looked at the water. Somewhere under that gray-green floor was the reef where my father died working off a debt that grew the more he paid it. "Strike it through. All of it. Every balance, every advance, every carried figure. Strike them all through and write *paid* beside them. Paid in full. Let the book end clean."

He studied me for a moment, and something almost human crossed the officer's face. "That's a strange kind of reward, son."

"It's the only one that weighs anything."

They brought the ledger out one last time for the formal order, and the assessor drew his pen down the columns, strike, strike, strike, and I watched nine years of arithmetic die in ink. Then he paused at the final page, where the notarization ended, and looked at me.

"There's space," he said. "For a signature. If anyone should care to claim the deposition."

My hand shook. Not from fear, for once. From the strangeness of it — that a page could hold my name, that a name could hold a man. I had copied thousands of entries in other people's hands, invisible, useful, unremarkable. I took the pen.

*Quentin Vane.*

Full name. My own hand. Beneath my father's, in the book that buried him and, now, had dug him up again.

Sefa squeezed my shoulder from behind, hard enough to hurt, which is how she said everything that mattered.

The captain cleared his throat. "One more matter. The Commission requires a custodian for the archive. Someone literate, familiar with maritime accounts, of proven... discretion." He almost smiled. "It pays a salary."

I looked out at the harbor, at the *Vesperine* riding at anchor, at the divers walking home through the square with their backs straighter than I'd ever seen them.

"Well," I said, taking the pen up again. "Someone ought to keep the books honest."
