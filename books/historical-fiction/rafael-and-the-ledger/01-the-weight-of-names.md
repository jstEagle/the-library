## 1. The Weight of Names

The ledger opened the day the way it always did: with a sigh of paper and the smell of salt, tallow, and old ink. Gideon Kavanagh set it on the counter of the Meridian Trading Company's counting house, weighted its corners with four brass objects he had collected over nineteen years — a lamp base, a paperweight shaped like a fish that no longer resembled any fish, a cannonball the size of an apple that Vane claimed was decorative but which Gideon suspected was ballast for something, and his father's pearl knife, which was not brass at all but bone-handled steel worn smooth as sea glass.

He had measured his life in portable things since he was six years old. That was the year the tide took the family boat with everything in it except the knife, which his mother had made him wear on a cord around his neck because he kept losing his shoes. A boy learns early, in Saltmere, that everything can be carried away. The trick — the whole trick of living, as far as Gideon could tell — was deciding what you would carry before the water decided for you.

So: four brass weights, one knife, one ledger, one life.

He dipped his pen and began.

* * *

By mid-morning the counting house smelled of coffee Vane would not share and tar from the harbor, and the day's business arrived in the usual order of small sorrows. A chandler came to argue about rope. Two divers from the eastern beds came to draw advances against next month's catch, signing their names with the careful, self-conscious loops of men who wrote their own names perhaps four times a year and wanted each time to be worth looking at. Tomas Pell, junior clerk and the closest thing Gideon had to a friend, sorted requisition receipts behind him, humming something off-key and cheerful about a girl from upriver.

"You're doing the death entries again," Tomas observed, leaning over the counter. "You always take the death entries."

"Somebody has to."

"Vane would do them in half the time."

"Vane does everything in half the time." Gideon kept his eyes on the page. "That's why the columns don't add up."

Tomas laughed and went back to his receipts. He was twenty-two, round-faced, perpetually slightly damp, as though he'd been pulled out of the harbor recently and never quite finished drying. His debts were a matter of some delicacy — three years' indenture bought down to two by steady work and a weakness for cards that Gideon, keeper of all debts, knew about and never mentioned. Friendship, in the ledger district, meant knowing someone's column and keeping quiet about it.

The morning's entry waited under Gideon's hand. *Diver, Jorunn Bekk, aged 34, eastern beds.* Dead two days now, drowned when his air hose fouled on a coral spur forty feet down while his tender hauled and screamed on the surface. The company's form required: name, age, bed assignment, cause, outstanding balance, disposition of account.

Outstanding balance: eleven crowns, four shillings.

Gideon sat with that for a moment. Eleven crowns, four shillings. Jorunn Bekk had spent fourteen years diving pearls out of cold green water, had raised three children on what remained after the company took its share, had once — Gideon remembered this — carried young Gideon on his shoulders through the Fish Gate market so the boy could see over the crowd to the jugglers. And the sum total of it, the arithmetic residue of a man, was eleven crowns, four shillings, which the company would now recover against whatever poor gear of his could be auctioned, and against whatever labor his widow could be persuaded to sign.

Gideon wrote the line. His handwriting, he noticed, was very neat. That was the thing about the ledger — it demanded neatness even when your hands wanted to shake. *J. Bekk, diver, deceased 14th inst., hose failure, eastern beds. Balance 11/4/0. Account closed.*

*Account closed.* As if an account were a door, and death merely someone leaving the room and drawing it shut behind him.

His father had an account too. Gideon didn't look at it — it lived twelve pages back, in the older ledger, the fat one Vane kept locked — but he didn't need to look. He knew the line the way other men knew prayers. *Kavanagh, Edran, diver, deceased, pearl beds, cause: sea.* Cause: sea. Fourteen words of accounting for a man who had taught him the names of every current between Saltmere and the far shoals, who could hold his breath long enough to make a child worry, who sang badly and laughed easily and came home every evening smelling of brine and candle wax until the evening he didn't come home at all.

Cause: sea. As though the sea were a person who could be billed.

Gideon had been nine. He remembered the waiting more than anything else — three days of it, the whole town watching the water, his mother going down to the harbor at dawn each morning with her arms folded tight across her chest as if holding herself together by main force. And he remembered, with a shame that had never entirely faded, that on the third day he had gone into the counting house because it was warm, and stood near the stove, and watched the clerks write things in books. Numbers going on living when people stopped. It had seemed, to a nine-year-old, like a kind of magic. It seemed, to a twenty-five-year-old clerk, like something else entirely.

* * *

At noon Aldous Vane emerged from his office, buttoned his coat against a wind that wasn't there, and surveyed the counting floor like a man inspecting troops he intended to sell.

"Kavanagh. The quarterly reconciliation. I want the master figures ready for the auditor."

"When is the auditor coming?"

"Soon." Vane's eyes did a small private dance, the way they did whenever the word *soon* involved someone arriving with authority. "There are changes coming to Saltmere, Kavanagh. Big ones. Men who count will matter." He said this last part warmly, almost paternally, which from Vane was the most alarming register available. "Keep your books clean."

Vane was fifty, grey at the temples, with the polished, well-fed look of a man who had discovered early that he was not brave or brilliant and had compensated with punctuality. Gideon had worked under him seven years and had catalogued exactly two genuine emotions from him: greed, and a nervousness that appeared whenever ships anchored at the harbor mouth without filing manifests. Both were visible now, layered, like weather.

"Clean as church linen," Gideon said.

"Mm." Vane paused at the counter, looked down at the open ledger, at the day's entries. His finger hovered over the new line without touching it. "Bekk. Eastern beds. Shame." And then, moving along: "Pell! Those receipts won't sort themselves."

But Gideon noticed that Vane had read only the name, not the balance. In seven years he had never once seen Vane read a balance on a death entry. Names interested him; sums he delegated. A man who thought of people as currency didn't trouble himself with their exchange rates.

* * *

The afternoon went long and gold. Light came through the harbor windows at a slant and turned the dust in the counting house into something almost beautiful, which Gideon felt was the counting house's single redeeming feature. Tomas hummed. The chandler's dispute was resolved with rope. A woman brought a basket of dried fish for Vane's approval and left with a contract instead, having come in for fish, which was how the Meridian Trading Company preferred its customers.

At four o'clock Maren Oduya came in.

She was a diver's widow already — her husband lost three winters ago — and she worked the shallows herself now, gathering what the deep beds wouldn't give her. She set a small cloth bag on the counter: nine pearls, none of them large, the smallest still crusted with shell.

"For the Bekk children," she said. "Sell them and put it against his balance. I'll not have the company taking their father's tools over eleven crowns."

Gideon looked at the pearls, then at her. She was maybe forty, wind-burnt, with the flat practical stare of someone who had stopped expecting fairness and settled for accuracy. "Maren, you can't—"

"I can and I have." She pushed the bag an inch toward him. "Write it in your book, Kavanagh. Make it official. Credit to the estate of Jorunn Bekk, from Maren Oduya, who remembers being widowed and doesn't wish it on children."

He wrote it. His hand was less neat than it had been at noon. When she left, she paused at the door and looked back at him with an expression he couldn't read — not pity, nothing so soft — something more like assessment, as though she were weighing him against some standard he hadn't been told about.

"You write good lines," she said. "Mind someday what they're for."

Then she was gone into the gold light, and Gideon sat with the strange ringing feeling her words left, like a bell struck somewhere below hearing.

* * *

Evening. He closed the ledger, squared the brass weights, and put the pearl knife back in his pocket where it had ridden since he was six years old. Tomas was pulling on his coat, talking about cards tonight, a game at the Sailmaker's Rest, did Gideon want — no? Suit yourself, misery, enjoy your numbers.

Alone, Gideon did what he did every night: he inventoried.

Four brass weights. One knife. One copybook of his own, hidden under a floorboard, filled with practice script and, lately, with something he hadn't let himself think about directly — notes. Dates. Ship arrivals without manifests. Wage figures that didn't match the wage pages Vane kept locked. Small wrongnesses, gathered the way a beachcomber gathers sea glass, without yet knowing what he was building.

And names. He carried names too, though they weighed nothing and everything. His father's. Jorunn Bekk's. Forty-one divers dead in the beds in the last ten years, and he knew every one, because he had written every one, and because a boy whose father drowned in the pearl beds does not forget what the ledger says about drowning.

He blew out the lamp. Through the window, the harbor lay dark and ordinary, masts against the stars.

Three days later, the gunboats came.
