## 2. Gunboats at the Harbor Mouth

The gunboats came on a Tuesday, which Gideon Kavanagh thought was typical of the Empire. Nothing so dramatic as a Sunday, nothing so honest as a market day. A Tuesday — the day when Saltmere's fishermen were out past the shoals and couldn't see their own harbor swallowed whole.

He was balancing the third column of the quarterly wage accounts when he heard the first whistle from the point. It was not the deep, familiar bellow of the Meridian's own steamer, *Charity*, whose whistle everyone in town could identify blindfolded, half-deaf, or asleep. This was sharper, doubled, with an arrogance to it that carried across three miles of gray water.

Gideon put down his pen and did what he always did when the world threatened to become unmanageable: he measured it. The ledger before him weighed perhaps four pounds. His inkwell, brass, two pounds when full. The pearl knife in his desk drawer — his father's, its handle worn smooth as sea glass — barely four ounces, and worth more than everything else combined. These were the portable things of his life. He had learned the habit as a boy in the divers' quarter, where every family kept a bundle packed against the day the company might turn them out. You learned what you could carry. You learned it young, or you learned it weeping.

The whistle sounded again, closer now.

"Mr. Kavanagh!" Aldous Vane appeared in the doorway of the counting room with his coat already buttoned, which was itself an omen. Vane buttoned his coat for funerals, audits, and the Bishop. "Ships in the harbor mouth. Three of them. Gray."

"I can hear them, sir."

"Yes, well." Vane's long fingers drummed once against the doorframe, a habit Gideon had catalogued years ago alongside forty others: the manager drummed when calculating, hummed when lying, and straightened his cuffs when frightened. At present both hands were occupied with the drumming, which meant the fear hadn't arrived yet. "See that the clerks keep to their desks. If it is what I suspect, there will be forms. There are always forms."

Vane departed toward the harbor road at a pace that suggested the forms were, in fact, already late.

* * *

By noon the town square held more soldiers than Saltmere had residents under thirty. They were efficient about it, Gideon would grant them that. No shouting, no broken windows — just a lieutenant with a clipboard reading a requisition order from the steps of the customs house while gulls screamed overhead as though they, too, objected to the annexation.

Saltmere, port and district, was hereby placed under military administration for the duration of the coastal campaign. All stores, wharves, vessels, and warehouses were subject to seizure for imperial use. All commercial records were to be surrendered for audit.

That last phrase landed strangely. Gideon heard it twice — the lieutenant read the order aloud, then handed a printed copy to Vane, who read it a third time to himself with his lips moving slightly, like a man tasting each word.

*All commercial records.*

Old Petros, who mended nets by the customs house and had opinions on everything from tides to theology, leaned close to Gideon as the crowd dispersed. "Records," he said. "They want the paper, boy. Not the fish, not the boats. Well — the boats too. But the paper first."

"They're at war up the coast," Gideon said. "They need supplies. Ledgers don't shoot."

Petros looked at him with the mild pity the old reserved for the young. "No. But ledgers remember. And armies have long memories and short consciences." He gathered his net-mending and shuffled off, leaving Gideon standing in the square with the phrase turning over in him like a stone in a shallows.

* * *

The trading house spent the afternoon preparing for surrender, which was a sentence Gideon never expected to think, let alone think with such precision. Vane directed operations with something perilously close to cheer. Crates were fetched. Files were sorted — wages here, shipping manifests there, indenture rolls bound separately with fresh twine. Vane himself tied the twine, testing each knot, and Gideon noticed that his cuffs stayed perfectly straight all afternoon.

"You seem in good spirits, Mr. Vane," said Mrs. Halloway, the housekeeper, who feared neither God nor management.

"Civic duty, Mrs. Halloway. The Empire requires our cooperation, and cooperation freely given reflects well upon us all." He smiled down at a crate of shipping invoices as fondly as if they were grandchildren.

It was the eagerness that troubled Gideon. He had served Vane nine years, since the manager had lifted him off the shingle at fourteen and made him a clerk instead of a diver — the only kindness, or the most practical one, anyone had ever done him, depending on the day. In nine years Gideon had watched Vane haggle over a widow's pearl price until she wept, dock a dead man's final wage for a torn diving suit, and charge the mission school rent on its own chapel. Aldous Vane did not cooperate freely with anything. Every generosity of his had a hook in it somewhere, and every compliance had a profit.

So when Vane announced, near dusk, that he would "make a final survey of the record room" before the soldiers' inventory began in the morning, Gideon found a reason to be in the corridor outside it. He told himself it was diligence. He was not entirely sure he believed himself, but he had learned to live with a certain amount of disagreement between his actions and his explanations.

The record room smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the brine that lived in everything in Saltmere, in the walls and the books and the people. Gideon stood in the dark of the corridor with a stack of blank invoice forms as his excuse and listened to Vane moving among the shelves.

There was a long silence. Then the sound of a drawer sliding — not one of the archive drawers along the wall, Gideon knew their every squeal, but the shallow drawer in the writing desk. A pause. A soft sound like cloth over leather. Another silence, longer.

When Gideon risked a glance through the gap of the door, Vane was standing by the cold hearth with his coat unbuttoned after all, and inside the coat, against his waistcoat, was a slim dark volume such as no clerk in Saltmere was permitted to touch. The master ledger. The book of books — the one kept in Vane's own hand, or under his eye, that reconciled all the others. Gideon had seen it exactly four times in nine years, always carried between office and strongroom under escort, as though it were a relic or a hostage.

Vane buttoned his coat. He straightened his cuffs.

Then he turned, and Gideon stepped back into shadow with his heart going like a diver's hammer, and by the time Vane passed him in the corridor, Gideon was studying a blank invoice form with the absorption of a man contemplating eternity.

"Working late, Kavanagh?"

"Inventory lists, sir. For the soldiers. Thought I'd save them the trouble."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — approval, perhaps, or amusement, or the particular satisfaction of a man who has hidden a thing successfully and enjoys knowing that hiding is possible. "Thorough as ever. Go home. Tomorrow will be a long day of other people counting our sins."

* * *

Gideon walked home through streets full of soldiers' cook-fires and tried to make sense of what he carried, which was nothing, and what he knew, which was beginning to feel heavier than anything portable.

An army does not cross open water for ledgers. An army takes grain, coal, boots, boats. Paper is the last spoil of any war and the first concern of every government, because paper tells you where the bodies are buried and who signed the shovel receipts. Petros was right. Ledgers remember.

And Vane — Vane who surrendered the rest with a smile and a bow and freshly tied twine — Vane had kept one book back. Which meant the master ledger contained something worth a hanging, or worth a fortune, or both, and Vane meant to decide later which it was.

At his lodgings above the chandlery, Gideon performed the old ritual without thinking: knife, inkwell, spare shirt, the photograph of his father that wasn't a photograph because no one had owned such a thing, but instead a small card on which Gideon had copied out, in his best clerk's hand, the only line his father ever spoke that he could bear to repeat: *A name is the one debt you owe yourself.* He laid the items on the bed and looked at them the way his mother used to look at the sea before sending her husband out into it.

His father's name was written in the Meridian rolls. Gideon entered it often enough — pension disbursements, halted; widow's claim, settled 1889 for eleven pounds. He had written his father into columns a hundred times. Drowned in the pearl beds off Corren Shoal when Gideon was six, one of nine men lost that season, each of them reduced by degrees to figures in a book Gideon now kept.

He thought suddenly, absurdly, of the master ledger pressed against Vane's waistcoat. What names did it hold that were worth more than eleven pounds?

Below his window, a soldier laughed at something, and the sound was ordinary and human and did not help at all.

Gideon slept badly and woke before dawn to the sound of crates being loaded at the trading house — earlier than any inventory should begin. He dressed in the dark, took up his father's knife on an impulse he didn't examine, and went down into the blue-gray morning to find the soldiers already at the doors and Vane already waiting, coat buttoned, face composed, wearing the expression of a man about to hand over everything except the one thing that mattered.

"Ah, Kavanagh," Vane said pleasantly. "You'll assist Lieutenant Dorset with the count. Start with the indenture rolls. And Kavanagh—" the manager paused, and for one strange moment Gideon thought he saw fear there, plain and naked, before it was folded away again behind the civility, "—be exact. In my experience, it is always the exact pages that get a man into trouble."

The soldiers began carrying out the crates. And Gideon, taking up his pen, wondered what precisely Vane was afraid the army would find — and why a man who had hidden the worst of it still looked like a drowning man watching the water rise.
