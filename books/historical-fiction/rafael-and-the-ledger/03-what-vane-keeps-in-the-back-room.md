## 3. What Vane Keeps in the Back Room

The auditor's summons came on a Tuesday, which Gideon considered a small mercy. Mondays were inventory days, when the trading house smelled of tar and panic in equal measure, and no man could be spared for errands. Tuesdays were quieter. Tuesdays, a clerk might be sent walking.

"Kavanagh." Vane's voice carried from the manager's office the way a fishhook carries — small, barbed, and meant for exactly one throat. "In here."

Gideon set down his pen, blotted the column of herring weights he'd been reconciling, and went. Aldous Vane sat behind a desk so large it might have been commissioned to compensate for something, though Gideon had never been unkind enough to speculate aloud what. Vane was a narrow man made broad by tailoring, with a mustache he groomed the way other men groomed their reputations — constantly, and with visible anxiety.

"You're to go to Captain Weiss at the harbor office," Vane said. "The military auditor. He requires the district master ledger for inspection."

Gideon kept his face agreeably blank, which was a skill he'd been perfecting since the age of nine, when he'd learned that a diver's son who looked surprised was a diver's son who got corrected. "The master ledger, sir. The full one?"

"Of course the full one. What would I send him, a sampler?" Vane's mustache twitched. "It's in the back room. Second shelf, the green-bound volume with the brass clasps. Fetch it and take it directly to Weiss. Directly, Kavanagh. No detours. No reading along the way."

"Sir, I can't read and walk at the same time. I've tried. I fell in the harbor."

"That was a different incident entirely and we agreed never to discuss it." Vane flapped a hand toward the corridor. "Go."

The back room of the Meridian Trading Company was the kind of room that existed in every trading house on the coast and was spoken of by no one, in the way that cellars and marriages were not spoken of. It was long and dim and smelled of camphor, dust, and the particular sweetness of paper that had been kept past its natural life. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, crammed with ledgers, contract boxes, and bundles of correspondence tied with string that had outlasted most of the friendships it had once recorded.

Gideon found the green volume on the second shelf, exactly where Vane had said. It was heavier than he remembered. He hefted it, turned to leave — and stopped.

Because the shelf was wrong.

Gideon had spent eleven years in this building. He knew its shelves the way a fisherman knew his nets: by their weight, their gaps, their small dishonesties. The second shelf had always held the master ledger flanked by the harbor dues registers. Now there was a gap on the left where the dues registers should have been, and in the gap, wedged flat and half-hidden behind a stack of customs declarations, lay a slim folio he did not recognize. It was bound in plain grey, unmarked, and it had the particular density of a book that had been moved recently and often.

He should have taken the ledger and gone. Captain Weiss was waiting. Vane was waiting, in the sense that a spider waits. Every sensible instinct Gideon owned — and he owned a respectable collection, having inherited them from a father who had died of not owning enough — said: take the book, walk, live.

Instead he set the master ledger down on the worktable and drew out the grey folio.

His hands were steady, which surprised him. He'd expected them to shake. Perhaps they would later; hands, like debts, always came due eventually.

He opened it.

The first pages were ordinary enough — requisition schedules, labor allocations, the dry arithmetic of empire. Then he turned a page and the ordinary ended.

It was a contract. Not one contract — a series of them, each a single dense paragraph in the company's own house style, the style Gideon himself had been trained to write. *The Meridian Trading Company hereby transfers to the Imperial Army Labor Directorate the services of the following indentured persons for a period of no less than three years, consideration paid per head as itemized.* Below the paragraph, a list. Names. Ages. Trades. And beside each name, a figure — a sum of money, written in the neat columns Gideon knew better than his own handwriting.

Men he knew. Men he'd recorded wages for, deducted debts from, entered illnesses against. Old Piet who mended nets and cheated at dominoes. The Ferro brothers, who between them owned one shirt and an argument. Divers, porters, a boy of fifteen whose indenture Gideon himself had copied into the register two winters ago.

*Transferred. Per head.* They had been sold. Not lent, not conscripted by the army directly — *sold*, at so much apiece, by the company, with the company's seal and the company's margins.

Gideon turned the pages with a care that felt like blasphemy. There was a schedule of payments received. There was a note in Vane's handwriting — he'd have known it anywhere, that fussy, upward-slanting script — recording a bonus paid to *A. Vane, Esq., for facilitation of the labor transfer, per the Director's gratitude.*

And then he reached the page with the pearl divers.

He knew before he read it. Some part of him, the part that had measured his whole life in portable things, the part that had learned as a child that everything could be taken and weighed, knew what he would find. He read it anyway.

There, in the middle of the list, between men he'd shared a wharf with and men he'd shared a funeral with: **Kavanagh, D. — diver, age 41.** Beside it, in the margin, in the auditor's ink, the notation: *deceased — asset closed.*

Asset closed.

His father. The man who had taught him to hold his breath until the world went silver, who had once spent an entire week's wages on a tin whistle because his son had looked at it in a shop window with what he'd called *your mother's eyes*. Who had drowned in the company's pearl beds in a season when the company had sent the divers down in weather no sensible man would dive in, because the price of shell was high and the price of divers, the ledger had always made plain, was not.

Gideon had spent eleven years believing it was the sea that killed his father. The sea, and weather, and the ordinary arithmetic of chance. It had never once occurred to him that the arithmetic might have been written down in advance. That somewhere in this building — this building he had walked through every day of his adult life — there was a page where his father's death had already been priced in.

He stood very still in the dim room. Somewhere overhead, a soldier's boot creaked across the floorboards, and Gideon thought: *I am standing in a room full of names, and every one of them was money to somebody.*

He did not cry. He would have said, if anyone had asked, that he was past it — eleven years past it. But his eyes burned, and his hands, steady until now, had finally come due. He gripped the edge of the worktable and waited for the wave to pass, the way his father had taught him to wait out a swell: don't fight it, don't breathe into it, let it lift you and set you down.

When it had set him down, he did something he could not afterward fully account for.

He could not take the folio. Its absence would be noticed within the hour — Vane checked that shelf the way a miser checked his strongbox, Gideon understood that now, understood what the grey book's recent and frequent handling meant. He could not copy the pages; there was no time, and the sound of a pen scratching in a silent building carries like a shout.

But a clerk's satchel holds many things, and among them that morning was a stick of copying wax, a sheet of thin rice paper, and a soft graphite pencil — the ordinary tools of a man whose job was making records of records.

Gideon laid the rice paper over the contract page. He took the pencil on its side, the way one shades a rubbing of a grave marker — and the comparison arrived uninvited and stayed — and he drew it across in slow, even strokes. The paper took the impression of the ink beneath: the company's seal, the paragraph of transfer, the columns of names and sums. He did it again for the schedule of payments, and again for the pearl divers' page, and a third time for that page, pressing harder, until *Kavanagh, D.* came through the paper dark and unmistakable, with *asset closed* beneath it like a headstone.

He memorized nothing. That was the strange part. He had always had a good memory — he could recite the harbor dues schedule for 1893 — but standing over those pages, his mind refused to hold them, as if it understood that to carry them in the head was to be hanged by them, and the paper would at least have the decency to be found on his body rather than extracted from it. So he memorized nothing and rubbed everything, working fast and quiet, a clerk performing the only honest act of his career with the tools of his dishonest trade.

When it was done he folded the rubbings small — small enough to fit in a boot, in a hatband, in the lining of a coat; he had spent his whole life measuring things by what could be carried, and these pages, he now understood, were the heaviest portable thing he would ever hold — and slid them into the false bottom of his satchel, a hiding place he'd built at fifteen for smuggled sweets and had kept, on principle, ever after.

Then he replaced the grey folio exactly as he'd found it, at the same angle, with the customs declarations resting against it the same way, because if he was going to be hanged for theft he did not wish to be hanged for *slovenly* theft. He picked up the master ledger. He checked the room. He left.

* * *

Captain Weiss turned out to be a broad, pink, unhurried man who received the master ledger the way a man receives a delivery of gravel — with a grunt and a stamp. He flipped through it at random, ran a finger down a column, and pronounced it, "In order, I expect," which Gideon felt was a generous assessment from a man who had read perhaps four lines.

"You'll want a receipt," Weiss said, already looking past him out the window at the gunboats. "Sign here. Your name?"

"Kavanagh, sir. Gideon Kavanagh. Clerk to the company."

Weiss wrote it down, and Gideon watched his father's name — his own name, the last true thing his father had given him — enter the army's records as a signature on a receipt for a book. There was a symmetry to it that he suspected he'd appreciate more later, when his hands had stopped wanting to shake.

"Company treats its people well, does it?" Weiss asked, in the tone of a man making conversation with furniture.

"Very well, sir," said Gideon Kavanagh, clerk, indentured, son of a closed asset. "We're all very well provided for."

He walked back through the occupied streets with the satchel against his ribs, feeling the folded papers there like a second heartbeat. The town looked the same as it had yesterday — the same gulls, the same fish smell, the same women mending the same nets — and entirely different, the way a room looks different once you know which floorboard is loose.

Vane was waiting in the corridor when he returned, mustache at full anxious attention. "Well? Did Weiss say anything?"

"He said it was in order, sir."

"Good. Good." Vane took the ledger, hugged it briefly to his chest — an odd, involuntary gesture, the way a man holds something he is afraid of — and then paused. His eyes, quick and grey, moved over Gideon's face. "You were a while."

"The auditor writes slowly, sir. He had to copy your name."

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes — suspicion, or indigestion; with Vane the two were chronically difficult to distinguish. "Mind you keep the back room tidy, Kavanagh. There are things in this building that are no concern of clerks."

"Yes, sir," said Gideon, who had spent the last hour with his hands pressed flat against every one of them.

That night, in his narrow room above the cooper's yard, he took out the rubbings and read them again by the light of a candle stub, and found that the horror had done something to itself in the hours since the back room. It had cooled. It had set, like wax. And beneath it, where the grief had been, something harder had taken its place — not rage, exactly. Rage was a fire, and fires could be put out. This was more like a debt. A debt with his father's name on it, marked closed in someone else's ink, and Gideon Kavanagh had spent his whole life learning what happened to debts that were marked closed while the money was still owed.

They got collected.

He folded the papers away, blew out the candle, and began, in the dark, to plan how a man might copy a book that was never supposed to leave a locked room — and how he might get the copy past soldiers who were, even now, learning to search a town.
