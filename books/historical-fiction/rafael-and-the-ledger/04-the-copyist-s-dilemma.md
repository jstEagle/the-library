## 4. The Copyist's Dilemma

The trouble with copying a forbidden book, Gideon discovered on the first night, was that forbidden books are heavy.

Not in the hand — the master ledger weighed no more than a hymnal — but in the arm. He had taken to transcribing by candlelight in the counting room's back corner, where the shadow of the great iron safe fell across his desk like a curtain, and after an hour of holding the ledger open at an angle that kept its pages hidden from the doorway, his shoulder complained in a voice he could hear over his own conscience.

*You will ruin yourself,* the shoulder said. *For paper.*

"For truth," Gideon murmured, and dipped his pen.

"For cramp," said the shoulder.

He had worked out the arithmetic of it the way he worked out all arithmetic: patiently, and with an eye to what could be carried. Eleven pages mattered. Eleven pages of contracts, each one a sale — not of cotton or copra or salt fish, but of men. *Fifty laborers, indentured, transferred to Imperial Works Directorate, Northern Mines. Consideration rendered. Deceased assets closed per attached schedule.* And there, third from the top of the first schedule, in Vane's own cramped hand: *Kavanagh, Edmun. Diver. Deceased — asset closed.*

His father had been worth, according to the company, four pounds eleven shillings. Gideon had done the sum three times hoping he'd been wrong. He was never wrong. That was the whole tragedy of being good at figures: they always came out true.

So. Eleven pages. At perhaps forty minutes a page, working small and clean and by a candle stub he'd begun rationing like wartime bread — seven hours a night, give or take the nights his hands shook too badly to make a fair copy. A shaking hand made a suspicious document. Nobody questioned a clerk's tidy script; everybody questioned a clerk's trembling one.

He copied onto loose folios cut down to receipt-size, because nobody searched receipts. Soldiers had learned to fear ledgers and love paperwork; a man carrying a bundle of requisition forms through an occupied town drew about as much attention as a man carrying rain. It was, Gideon thought, the single useful thing the army had ever taught him: that the empire ran on documents, and therefore documents were where you hid.

The candle guttered. Outside, Saltmere lay under curfew so complete that even the gulls seemed to have signed something promising silence. Somewhere along the harbor road a boot rang on stone, paused, moved on. Gideon turned the page — *Kestrel, Ovid. Dredger. Deceased — asset closed* — and kept writing.

He was six pages into eleven when the door handle turned.

Gideon did what any honest man does with a guilty conscience: he went perfectly still and thought about fish. The candle he had already screened behind the safe's shadow; the folios sat under the day-book, which sat under his elbow, which sat under an expression of profound administrative boredom. The door opened on Aldous Vane, manager of the Meridian Trading Company, wearing a dressing gown that had once belonged to a richer man.

"Kavanagh." Vane's voice was soft, which was worse than loud. "Burning midnight tallow?"

"Balancing," Gideon said. He tilted the day-book toward the light as if offering proof. "The requisitions don't reconcile. The army took forty barrels of salted herring and paid for thirty-eight. I can't close the month on a two-herring discrepancy."

A pause. In the pause, Gideon watched Vane do the thing Vane did best, which was decide whether a subordinate was a tool or a threat. It was like watching a moneylender weigh a coin between finger and thumb — no scales needed, just a lifetime of practice at telling false weight.

"Herring," Vane said at last.

"The empire runs on documents, sir."

Something flickered across Vane's face — not suspicion exactly; recognition, maybe, the way one gambler recognizes another's system. Then it closed over. "Leave the herrings for morning. And Kavanagh—" he paused in the doorway, silhouetted against the hall lamp, a thin man made thinner by lamplight, "—the auditor arrives Friday. Everything in this house should be exactly where it appears to be."

"Yes, sir."

The door shut. Gideon sat without moving for the length of ten slow breaths, listening to Vane's footsteps climb the stairs, stop outside the clerks' room — where Tomas slept, and where Gideon's cot now stood conspicuously empty — and continue on.

*Everything should be exactly where it appears to be.* It was almost a kindness, Vane saying it aloud. It told Gideon precisely how much time he had left: five pages' worth, and not a page more.

* * *

Sergeant Ilsa Rehn had commanded checkpoints before. She had commanded them on the eastern frontier, where the smugglers were clever; on the river roads, where the smugglers were desperate; and now in Saltmere, where the smugglers, as far as she could determine, were accountants.

This offended her professionally.

She stood in the Fish Gate checkpoint at dawn with her second, Corporal Brandt, watching the porters file through with their barrels and crates, and she held in her gloved hand the inventory manifest of the Meridian Trading Company, and she read it the way her mother used to read tea leaves — looking not at what was written but at what had been smoothed over.

"Read me the count again," she said.

Brandt read it. "Salted herring, forty barrels. Salt, twenty-six casks. Rope, coil, assorted—"

"No." Rehn tapped the page. "Forty barrels. But their own requisition ledger — the duplicate the company keeps — says forty-two drawn from stores the week before the occupation." She handed it back. "Two barrels missing, Corporal. From a trading house whose manager handed over his records smiling like a bridegroom."

"Maybe he miscounted."

"Aldous Vane has never miscounted anything in his life. I've seen his handwriting. The man crosses his sevens. You don't cross your sevens if you're capable of error." She watched a porter shuffle past under a load of salt casks, bent double, moving with the particular carefulness of a man who is carrying something more fragile than salt. Her eyes followed him all the way to the gate and out the other side. "Somebody in that house knows what those two barrels were really for."

"You think it's smuggling, Sergeant?"

"I think," Rehn said slowly, "that when the army requisitions records, records are the only cargo left worth stealing. Think about it. We took their books. Books are the whole wealth of a place like this — not the herring, not the salt. Names. Debts. Who owes whom and what happens when they can't pay. If I wanted to hurt Aldous Vane, I wouldn't rob his warehouse." She pulled her gloves tighter. "I'd steal his handwriting."

Brandt looked faintly alarmed, the way he did whenever she thought aloud. She had learned to ignore it. Twenty years in the service taught you that most men preferred orders to reasons, and that the ones who wanted reasons were either going to be very good soldiers or very dead ones.

She tightened the inspections that afternoon. Every cart weighed. Every barrel sounded — tap the staves, listen for hollow where there should be brine, brine where there should be hollow. Every porter's papers read twice, once for the words and once for whether the words had been written in fear. Fearful handwriting, she had found, leaned backward, as though the writer were trying to stand away from his own sentences.

And she requested, through proper channels, a transfer order for herself to the Capital Gate. Not because she suspected Saltmere would produce anything — but because whatever came out of the Meridian Trading House would eventually travel toward the capital, toward newspapers, toward ministries, toward the kind of people who read requisition schedules the way she did. Things flowed downhill to the sea and uphill to the capital, and Rehn intended to be standing at the top of the hill when they arrived.

Her brother was stationed at the mines. That fact sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, and she did not look at it directly. She only knew that she had begun reading every transfer list twice.

That evening, walking back past the trading house on her rounds, she saw what she had half expected to see: a light in the counting-room window, burning well past midnight, shuttered carelessly — or carefully. Behind it, a shadow that bent over a desk and did not move for hours.

"Accountants," she said to the empty street, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling.

* * *

Seven pages. Eight. On the ninth night, Gideon's candle burned so low he had to finish the page by moonlight, and the moon cooperated the way moons do, which is beautifully and uselessly.

Tomas noticed things. That was the danger of Tomas. Tomas was the closest thing Gideon had to a friend in the world — a round, cheerful boy from upriver who owed Vane nine pounds for his mother's medicine and mentioned the debt the way other men mention weather, constantly and with dread. Tomas noticed that Gideon ate less. That he flinched at boots. That his ink bottle emptied faster than his columns justified.

"You're writing letters," Tomas said on the ninth night, catching him refilling the pen in the dark of the clerks' room. "Who writes letters at midnight?"

"Accounts to my conscience."

"Do they answer?"

"Only in arrears."

Tomas laughed, but he laughed a beat too late, and Gideon lay in his cot afterward doing sums he hated: nine pounds against eleven pages; a friend's hunger against a father's name. He had seen the arithmetic before, in the ledger itself, in every line where a man's life resolved into a figure. He had sworn, copying those contracts, never to be the one who did such sums.

But the net was closing — he could feel it in the way Vane's footsteps slowed outside the clerks' room now, every night, a little pause, a little weighing. He could feel it in the doubled patrols at the Fish Gate, in the sound of barrels being tapped like melons at market. Two pages remained. One night's work. Maybe less.

On the tenth night, Gideon waited for Vane's footsteps to climb the stairs and fade, waited until the house breathed the deep breath of the nearly-asleep, and slipped downstairs with his candle shielded in his fist.

He had five lines left to copy. Five lines, and then eleven pages folded into a bundle of requisition forms, and then the Fish Gate at dawn.

The counting room was dark. The safe stood open — Vane, careless now, or confident, or both — and the master ledger lay inside where Gideon had returned it every night, faithful as a churchgoer.

It was gone.

In its place, squared neatly on the empty shelf, lay a single sheet of company stationery. Gideon held his candle to it with a hand that had finally, after ten nights, earned its tremble.

The note was in Vane's crossed-sevens hand, and it said only:

*Kavanagh — we should discuss the herrings. My office, morning. Bring nothing.*
