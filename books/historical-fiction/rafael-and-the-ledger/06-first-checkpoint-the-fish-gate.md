## 6. First Checkpoint: The Fish Gate

The fish smell was the worst of it. Gideon had expected the fear — fear he had packed for, like a man packing for weather — but nobody had warned him that a porter's coat came soaked in brine and herring guts, and that by his third hour wearing it he would no longer be able to tell which stench was the fish and which was himself.

He had learned the coat from a man called Petchy, who sold coats the way other men sold opinions — cheaply, loudly, and without any guarantee of quality. Petchy had looked him over at the back door of the Drowned Anchor, taken in the soft clerk's hands, and said, "You'll want to carry something heavy first. Hands give you away faster than faces do." So Gideon had hauled crates of ice for an hour until his palms burned red and honest, and Petchy had pronounced him *adequate*, which Gideon suspected was the highest grade Petchy ever awarded.

The pages were already gone by then. He had sewn them — forty-one sheets, folded small as communion wafers — into oilcloth the night before, working by candle stub with a sailmaker's needle borrowed from a woman who asked no questions because she was paid not to. The bundle weighed almost nothing. That was the thing that kept astonishing him: all that grief, all those names, his father among them, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread.

It went into the barrel at dawn.

* * *

Old Sef's catch barrels were famous in Saltmere for two reasons: the salt cure, which could keep a herring edible clear through to Lent, and Old Sef himself, who could keep a secret even longer. Maren had arranged everything through him — Maren, who had appeared at his elbow two nights ago outside the trading house wall like a gull appearing beside a fishing boat, saying only, "I heard you needed the fish road. I have the fish road."

Gideon still didn't know how she'd heard. He suspected he never would. Widows of divers, he was learning, ran on their own currents.

The barrel stood third from the left in Sef's cart, wedged between two others that smelled authentically of the sea's least flattering moods. The pages sat in their oilcloth cocoon beneath six inches of coarse salt and a layer of split herring, which Sef had laid over them with the tenderness of a man tucking in children.

"Salt eats paper," Gideon had said, watching.

"Salt eats slow," Sef said, "and soldiers read fast. You want it slow or fast?"

Gideon wanted it slow. They loaded the cart as the sky went the color of dishwater, and Maren walked beside it with her basket on her hip, playing the part of a widow selling her dead husband's catch because her dead husband's catch did not sell itself.

"You don't have to come," Gideon said for the fourth time.

"And leave you to talk your way past Sergeant Rehn?" Maren said. "You'd confess to piracy inside a minute. You've got an honest face. It's a terrible affliction."

"I thought my face was unremarkable."

"That too. Remarkably honest." She adjusted her shawl. "Walk slower. Porters who hurry look like porters carrying something worth hurrying about."

So they went slowly through streets that were slowly waking, past shuttered stalls and the first stubborn gulls, toward the Fish Gate — the arched mouth in Saltmere's seawall where every cart of catch had passed since before anyone's grandmother, and where now, since the requisition, two soldiers stood with rifles and a sergeant's table set up across the cobbles like an altar to paperwork.

Gideon knew the checkpoint's rhythm by then; he'd watched it for three days from the roof of the cooperage. Most carts got a glance and a wave. Every fifth or sixth got opened. And twice, in three days, the inspections had been done personally by Sergeant Ilsa Rehn herself, who had a way of walking around a barrel the way a surgeon walks around a patient — as if she already knew what was wrong and was deciding whether to mention it.

Rehn. He'd written out her supply requisitions for a year. He knew her boot size and her ink preference and that she ordered lamp oil in quantities suggesting she did not sleep much. She had signed her name in a hand so precise it looked typeset. He had imagined, in the safe arithmetic of the ledger room, that such precision meant predictability.

Standing fifty yards from her table now, watching her turn a fisherman's papers over twice, he revised the theory entirely. Precision, it turned out, also meant thoroughness.

"Don't look at her," Maren murmured. "Look at the cart ahead. Count its staves. Clerks count things when they're frightened — it's what clerks do instead of praying."

"How do you know what clerks do?"

"My husband counted his breaths between dives. Same disease." She shifted her basket. "Forty-one staves on that cart, if you're wondering. Now stop counting."

The cart ahead cleared. A soldier waved it through with the boredom of a man who had inspected four hundred barrels of fish and found in none of them the revolution he'd been promised. Then the soldier looked at Sef's cart, and then at Gideon, and jerked his thumb toward the table.

* * *

Sergeant Rehn was smaller than Gideon remembered, though he had only ever seen her seated behind Vane's desk while Vane performed his anxious hospitality. Standing, she was compact and deliberate, with the kind of face that gave away nothing except, occasionally, tiredness. There was a good deal of tiredness in it this morning.

"Cart owner?" she said to Sef.

"Owner," Sef agreed.

"Papers."

Sef produced papers. Gideon kept his eyes down and his hands loose at his sides and thought hard about nothing, which turned out to be very difficult, because thinking about nothing is just thinking about everything with the volume turned down.

Rehn read. Somewhere behind them a gull screamed its opinion. Then she said, without looking up, "And these two?"

"Widow Maren Ostrander. Selling what the sea left me." Maren's voice was easy, worn smooth like beach glass. "This is the porter. Strong back, weak mind, standard model."

The soldier nearest Gideon grunted what might have been a laugh.

Rehn looked up. Her eyes crossed Gideon's face the way a lighthouse beam crosses water — once, thoroughly, moving on. Except it didn't move on. It came back.

Gideon felt the moment happen. He felt it the way you feel a floorboard shift underfoot. Something in his posture, maybe — a porter would have slumped; he was standing the way men stand in offices. Or his hands — Petchy's warning echoing — hands that had held pens for ten years and ice crates for one hour.

She knows, he thought. She has known since the second look.

"Open the third barrel," Rehn said.

The world narrowed to the sound of his own pulse. The soldier took up the pry bar. Third barrel. Third from the left. Forty-one sheets of paper under six inches of salt, and above them, Gideon understood suddenly and completely, there was nothing whatsoever he could do — no cleverness, no coin, no word that would survive the next thirty seconds.

Maren stepped forward.

"Sergeant." Her voice hadn't changed, but something underneath it had — a keel showing. "That barrel's mine to answer for, and I'll answer plain. My man dove eleven years for the Meridian. Eleven years, and what the company paid me after the beds took him wouldn't buy the rope they buried him with. If you're going to dump a widow's winter onto those cobbles, I'd take it kindly if you did it yourself, so I know whose hands did it."

The words landed in the checkpoint like stones into still water. Even the gull seemed to reconsider.

Rehn regarded her for a long moment. Then she said, quietly, "Your husband's name."

"Bren Ostrander. Drowned off the north beds, four years past. You can look him up in the company ledger, if they haven't burned it yet."

Something moved through Rehn's face then — not softness exactly. Recognition, perhaps. The look of a woman hearing an echo from a room she'd been trying not to enter. Her hand came up — a small motion, barely a gesture — and the soldier with the pry bar stopped, bar half-raised.

"The salt cure in these," Rehn said, turning back to Sef as if the last minute had been struck from the record, "is it the old method?"

"Older than me," Sef said.

"Herring rot from the inside out when it's cured lazy." She nodded at the barrel, unopened, and stepped back from the table. "Move along. All of you."

They moved. Gideon took the cart's rail because his legs suggested it might be wise, and they went through the Fish Gate arch into the shadow of the stone, and out the far side, where the coast road opened gray and long toward the river country.

Nobody spoke for a quarter mile. The wheels rattled. The sea fell away behind them.

Then Gideon said, "You didn't have to—"

"No," Maren said. "I didn't." She walked a few steps more. "But Bren used to say the sea keeps everyone's debts eventually. Seems to me the army's been keeping ours, and charging interest besides." She glanced at him sideways, and for the first time there was something wry in it. "Besides, someone had to save you. You were standing there like a man at his own audit."

"I was composing a confession," Gideon admitted. "A fairly complete one. I'd gotten as far as my mother's maiden name."

"There it is," Maren said. "Honest face."

* * *

They made eight miles by dusk, cart creaking, herring ripening nobly around their cargo. At a bend where the road dropped toward a creek, Maren stopped and told Sef to water the horse, and drew Gideon aside under a wind-bent rowan.

"Now you listen," she said. "Past the river toll, the road splits, and I go home — I've a daughter and nets that won't mend themselves, and I've spent enough courage for one week. From there you're alone, and alone is exactly what you must not be." She pressed a scrap of paper into his palm, folded small. "There's a ferryman at the toll, Tammas Ode, who owes me for two winters of fish when his boat was cracked. Tell him the widow remembers. He'll grumble. He grumbles beautifully. But he'll take you."

"Maren—" Gideon started, and found he couldn't finish it. Everything he might say sounded thin against what she'd actually done. "Why? You don't know what's in that barrel."

"I know it's heavier than it looks." She resettled her basket. "And I know what it's like to have a name put down in somebody else's book and forgotten. Whatever you're carrying — carry it well. For all of us who got a line instead of a life."

She turned back toward the cart, and Gideon unfolded the paper with fingers that had finally stopped shaking, expecting an address.

It wasn't an address. It was a name — Bren Ostrander — and beside it, in a diver's widow's careful hand, a single line:

*He counted his breaths. Count yours.*

Down the road behind them, faint on the wind, came the sound Gideon had been dreading since dawn: hoofbeats, more than one, coming fast from the direction of the Fish Gate.
