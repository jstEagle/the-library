## 7. Second Checkpoint: The River Toll

The river barge *Patient Maggie* sat low in the water with a cargo of salt fish, tar barrels, and one clerk pretending to be a porter's cousin. Gideon stood at the rail with a rope coiled in his hands because Sergeant Rehn had taught him — by nearly arresting him at the Fish Gate two days past — that idle men attract questions.

"You hold that rope like it owes you money," Maren said beside him.

"It might."

"Then hold it like you've forgiven it." She took the coil from him and slung it over her shoulder with the ease of thirty years hauling nets. "A porter carries things. He doesn't contemplate them."

Gideon watched the river toll grow out of the morning mist — a stone arch straddling the channel, a chain lowered to stop the traffic, soldiers on the quay with a board of papers pinned under glass. His stomach performed a slow revolution.

"They'll have the list already," he murmured. "Vane's description of me will have gone upriver ahead of us. That's how he does everything — ahead, always ahead."

"Then we don't look like the man on the list," Maren said. "We look like a widow and her sister's boy bringing fish to market, which is what we are, if you squint."

"I'm carrying four hundred pages of treason in a false-bottomed crate."

"Then squint harder."

* * *

Maren Ostrand had buried a husband, raised no children, and outlived three company managers, and she considered this last fact her proudest achievement. She had known Gideon Kavanagh since he was a boy who measured everything — coins, bread crusts, the distance between himself and trouble. She'd liked him for it. A man who knew what he could carry was a man who could be trusted with what mattered.

What mattered was currently disguised as salt cod, and Maren intended to get it up the river or die of herring stench trying.

Her nephew Denny stood the toll. She'd learned this from a letter her sister wrote in October, full of pride: *Denny has a uniform now, and boots the government pays for.* Maren had read the letter twice and felt something complicated move in her chest — part pride, part shame, part the old grief of a town whose sons kept getting bought by the same empire that bought its fathers.

She saw him before the barge touched the chain. Taller than she remembered. Younger-looking in the uniform, somehow, as though the coat swallowed him rather than the other way round.

"Well," she said to Gideon, low. "The gods have a sense of humor after all."

"That's your nephew?"

"That's Denny. Don't gawp. Gawping is a crime here too, I expect."

* * *

The chain came up. The *Patient Maggie* bumped the quay, and soldiers swarmed her flanks with the cheerful brutality of men who enjoyed boarding things. A corporal demanded manifests. Another began poking at the tar barrels with his bayonet, which struck Gideon as a good way to lose an eye but not, apparently, his concern.

And then the sergeant of the post stepped forward with the board of papers, and Gideon's blood went cold in a very orderly fashion, the way it did everything.

"New orders," the sergeant announced to the deck generally. "Wanted man. Company clerk, name of Kavanagh. Twenty-three years, dark hair, scar through the left eyebrow." He held up the notice. Someone had drawn Gideon's face on it, and drawn it badly — he looked like a criminal and also slightly like a horse. "Reward posted. Fifty crowns."

Fifty crowns. Gideon did the arithmetic without meaning to — a clerk's indenture was worth perhaps forty. Vane had priced him above his own ledger value. Some small, absurd part of him was flattered.

The corporal with the bayonet wandered toward the fish crates.

* * *

Denny Quill had been standing tolls for eight months and had learned three things: the army fed you worse than your mother, the officers lied better than the merchants, and every person coming up the river was tired enough to cry if you were only patient.

He saw his Aunt Maren before he saw anything else, and his stomach dropped through the deck planks into the river.

She looked older. Thinner. There was a young man with her — big shoulders, sunburnt neck, holding a rope like a man who'd never held one before, which was odd, because everything else about him said he'd worked docks all his life. His hands were wrong for a porter. Too clean at the nails. Ink-stained, maybe.

*Aunt Maren,* Denny thought, *what have you got in those fish?*

Because he'd seen the notice. Everyone had. Clerk, wanted, fifty crowns. And there was something in the way the young man stood slightly behind Maren — not hiding, exactly, but positioned, the way a man stands when he's been thinking about exits.

"Manifest," Denny said, and hated how official his voice sounded.

"Fish, tar, and my sister's boy helping me to market," Maren said. "Though he's more hindrance than help. Holds a rope like it insulted him."

The young man smiled — a quick, startled smile — and Denny felt something turn over in his chest.

"This him?" asked the post sergeant, arriving with the notice. "Sister's boy?"

"That's him. Tomas Ostrand's lad, from up the coast." She said the name easily. Denny didn't know any Tomas Ostrand. He suspected nobody did. His aunt had invented a dead relative between one breath and the next, smooth as water.

The sergeant compared the notice against the young man's face. The drawing looked nothing like him, thank God — whoever had drawn it had given the fellow a chin like a shovel. But the sergeant wasn't looking at the drawing anymore. He was looking at the hands.

"Porter, are you?"

"Cousin," the young man said. "On the mother's side. I keep accounts for the fish sheds mostly. Lending a hand."

"Accounts." The sergeant's eyes narrowed pleasantly. "Whereabouts?"

"Harrow Quay," Maren cut in. "You want to check, you can write to the shed master, but he'll only write back complaining about the cost of postage, so save the crown." She dug in her basket and produced a paper — a bill of lading, stamped, genuine. Denny had no idea where she'd got it. He suspected she'd got it the way she got everything, which was by being Maren Ostrand.

The sergeant examined it. The corporal, meanwhile, had reached the fish crates, and drove his bayonet into the topmost barrel with a grunt. Brine slopped over the rim. Gideon — cousin — made a small sound like a man watching his own funeral.

"Mind the catch," Maren said sharply. "That's paid for."

"Inspection's free," the corporal said, but he withdrew the blade and wiped it, having found nothing but herring. The false bottom sat beneath the second layer, weighted, sealed with wax and fish blood. Gideon had learned from Rehn's inspection at the Fish Gate: hide a thing where the smell is worst. Nobody digs twice into herring.

"Let them through," the sergeant said finally, and Denny heard his own exhale and hoped nobody else had.

But then his aunt paused, one foot on the gangplank, and looked up at him. Just looked. And in that look was the whole of Saltmere — the pearl beds his grandfather had drowned in, the wage lines his mother still couldn't read, the way the company spelled their family name wrong in its own books for forty years until they stopped correcting it.

"Aunt Maren—" he started.

"Eat properly," she said. "You're thin. Your mother worries."

She went down the plank. The cousin followed. The chain rose behind them.

Denny stood at the toll and thought: fifty crowns. He thought: *she'd have given me the crown herself if I asked, and that's why I can't take hers.* He could not have named what he felt. It sat somewhere below shame and beside love and wore a uniform it hadn't chosen.

He tore the wanted notice off the board, folded it small, and put it in his pocket, where it stayed for the rest of his life.

* * *

They cleared the arch by half a mile before either of them spoke.

"Your nephew," Gideon said. "He knew."

"He suspected."

"He looked right through me."

"He looked right *past* you, which is different, and harder." Maren adjusted her basket. "I've watched that boy pretend not to see things since he was four. It's the family gift. We come from people who survived by not noticing."

Gideon leaned on the rail. Behind them, faint on the wind, came the sound he'd been dreading since dawn — a steam launch, working hard against the current.

"Maren," he said. "Don't turn around."

She turned around.

The launch flew a Meridian pennant. At its bow stood a narrow figure in a grey coat, hands clasped behind him, and even at distance there was no mistaking Aldous Vane's posture — the posture of a man perpetually waiting for the world to be delivered to him.

"He's offering fifty crowns for me," Gideon said. "How much do you suppose he'd offer for the both of us?"

"Less for me. Widows depreciate." But her knuckles had whitened on the rail. "There's a landing at Millstone Bend. We jump, we're in the reeds, we walk the towpath."

"We jump, we abandon the cargo, and the crate goes to market without us — straight into whatever warehouse Vane chooses."

"So we don't jump." She was already moving, calm as tide. "So we arrive faster. Porter! Get that rope. If we're going to be caught, we'll be caught punctual, and let him explain to the toll sergeant why a manager chases fish barges."

* * *

The toll sergeant watched the steam launch come alongside ten minutes after the barge had passed, and he watched the grey-coated gentleman step onto his quay with the air of a man arriving somewhere he owned.

"Meridian Trading," the gentleman said. "A clerk named Kavanagh may have passed on a fish barge. There is a reward. Fifty crowns. One hundred, if the man *and* certain documents are detained."

The sergeant thought of the sister's boy with the ink-stained hands, and of the aunt who'd invented a dead man without blinking, and of the folded paper in his own pocket bearing a face like a horse.

"No barge stopped here," he said, "but one went through. Half an hour back. You'll not catch her before Millstone Bend — current's with her."

Vane's face did something complicated and expensive. Then he produced a leather wallet. "Two hundred crowns," he said, "for the man."

The sergeant looked at the money. Two hundred crowns was two years' pay. Two hundred crowns was a cottage. Two hundred crowns was every debt he had, settled twice over, with change left for drink.

Everyone has a price, the sergeant's father used to say. The trick is making sure yours isn't for sale on a Tuesday.

"Not on my toll," he said. "Ask at the capital gate. They've a new commander up there — Rehn, they say. Strict one."

Something crossed the manager's face at that name — something almost like fear — and he stepped back aboard his launch, and the launch turned upriver, chasing a barge that was, at that moment, rounding the bend and out of sight.

In his pocket, the sergeant's hand closed around the folded notice, and he wondered — not for the first time that morning — which side of the ledger he was standing on.

* * *

At Millstone Bend the *Patient Maggie* slowed for the landing, and Gideon made his choice quickly, the way he'd learned to make choices as a child — by weight, by portability, by what could be carried.

"The crate stays with the cargo," he told Maren. "It goes to Calloway's address on the manifest. I go overland from here, alone. If Vane catches the barge, he finds fish and a delivery note, and you find a widow who knows nothing."

"And if he catches *you*?"

Gideon weighed the question the way he weighed everything. "Then I'm lighter than I look."

Maren pressed something into his hand — small, wrapped in oilcloth. Her husband's old compass, brass, worn smooth. "For finding north," she said. "You strike me as a man who's been navigating by grief. It works, but it wears you thin."

He laughed — actually laughed — and for a moment the river was just a river, and he was just a porter's cousin with clean nails and four hundred pages of truth hidden somewhere behind him, going up to the capital ahead of the man who owned his name.

Ahead lay the Capital Gate. Ahead lay Rehn, transferred, they said — the strict one.

Ahead lay everything he could not carry and would have to anyway.
