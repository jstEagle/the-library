## 8. Third Checkpoint: The Capital Gate

The road to the capital climbed out of the river valley in long, patient switchbacks, as if the empire itself wanted to make certain no one arrived at its gates without ample time to reconsider. Gideon rode on the back of Maren's cousin's cart, wedged between crates of dried herring and a wheel of cheese that smelled like it had opinions about him. His bundle of requisition receipts sat under his knees, warm from the road, warm, he imagined, like something alive.

"You keep looking at it," Maren said.

"I'm not."

"You've looked at it eleven times since the toll bridge. I counted."

Gideon shifted his weight so the bundle pressed less conspicuously against his thigh. "Habit," he said. "I measure my life in portable things. It's a bad habit for a man carrying the least portable thing in the empire: the truth."

Maren snorted. "That's not truth you're sitting on, boy. That's paper. Truth is what happens when somebody reads it." She flicked the reins at a mule that had stopped to consider its future. "My husband read his indenture contract once. Read it aloud to me by candlelight, every clause. Then they sold him anyway. Paper's only worth what the reader decides it's worth."

They crested the last hill an hour before dusk, and the capital gate rose out of the haze like a ledger entry made of stone — tall, gray, and balanced to the last brick. The wall ran off east and west until it dissolved into the evening smudge, and through the arch itself flowed the evening traffic: carts, pilgrims, fishmongers with the day's unsold catch, clerks going home to suppers Gideon tried very hard not to envy.

And beneath the arch, where the road narrowed to a single lane wide enough for suspicion, stood the third checkpoint.

Two soldiers at the barrier. A table. A lantern already lit, though the light was still good. And beside the table, hands clasped behind her back, posture straight enough to hang laundry on —

Sergeant Ilsa Rehn.

Gideon felt the bottom drop out of him with the particular courtesy of a trapdoor: quietly, completely, without any drama whatsoever.

"That's her," he said. "That's the one from the Fish Gate."

"I know who it is," Maren said. Her knuckles had gone white on the reins. "I vouched for you once, Kavanagh. I'll not be able to vouch twice. A widow can be wrong once. Twice makes me a conspirator."

"Then don't vouch. Drop me here and drive on."

"And let them pull you apart at the gate and trace you back to my cart by suppertime?" She shook her head. "You're in my cart now. That makes you cargo. I don't abandon cargo."

He might have argued — he had spent seven years among men who could argue the shine off a coin — but there was no time. The cart ahead of them was moving up to the table, and the soldier there was waving them forward with two bored fingers.

* * *

What struck Gideon first, as the cart rolled into the lantern light, was how tired Rehn looked.

At the Fish Gate she had been all edges, a woman carved out of regulations. Now there were shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose small objects in, and her uniform jacket, though immaculate, hung on her as if she'd recently lost weight or sleep or both. He knew the look. He'd seen it in divers coming up too many times from the pearl beds — the body present, the person somewhere below, still descending.

"Papers," said the soldier at the table, without looking up.

Maren handed over her cart license, her market permit, a letter of passage signed by someone whose signature was probably worth more than the letter. Gideon produced his own documents: the forged porter's chit that had gotten him past the Fish Gate, a laborer's travel pass bought in the river town with money he didn't have to spare, and, folded inside his coat, the bundle of requisition receipts with their false lining of innocent arithmetic.

The soldier read. He read slowly, the way soldiers read when they want you to know they're reading.

"Name?"

"Gil Marsh," Gideon said. The name tasted like copper. Every lie did; he'd never developed the taste for it.

"Business in the capital?"

"Delivery for a merchant house. Ledgers and receipts." He heard himself say it and nearly laughed at the absurdity of it — the truest lie he'd ever told. "Bookwork."

Rehn hadn't spoken. She had come around the table without hurrying, the way weather comes around a headland, and now she stood at the cart's side, looking at the herring crates, the cheese, the straw, and finally at Gideon himself.

She looked at him for a long moment.

He watched her eyes do the work: the face beneath the porter's cap, the ink stains no amount of scrubbing ever fully lifted from a clerk's fingers, the way a man sits when he is guarding something with his body rather than his hands. He watched recognition arrive in her like a tide coming in — not sudden, not loud, just inexorable, covering everything.

"Sergeant?" said the soldier with the papers.

Rehn held up one finger. Still looking at Gideon, she reached into the cart, lifted the bundle of requisition receipts from beside his knee, and weighed it in her hands.

The road went very quiet. Somewhere behind them a mule complained about the wait, and somewhere ahead the capital glittered like a harbor full of lamplit ships, close enough to smell the supper smoke.

"This shipment," Rehn said at last, to no one in particular, "is listed for inspection."

"Sergeant, we're backed up to the—"

"*Inspection,*" she repeated, in the voice of a woman who has said the word ten thousand times and would happily say it ten thousand more if it meant not saying anything else.

She carried the bundle to the table. She set it down under the lantern. And then, while the soldier watched and Maren gripped the rail of her cart and Gideon stood in the open with his heart going like a diver's pulse at thirty fathoms, she untied the cord.

She peeled back the top layer of receipts. She looked at them. Ordinary requisitions — grain, boot leather, lamp oil, the empire digesting itself. Then her fingers found the seam in the lining, because of course they did; she was the checkpoint commander, seams were her profession.

For a moment she went absolutely still.

Gideon could not see what she saw. He knew what it was: the pages he had copied in the dark of the trading house, night after night, Vane's footsteps overhead, the pen scratching like a mouse in the walls. Contracts of sale. Names. Columns of names sold to the army as forced labor — divers, dockhands, debtors — each one marked with a price, and some marked *deceased — asset closed*, and one name, third column, ninth line, in his father's own borrowed spelling: *Kavanagh, G. senior.*

Rehn read. Her lips moved slightly, the way people's do when they are counting, or praying, or doing both at once.

Then she turned a page, and Gideon saw her stop.

Not at his father's name. At another one. A name further down the column, in a section headed *MINES — NORTHERN DISTRICT*, where the handwriting changed because a different clerk had entered it, and where among the laborers' names was one that made Sergeant Ilsa Rehn's hand flatten slowly against the paper as though holding down something alive.

*Rehn, T.*

Her brother. He remembered it then, from the ledger's cross-references — a soldier's name, seconded to labor transport three years back, listed without rank, without unit, without explanation. Just a name in a column, converted from a man into a line item. He had copied it along with all the others, not knowing what he was copying.

The lantern hissed. The queue behind them grew. Maren's hand found Gideon's sleeve and gripped it, not for his comfort, he suspected, but for hers.

"Problem with the shipment, Sergeant?" asked the soldier, drifting over.

Rehn tied the cord again. Her fingers were steady. Gideon would think about that afterward — how steady they were, the steadiness it must have cost.

"No problem," she said. "It's bookwork." Something crossed her face then, brief and bitter, almost a smile. "Nobody inspects bookwork. It's only numbers."

She handed the bundle back — handed it, Gideon noted, to *him*, not to the soldier — and turned to the table. "Cart's clear. Herring's herring, cheese is cheese, and this man is a delivery clerk with honest, tedious paperwork. Wave them through."

"Sergeant, the wanted list says—"

"The wanted list says a clerk named Gideon Kavanagh, five foot eight, ink-stained fingers, traveling alone." She glanced at Maren. "This man is traveling with a widow, her herring, and her cheese, which no fugitive with any sense would do, which proves he hasn't the sense to be a fugitive." She picked up her stamp and brought it down on the travel pass with the finality of a gavel. "Next cart."

* * *

The barrier lifted. The wheels crunched forward onto the capital's paving stones, and Saltmere and everything behind them fell away like water off an oar.

Gideon didn't breathe properly for a hundred yards. When he finally did, it came out ragged, and Maren looked over at him with something between pity and exasperation.

"She knew," he said. "She knew it was me from the Fish Gate. She must have. And she—" He stopped, because finishing the sentence felt like tempting fate. "Why?"

Maren drove in silence for a while. Ahead, the capital opened its evening streets to them, all lamplight and noise and ten thousand strangers who didn't know yet what was coming to them in tomorrow's print.

"A woman reads a list of names," Maren said finally, "and finds her brother's at the bottom of it. After that, son, there aren't two sides anymore. There's just the list, and everybody standing somewhere along it." She clicked her tongue at the mules. "Your sergeant just moved herself to our side of the page. God help her for it."

Gideon looked back once. Under the arch, small in the distance, Ilsa Rehn stood at her table stamping papers, straight-backed as ever, while the soldier beside her wrote something in a report book — writing, perhaps, the words that would end her career, her uniform, everything she had built her life out of. She had bought his passage with her own ruin and had not even charged him the dignity of a goodbye.

He faced forward. In his coat, against his chest, the pages rode warm and light as a bird.

Somewhere in this city, he thought, there is a journalist named Edith Calloway. And somewhere behind him, a ledger is being packed for burning, and a manager named Vane is discovering, one receipt at a time, exactly what has slipped through his gate.

The cart rolled on into the capital, and Gideon Kavanagh — clerk, liar, orphan of the pearl beds, carrier of names — began, for the first time in his life, to hurry toward daylight instead of away from the dark.
