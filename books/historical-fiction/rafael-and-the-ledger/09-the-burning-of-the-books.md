## 9. The Burning of the Books

The offices of *The Meridian Courier* smelled of ink, cold tea, and the particular desperation of men who had been awake since before the harbor fog lifted. Gideon stood in the doorway with a fishmonger's bundle under his arm and salt still crusted in his hair, feeling like the least impressive parcel ever delivered to that address.

Edith Calloway looked up from behind a barricade of paper that could have rivaled Vane's back room, took one look at him, and said, "You're either the most punctual disaster I've ever hired or the answer to a letter I sent three weeks ago."

"Neither," Gideon said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He hadn't spoken to anyone but Maren in four days. "I'm the thing your story is missing."

She came around the desk slowly, the way one approaches a bird that might fly off, or explode. "And what thing is that?"

He set the bundle on her desk and unwrapped it with hands that shook more than he wanted them to. The requisition receipts were stiff with salt water where Maren had soaked the wrapping to disguise the smell of fresh paper. Beneath them, forty-one pages in his own careful clerk's hand: contracts, signatures, sums. Names sold. Names closed.

Calloway read for a long time. Long enough that a typesetter poked his head in twice and was waved away without her looking up. When she finally raised her eyes, they were wet, which seemed to annoy her considerably.

"Do you know what I've been running for two years?" she said. "Rumors. Whispers from sailors who'd only talk if I bought them dinner first. I've written nine stories about forced labor in the mines and every single one has been killed upstairs for lack of a document." She pressed her palm flat against the pages. "This is not a rumor."

"No," Gideon said. "It's arithmetic. I checked it three times."

Something cracked in her face then — half a laugh, half something else entirely. "A bookkeeper. God save me, the empire is going to be brought down by a *bookkeeper*."

"I prefer 'clerk.' It sounds less like an accusation."

* * *

In Saltmere, they built the pyre in the square on a Tuesday morning, because Sergeant Rehn's orders specified Tuesday, and whatever else might be said of Sergeant Rehn, she followed orders the way a plumb line follows gravity.

The soldiers stacked the trading house records in a heap taller than Aldous Vane himself: wage books, indenture rolls, shipping manifests, forty years of the district's memory in ink and foolscap. The townsfolk gathered at a distance the soldiers pretended not to measure. Old Sela Brannigan brought her stool. The widow Maren stood at the front with her arms folded, watching with the flat expression of a woman who had already buried everything worth burning.

Tomas Pell stood among the deportees, wrists bound with cord, flanked by two soldiers who kept glancing at their sergeant as though hoping to be told this was all some elaborate joke.

Vane had questioned him again the night before, privately, in the back room that no longer contained anything worth hiding. "Think carefully," Vane had said, in the reasonable voice he used on creditors. "You owe me eleven pounds, four shillings. A word about where Kavanagh went, and we can call it eight. A name and an address, and we can call it nothing at all."

And Tomas — poor, frightened, perpetually-in-debt Tomas, who had sold his friend for the price of breathing room — had looked at the man who owned his debt and said, very quietly, "My mother's name was in that ledger too, Mr. Vane. Page sixty-one. Sold to the dredging crews when I was nine years old." He'd swallowed. "You want to know what Kavanagh carried? Nothing you can buy back. I did the arithmetic myself."

Vane had stared at him for a long moment, and for the first time in twenty years of managing other people's lives by the pound, Aldous Vane had nothing to enter into any column.

Now, at the pyre, the auditor gave the signal. Petrol sloshed. A match scratched.

The ledger went up like a living thing — pages curling, ink blistering, names lifting on the heat in black spirals that rose over Saltmere and out to sea. Gideon's father was in there somewhere, marked *deceased — asset closed*. So were four hundred others. The smoke didn't distinguish between the guilty entries and the innocent ones; it simply took everything.

Maren watched until the last page surrendered, then turned away and walked home, and if anyone had been close enough they might have heard her say, to nobody, "Too late, you bastards. Too late by four days."

* * *

"You're sure the original is destroyed?" Calloway asked. It was past midnight. Her office had acquired cold tea number two and a typesetter who refused to go home.

"The burn order was signed before I left Saltmere. Vane wouldn't risk the originals surviving an audit. He thinks fire solves everything." Gideon turned his teacup, a habit from childhood, measuring the room in portable things out of pure nerves. "He doesn't know I exist anymore. Legally speaking, my papers burned with everything else. As far as the empire is concerned, I am a rounding error."

"That," said Calloway, "is either the freest or the most dangerous way to be a man in this country, and possibly both." She fed a page to the typesetter. "We print Thursday morning. After that, there will be men at your door. Some with questions. One or two, I suspect, with handcuffs."

"I've been carrying those pages through three checkpoints for a week, madam. Handcuffs sound restful."

It was meant as a joke, and she treated it as one, but her eyes stayed serious. "What will you do? You can't go back to Saltmere."

Gideon considered the window, beyond which the capital glittered with the indifferent confidence of a city that believed itself permanent. Somewhere south of it, Saltmere was sweeping ash out of its gutters. Somewhere in the mines, men who appeared in no surviving record were swinging picks in the dark, and for the first time, someone above ground knew their names.

"There's a ship," he said. "The *Corvessa*, out on the morning tide. Calloway — do you ever feel like a man made of paper? Everything important about me was written down somewhere, and now it's ash, and I keep waiting to blow away."

"No," she said. "I feel like a printing press. What matters isn't the sheet in the machine. It's how many copies get out." She tapped the galleys. "Four hundred copies tomorrow. Then four thousand. You didn't carry the truth to safety, Kavanagh. You carried it to a *press*. There's a difference, and it's measured in tons."

He laughed — actually laughed — for the first time since the Fish Gate. It surprised them both.

* * *

Thursday morning, in Saltmere, Aldous Vane bought a newspaper.

He did not read newspapers as a rule; he considered them gossip with ambitions. But a junior officer had left one in the requisition office, face-up, and the headline reached across the room and took hold of his collar before he'd taken three steps.

**EMPIRE SELLS ITS OWN: LEDGER EVIDENCE EXPOSES FORCED LABOR TRADE**

He read standing up. He read it twice. Around him, the office went quiet the way a room goes quiet around a man holding something that might detonate.

The article named dates. It named sums. It quoted, verbatim, contract language Vane had personally drafted at two in the morning seven years ago, certain that no one would ever see it but him and the men who profited. And beneath the quotations, in the journalist's dry, merciless prose:

*"The original documents were burned by military order in Saltmere on Tuesday. However, this newspaper has obtained a complete copy of the relevant contracts, verified independently by three parties."*

Three parties.

Vane sat down slowly on a crate of confiscated tea. Forty years. Forty years of columns and cross-references, of knowing exactly where every soul in Saltmere stood because he held the paper that said so — and somewhere between his back room and a bonfire, one clerk with good handwriting and bad prospects had undone the whole architecture with forty-one sheets of copying paper and a week's walking.

An empire of paper, he thought, and paper cuts both ways.

He looked up. Sergeant Rehn stood in the doorway, reading her own copy, her face giving away precisely nothing except, perhaps, at the very corner of her mouth, the ghost of something that in a less disciplined woman might have been called satisfaction.

"Sergeant," Vane said, and his voice sounded strange to him, thin, like a page tearing. "I'd like to file a complaint."

"With whom?" she asked. "Sir, I believe the department you're complaining about just became the front page."

* * *

The *Corvessa* cast off at dawn Friday, riding a low tide and a rumor. Gideon stood at the stern rail with everything he owned in the world: one coat, one pearl knife that had belonged to a drowned man, and one newspaper, purchased from a boy on the quay who had charged him double and deserved every penny.

Behind him the capital shrank, and beyond the capital, invisible over the horizon, a town called Saltmere woke to find itself famous. He thought of Maren's kitchen. Of Sela Brannigan's stool. Of Tomas on a prison barge, having finally paid a debt that mattered. Of a sergeant who had let a stranger walk past her rifle, and whatever price she would pay for it.

Edith Calloway had pressed a note into his hand at the quay — her address, and one line beneath it: *"When this blows over, come write the book. You check figures better than I do."*

Gideon unfolded the paper against the rail and read the story again in the growing light. Four hundred names, printed in ordinary newsprint, unburnable now, multiplied by ten thousand presses across the country. His father's name was among them — not *deceased, asset closed*, but simply *Nathaniel Kavanagh*, a pearl diver's son's father, restored to the world in ink.

The wind came off the water, smelling of salt and tar and open distance. Gideon Kavanagh — legally nobody, practically a rumor, arithmetically a rounding error — watched the coast fall away and found, to his considerable surprise, that he did not blow away at all.

He weighed almost nothing. He carried everything.

Ahead, the sea. Behind, the scandal breaking like weather. And in his pocket, a knife that had once cut pearls from the dark, waiting to be useful again.
