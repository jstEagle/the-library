## 10. What Can Be Carried

The scandal broke on a Tuesday, which Gideon found fitting. Tuesdays at the Meridian Trading Company had always been reckoning days — the day Vane balanced accounts, called in debts, and read out the week's shortfalls in a voice like wet gravel. It was only proper that the empire's own arithmetic should come due on a Tuesday as well.

Gideon learned of it the way he learned of most things now: from a newsboy shouting beneath his window at the Harborside Lodging House, where he'd rented a room with the last coins Maren had pressed into his hand and refused to take back.

"MERIDIAN SCANDAL!" the boy cried, his voice cracking with the effort and the glory of it. "FORCED LABOR IN THE PEARL DISTRICT! MINISTER RESIGNS! READ ALL ABOUT IT!"

Gideon did not buy a paper. He already knew what it said, because he had written it — not the words, but the truth underneath them, in a clerk's careful hand, forty-one pages copied by candlelight while Sergeant Rehn's patrols walked the streets outside.

He went down anyway, and bought one, because some things you do not do for information. You do them for the weight of it in your hands.

The front page carried Edith Calloway's name above a headline that ran the full width of the sheet: *SOLD BY THE LEDGER: HOW THE MERIDIAN COMPANY TRADED MEN LIKE TONNAGE.* Below it, a woodcut illustration of a trading house, and beneath that, in smaller type, the sentence Gideon had read four hundred times since Calloway first showed him the galley proofs:

*"Among those listed as 'assets transferred' was one Kavanagh, G. — pearl diver, deceased, asset closed."*

His father had become news. A man who had drowned nameless in the company's beds, entered into a ledger between a shipment of copra and a broken winch, now stared out of every breakfast table in the capital. It was not justice. Justice would have looked different — quieter, maybe, or louder. But it was something, and Gideon held the paper against his chest for a moment in the doorway before folding it into his coat, where it rode over his heart like a second lung.

* * *

He went to see Calloway that morning, because she sent a note that said only: *"Come before I'm famous. It'll be the last chance either of us gets to be ordinary."*

Her office above the print shop smelled of ink, cold tea, and the particular smoke of someone who had been smoking too much and enjoying none of it. She sat amid drifts of paper like a woman shipwrecked on her own success, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its pins in every direction, and when she saw him she threw up her hands.

"There he is," she said. "The most wanted man in the empire for eleven days, and now nobody wants him at all."

"That's usually how it goes with me."

"Nonsense. Sit down. Drink this." She pushed a cup of tea at him that had gone the color and temperature of harbor water, and he drank it anyway, because refusing Edith Calloway's hospitality had never once been survivable.

"You know they arrested Vane yesterday," she said. "At his own house. He was burning papers in the study — his *personal* correspondence, nothing to do with us, he insisted — and they took him with the ashes still warm. He gave them such a speech about loyal service. The officer in charge just stood there until he ran out of words. Which, from all accounts, took a very long time."

"What happens to him?"

"A trial, eventually. A long one. There are men in the mines still alive who will testify, if we can get them home. Some won't live to see it." She said this without flinching, because flinching was not among her talents. "And there are widows. And sons. The government has promised compensation." Her mouth twisted around the word. "They've opened a *ledger*, Gideon. To pay for the crimes of a ledger. Sometimes I think we will never build anything in this country that isn't an accounting."

"Maybe accounting is honest," Gideon said, "if you put the right things in the columns."

Calloway looked at him for a moment, then laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her. "You should write that down. I may steal it for my next piece."

"I don't write. I copy."

"Well," she said, and her voice gentled in a way that made him look at his tea, "you copied the only document in this whole affair worth reading. Don't undersell the trade."

* * *

The matter of his indenture came up, as it inevitably did, in the form of a letter from the Ministry of Labor — a stiff, official thing that Gideon read twice on the walk back to the lodging house and once more sitting on his narrow bed.

*The records of the Meridian Trading Company having been destroyed by order of the military commission, and the indenture registry of the Saltmere district having been lost in the same conflagration, the Ministry regrets that it is unable to verify the contractual status of the individual known as Gideon Kavanagh. In the absence of documentation, the said individual must be considered legally unregistered.*

There followed two paragraphs of bureaucratic apology, a form to fill out should he wish to establish a new identity through "proper channels," and a stamp so official-looking it might have been carved from stone.

Legally unregistered. Legally nameless.

Gideon set the letter down and found, to his surprise, that he was smiling.

All his life he had measured himself in portable things. As a boy on the diver's boats he had learned that everything you loved could be taken, and so you learned to carry only what could not be weighed — a song his mother hummed, the shape of his father's hands on the rope, the exact blue of the water over the beds at dawn. When the fever took his mother and the company took his father's debt, they had given him an indenture paper instead of chains and told him it was the same thing, and he had believed them, because a boy believes what keeps him fed.

Now the chain was ash in Saltmere's square along with every other record of who he owed and what he cost. Somewhere in the Ministry's files there was a hole shaped exactly like Gideon Kavanagh, and no power in the empire could prove he belonged inside it.

Free, then. Free the way a man is free whose house has burned: nothing left to hold him, nothing left to hold.

He took out his father's pearl knife and turned it in the light. The blade was worn thin from years of prying shells off the rocks, the handle dark where a dead man's grip had polished it. It was the only object Gideon owned that had ever been chosen rather than issued, inherited rather than inventoried. He thought of his father using it, patient and strong, in water the color of glass, and he thought: *this is what can be carried.* Not the ledger, not the debt, not even the name. Just this. Just the truth of where you come from, small enough to fit in a pocket, sharp enough to open what is closed.

* * *

The envelope came three days later, forwarded through Calloway's office with no return address. Military postmark. Inside, a single line in a hand so precise it might have been typeset:

*"Some debts forgive themselves. — I.R."*

No signature beyond the initials. No explanation. Gideon stood in Calloway's office holding it and felt the strange gravity of it, the way you feel the pull of deep water through the hull of a boat.

"She's been court-martialed," Calloway said quietly. "Neglect of duty, failure to detain a fugitive, unauthorized disclosure — they're throwing everything but the stove at her. Stripped of rank. Prison term, probably. My sources say she didn't say one word in her own defense. Not one." Calloway shook her head. "I offered to publish her side of it. Make her a hero of conscience, get the public howling. She refused. Said the story wasn't about her."

"Wasn't it?"

"No." Calloway smiled thinly. "But don't tell her I agree with her."

Gideon folded the letter along its original creases and put it inside his coat, over his heart, where the newspaper had ridden and before that the copies, layer upon layer, the truth accumulating against his ribs like armor made of paper.

He had never met Sergeant Ilsa Rehn except across a checkpoint table, twice, with his life balanced on whether she recognized the porter's face above the fish-stink collar. Both times she had looked at him and let him pass, and both times he had understood that he was not being spared — he was being spent, like coin, by a woman settling accounts of her own. He wondered about the brother in the mines. Whether he lived. Whether he would ever learn what his sister had paid. Whether he would understand it, which was a harder currency to spend.

Some debts forgive themselves. He turned the sentence over the way he used to turn pearls, looking for flaws in the nacre. It was not quite true, he decided — debts did not forgive themselves, any more than ledgers balanced themselves. Somebody always did the arithmetic. Somebody always stood in the cold at a checkpoint and chose. But the sentiment was generous, and generosity from a court-martialed sergeant was rarer than black pearls, and he resolved to carry the line with him the way his father had carried the knife: carefully, and always.

* * *

The *Coriander* sailed on Friday morning with the tide, bound south along the coast, then east — wherever the shipping lanes went, Gideon hadn't asked. Calloway had arranged the passage and refused to discuss it. "Witnesses get deported," she said. "Sources get invited to lunch. Choose which one you want to be."

So he stood at the rail as Saltmere — no, not Saltmere; the capital; Saltmere was two hundred miles behind him and burning in other ways — as the capital fell away in gray terraces and gull-hung masts, and he watched the morning paper come aboard with the pilot boat, fresh off the presses, Calloway's follow-up splashed across the front: hearings announced, witnesses summoned home, the mines under official investigation. Somewhere in the crowd on the dock a newsboy was already selling tomorrow's ruin of Aldous Vane, and Gideon wished the boy good trade silently, and meant it.

A sailor near him whistled while coiling rope. The gulls argued over the wake. Ordinary sounds. The sound of a world that had not stopped turning merely because its books had been opened.

Gideon Kavanagh — legally unregistered, legally nameless, legally nothing at all — leaned on the rail with his father's knife in his pocket and forty-one pages of memory in his chest, and discovered that he felt lighter than he had since he was nine years old standing on a diver's deck watching the water take his father.

Not happy. Lighter. There was a difference, and he suspected he would spend years learning it.

The coast slid by — fishing villages, watchtowers, the white smudge of the salt pans — and somewhere past the headland the wind came round favorable and the sails filled and the *Coriander* leaned into her work like a woman glad of a task. Gideon watched a pearl boat far out in the bay, tiny with distance, its divers' flags fluttering, and he raised one hand to it, briefly, the way you greet a stranger who cannot know why you salute.

Then he went below to find out what work a nameless man might do on a working ship, because freedom, he was beginning to suspect, was not a place you arrived. It was a cargo. And somebody had to carry it.
