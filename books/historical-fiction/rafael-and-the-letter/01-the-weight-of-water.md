## 1. The Weight of Water

The baskets came up heavier in the morning, before the sun had burned the damp out of the ropes. Rafael Montrose had learned to read their weight from the first heave — a light basket meant thin beds, meant the foreman's mouth pulling down at the corners, meant less rice in the evening pot. A heavy basket meant his shoulders would ache by noon and there would be food. He had stopped deciding which he preferred years ago.

"Montrose! Count them, don't admire them."

He counted. Forty-one oysters in the third basket, forty-four in the fourth, each shell crusted with weed and the small blind barnacles that bit through canvas gloves if you gripped wrong. He wrote the numbers on his tally slate with the stub of pencil he kept tied to his wrist by a length of tarred twine. The slate was company property. The pencil was company property. The twine, he suspected, was also company property, though no one had ever asked for it back.

The morning light came off the harbor in flat silver sheets. Beyond the sorting tables the pearl fleet rode at anchor, masts swaying like reeds, and beyond them the customs house flew the colonial colors at half-mast for some official Rafael had never heard of and was not expected to mourn. The Verrone Trading Company's own flag — a black oyster on white — hung limp above the counting-house, too windless even to pretend at motion.

He hauled the next basket onto the scales. Sixty pounds, maybe more. The scale clerk, a free-born man named Odo Ferran with soft hands and a permanent expression of having smelled something, watched him do it without offering to help, which was normal, and then made a show of checking his pocket watch, which was also normal.

"You're slow today," Ferran said.

"The tide's against us."

"The tide doesn't sign your indenture." Ferran smiled at his own wit and moved down the line to bother someone else.

Rafael said nothing. This was the first rule of survival at Verrone Trading, learned so early it felt less like knowledge than like bone: *say nothing*. Not because silence earned anything — it didn't; it earned only the absence of worse — but because every word given to a free-born clerk was a word that could be repeated, twisted, entered into some record against you. His father had talked. His father had laughed with the foremen, traded jokes across the sorting tables, once carried Silas Grebe's diving chest up the hill road without being asked. And where had talking gotten him? A hole in the water off Serpent Rock, and a line in a ledger, and no name on any wall.

Rafael wrote *forty-four* and did not look up.

* * *

The midday bell released the divers' crew to the shade of the net shed, where they ate cold rice and salt fish off banana leaves and mended what the sea had torn. Rafael sat at the end of the bench nearest the door, as always, where he could see who came in. It wasn't fear exactly anymore. It was habit, and habits were cheaper than fear.

Elio sat across from him, thirteen and all elbows, worrying a netting needle through a tear in the trammel mesh like the mesh had insulted his mother.

"You missed the count this morning," Elio said. "Old Grebe's replacement — Corvo — he counted short again. Marta says her father's basket went in at sixty and came back marked fifty-two."

"Marta counts everything."

"Somebody has to." Elio pulled the needle through, snipped, started again. "You count too. You just don't say."

Rafael ate his rice. Around them the shed filled with the smell of hemp and brine and the particular sourness of men who had been underwater since dawn. The divers themselves were older men mostly, thick-shouldered, thick-eared, their hearing gone soft from pressure, their hands scarred white where the shells had opened them year after year. They spoke little. When they spoke it was in the low harbor dialect, and when they spoke about the company it was in the lower voice beneath the dialect, the one that didn't carry past the bench.

One of them, old Bruno with the clouded eye, looked up suddenly and said, to no one in particular: "Water keeps what it takes. That's what my mother said. Water keeps what it takes, and paper keeps what it's paid to keep."

Nobody answered him. Nobody ever answered Bruno. But Rafael felt the words go into him like a splinter, deep enough that he'd need years to work it out.

The afternoon passed in weights and tallies. Basket after basket. Slate after slate. By the time the last skiff came in, his shoulders had gone from ache to numbness, which was the body's way of being kind. He handed his tally slate to the day-clerk, who copied the numbers into the master ledger without once looking at Rafael's face, because to the master ledger Rafael was not a face. He was a column entry: *Montrose, R., indentured, age 16, diver's son, term remaining 9 years.*

Nine years. He had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn smooth. Seven now, if you counted the way the company counted, which was generously in its own favor. He would be twenty-five when the paper released him. Old enough to have spent his whole strong life in their service, young enough — they would say — to start over.

Start over with what?

He folded the question away with the others and walked the long way home along the harbor wall.

* * *

The shrine stood where the breakwater met the land, a squat stone alcove facing the water, older than the company, older than the colonial administration, maybe older than the town itself. Fishermen had built it, or their grandmothers had, and no one had ever quite dared tear it down, though the census maps marked it as an "unregistered structure." Inside, the walls were crowded with carved names — thousands of them, layered over each other like barnacles, names of the drowned going back further than anyone could read. Sailors cut theirs shallow with knives. Divers' families paid stone-cutters for proper letters. There was a whole economy of grief at that wall, and everyone in the harbor knew its prices.

Rafael went there most evenings. Not every evening — every evening would be devotion, and devotion invited attention — but often enough that the old women selling dried fish nearby had stopped noticing him, which was the point.

His father's name should have been on that wall.

That was the custom and everyone knew it was the custom: a diver lost in service, the company paid the stone-cutter's fee, the name went up within the season. Rafael had been nine years old when they pulled what could be pulled from the water off Serpent Rock. He remembered standing at this same wall with his mother, waiting, while other families' new names went up around them — *Almeida, 1887; Cruz the younger, 1888* — and their place stayed empty. His mother had asked. She had asked the foreman, she had asked the counting-house, she had asked the stone-cutter, who spread his hands and said he was sorry but he'd received no order and no fee. She asked until asking became dangerous, and then she stopped, and then she died — slowly, of the lungs, the way divers' widows did, breathing the salt damp of the company barracks — and the name still hadn't gone up.

So Rafael had come here instead, for seven years, to the empty space on the wall between *Cruz the younger* and a knife-scratched sailor's name half worn away, and he stood in front of the space the way other people stood in front of a grave.

There was room for four letters on either side. He had measured it with his thumb a hundred times. *JOSÉ MONTROSE.* It would fit. He had even practiced, once, with a nail, pressing hard enough to leave the faintest ghost of grooves — and then stopped, heart hammering, because defacing the shrine was a fineable offense and fines went onto the indenture account, and debts on the indenture account extended the term. He had learned that from watching Marta's father lose three months to a broken window.

Seven more years. Then he could pay a stone-cutter himself, honestly, with free money, and the name would go up, and that would be that.

It was a good plan. It was a patient plan. He recited it to himself the way other men recited prayers.

Tonight the tide was low and the light came in long and gold across the flats, and the wall was warm under his hand. He put his palm flat against the empty space, as he always did, as if he could pass warmth through the stone to whoever was on the other side of it.

"Papa," he said. He said it quietly, in the dialect, barely voicing it. "Still working on it."

The dried-fish woman glanced over and away. Somewhere behind him a gull screamed and was answered. The harbor turned gold, then copper, then the dull pewter of early dark, and the lamps of the counting-house came on one by one, orderly as arithmetic.

He was turning to go when he saw the notice.

It had been pasted to the board beside the shrine — the public board where the harbormaster's tide tables went up, and auction notices, and the occasional wanted bill. New paste, new paper, crisp in the failing light. Rafael read it twice because the first reading refused to make sense.

*CENSUS OF INHABITANTS AND DWELLINGS. By order of the Colonial Administration. Every person resident within the port district shall present themselves for enumeration and shall answer truly upon oath as to name, age, parentage, occupation, and dependents. Enumerators will call at all barracks, dormitories, and lodging houses beginning Monday next.*

Every person. Parentage. Answered truly upon oath.

Rafael stood very still. Behind him the water worked at the stones, patient, keeping what it took. He thought of the master ledger with his name in it, a column entry nine lines long. He thought of his father, who existed everywhere in the harbor's memory and nowhere in the company's paper.

On Monday, someone with an official ledger would ask him who his father was, and when his father died, and how.

And for the first time in seven years, Rafael Montrose understood that the careful silence he had built his whole life inside of might not hold — because a question was coming that he could not answer without lying to the government, or breaking open the thing the company had buried.

He pulled his cap down and walked back toward the dormitory, fast, like a man with somewhere ordinary to be.
