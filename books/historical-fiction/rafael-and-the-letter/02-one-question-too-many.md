## 2. One Question Too Many

The census taker came on a Tuesday, which was worse than Monday because it meant Rafael had spent two days not sleeping for nothing.

She arrived at the Verrone dormitory at half past eight in the morning, when the divers' children were supposed to be at the sorting tables and the free-born clerks were supposed to be pretending they weren't watching through the windows. She came with a leather satchel, a folding stool she did not open, and a manner that made the overseer, Mr. Pell, stand straighter than Rafael had ever seen him stand — and Pell was a man who took pride in never standing straight for anyone below a ship's captain.

"Census of persons," she said, loud enough for the whole courtyard. "Every soul under this roof is to be counted and recorded. Company policy permits no exceptions."

"Company *policy*," Pell repeated, as if tasting something he'd been told was food. He had a way of saying words that made them sound like they'd been lowered into his mouth by rope. "We've a schedule, madam. The boys work."

"Then we'll be quick." She set her satchel on the one dry crate by the rain barrel and began unbuckling it with small, efficient movements. Her hands were brown and inkstained at the second knuckle of every finger, both hands, which meant she wrote more than she read, or read more than anyone gave her credit for. Her coat was good wool gone soft at the cuffs. Not harbor money. Government money, then, which in this city was a different kind of poor.

Rafael stood at the back of the line of eleven indentured children, where he always stood, where standing had become a skill like net-mending. Marta was two places ahead of him, fourteen years old and built like her mother, who had drowned the year before her father did. The twins, Joaquín and Remy, were fidgeting the way they fidgeted whenever an adult with paper appeared, because paper was the thing that had eaten their family. Elio, youngest, kept glancing at Rafael the way Elio glanced at him before storms, as if Rafael were some kind of barometer.

"You first," the census taker said, and pointed — not at the head of the line, which would have been proper, but at Elio.

Pell cleared his throat. "The order of interview—"

"Is mine to determine, Mr. Pell, per the Enumeration Act of 1893, section four." She didn't look up from her ledger. "Unless Verrone Trading has amended the statutes. I understand they're ambitious."

Rafael felt something move in his chest that he refused to name. It might have been fear. It might have been the other thing, the thing that lived under the fear and only surfaced when someone spoke to power in a flat voice and didn't flinch.

Elio answered her questions badly, the way children do — dates wandering, ages guessed, the year of his father's drowning offered as "the winter it rained so hard the shrine wall went green." She wrote it all down without correcting him, and thanked him, and moved up the line.

Marta next. Marta answered like a soldier: names exact, dates exact, her father's death entered in a voice so level it could have been used to calibrate instruments. But Rafael saw the census taker's pen pause once, briefly, over the page, and resume.

Joaquín. Remy. Then the three others whose fathers hadn't drowned but had simply stopped coming back from the banks one season, which the company recorded as desertion and the harbor recorded as nothing at all. Then there was no one left between Rafael and the crate except the morning air.

"Name?"

"Rafael Montrose."

Her pen stopped.

It wasn't a dramatic stopping. It was the stop of a woman whose hand had encountered something unexpected in a familiar road — a stone, a root. She looked at her ledger, then at him, then back at the ledger, and the looking lasted perhaps three seconds, which in a courtyard full of people trying not to watch is a very long time.

"Rafael Montrose," she repeated. "Son of Tomás Montrose, diver, entered service of the Verrone Trading Company in the year 1885 upon the death of the father."

"That's what they tell me," Rafael said.

It was a safe sentence. He had been building sentences like it since he was nine years old — sentences with no walls in them, no doors, nothing a man could grip. *That's what they tell me.* *I wouldn't know.* *I was young.* They were the mortar of his silence, and he laid another brick now without thinking, smooth and gray and load-bearing.

But she wasn't listening to the sentence. She was reading her ledger, and her brow had done something small and technical — a furrow, quickly smoothed, the way a clerk corrects an entry she's decided not to make.

"The father's death is recorded here as the twelfth of March, 1889," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you witnessed the drowning."

"I was there." Seven years old, on the sorting dock, close enough to see the boat come back light. Close enough to hear the foreman say *rope parted* in a voice that didn't sound like a man reporting an accident but like a man reciting something he'd rehearsed. Close enough to remember, forever, that the sea was flat as poured metal that day, that no rope parts in a sea like that without help, that he had thought these things at seven and been taught by the following seven years never to think them aloud.

"The twelfth of March, 1889," she said again. "You were present."

"Yes, ma'am."

She turned the ledger toward him — not all the way, just tilted, the way you tilt something to show a child rather than a colleague. "Read me the entry beside your father's name."

He could have refused. He should have refused; every instinct he owned said refuse, defer to Pell, defer to the company, let the gray sentences do their work. But her eyes were brown and unhurried and entirely without cruelty, and Rafael was sixteen and tired in a way that sleep doesn't touch, and he looked.

Beside *Montrose, Tomás, diver*, in the cramped official hand, was a date. The twelfth of March, **1887**.

Two years early.

His body knew before his mind did. His body went cold in the specific way it went cold underwater — a pressure change, a depth he hadn't chosen to descend to. Because he had stood on that dock in 1889. He had watched the boat. He had carried the memory of that morning like a stone in his chest for seven years, had built his whole careful architecture of silence around the weight of it, and here was a government ledger saying his father had died when Rafael was nine, two years and a hundred small humiliations before the rope parted on a flat sea.

"That's wrong," he heard himself say.

The courtyard went very quiet. Even the gulls seemed to bank away.

"What's wrong?" the census taker asked, mild as milk.

"The date." His voice came out steady, which surprised him, and steady was dangerous, because steady invited questions. "My father died in '89. I was seven. I was—" *On the dock. I saw it. I have counted every morning since against that morning.* "I was there."

"Mm." She drew the ledger back and studied it with the expression of a woman examining a coin she suspected was shaved. "Entries in this register are copied from company records supplied to the census office. If the company's record says 1887, the census record says 1887."

"But my father died in '89."

"Then either your memory is mistaken, or the company's records are." She said it lightly, the way you'd discuss the weather, and Rafael understood suddenly that the lightness was armor too — hers, not his. That this woman with ink on both hands had learned to say dangerous things in a voice that couldn't be quoted. "Which is it, Rafael Montrose?"

*Say the gray sentence,* everything in him screamed. *Say I was young, say I wouldn't know, say that's what they tell me.*

"I was there," he said.

Mr. Pell made a sound like a man swallowing a fishbone.

The census taker looked at Rafael for a long moment. Not at the dormitory, not at Pell, not at the free-born clerks now openly pressed against the windows of the counting-house like moths against glass. At him. And in that look Rafael felt something he had not felt since his father's boat came back light: the sensation of being *seen* — not the indentured boy, not the diver's orphan, not the quiet one who mends nets and asks nothing — but Rafael Montrose, whoever that was, whatever was left of him under seven years of silence.

"How many men drowned in Verrone service in 1889?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"Guess."

"One," he said. "One that mattered."

Pell stepped forward, finally, unable to bear the shape of the conversation any longer. "Madam, the boys have duties. The census can be completed through the office—"

"The census can be completed," she said, closing her ledger with a snap that ended the sentence like a door, "when every person has been enumerated. We are nearly done." She made one final note, and Rafael craned despite himself and caught only the tail of it — a question mark, drawn twice, heavy.

"Thank you for your time," she said, to Rafael, and then, collecting her satchel, to the courtyard at large: "Enumeration continues tomorrow. Same hour. Anyone absent will be recorded as absent *by choice*, and that goes in the book too."

She walked out through the gate without waiting for Pell to escort her, which was its own small revolution, and the courtyard breathed again all at once, like a held wave releasing.

* * *

Pell found him that evening in the net shed, which was where Rafael went when he needed to think, because nets forgive silence and even encourage it.

"You want to explain to me," Pell said, leaning in the doorway, "what that was today."

Rafael kept his eyes on the mesh. "She asked. I answered."

"She asked if your father died in '87 or '89, and you told her the government its business."

"I told her what happened."

"What happened," Pell repeated. He came into the shed, and Rafael smelled the rum on him, the evening ration Pell allowed himself from the company stores and paid for, everyone understood, in ways that were never written down. "Boy. Do you know what happens to a record that disagrees with the company's record?"

"No, sir."

"It stops being a record." Pell crouched, bringing his face into the lamplight, and for once there was no sneer in it, which was somehow worse. There was something almost like pity, and pity from Pell was the most frightening thing Rafael had seen all day. "Your father was a good diver. Best on the banks. And when he drowned, the company did right by you — fed you, housed you, taught you letters. You owe the company a clean tongue, Rafael. Whatever that woman is fishing for, you were not there when anything happened, you don't remember anything clearly, you were a *child*. Can you say that?"

"I was seven."

"You were a *child*," Pell said, harder now. "Tomorrow, if she comes, you'll say it. Or I'll write it for you, and you'll sign it, and you'll discover that my handwriting in that book is worth more than your memory." He stood, joints cracking, and paused at the door. "Seven years I've had no trouble from you. Don't start having trouble now, when the whole city's about to fill up with historians and magistrates and God knows what, all of them wanting everything tidy."

He left. The lamp guttered. Rafael sat among the nets with his hands still moving through the repair work, knot after knot, and thought about what Pell had actually said, underneath the threat, underneath the rum.

*Don't start having trouble now.*

Which meant Pell expected trouble. Which meant Pell knew — or guessed, or feared — that something in the company's records was not tidy, and that a census taker asking about dates was how untidiness got out. Seven years, Rafael had believed his silence was his own invention, his own shelter, his own cowardice maybe, but *his*. Now he understood it was also maintenance. He was not keeping quiet the way a man keeps quiet. He was keeping quiet the way a man keeps a wall patched — because someone, somewhere, had built the wall to hold something in.

1887. Two years early. A debt erased two years early was a debt never owed. And compensation for a drowned diver was —

He stopped himself. This was the edge, and he knew the edge when he felt it. Follow that thread and you were no longer a boy remembering his father; you were a problem, and problems got corrected the way records got corrected.

But the number wouldn't stop. Compensation for a drowned diver, in 1891 rates, was four hundred pesos and freedom papers for the child. Four hundred pesos and papers. He'd have been free at eleven. Instead he was sixteen and indentured and standing in a net shed being told his own memory was worth less than a foreman's pen.

Rafael set down the net. He crossed the shed to where he kept his few things — a spare shirt, a bar of soap worn thin, his father's diving knife wrapped in oilcloth that he was legally required to have surrendered years ago and had not. From beneath the shirt he took a stub of pencil and the back of a pay chit, blank on its reverse.

And there, in the smallest handwriting he could manage, he wrote:

*T. Montrose, d. 12 March 1889. Witnessed. Ledger says 1887.*

He looked at it a long time. Sixteen words. Enough to hang him if found, meaningless to anyone else, useless even to him unless—

Unless there were others. Unless Marta's father, drowned in '91, sat in some ledger dead in '89. Unless the twins' father, unless Elio's. Unless the pattern ran through the company's books like a seam of rotten pearl, beautiful on the surface, hollow all the way through.

He folded the chit small and sewed it into the hem of his spare shirt, three stitches, the kind that look like wear. Then he picked the net back up, because a boy sitting idle in the net shed after dark was itself a kind of record, and knotted until the lamp died.

Outside, the harbor lay black and flat as poured metal, the same sea that had killed his father lying to the world about how gentle it was. Tomorrow the census taker would return at the same hour. Tomorrow Pell would be standing there with his prepared statement and his pen.

Rafael went to bed not knowing which sentence he would say — the gray one, the safe one, the one that had kept him alive for seven years — or the true one, the one that had slipped out of him today like water finding a crack.

He only knew, falling asleep, that the crack was there now. And water, his father could have told him, always finds the crack.
