## 4. Hands That Remember

The overseer's office smelled of ink and tallow and the particular sourness of a man who had been drinking since noon but wanted you to believe he hadn't.

"Sit down, Montrose."

Rafael sat. The chair was too low for the desk, a child's chair in a grown man's room, and he suspected it was deliberate — everything in the Verrone dormitory was deliberate, from the height of the bunks to the width of the doorways to the way the lamplight fell short of the corners where a boy might hide something. Nothing here happened by accident. That was the first lesson the company taught, before reading, before figures, before diving: *nothing happens by accident.*

Overseer Pell spread a sheet of paper on the desk and turned it so Rafael could read it upside down, which took him a moment longer than it would have taken right-side up, and Rafael understood that this too was deliberate. A small humiliation to open with. Set the terms early.

"A census officer came through yesterday," Pell said. "Asking questions. You spoke with her."

"Yes, sir."

"She says you couldn't confirm your father's date of death." Pell's finger came down on the paper like a gull taking a fish. "That's an embarrassment. To the company, and to you. So we've prepared a statement. Clean, correct, witnessed. You'll sign it, and the matter closes."

Rafael read. He read slowly, the way he read everything now, because slowness looked like dullness and dullness looked safe. The statement gave his name, his indenture number, his father's name — *Emilio Montrose, diver, entered company service 1881* — and then the line that mattered: *died in company service on the fourteenth of March, 1887, debts of service discharged against wages earned, no compensation due.*

Fourteenth of March, 1887.

His father had drowned in November of 1889. Rafael knew this the way he knew his own hands: not as information but as fact of the body. He had been there. He had been eleven years old, standing on the staging platform at the north anchorage with the other divers' children, watching the signal rope go slack and then go still, and he remembered — he would always remember — that the water that day had been the color of pewter, and that the wind had come off the sea flat and cold, and that when the men finally hauled up the basket instead of his father, Foreman Grebe had turned away from the rail and said, in a voice pitched not for the family but for the tally-clerk beside him, "Put it down as spring. The old entry. Nobody checks the old entries."

Rafael had been eleven. He had not understood what the words meant. He understood them now, sitting in a too-low chair under insufficient light, looking at a piece of paper that said March 1887 in a clerk's careful hand.

"You're hesitating," Pell said.

"No, sir."

"You are. I can see it in your face. Your mother was the same — a face like a shut window." Pell leaned back. "Let me explain something to you, boy, since nobody else will bother. The census woman is a clerk of the colonial office. She writes things down because writing things down is her trade. But what she writes goes into files, and files get read by people who don't love us. If she records that an indentured diver's son doesn't know when his own father died, do you know what that means? It means questions. It means someone comes back. It means the historians over at the annexation commission hear a rumor of disorder, and rumors have a way of getting attached to names." He tapped the paper again. "Sign it, and there's no rumor. There's one clean record, and you're just another orphan the company raised."

*Just another orphan.* Rafael thought: I am sixteen years old and I have been an orphan for five years and in all that time no one has ever once said my father's name aloud to me except to spell it wrong.

"The date is wrong," he said. It came out smaller than he intended.

Pell's face did something complicated — surprise, then recalculation, then the smooth settling of a man returning to familiar ground. "The date," he repeated. "You'd know better than the company ledger?"

"I was there."

"You were eleven."

"I remember."

Pell studied him for a long moment, and Rafael watched him do the arithmetic behind his eyes: how much trouble could one diver's brat be worth, weighed against how much easier everything becomes if he signs. Rafael had spent his whole life being weighed like that. Baskets of oyster shell were weighed the same way, on the same principle — what's inside is worth only what the buyer agrees it's worth.

"Memory's a treacherous thing," Pell said finally, almost gently, and the gentleness frightened Rafael more than the shouting would have. "Grief plays tricks. A boy loses his father, he wants the day to be important, he moves it around in his head. Winter, spring — who among us keeps those straight?" He slid the pen across the desk. "Sign the clean version. For your own sake. And Rafael—" the use of his given name landed like a hand on the back of the neck, "—if the census woman comes asking again, you'll tell her you misremembered, and that the company kindly corrected you. Won't you."

It was not a question. Questions, in this building, were things asked by people who already owned the answer.

Rafael picked up the pen. His hand was steady — he made it be steady; steadiness was a skill like net-mending, learned through repetition, through failure, through doing it again until the fingers obeyed without consulting the heart. He signed *Rafael Emilio Montrose* beneath the false words, in the small neat script that free-born clerks sneered at for being too careful, too schoolroom, too much like a boy who had been taught that his handwriting was the only property he would ever hold.

"There," Pell said, taking the paper. "Was that so hard?"

"No, sir."

"Back to work."

Rafael went back to work.

* * *

He mended nets until the light failed, sitting on the dock with the tar-pot warming between his knees, and all the while his hands moved and his mind moved somewhere else entirely, back along five years to a November afternoon and a pewter-colored sea.

They had gone out at first light, the north-anchorage crew, twelve divers and two tenders and Grebe riding the stern like a figurehead carved by someone with a grudge. His father had eaten nothing that morning — Rafael remembered that too, remembered pressing half a bread roll into his father's hand on the staging platform and his father smiling and putting it in his pocket instead of his mouth. *For you, after.* Divers' superstitions ran thick as the weed on the pilings: no salt bread before a descent, no whistling, no speaking your own name over open water. His father had laughed at most of them and kept every one, the way practical men keep religion — not because they believe but because the cost of believing is small and the cost of not believing, if you're wrong, is everything.

The third dive of the day, the wind shifted. Rafael remembered the shift itself, the sudden flattening smell of weather coming, because the tenders had muttered about it and Grebe had said, "Dive while the light holds," and the tenders had looked at each other and said nothing, because saying nothing was also a superstition, the oldest one, the company's own.

His father went down on the fourth pass. Rafael had watched the rope pay out — he always counted, a private arithmetic, the turns of the drum like beads on a string — and the count had gone long. Longer than long. The tenders felt it too; he saw their shoulders change. Then the rope stopped paying out altogether, and hung, and the sea held its breath around it, and then the rope went slack in the way that ropes never go slack unless the thing they are tied to has let go of the world.

What happened next had happened many times before on that anchorage and would happen many times after: the hauling, the calling, the basket coming up heavy with shell and empty of man, the widow-to-be arriving too late or being told too late. What was different — what Rafael had carried for five years without knowing its weight until a census woman frowned at her ledger — was Grebe at the rail, turning away before the basket even cleared the water, saying to the tally-clerk in that flat administrative voice: *Put it down as spring. The old entry.*

And the tally-clerk — Tomás, people called him, a soft-bellied man who chewed cloves — had written something in his book without arguing, and that absence of argument had frightened Rafael even then, at eleven, though he couldn't have said why. Now he could. Men argue when they believe they can win. The tally-clerk had not argued because he already knew the shape of the record, the way a man knows the shape of a room in the dark. The entry existed. It had existed since March of 1887 — two years before his father died. Someone had written his father's death into the book before his father ever drowned.

Which meant the drowning was expected. Which meant — Rafael's mind circled this the way a gull circles offal, unable to leave it alone — which meant either the ledger was wrong in advance, which was sloth, or the ledger was right in advance, which was something else entirely. Something with a purpose. Because a diver recorded dead in 1887 owed the company nothing past 1887, and the company owed the diver nothing either — no pension to the widow, no discharge of the indenture debt, no compensation, nothing. A man erased two years early was a man whose death cost the company exactly what it had cost in 1887: a line in a book, and no more.

How many lines like it were in that book?

Rafael laid down the netting needle and looked at his hands. They were scarred and tar-stained and clever, and they had learned every trick the harbor taught: how to hold a knife so it wouldn't slip, how to knot wet hemp in the dark, how to write small enough to fit a whole life into a margin. They remembered things his mind tried to make him forget. Hands that remember. His father used to say it — *the head forgets, boy, but the hands keep faith* — usually about knots, usually with laughter in it.

Well. His hands would keep faith now.

* * *

After the evening meal — barley and salt fish, eaten standing, because sitting invited conversation and conversation invited attention — Rafael climbed to his bunk in the corner of the dormitory's third tier, the one with the broken slat he had learned to sleep around, and waited. The dormitory lamps went out at nine. The overseer's boots made their rounds at ten. After the boots, silence, except for the breathing of forty boys and young men and the creak of the building holding them, and the far-off sound of the harbor working all night as harbors do, ropes and hawsers and water worrying at stone.

In the dark, Rafael did what he had begun doing three nights ago, and which he had not yet admitted even to himself was a plan rather than a habit.

He reconstructed ledgers.

He had worked the counting-house copy-room for two years — hauling baskets by day was only half his indenture; the company liked its diver-boys literate because literate labor cost less than two kinds of hired help. In the copy-room he had copied, fair and blind, hundreds of pages he was not permitted to read. But hands remember. His hands remembered the weight of each volume, the color of its binding, the wear on its spine, the year stamped in gilt or stamped not at all. His hands remembered where in the cabinet each volume lived — second shelf, left bay, behind the shipping manifests, because the compensation registers were shelved among the manifests the way contraband is shelved among cargo, hidden by being ordinary.

So now, in the dark, he built the cabinet in his mind. Bay by bay, shelf by shelf. And into the shelves he set what memory gave him:

The 1887 register, blue binding, spine cracked at the third gathering. Compensation entries clustered at the front, before the page-numbering changed format in the autumn — he knew this because he had copied forty consecutive pages from that section during an inventory week and the folio numbers had run strange, jumping style mid-volume. Entries before the jump: low numbers, double digits mostly. Deaths recorded January through June of '87 — he could not recall how many, but more than there should have been; he remembered thinking, even at fourteen, copying without comprehension, that the front of that book was crowded for a season when the fleet had barely put out.

The 1888 register, grey, thinner, shelved directly above. Fewer deaths. Or fewer deaths *recorded* — and these were different things, and he was only now learning they were different things.

And the 1889 register, brown leather, the current decade's last full volume, which should have held his father's true death entry and which he had never once been allowed to touch, because the brown volumes went to the strongroom at year's end and did not come out again except under foreman's key.

His father's entry would be there. Or it would not be there. Either answer meant something, and both answers were locked behind a door Rafael had no right to approach.

Unless.

He turned onto his side, carefully, so the broken slat wouldn't speak. Unless the record could be caught in the act of lying — unless someone stood in front of the magistrates with the 1887 register open and the parish burial rolls open beside it and showed that the book said March where the ground said November. Parish rolls. Burial lists. Gravestones. His father was in the hill-village ground above the harbor with a wooden marker and a date cut by the village carver, a date that no one had thought to falsify because no one imagined a dead man's son would ever be allowed near a comparison.

Paper lied. Wood and stone, so far, told the truth.

He needed the page numbers. Not the volumes — he couldn't lift a volume out of the counting-house without the theft being known by nightfall — but the numbers. A list of folio references, memorized, carried nowhere, existing only in the place no one could search: his head. If it ever came to a reckoning — if there was ever a magistrate, a commission, a moment when the company's books could be compelled to open — he could walk in with nothing in his pockets and say: *second bay, third shelf, blue register, folios thirty-one through forty-four, compare against the parish rolls of San Amaro village,* and the truth would be found waiting for him, exactly where his hands remembered it.

Folio numbers were not theft. Folio numbers were not even evidence. They were the memory of where evidence slept.

Rafael lay in the dark and recited silently, the way he had once recited catechism, the way his father had once recited dive-counts against the drum-turns: *blue, '87, folios thirty-one through forty-four, front gathering, before the numbering changes.* Then the grey, '88, whatever fragments he had: *fourteen entries, spring cluster, folios six through nineteen, shelved above the blue.* Then the gap where the brown should be, and the strongroom door behind the gap, and behind the door his father's real death, filed or unfiled, avenged or unavenged.

Below him, one of the twins coughed in his sleep. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's bell counted the middle watch. Rafael pressed his palms together in the dark — not praying, though it might have looked like praying to anyone foolish enough to be watching, and no one was — just pressing them together, feeling the calluses meet, the tar-lines cross the skin like the lines of a page.

*The head forgets,* his father used to say. *But the hands keep faith.*

Three days ago he had signed a paper that called his father's death a lie, because refusing had seemed impossible and surviving had seemed the point. Tonight, in the dark, with a list growing silently behind his eyes — a list that would lengthen every copy-day, every shelving-shift, every stolen glance at a spine or a stamp — Rafael understood that survival and silence had quietly come apart in his hands, the way a worn rope parts, almost without sound.

He did not know yet what he would do with the list. He only knew he would finish it.

And he did not know — could not know, lying in the dark while the harbor worked and the company slept — that the census woman was at that very hour sitting under her own lamp across the city, frowning at a retired clerk's pension file, at a single penciled line that mentioned a letter, and a foreman named Grebe, and a confession that had never been filed anywhere at all.
