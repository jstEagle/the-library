## 5. The Historians Arrive

They came on a Tuesday, in the gray hour before the market bells, when the harbor fog still lay over the water like a sheet drawn up to the city's chin.

Rafael was carrying oyster baskets up from the third skiff when he heard the wagon. Not the usual rattle of a fish cart or the groan of a pearl-laden dray — this was heavier, deliberate, the sound of something important being moved slowly so that everyone would know it was important. He set his basket down on the quay and watched it come.

Two wagons, actually. The first carried men in good coats — three of them, sitting upright like judges, their luggage strapped behind them in leather cases too fine for this part of the harbor. The second carried crates. Dozens of them, stacked and roped, each one stenciled in fresh black paint: *VTC ARCHIVE — DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION.*

The wagons turned off the quay road and rolled through the gates of the Verrone compound, and a boy ran ahead of them shouting to the gatekeepers, and the gatekeepers were already standing straighter than Rafael had ever seen them stand.

"Who are they?" asked Elio, coming up beside him with a coil of netting over his shoulder.

"Nobody," Rafael said.

"They're somebody. Look at old Ferran bowing."

Ferran the gatekeeper was bowing. In eleven years of indenture, Rafael had seen Ferran bow exactly once, and that had been to the colonial magistrate himself, and even then it had been more of a nod. Now he was bent at the waist like a reed, holding the gate open while the first wagon passed into the courtyard beyond.

"Verrone's historians," said a voice behind them. Old Mateo, who mended boats and remembered everything. "Didn't you hear? For the annexation. They're going to write the history of the pearl trade — our trade — and have it printed before the Governor-General arrives. So there'll be a book to put in his hands." He spat, expertly, into the water between the pilings. "A hundred years of us hauling shells out of the dark, and they'll tell it in three months, in a book with gold letters on the spine."

"Three months?" Elio said. "How can anyone write a hundred years in three months?"

Mateo looked at the crates disappearing through the gate. "They don't write it," he said. "They choose it. There's a difference, boy, and the difference is everything."

Rafael said nothing. He picked up his basket and carried it up the ramp, but he felt the weight of the morning settle onto his shoulders and stay there, like wet rope.

Because he had seen the stencils on those crates. He had read them, quickly, the way an indentured clerk reads anything official — fast, hungry, memorizing without seeming to look. And two of the crates near the back, half-hidden under tarpaulin, had been marked differently. Older paint, faded. One had read *COMPENSATION 1887–1891*.

The year his father died.

* * *

By noon the whole city was talking about it.

Adelina Cruz heard it first in the census office, where her supervisor, Mr. Oviedo, called her into his office with unusual ceremony — a pot of real coffee, imported, which meant either trouble or flattery.

"The company is commissioning an official history," Oviedo said, pouring for both of them though she hadn't asked. "For the annexation celebrations. The Governor-General's office has endorsed it. Which means, Cruz, that every document in that building is about to become very significant."

"That sounds like a good thing," Adelina said carefully. "Records being taken seriously."

"It means," Oviedo said, setting down the pot with a click, "that if any of your census returns contradict what the historians are about to publish, yours will be the returns found to be in error. Do you understand me? A commissioned history is not a question. It is an answer. It arrives already finished, and the rest of us are expected to arrange ourselves around it."

He looked at her over the rim of his cup, and Adelina understood that this was not a general warning. This was a warning aimed at her, at the three diver families sitting in her working file like splinters under skin.

"I take accurate returns," she said. "That's all I've ever done."

"Mm." Oviedo drank his coffee. "There's a man named Wren among the historians. Elias Wren. Second chair, they say, but sharp. He'll be liaising with the municipal offices — including ours. When he comes, you will give him exactly what he asks for and nothing else. No commentary. No observations. No—" he waved his hand vaguely— "*theories*."

"I don't have theories," Adelina said. "I have arithmetic."

Oviedo smiled the thin smile of a man who has survived twenty years in a colonial office by knowing which storms not to stand in. "Then keep your arithmetic to yourself, Señora Cruz, and we will both get through the annexation with our posts intact."

She walked back to her desk past the window that overlooked the harbor, and she saw the Verrone compound below, its flags newly washed, and she thought: *they are going to bury it.* Whatever the ledgers held — whatever she'd found in those three identical discrepancies — within three months it would be sealed inside an official volume, stamped and bound and blessed by the Governor-General, and no clerk's arithmetic would ever dig it out again.

Unless someone dug first.

She sat down at her desk and pulled the pension file toward her — the retired clerk, Tomás Iriarte, whose file she still hadn't returned to the archive because some instinct told her not to let go of it yet. She smoothed the page flat and read the marginal note again, the one written in a dead man's hand:

*Letter of account — Grebe — never filed. See strongroom.*

Three months, Oviedo had said. She looked out the window again, at the compound, at the crates going in.

Less than that now.

* * *

In the counting-house, Aurelio Bass stood at the window of his office and watched the historians' crates being carried into the archive wing, and permitted himself, for the first time in weeks, the luxury of satisfaction.

It had been a difficult season. The census girl's questions had been handled — Oviedo assured him the matter would die quietly — but Bass trusted quiet matters the way he trusted weather. A thing suppressed could always resurface; a thing *absorbed* was gone forever. And annexation offered absorption on a scale the company had never before enjoyed.

"Herr Wren," he said, turning from the window. "You wanted to see the ledgers personally?"

Elias Wren stood in the middle of the office looking faintly uncomfortable in his new commission coat, a lean man of perhaps forty with ink-stained fingers and the wary eyes of someone who had spent his life in other people's archives. "Dr. Halvard prefers to delegate the primary sources," Wren said. "I'm to catalog and abstract. Under supervision, of course."

"Of course." Bass gestured toward the door, and a clerk opened it. "Then let us begin as we mean to continue. With generosity. You may consider the entire company archive open to your commission — every ledger, every correspondence file, every shipping manifest from the founding forward."

Wren blinked. "That's… unusually open. Most firms curate."

"We have nothing to hide, Herr Wren. We have a hundred years of honest trade and a city that owes its prosperity to the pearl divers who made it possible. It's time someone wrote that down properly." Bass smiled his best smile, the one that had closed a thousand negotiations. "History has been unkind to companies like ours. Written by pamphleteers, by agitators, by men who never balanced a ledger in their lives. We intend to correct the record. That is all."

"That is all," Wren repeated, in a tone Bass couldn't quite read.

Bass decided not to try. He walked the historian down through the archive wing himself, past the shelving rows, past the iron door of the strongroom, pausing just long enough to rest his hand on the cool metal — a small theatrical gesture, ownership made visible.

"The strongroom holds the oldest materials," he said. "Founding documents, early compensation records, the like. Access by escort only, I'm afraid. Paper that old crumbles if you sneeze at it wrong."

"Naturally," Wren said. His eyes moved across the door — the lock, the hinges, the little brass plate with its worn number — and then away, politely, the way a well-trained man looks away from things that are none of his business.

Bass noted the speed of that looking-away, and filed it.

Afterward, alone, he wrote a memorandum to the dormitory overseer: *Send up the Montrose boy. The diver's son. Good with figures. The commission will need clerks.*

It was, he reflected, a tidy arrangement. Keep the boy close. Give him work that filled his hands. A young man copying historical documents twelve hours a day had no time to go digging through living ones — and if any inconvenient curiosity did stir in him, it would stir where Bass could see it.

He blotted the memo and thought of the boy's father, drowned these seven years in company water, and felt nothing at all, which was the most reliable feeling a manager could own.

* * *

The summons came to Rafael that evening, delivered by the overseer with a smirk that Rafael had learned to read across a dormitory room: *something good for you, which means something bad for someone.*

"You're wanted upstairs, clerk. Counting-house. Tomorrow, six sharp. Wash your neck." The overseer paused at the door, enjoying it. "They say you're getting promoted. Out of the baskets. Into the archive." He laughed. "Imagine that. A diver's brat handling paper."

Rafael kept his face empty, the way he'd trained it since he was nine years old. Empty faces got left alone.

But inside the dormitory, after lights-out, lying in his bunk listening to the breathing of thirty other indentured boys, Rafael did not feel promoted. He felt *moved*. A piece on a board, shifted closer to something.

He turned the day over in his mind, piece by piece, the way he turned an oyster in his hands before opening it.

Historians. Commissioned by Verrone. Arriving with crates — including crates from the old compensation files. Given full access to the archive, the counting-house manager had announced it publicly, proudly. Full access, except the strongroom, which required escort, which was where the 1889 papers lived.

And now he was being invited inside. Handed a position at the very edge of the truth he needed, with escorts and supervisors watching, with Bass's smile somewhere overhead like a lamp hung too low.

It could be a trap. He knew it could be a trap. Men like Bass did not promote diver's sons out of generosity; they promoted them the way fishermen weighted a line — to sink something quietly, far from view.

Or it could be the only door that would ever open.

Rafael lay in the dark and did the thing he had done every night since the census woman asked her impossible question: he recited, silently, the numbers he had memorized. Ledger volume nineteen, compensation register, 1889. Page forty-one. Page forty-two. The foreman Grebe's initials in the margin column. His father's name spelled wrong — *Montros*, missing the final e, as though the ledger itself couldn't be bothered to hold the whole of him.

Page forty-three, where the entry simply ended, mid-column, as if someone had torn the account short.

He recited them until the numbers wore smooth, until sleep came.

Outside the dormitory window, the compound lamps burned late. He could see the glow of them through the shutter slats, and the shadows of men moving — the historians, working already, unpacking their crates, choosing what a hundred years had been.

Tomorrow he would walk through the gate into the middle of it, and bow to the right people, and keep his face empty, and watch everything.

The truth was going into boxes. But boxes, Rafael thought, closing his eyes, could be opened from the inside — if you were small enough, patient enough, and already in the room.
