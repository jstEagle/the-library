## 6. A Letter Mentioned Once

The pension files lived in the bottom drawer of a cabinet nobody opened, in the annex of the census office that smelled of mildew and old ink and the particular sweetness of paper slowly giving up. Adelina Cruz had been sent there on her third day back from the dormitories — not as punishment, her supervisor had said, smoothing his mustache, but as a matter of proper sequence. Verification work required cross-referencing. Cross-referencing required the archives. The archives were downstairs.

She understood what he was telling her. She was being filed away herself.

Fine. Let them. The bottom drawers were where the truth went to be forgotten, which meant they were also where it went to wait.

She worked through them methodically: clerks retired before 1885, clerks discharged for cause, clerks dead. Each folder was thin. A company that underwrote the census office did not keep generous records of its castoffs. She read names she'd never heard, salaries she could barely imagine, reasons for separation rendered in the bland officialese of men who had never once written an uncomfortable sentence: *services no longer required. Position eliminated. Conduct unsatisfactory.*

It was the fourth week, on a Tuesday when rain hammered the shutters so hard she had to light a lamp at noon, that she pulled the file of Tomás Iriarte.

She almost put it back. The folder was the thinnest yet — three sheets, one of them blank but for a coffee ring. Discharged clerk, Counting-House Division, year of service ending 1891. No reason given at all, which was itself unusual; even *conduct unsatisfactory* was a reason. A man who left no reason behind had been erased deliberately, and Adelina had learned enough in six weeks to know that deliberate erasure was the most interesting category of document there was.

The second sheet was his pension calculation. Canceled. Stamped across it in red: *FORFEIT.*

The third sheet was a memorandum, dated March 1891, initialed by someone in the comptroller's office whose initials meant nothing to her. It concerned the disposition of certain papers upon Iriarte's departure. Inventory, standard language, item by item. And then, near the bottom, in the dry voice of a man listing desk furniture:

*—one (1) letter of account, foreman S. Grebe to comptroller, ref. diver compensations 1889, UNFILED. Retained by office. Not to be released to departing staff or entered into general record.*

Adelina read it four times.

*A letter of account.* In the counting-house vocabulary she had absorbed from her father — twenty years a customs assessor before the fever took him, and a man who had taught his daughter to read ledgers before she could read stories — a letter of account was a formal confession of discrepancy. A document in which an officer of a firm set down, in his own hand, that the books and the truth had parted ways. They were rare. They were almost always destroyed. And they were, above all things, *filed* — because the entire legal value of a letter of account was its existence on record. An unfiled letter of account was not an accounting instrument. It was a time bomb wrapped in courtesy.

Foreman S. Grebe. She turned back through her own notes, the ones she kept now in cipher in the margins of a book of tide tables, because she no longer trusted the census office with her handwriting. Grebe. Silas Grebe, foreman of the diving fleets, 1884 to 1892. The name appeared in three of her four discrepant families. Montrose — death recorded 1887, drowning witnessed 1889. Okonkwo — recorded 1886, buried 1888. The Ferreira twins' father — recorded twice, once alive and once dead in the same season, a clerical impossibility nobody had ever bothered to fix.

And here was Grebe's own hand — or at least the memory of his own hand, preserved in an inventory memo — admitting that the diver compensation accounts of 1889 were false. Unfiled. Retained. *Not to be released.*

Someone in the comptroller's office in 1891 had known. Had held the proof in a drawer, exactly as Adelina was holding this memo in a drawer, and had chosen the drawer.

Her first feeling was not triumph. It was a cold, crawling anger, the kind that starts in the hands. Somewhere in that building across the harbor — or in whatever strongroom Verrone kept, or in whatever crate the historians would seal and shelve and eventually pulp — that letter might still exist. Or it might have been burned the week after it was retained. There was no way to know without asking questions that would end her career faster than the discrepancies already had.

But there was another path, and it was standing in a dormitory corridor with salt-cracked hands and a silence around him like a coat worn too long.

She closed the drawer. She locked it. Then she sat for a long moment with the rain loud against the glass, doing the arithmetic of risk the way she did all arithmetic: honestly.

If she pursued this through the office, she would be stopped. Her supervisor took Verrone money; she'd seen the arrangement in the budget lines, dressed up as a printing contract. If she pursued it outside the office, she was no longer a census taker working a discrepancy. She was a private citizen conspiring against a chartered company, and everything she touched afterward would carry the stain of illegitimacy — unless it ended in something public. A sworn statement. A reading on the record. Something a magistrate could not unhear.

That was later. First: the boy. Rafael Montrose. Sixteen years old, eyes like a man deciding whether to trust you with the location of a well in a drought.

She had thought about him every day since the dormitory. Not sentimentally — professionally, mostly, though she was honest enough with herself to admit the categories had begun to blur. What stayed with her was the moment after she'd asked him to confirm his father's death date. Most people, confronted with an error in their own family's record, argued, or laughed, or shrugged. He had gone still. Utterly still, the stillness of a creature that has survived by not moving, and then he had said, *the ledger is probably right,* in a voice that told her he believed the ledger was wrong, and knew it was wrong, and knew that knowing it was wrong was dangerous.

He had witnessed the drowning. He had said so — nine years old, on the jetty, the basket lines going slack. And the ledger said his father died two years earlier, in a different fleet, in waters the man had never worked.

Somebody had killed Emilio Montrose on paper years before the sea killed him in fact. And somebody had done it to void a debt.

Adelina blew out the lamp, put on her oilcloth, and went out into the rain to commit her first act of conspiracy.

* * *

She could not go to the dormitory. Overseer Dax watched the gate, and she had been recognized there; a second visit from the census office would travel up the chain to Bass within the hour, and whatever fragile thing she hoped to build with Rafael Montrose would be crushed under the weight of official attention before it bore anything.

So she did what women of no particular consequence are freely permitted to do in port cities: she went shopping.

The company store near the drying sheds sold rope, tar, lamp oil, and — crucially — let indentured workers queue at the side door on Thursday evenings to draw their chits. Adelina arrived at dusk with a basket on her arm and bought a spool of twine she didn't need, and waited, browsing, while the line shuffled forward. He came out just past seven, collar up against the drizzle, a canvas satchel over his shoulder, walking with the economical gait of someone who has learned that hurrying attracts the eye.

"Señor Montrose."

He stopped the way a deer stops — all at once, weight already shifting toward flight. Then he saw who she was, and something in his face closed like a shutter.

"Señora Cruz." His voice was flat, careful. "I gave my answers already."

"You gave the answers you were allowed to give." She kept her tone level, unhurried, the voice of a woman discussing twine. "I'm not here as the census. I've resigned myself from it, more or less — they've filed me in the basement with the dead clerks. I'm here as someone who reads documents."

"I don't know anything about documents."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You only know your father died in 1889, and the company says 1887, and you've spent seven years pretending not to know the difference. I'd call that knowing quite a lot about documents."

A cart rattled past. Two shed workers glanced their way, looked away. Rafael's gaze tracked them with a precision that told her how much surveillance he lived inside.

"Don't say such things in the street," he said quietly.

"Then walk with me to the breakwater, where the wind eats words, and say nothing there instead."

For a long moment he didn't move. She watched him weigh it — she could nearly see the scales, the fear on one pan and something else on the other, something that had been waiting seven years for someone to hold out a hand. Then he adjusted his satchel strap and walked beside her, three feet away, close enough to converse and far enough to deny.

The breakwater ran out into the dark water like a spine. The wind came off the gulf hard and clean, tearing the rain sideways, and beyond the mole the harbor lights of the Verrone counting-house burned in a neat row — warm, golden, indifferent.

"My father worked that quay," Rafael said suddenly, not looking at her. He nodded at the lights. "Before the diving. He used to carry me on his shoulders and tell me every window had a different man behind it counting the same oyster twice."

"And then he dove for them instead."

"Everyone dives, eventually. Free men dive for wages. Indentured men dive because their fathers dove, and their fathers dove because a debt compounds faster than a boy grows." He paused. "Why are you really here?"

Because she liked him, was part of it, and she set that part aside. Because the discrepancy had hooked into her like a fishhook and she could not stop pulling, was another part. But those were not answers for a frightened sixteen-year-old.

"I found a memo," she said. "In a pension file. A clerk named Tomás Iriarte, discharged in 1891 — no reason given, pension forfeited. In his exit inventory there's an item listed and never filed. A letter of account, written by Foreman Silas Grebe, concerning the diver compensation accounts of 1889. Your father's accounts, Señor Montrose. The letter admits the falsifications. It existed. It may still exist."

He had gone very pale. In the lamplight from the harbor she could see the tendons stand out in his neck. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the wind.

"Grebe." He said the name like something spat from the mouth of the sea. "Grebe was there. That night. My father's boat came in short-handed and Grebe wrote the report himself, and he stood on the jetty with his hat off, looking at the water, and I remember thinking — I was nine, but I remember thinking he looked less like a mourner than a man balancing sums." His jaw worked. "They told us the compensation was voided because the debt was already settled. Settled how? By a dead man? Dead men don't sign chits."

"Dead men don't argue either," Adelina said. "That's the whole point of killing them early. On paper. You record the death in '87, void the compensation obligation in '88, and by '89 the man can drown in front of his own son and there is nothing left to compensate. The sea gets blamed. The ledger stays clean. And a foreman who helped arrange it writes a letter of account because his conscience finally outweighs his salary — and the company puts that letter in a drawer instead of a file, because a filed letter is evidence and a drawer is a tomb."

Rafael turned to look at her fully for the first time. Up close his eyes were lighter than she'd remembered, gray-green, sea-colored, and exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Why?" he said. "Why do you care? You're — you were — the census. You count us. Nobody counts us and then comes back."

The honesty of it stung, because it was true, and because she deserved it. She made herself answer plainly.

"Because my job was to make the record true, and I discovered the record is a weapon pointed at you. Because if I let it lie, then every morning I tie my bootlaces I do it in a city built on a lie small enough to fit in a ledger line and heavy enough to drown forty families. And because—" she hesitated, then gave him the last of it, "—because someone did this to my father too. Not Verrone. The fever. But the customs house lost his final assessments in a fire that conveniently burned only the wing where disputed duties were stored, and my mother spent eleven years writing letters asking where they went, and received eleven years of polite replies. I know what it is to watch paper defeat a family. I swore I'd be on the other side of the desk next time."

The wind filled the pause. Out past the mole a late lugger was coming in, its riding light swinging.

"What do you want from me?" Rafael asked at last. It was not a soft question. It was a test, and she respected it.

"The truth, and nothing you can't afford to give. Names. Dates. What you remember of that night — not for me, but for the record we're going to build. And eventually, perhaps, your signature on a sworn statement, when we have something worth swearing to." She held up a hand before he could speak. "Not today. Not tomorrow. Today I want one thing only: I want to find Tomás Iriarte. He handled the counting-house papers. He saw that letter leave Grebe's hands. He was ruined for it — no reason given, pension forfeited, which means someone wanted him poor and quiet. Ruined men remember everything, Señor Montrose, because memory is the only property they weren't allowed to lose. Do you know where he went?"

Something moved through Rafael's face — recognition, wariness, a door opening onto a room he'd kept locked.

"I know the name," he said slowly. "The old divers talk about him. Tomás Iriarte. They say he keeps above the fishmonger's stall on Salt Lane — the one with the green awning. They say he drinks what the company stole from him and shouts at gulls." He looked at her sidelong. "They also say he asked one question too many about the '89 accounts, and Verrone broke him for it. If you go asking him about letters, señora, you'll be asking the one man in this city with nothing left to fear and nothing left to lose. That makes him either the best ally we could—"

He stopped. She heard it too: the *we*, hanging in the wind between them, spoken before he'd decided to speak it. A conspiracy requires at least two, and he had just enlisted himself.

Rafael stared out at the black water for a long moment, and when he turned back, the careful flatness was gone from his voice. What remained was quieter and far more dangerous.

"That makes him the only witness alive," he said. "Tomorrow, then. Before the sheds open. I'll meet you at the green awning — if I'm late, don't wait in sight of it. Dax counts heads on Thursdays." He shifted his satchel, and added, almost against his will, "My father's name isn't on the shrine wall. Under the harbor shrine, where the drowned divers' names are carved. Everyone's is. His place is blank."

"Then that," Adelina said, "is the first line of the true record. We'll start there and work backward to the letter."

She left him at the landward end of the breakwater, watching until he was swallowed by the dormitory district's narrow streets, and only then did she let herself feel the size of what she'd just done. By morning, if anyone had marked them on the quay, she would be finished. By tomorrow night, if Iriarte sobered long enough to talk, they would know whether the letter still breathed somewhere in the dark — or whether they were two fools chasing ash.

At the mouth of Salt Lane, the green awning sagged in the rain, and from the stair above the fishmonger's stall came the sound of a bottle being set down with terrible deliberation, and a man's voice slurring at the gulls:

"You again. You again. All right — come up, then. Everybody comes up eventually."
