## 7. Old Bones, Old Debts

The fishmonger's stall on Salt Lane opened before dawn and stank by nine. Rafael had passed it a hundred times without once looking up at the window above it — a square of clouded glass with a curtain that never quite met in the middle. He'd known who lived there the way everyone in the harbor district knew: Tomás Iriarte, once a clerk of some standing at Verrone Trading, now a man who bought his wine with whatever coins he could charm from sailors and spent his mornings shouting down through the floorboards about the smell.

Adelina stopped at the stall, lifted her skirts clear of the fish water running in the gutter, and looked up at the crooked curtain.

"He'll not come down for a woman," she said. "And he'll not come down for an official. So we send neither."

She picked up a herring from the display, held it to the light as though judging its quality, and set it down again. The fishwife watched her with the flat patience of someone who had seen every kind of customer.

"Señor Iriarte," Adelina called up toward the window, conversational, not loud. "I have brought a young man who mends nets for Verrone Trading, and between us we know nothing at all about ledgers. Perhaps you'd like to tell us how little you remember."

Silence. A gull screamed somewhere over the roofs. Then the curtain twitched, and a voice came down, roughened by wine and disuse but carrying underneath it the ghost of a counting-house diction — vowels rounded, consonants precise, all of it gone soft at the edges now.

"Nets," the voice said. "You mend nets."

"Yes, señor."

A long pause. "Verrone nets?"

"Every net in the harbor fleet," Rafael said.

Another pause, longer. Then footsteps, uneven, and the door at the top of the outside stair swung open, and Tomás Iriarte stood looking down at them.

Rafael had expected ruin to look a particular way — sodden, slack, a man dissolved. But Iriarte was upright, if unsteady, with a lean face that might once have been handsome and eyes that were too clear for a drunkard's. He wore a shirt that had been fine ten years ago and a waistcoat buttoned wrong. He looked at Rafael the way a man looks at a fish he isn't sure is fresh.

"How old are you?"

"Sixteen."

"Diver's son?"

"Yes, señor."

Something moved behind the clear eyes. "Then you're one of hers," he said, meaning, apparently, the harbor itself, or the sea, or the whole drowned population of it. "Come up, then. Both of you. But mind the third step. It's been loose since the flood year and I've no intention of fixing it."

* * *

The room above the fishmonger's was smaller than it looked from the street, which Rafael would not have thought possible. A bed, a chair, a table bearing a bottle, two cups, and a drift of papers that might once have been organized. On the wall, nailed with more care than anything else in the room, hung a framed certificate: *Tomás Iriarte, Chief Ledger Clerk, Verrone Trading Company*, dated 1881, signed by a manager whose name Rafael recognized from the spines of ledger volumes — Bass's predecessor, Don Emilio Sarría.

Iriarte saw him looking. "Don't," he said. "Whatever you're about to say about it, don't. I keep it to remind myself what I was when they took everything else." He lowered himself into the chair with the care of a man managing pain, and waved at the bed's edge. "Sit. You too, census woman. And don't tell me you're not here as the census woman. Nobody climbs my stairs for any other reason."

Adelina sat, back straight, hands folded. Rafael perched on the bed's edge, which groaned.

"I found a reference," she said. "In a pension file. Yours. There's a line in it — a note in another hand, undated — that says *re: letter of account, Grebe, never filed*. Do you know what that means?"

Iriarte laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as a cork coming out. "Never filed. Yes. That's one way to put it." He reached for the bottle, poured a finger into each cup, pushed one across the table toward Adelina, and then, after a moment's consideration, a second toward Rafael. "Drink or don't. But hold the cup. A man should have something in his hands when he hears this."

Rafael held the cup. It was warm from the room and smelled of something cheap and strong.

"You want to know about Silas Grebe's letter," Iriarte said. "So let me tell you about Silas Grebe first, because nobody remembers him right. They remember the foreman. The man who stood on the deck with the tally board and shouted the counts. That's true, that's what he was. But Grebe was also the one who wrote the death reports. Every drowning in the fleet went through his hand before it went into the ledger. His figures, his descriptions, his dates. And Grebe drank — not the way I drink, mind, not to forget. He drank because he couldn't sleep. And when a man can't sleep, eventually he talks."

"To you," Adelina said.

"I was chief ledger clerk. Everything came through me. In '89 I noticed — you notice things, after enough years, the numbers start talking to you — I noticed that the compensation accounts balanced too well. Deaths recorded, debts voided, all tidy. Too tidy. Real drownings are messy. Widows dispute. Dates wander. These didn't. So I started pulling the underlying reports, and I found three — no, four — where the date of death in the report didn't match the date in the ledger. Years off. Whole years. And in each case, the correction ran the same direction: earlier. Dead earlier means the indenture debt dies with them, means the company owes nothing." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "I asked Grebe about it. Quietly. Man to man. And Grebe went white as a landed fish and said, *Tomás, I wrote a letter.*"

Rafael's hands had gone still around the cup. "What kind of letter?"

"A letter of account. That's what he called it. A full confession, written out proper, the way a clerk writes — names, dates, entries, page references. Every falsification he'd made under orders, and whose orders. He'd written it years before, after a bad night, and sealed it, and it sat in the strongroom among the permanent files. He told me because he wanted it destroyed and couldn't bring himself to do it. Or maybe he wanted it found and couldn't bring himself to say so. Drunk men are honest in their cowardice." Iriarte drank. "He said there were men in the fleet who weren't dead when the paper said they were. Men who died later, or — worse — men who didn't die at all when the ledger killed them, just got lamed or lost or run off, and the company buried them on paper to close the account."

The room seemed to tilt slightly. Rafael heard his own voice from far away. "My father. Esteban Montrose. Drowned in '89, off the San Cristóbal grounds."

"I know the name." Iriarte said it simply, without cruelty, and somehow that was worse than cruelty. "It was one of the four. The ledger has him dying in '87."

"Two years early."

"Two years early, and the compensation debt voided, and his indenture — your indenture — transferred clean to the company with no obligation attached. That's the arithmetic of it. That's what a boy costs when the paper says his father died before he did."

Adelina leaned forward. "Where is the letter now?"

"In the strongroom." Iriarte's mouth twisted. "Or it was. Sealed file, permanent cabinet, under lock and key and the signature of Don Emilio Sarría himself. Because here's the part you actually climbed my stairs for, so listen. When Grebe told me, I did what a clerk does. I made a memorandum. Respectful. Procedural. Requesting verification of certain death entries against source reports. I routed it up through channels, because I was a fool who believed in channels." He set the cup down with a small, deliberate click. "Within a month I was accused of irregularities in the pension disbursements. Within two, dismissed. Within six, blacklisted at every trading house in the port. Don Emilio never spoke to me again. Not once. Not even to have me removed — he had a junior do it. As if I'd already stopped existing. Which, you understand, is exactly what the company does. It doesn't kill men. It amends them."

The fish smell rose through the floorboards. Somewhere below, the fishwife called a price to a customer, ordinary as weather.

"They ruined you for asking," Adelina said. Not a question.

"For *writing it down*," Iriarte said. "Asking is nothing. Asking is a man muttering in a tavern. But a memorandum enters the record. A memorandum can be found. They couldn't allow a question to live in the files, so they killed the man who asked it — on paper, of course. Dismissed for cause. My cause is still in their ledger too, if you go looking. Somewhere near your father's false death, probably. We're neighbors in there." He looked at Rafael then, really looked, and his voice dropped. "Boy. What do you want with this? Truth? Truth is a luxury good. I used to be able to afford it. Look at me now."

"I want the letter," Rafael said.

"Why?"

Because his father's name wasn't on the shrine wall. Because a foreman had behaved strangely on the night of the drowning, wringing his hands, refusing to meet anyone's eye, ordering the boats back early. Because he had spent seven years being silent and calling it safety. He didn't say any of this. He said:

"Because it's mine. The debt they voided was owed to me."

Iriarte studied him for a long moment, and something in the clear eyes shifted — recognition, maybe, of a younger version of himself, the one who had believed in channels.

"It may not even be there anymore," he said. "That's the price tag on hope, and I'll give it to you straight because everyone else will lie to you. One: Grebe may have destroyed it himself, finally, in one of his bad nights. Two: Sarría may have destroyed it when I raised my memorandum — the first thing a guilty man does is burn his confession. Three: it's been — what, nine years? Files get moved, mislabeled, eaten by damp. Four—" he raised a fourth finger, and his hand was not quite steady, "—and this is the one that matters. Say you find it. Say it's real, signed, damning. What then? Paper against a company that owns the magistrates, underwrites the census office, and is presently paying historians to write its sainthood. You think a letter wins? A letter is a beginning. A beginning that will cost you everything you have, which I can see from here is not much, and cost her—" he nodded at Adelina "—her post, which I'm told is worth having."

"I've been suspended," Adelina said evenly. "As of yesterday. Pending review of inconsistencies in my returns."

Iriarte barked that dry laugh again. "There. You see? They amend people. Fast, too, when they're frightened." He refilled his cup, and his hand steadied as he did it, as if the ritual of pouring restored him. "So. You've had the testimony. Letter existed, sealed in the strongroom, permanent cabinet, 1889 files, Sarría's seal. I was ruined for going through channels. That's the whole of what I know and most of what I am."

"That's not nothing," Rafael said.

"No," Iriarte agreed. "It's less than nothing. Nothing costs nothing." He drank, set down the cup, and looked out the clouded window at a slice of gray harbor. For a while none of them spoke.

Then he said, quietly, almost to himself: "Grebe's dead, you know. Three years back. Buried up in the hill villages somewhere — his widow took the body home to her people. Took his things, too. His diving chest went with her." He turned from the window. "A man like Grebe, who couldn't sleep for what he'd done, who wrote a confession and locked it away instead of burning it — such a man keeps copies. Such a man tells his wife things. If the strongroom copy is gone, the truth didn't die with him. It went up the hill in a chest."

Adelina was already reaching for her notebook. "Which village?"

"Santo Río. Her family's ground. But—" and Iriarte's voice hardened, clerk-precise one last time, "—understand what you're doing. The moment you start pulling these threads in daylight, the company feels it. And the company doesn't ruin only clerks. Ask me how I know. Ask the widow how she knows, if you find her. Everyone who ever touched this letter has paid. That's the price tag. I've shown you mine. Count yours before you climb another stair."

Rafael stood. The bed creaked. He set the untouched cup back on the table.

"My father paid already," he said. "I'm only collecting."

At the door, Adelina paused. "Why tell us about the chest? You could have kept it. Kept us chasing the strongroom."

Iriarte smiled, and for a second the ruined clerk of the framed certificate looked out through the drunkard's face.

"Because I've waited nine years for somebody to ask," he said. "And because if you do pull this thread, I want it remembered — write it down somewhere, census woman — that Tomás Iriarte helped. Even if it comes to nothing. *Especially* if it comes to nothing."

They went down past the loose third step, out into the fish-smelling morning. Behind them, faintly, they heard him moving the cups on the table, arranging them, a clerk tidying his desk.

Adelina tucked the notebook away and looked at Rafael. "Santo Río is a day's walk into the hills. I can't travel as a census officer anymore. I'd be traveling as nothing at all."

"Then we travel as nothing," Rafael said. "It's the safest thing to be."

She almost smiled. And they turned toward the hill road — not knowing that in the counting-house behind them, a manager named Aurelio Bass was that same morning reading a census return with three familiar names on it, and reaching for his pen.
