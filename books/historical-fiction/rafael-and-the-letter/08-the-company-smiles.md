## 8. The Company Smiles

Aurelio Bass had three rules for reading paper, and he had learned all of them in the same year, from the same man — old Don Emilio Verrone himself, who used to say that a ledger never lied but a clerk always might. Rule one: read what is there. Rule two: read what is not there. Rule three: ask who benefits from the difference.

He applied them now, in the grey light of his office above the counting-house floor, to a census return that should have taken him four minutes and was taking considerably longer.

The return covered the harbor district east of the salt warehouses. Ordinary work: names, ages, trades, family groupings, the small bureaucratic archaeology by which a city counted itself for its new masters. Annexation was eleven months away, and the colonial administration wanted clean numbers, and Verrone Trading wanted the administration to want nothing at all, which was nearly the same thing so long as the numbers stayed clean.

Bass's pen hovered over the third page.

*Montrose, Rafael. Age 16. Clerk-diver, indentured to Verrone Trading Company since 1886. Father: Montrose, Esteban, diver. Deceased 1887.*

He read it again. Then he did something he almost never did in the morning, before the second coffee: he reached for another document without finishing the first.

The compensation register for 1887–89 sat in the locked cabinet behind his chair, because Bass kept the sensitive years close, the way some men kept pistols. He found the Montrose entry in under a minute. He knew it would be there; he had signed off on the annual audit of it every year since he'd taken the manager's chair, and an audit you sign without reading is just a signature with ambitions. Still.

*Montrose, Esteban. Diver, first class. Lost at sea, night of 14 October 1889. Compensation owed per Company Schedule D: 240 pesos, payable to dependents or deducted against outstanding indenture debts of minor children.*

Deducted against outstanding indenture debts of minor children. Which meant Rafael's indenture — purchased at seven years old when his father signed him over against a debt of boat hire and advance wages — had been extended, twice, on the strength of a debt that the schedule said should have been *extinguished* by the company's own payment.

Except the payment had never been made. Because the death had been recorded four years early.

Bass set both documents side by side on the green baize and looked at them the way a man looks at two witnesses telling contradictory stories, knowing that one of them is afraid and the other is lying, and that it is usually the calm one who lies.

14 October 1889. He had been in the counting-house then, junior under-manager, still learning which silences in the daily reports were routine and which were expensive. The storm of '89 had taken three divers off the shoal grounds in a single week. It had been a bad season and everyone had agreed not to make it worse by talking about it. Foreman Grebe had handled the filings — Grebe handled all the loss filings, that was his job, and Grebe had been efficient about it in the way of men who want a disagreeable task finished before anyone can look too closely.

Four years early. Not a clerical slip. A slip of four years is not a slip; it is a decision. Someone had written 1887 into a census-facing record, or into whatever record fed the census, and someone else had let it stand, and somewhere downstream a boy's indenture had been quietly extended on a debt that was owed to him rather than by him.

How much was that worth? Bass did the arithmetic without meaning to — it was a reflex, like blinking. Two extensions, compounding service fees, the differential between an indentured clerk-diver's keep and a free clerk's wage across — what, six years? Call it three hundred pesos, call it four. Peanuts. That was what made his jaw tighten. Not the money. The *sloppiness*. If this thread pulled loose, how many others ran off the same spool?

He pulled the census returns for the other two districts where Verrone held indentured labor. It took him twenty minutes and two trips to the records room, and he worked standing up, which he did only when something had gone wrong, though no one in the building would have dared notice.

*Cruz, widow of diver Cruz, Andrés. Deceased 1886.* Register: lost at sea, March 1890.

*Family of Ocampo, Teodoro, diver. Deceased 1885.* Register: lost at sea, September 1889.

Three families. Three deaths recorded years ahead of themselves, three compensation entries closed as already settled, three children whose debts had grown fat on money that should have killed those debts. All filed through the same foreman's desk. All in years when the season had been bad enough that nobody upstairs wanted to count the drowned carefully.

Grebe. Dead now, buried up in the hills, and conveniently unable to explain anything to anyone.

Bass stood at the window with his coffee going cold and looked out over the harbor, at the masts and the pearl sheds and the long stone spine of the quay, and performed the exercise that had made him manager at forty-one: he imagined himself being asked about this by someone hostile, in public, with paper in their hands.

Who could ask? The census office. And who paid the census office's rent? Verrone did, indirectly, through the municipal subvention — a fact so well known that the subvention wasn't even a secret, merely a fact, the way gravity was a fact. The new colonial magistrates? They were hungry and honest in roughly equal measure, which made them dangerous only if you gave them something specific to chew. The historians?

That thought stopped him mid-sip.

The historians arrived Thursday. Three of them, commissioned by the company itself — Wren and his seniors, come to write the official history of the pearl trade for the annexation celebrations, with full access to the archives, a suite of rooms on the second floor, and a budget that had been approved without a single objection from the board. Bass had signed the commission papers himself. It had seemed, at the time, like a masterstroke: better the company write its own history than let the annexation committee write it for them, better to hand the scholars the paper than leave the paper lying around for scholars to find.

Full access. To the archives. Including the compensation registers.

He set down the cup.

There were two ways to play a discovery like this, and he had watched men choose wrong between them his whole career. You could bury it — burn the pages, bribe the census clerk, lose the register in a flood, a fire, a reorganization. But the flood gambit assumed you knew every copy of everything, and you never did; paper bred in the dark like cockroaches. And a census taker who suspected falsification and then found her evidence burned would not go away. She would become a woman with a grievance and nothing to lose, and those were the most expensive people in the world.

Or you could own it. Curate it. Make sure that whatever anyone found, they found it inside walls you controlled, in a frame you had built, at a time you chose.

Bass went back to his desk, took a fresh sheet of the company's heavy cream stationery, and began to write. His hand was steady. His handwriting was famous in the counting-house for its steadiness — round, upright, patient letters, the script of a man who could not be hurried into anything.

*To Messrs. Halloran, Wren & Associates, Historians Commissioned, etc.*

Gentlemen —

*In anticipation of your arrival, I wish to ensure your access to the Company archive is both complete and orderly. The volume of source material is considerable, and portions of it — personnel files, compensation schedules, correspondence touching on individual employees and their families — involve matters of privacy and ongoing commercial sensitivity. To protect both the historical record and the persons named within it, I am appointing an archivist-liaison from among our own staff to curate materials for your review and to answer questions of provenance.*

*I have in mind a young man of exceptional memory and discretion, currently serving in the lower offices. You will find the arrangement, I trust, greatly to your convenience.*

*A. Bass, Manager*

He read it over. *Curate*. The word was perfect. Curation sounded like scholarship. It sounded like care. It meant: nothing leaves this room that I have not already seen, and no question gets answered by anyone I have not already coached.

He blotted the letter, rang for his secretary, and gave two more instructions before eight o'clock struck. First: pull the pension file of Tomás Iriarte, formerly junior clerk, dismissed '91, and put it on Bass's desk unopened — he wanted to know why Iriarte's name had surfaced in the same breath as the census districts, because it had, in a marginal note in the census supervisor's covering letter, a nervous little footnote asking whether former Company employees fell within the enumeration scope. Nervous footnotes were how trouble introduced itself politely.

Second: send word to the dormitory overseer to have the boy Montrose report to the counting-house at noon. Clean shirt. No reason given.

No reason given. That was important. A summons with a reason told a man what to prepare. A summons without one told him to prepare everything, and a frightened boy preparing everything would show Bass exactly which everything he had.

* * *

At half past nine, Bass walked the floor.

It was a habit the clerks had learned to read like weather. When Bass walked the floor slowly, with his hands clasped behind him, all was ordinary. When he stopped at desks, it meant questions were coming. Today he did neither. Today he walked fast, and did not stop, and the sound of his shoes on the boards moved through the room like the pressure drop before a squall, and thirty pens scratched faster for it.

He paused only once, at the tall window overlooking the quay, where he could see the harbor shrine at the end of the breakwater — a squat stone niche where the families of lost divers left oil lamps and paper flowers, and where the company, in a gesture of generosity dating back to Don Emilio's day, had offered to carve the names of the drowned. Some names had gone up. Bass had approved the list himself, years ago. He remembered signing it. He did not remember checking it.

Had Montrose senior been on it? He genuinely did not know. That was the thing about delegating mercy — you forgot whom you'd failed to extend it to, while the people below you counted every name on the wall every single day.

Below. That word again. Everything in this business was below or above; the whole port was organized around the idea that some men breathed water and others breathed air and the ledgers decided which was which. Bass had risen by understanding the ledgers. He intended to continue rising by the same method, and the first law of ledgers, older than Don Emilio's three rules, was this: a debt you can hide is an asset, but a debt someone else has found is a wound, and wounds are managed, never ignored.

At eleven, his secretary reported. "The pension file, sir. And the overseer says the boy will be here."

"Anyone ask why?"

"The overseer asked. I told him the manager doesn't owe the dormitory explanations." The secretary allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "He liked that even less."

"Good. Keep him unhappy. Unhappy overseers watch their boys closely." Bass took the file. Iriarte. Dismissed for irregularities in the '91 audit — that was the official language, *irregularities*, a word chosen precisely because it explained nothing. He skimmed the discharge papers, the references refused, the final entry, and found what he was looking for on the third page: a note in Grebe's hand, initialed, dated two weeks before the dismissal. *Subject made inquiries regarding closed compensation schedules. Advised such schedules fall outside clerk purview. Recommend severance.*

*Made inquiries regarding closed compensation schedules.*

So the boy hadn't started this. The itch was older than the census, older than any of them, and a man called Iriarte had been ruined for scratching it, and now a census taker had scratched it again, and somewhere out there the scratch had become a habit shared by several pairs of hands.

Bass closed the file. He felt — he examined the feeling honestly, because self-deception was the one extravagance he permitted himself never — something adjacent to respect. It took a certain quality to keep pulling a thread after watching the last man who pulled it disappear into the fish market with his career in his coat pockets. Whatever else happened, he would need to know who these people were. Not their names; he'd have those by evening. Their *natures*. Names you could buy or break. Natures you had to plan around.

* * *

The boy came at noon exactly, which told Bass something already. Indentured labor learned punctuality in the blood, but this was beyond training — this was a boy who arrived ten minutes early and spent the time arranging his face.

"Rafael Montrose, sir."

Bass let him stand. Not long — cruelty was a tool for men who lacked better ones — but long enough to watch the hands. The hands were the tell in young ones. They either wrung or froze. This boy's hung at his sides and did absolutely nothing, which meant discipline laid over fear like a plank over a hole, and Bass noted it, and adjusted his approach accordingly. You didn't frighten this one. You *valued* him. Frightened boys ran or broke; valued boys leaned in.

"Sit down, Montrose." He waited until the boy sat, spine straight, eyes on the middle distance — trained never to meet the manager's eye directly, but not staring at the floor either, which showed sense. "Do you know why you're here?"

"No, sir."

"Honest answer. Good." Bass opened a folder — not the census return, not the register; he'd rehearsed this. "You've been recommended to me. Your section head says your figures are exact. Your overseer says your conduct is exemplary. And the gentlemen arriving Thursday — the historians, writing the history of the pearl trade for the annexation — require a liaison from our own staff. An archivist. Someone who knows where every file sleeps and can lay hands on it quickly." He paused. "Someone discreet."

"I'm only a clerk-diver, sir. Fourth grade."

"You're a fourth-grade clerk with the memory of a counting-house and the manners of a churchwarden, and don't mistake modesty for strategy, boy, it doesn't work on me. I've been sixteen. I remember the trick of it." Bass let that sit, watching. No flinch. The plank held. "The position carries fifth-grade pay. A room of your own instead of the dormitory. And—" he lifted one finger, the smallest possible gesture of largesse— "a review of your indenture terms. There may be adjustments available, for a servant of the Company in a position of trust."

He watched the word land. *Trust*. Watched the boy's breathing change by exactly nothing, which meant it had changed by something underneath.

"You would be working with the historians," Bass continued, conversationally now, a man making conversation over a fence. "Scholars. They'll want to know everything about the trade — the grounds, the seasons, the men. They'll ask questions, some of them foolish, some of them less than foolish. Your job is to help them find what the Company wishes them to find." A beat. "And to bring to me anything that seems... unclear to you. Anything that puzzles you. I encourage my people to bring me puzzles. I dislike surprises far more than problems."

Silence. Then: "Yes, sir."

"Yes, sir, you accept?"

"If it pleases you, sir. Yes."

"It pleases me." Bass rose, extending his hand, and the boy took it — dry palm, careful grip, a handshake conducted like a signature on a document whose fine print he couldn't quite read. Bass smiled, warmly, the smile he saved for contracts. "Welcome to the second floor, Montrose. Report Thursday, six o'clock, before the gentlemen arrive. I'll walk you through the arrangement myself."

The boy went out. Bass remained standing, hands behind his back, looking at the door for a long moment.

Then he opened his desk drawer and took out the census return once more, and read the line again. *Father: deceased 1887.* And beneath it the boy's own name in the census taker's neat hand, and beside the name a small inked annotation he had missed the first time — the census officer's query mark, a tiny crescent of suspicion pressed into the margin like a thumbprint in wax:

*D. date unverifiable — confirm w/ subject?*

She had marked him. She had looked at this boy and seen the discrepancy and written a question beside his name, and now the boy stood in Bass's office having accepted a gilded leash, and Bass could not decide — and this unsettled him more than anything else that morning — whether he had just hired his best defense or invited the fox into the henhouse wearing the Company's own collar.

He rang for his secretary.

"Find out everything about the census officer who covers the eastern districts. Name, lodging, supervisors, debts, correspondents. Quietly."

"And the boy, sir?"

Bass looked out at the harbor, at the light coming off the water, at the historians' crates already stacked on the quay awaiting Thursday's carts — crates of source documents wheeled in plain view of everyone, the past being delivered like freight.

"The boy," Bass said, "I'll watch myself."
