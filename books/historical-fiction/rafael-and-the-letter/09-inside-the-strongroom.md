## 9. Inside the Strongroom

The paper they gave him said *Junior Assistant to the Commission of Historical Record*, and beneath that a salary figure so large Rafael read it three times before he believed it. Bass had slid it across the desk himself, smiling the way a man smiles when he feeds something out of his own hand.

"You'll be working under Mr. Wren," Bass had said. "Fetch, carry, sort. Honest work for an honest clerk." He had tapped the paper twice with one ringed finger. "Your father was a diver, I understand. The company remembers its own."

Rafael had signed. His hand did not shake until he was back in the stairwell, and then it shook so badly he had to press it flat against the cold stone wall and count to sixty, the way his father had taught him to count breaths underwater.

Now, on his first morning, he stood in the corridor outside the archive with a brass key on a ribbon around his neck — a key of his own, given without ceremony by the commission's registrar, who had not once looked at his face. The ribbon was long enough that the key hung over his heart. He felt it there all day, a small cold weight, like a coin dropped into a well that never stopped falling.

* * *

The strongroom occupied the lowest floor of the counting-house, below the water line, its walls thick as a ship's hull. You reached it by two flights of stone stairs and a door of banded iron that opened onto air smelling of salt rot, lamp oil, and old glue. Three lamps burned at all hours because the harbor damp ate paper alive if you let it.

There were four rooms. The first was the reading room, where the historians worked: long tables, green-shaded lamps, a map of the pearl grounds pinned to the wall with red thread marking the company's boundaries as if they were coastlines of a country that had always existed. The second was the sorting room, where clerks like Rafael broke open crates and laid documents out on racks to dry and flatten. The third was the archive proper — shelf upon shelf of ledgers, correspondence, manifests, going back ninety years, each volume numbered in white paint on the spine. And behind the fourth door, iron again, was the vault, where the oldest and most sensitive records slept. That door had its own lock, and the key lived on the registrar's belt from morning bell to evening bell, and no one else's.

Rafael learned all this in the first hour, because learning rooms was what he did. A diver's son learns space the way other boys learn faces: where the light comes from, where the exits sit, how many steps between one place and another. His father had taught him that too — *know your way up before you go down*.

He kept his eyes down and his hands busy. There was plenty to keep them busy with. Crates arrived daily from the company offices, wheeled through the corridor on trolleys by men who never spoke above a murmur, and Rafael carried their contents to the drying racks: manifests, wage rolls, letters bound in twine, photographs of company directors in stiff collars. Everything was destined for the historians' tables, and everything had been chosen by someone before it ever reached him. He understood that quickly. These were not all the papers the company owned. These were the papers the company wanted history made of.

And among them — he saw it on the second day, when he slit the cords on a crate stenciled *V.T.C. COMPENSATION, 1887–1891* — were the ledgers themselves. Not the originals. Duplicates, or near-duplicates; the ink looked fresher than thirty years, the pages trimmed unevenly, as if cut from a larger book. But the compensation ledgers, here, wheeled past him in daylight, catalogued and shelved within reach of any historian who cared to look.

He stood holding a volume for longer than a clerk should hold anything. The cover said *1889*. His thumb rested on the spine, and through the leather he imagined he could feel the page where his father's name would be — or wouldn't. Where a date would stand that was wrong, wrong by years, wrong in a way that had saved the company money and cost his mother her widow's claim before Rafael was old enough to know what a claim was.

"Careful with those," said the registrar, passing. "They're older than you are."

"Yes, sir," Rafael said, and put the ledger on the rack, spine outward, exactly where it belonged, in row eleven, bay four, third shelf from the floor, between 1888 and 1890. He did not let himself look at it again. He counted instead: row eleven, bay four, third shelf. Row eleven, bay four, third shelf. He would carry those numbers the way he carried his own name.

That night he drew the room from memory on the back of a laundry list, in pencil, pressing lightly. Then he rubbed it out, because even pencil could hang him. He didn't need the drawing. It was already in his head, fixed there like a knot tied wet.

* * *

The historians were two: Elias Wren, the second-chair, young-ish and precise, with ink-stained cuffs and a way of looking at documents as if they might lie to him personally; and Dr. Corvo, the first-chair, a heavy man with a heavy voice who had been sent from the capital and treated the port as a place that should feel honored by his attention. Between them moved a staff of copyists and, above everyone, the schedule: the volume was to be finished and printed before the annexation ceremonies, which meant everything was to be decided before it was discovered, which meant the writing was really a kind of closing.

Rafael fetched and carried. He sharpened pencils. He ran proofs across town in the rain and came back soaked and was not thanked, and found he preferred it that way. Unthanked men are unnoticed men.

Corvo talked to him only to give orders. Wren talked to him hardly at all, but watched him sometimes — Rafael felt it, the way you feel a current shift before the surface shows it — and Rafael made sure to give him nothing to watch. When Wren passed, Rafael became a machine: lifting, stacking, recording, bowing slightly, gone.

But the days were long and the work was close, and watching was mutual whether he willed it or not. By the fourth day he knew the building's blood. The strongroom opened at half past seven, when the registrar came down with the lamp-man. The vault was opened twice daily — mid-morning and midafternoon — for exactly the time it took to retrieve or return whatever Corvo had requested, never more than a quarter hour. At noon the registrar left for his meal and took the vault key with him, but the outer door stood unlocked while the lamp-man went for his own food, a gap of perhaps ten minutes when the iron door was barred from inside by nothing but habit. In the evening, everything closed at six bells sharp, and after six bells the stairs were dark and the harbor watch walked the quay outside, their boots audible through the walls, slow as a heartbeat.

Shift changes. Gaps. Habits. Rafael filed them away the way the strongroom filed its papers, in ordered rows, in the dark, waiting to be needed.

* * *

On the sixth day, Wren asked about his father.

It happened the worst possible way, which is to say it happened casually, in front of Corvo, over a crate of wage rolls from the diving fleet. Rafael had brought the crate to the table and was unpacking it, roll by roll, when Wren, who was checking a manifest against a payroll, said without looking up:

"These divers' names repeat year after year. Same men, same boats. It must have been a small world. Did you grow up around the fleet?"

The question was aimed past him, general, harmless. Rafael had an answer ready — clerks always had answers ready — "Yes, sir, my family worked the harbor trade." It was even true. He reached for it.

But then Wren turned a page, and the page turned to 1889, and Wren's finger stopped on the roster, and he said, mildly, reading aloud as scholars do: *"Montrose, E.*" — and Rafael's whole body went still, the way the sea goes still before it changes.

"My father," he heard himself say.

Wren looked up. Corvo looked up. Two pairs of eyes, and the room very quiet, and the lamps hissing, and somewhere above them a door banging in the wind off the water.

"He was a diver," Rafael said. His voice came out level. He was surprised by that later, the way a man is surprised to find a wall standing where he expected a fall. "He drowned. When I was seven."

"I'm sorry," Wren said, and he sounded as though he meant it, which was worse somehow. "In '89? There's a compensation entry here —" and his finger moved along the column, and Rafael watched that finger travel the way you watch a spark travel toward powder, knowing exactly what it will find, because he had spent nights imagining the entry, the false date, the neat company figures, and now here it was coming toward him in daylight under a scholar's honest finger —

"The record says 1886," Wren said, frowning slightly. "But you'd have been—"

"Four," Rafael said. Too fast. Too exact. A clerk's answer, a ledger's answer, and he saw Wren's frown deepen by a hair's width, because a boy of seven remembers his age at his father's drowning the way he remembers his own name, but a boy of four — a boy of four barely remembers the man at all, and Rafael had just recited the discrepancy like someone who had checked.

The silence lasted two seconds. It lasted two years.

"I remember the boats coming back," Rafael said, softer now, letting confusion into his voice like water into a bilge. Deliberate, controlled, every word placed. "My mother told me the year. Maybe the record's wrong. Records get things wrong, don't they, sir?"

Something crossed Wren's face — not suspicion, or not only suspicion. Interest. The particular hunger of a man whose whole trade is discrepancies. "They do," Wren said slowly. "Records get things wrong." He wrote something small in the margin of his proof sheet. "I'll note the discrepancy. These things matter. A history that can't survive a widow's memory isn't worth binding."

"Amen," said Corvo dryly, from behind his stack, not looking up. "Note it and move on, Wren. We haven't got the decade, we've got the month."

Rafael bowed and carried the empty crate to the corner. His hands were perfectly steady. Inside, everything was screaming.

* * *

That night he sat on the dormitory roof where the chimney vented warm air, and made himself think it through, because terror was useless unless it was sharpened.

Wren had noted the discrepancy. That was either a death sentence or a door. If Wren mentioned it to Bass — and why shouldn't he? A good historian reports anomalies — then the company would know that someone was pulling the thread, and the thread ended in Rafael's hands, and Bass had already shown how generously he handled problems: with promotions, with leashes, with quiet arrangements. Rafael had seen the overseer dismiss two indentured boys last season for less than what he'd done this week. One question answered too precisely. One margin note in a proof sheet.

But if Wren didn't report it — if Wren kept it, worried it, followed it the way scholars follow things — then Wren was digging toward the same grave Rafael was. And a man digging beside you is either help or witness, and Rafael couldn't yet tell which.

He thought about his father then, properly, for the first time since signing Bass's paper. Not the drowning — he kept that locked — but the man. Big hands, quiet voice, the smell of brine and tar. A man who had gone down into the dark on a rope held by other men, trusting them, and had not come up. Rafael had spent his whole life being the opposite of that: staying at the surface, staying shallow, staying silent. Now he was preparing to go down into the company's dark on a rope of his own tying, with no one above holding it.

His mother used to say his father was fearless. He hadn't been, she'd said. He'd been afraid every single dive, and he'd gone anyway, because the oysters were down there and the family was up here and the rope was the only road between. Fear wasn't the thing to master, she said. The rope was.

Rafael looked down at the counting-house, black against the harbor lights, and found the shape of the plan already waiting in him, patient as a knot tied wet.

Row eleven, bay four, third shelf. Vault opened twice daily, quarter hour at most. Registrar's lunch, ten minutes, outer door unbarred. Six bells, darkness, boots on the quay.

He had a week's knowledge and a key on a ribbon and a discrepancy sitting in a historian's margin like an ember that hadn't caught yet. What he didn't have was anyone. Not Adelina — dismissed now, stripped, watched; contact with her would confirm every suspicion. Not Tomás, drunk and unreliable. No one.

Except — and the thought arrived quietly, the way dangerous thoughts do — except the other children. Marta, who mended sails and missed nothing. Elio, who ran messages through every office in the district. The twins, who could pass unseen anywhere because everyone had forgotten they existed. Children of drowned divers, every one, each carrying some scrap the company had overlooked because it was only a child's keepsake.

If the night work went wrong — and night work went wrong, his father's had — someone had to know what he'd found. Someone had to carry it out.

Tomorrow, Rafael thought. Tomorrow I'll start with Marta.

Below him, the counting-house lamps went out one by one, and the strongroom sank into the dark with everything it held, and Rafael pressed his palm flat against the warm chimney brick and began, silently, to count — breaths, seconds, the rhythm of a descent — sixty for the stairs, forty for the corridor, twenty for the iron door.

By morning, Bass would receive his first weekly report from the registrar. And somewhere in it, if the registrar was thorough, would be a line about the new junior assistant: attentive, quiet, no complaints.

Rafael intended to make sure that was the most interesting thing anyone ever wrote about him — until the night he chose otherwise.
