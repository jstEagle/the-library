## 10. The Divers' Children

The trouble with a secret, Rafael had learned by his fourth day inside the historians' compound, was that it grew heavier the longer you carried it alone. By night it pressed on his chest like a basket of wet shell. He lay in his narrow cot in the clerks' dormitory and listened to thirty boys breathe, cough, turn over, and thought: *any one of them might be like me.*

He knew they were. Of course he knew. He had known them all his life without knowing them — the way you know the shape of your own street. The Verrone Company did not hire divers; it bred them, generation after generation, taking the children of drowned men at eight or nine years old as "apprentices," signing their indentures against the compensation debts that were never paid. Rafael could name them from memory: Marta Quillén, whose father went down in '91 off the Sable Bank. Elio Ferrán, whose mother had been a diver herself before the fever took her lungs. The twins, Osvaldo and Renzo Cadeau, small for fifteen, quick with knots, silent as their father had been loud.

Six of them in the dormitory and outbuildings. Six children of men whose deaths lived somewhere in the 1889 ledgers — or in the falsified versions of them.

Rafael had spent years perfecting invisibility. Now he needed the opposite: he needed to be seen, just slightly, by exactly five people, without being seen at all by anyone else. It was like mending a net underwater. Every motion had to look like nothing.

* * *

He started with Marta because Marta scared him least, which was not the same as saying she was easy.

She worked the sorting tables in the curing shed, prying shells open with a blade worn thin as a coin, her thumbs cracked from brine. She was seventeen and had been told since childhood she was too plain to marry out of her indenture, which had made her, in Rafael's opinion, the most dangerous person in the shed — a person with nothing to lose and a long memory.

He waited until the overseer went up the hill for the afternoon tally, then set himself at the far end of her table, sorting reject shell into the discard bin. Work. Nothing but work. Two sorters side by side was the most natural thing in the world.

"You knew my father," he said quietly, not looking up. "Tomás Montrose."

Marta's knife paused. One breath. Two. Then it resumed, steady, unhurried, and she said, without looking at him either, "Everyone knew Tomás Montrose."

"He dove the Sable Bank the season before he drowned."

"Yes."

"Did he ever talk about pay chits?"

The knife stopped again. This time she turned her head, slow, and looked at him the way she looked at shell before opening it — assessing where the seam ran.

"Why would a clerk-diver ask about pay chits?"

"Because I've seen the ledger," Rafael said. "And the ledger says my father died in 1887. And I watched him drown in 1889. And I think yours died in a year the paper doesn't agree with either."

The shed noise went on around them — the crack of shells, the drip of the curing racks, someone whistling three notes of a shanty and giving it up. In that small space between them, everything had gone very still.

Marta put down her knife. She wiped her hands on her apron with great deliberation, as if she needed her fingers clean for what came next, then reached into her bodice and drew out a scrap of paper folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth.

"My father's last pay chit," she said. "Dated four months *after* the day the company recorded his death. I was nine. My mother kept it pinned inside her dress until she died, and then I kept it." Her voice didn't shake. That frightened Rafael more than shaking would have. "I always thought it was a mistake. Clerks make mistakes. But it wasn't a mistake, was it."

"No," Rafael said. "It wasn't a mistake."

She looked at the chit a moment longer, then folded it away, back into the dark where it had lived for eight years. "What are you going to do?"

"I don't know yet. Find the rest first."

"The rest of what?"

"The truth," Rafael said. "All of it. Before the historians seal it in a book nobody will read twice."

Marta picked up her knife again. To anyone watching, two sorters had simply gone back to work. But when she spoke next, her voice had changed — lighter, almost casual, pitched for the room rather than for him.

"Elio's father signed the same petition mine did," she said. "The one about the safety lines, back in '88. Grebe tore it up in front of them." A pause, the knife turning over a shell. "Ask Elio about Grebe. Ask him what the foreman said the day he came to collect the body."

* * *

Elio Ferrán worked the winches, and you could hear him before you saw him — not talking, but singing under his breath, an old diving song about a girl who waits where the water ends. His arms were ropes, his neck scarred white from salt, and he had a way of laughing at things that weren't funny that Rafael recognized now as its own kind of armor.

Rafael caught him at the rope-walk at dusk, when the wind came off the harbor and most sensible people went indoors.

"Marta Quillén says you remember Silas Grebe."

Elio's laugh died mid-note. He coiled rope without stopping, eyes on his hands. "Everybody remembers Grebe. You didn't cross Grebe and keep your teeth."

"What did he say? When your father died?"

The coil went round. The coil went round again. When Elio finally spoke, his voice had dropped its laughter entirely, and what was underneath was worse.

"It wasn't what he said to me. It's what I heard him say to the tally-master, standing over my father's gear on the dock. I was eleven. I was carrying his chest." Elio's hands kept moving. "Grebe said — and I've carried this sentence twenty years, so I know every word of it — Grebe said, *'Put him down for March. The March entry closes clean.'*"

"March," Rafael repeated.

"My father drowned in July." Elio looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and calm all at once, which was the worst combination Rafael had ever seen on a human face. "Eleven years old, I thought the man misspoke. Grown men misspeak. But he didn't misspeak, did he. He *filed* my father in the wrong month like a mislaid invoice."

"And the petition—"

"Torn up. Yes. My father asked for safety lines on the deep banks, and Grebe told the crew that asking questions was how men learned to hold their breath." He tied off the coil with a sharp jerk. "Why are you asking me this now, Rafael? After all these years. Why now?"

Because Adelina Cruz had frowned at a census page. Because Tomás Iriarte drank above a fishmonger's stall. Because a letter existed — maybe — locked in a strongroom Rafael was being groomed to enter. None of which he could say aloud on a rope-walk within earshot of the dormitory windows.

"Because I'm inside the counting-house now," Rafael said instead. "On the historians' staff. And I've seen the 1889 files."

Elio went still. The harbor wind filled the silence between them, smelling of tar and low tide.

"They're going to write it all down pretty," Rafael went on, "put a ribbon on it, and lock the real numbers in a vault. Our fathers' names, wrong dates, no debts owed, no compensation, nothing. Forever. Unless somebody who was there says otherwise."

"Somebody like me."

"Somebody like you. Somebody like Marta. Like the twins."

Elio laughed once — the other kind of laugh, the one that meant fear wearing a grin. "You know what happens to people who ask the company questions. Ask Iriarte. Ask the widow Grebe."

"That's why we don't ask," Rafael said. "That's why we *remember*. There's a difference. Questions get answered. Memories have to be collected."

Elio stared at him a long moment. Then he held out his hand, rough as barnacle, and Rafael took it.

"I'll remember," Elio said. "God help us both, I'll remember."

* * *

The twins were harder, because the twins trusted no one — including, some days, each other.

Osvaldo and Renzo Cadeau slept in the loft above the net-mending room, shared a single blanket between them even in summer, and communicated half the time in a shorthand of glances they'd been developing since the womb. Their father, Bastien Cadeau, had been the loudest diver on the waterfront, a man who sang to his catch, who argued politics with magistrates, who vanished beneath the water one morning in 1890 and came up never.

Rafael approached them on a Sunday, in the yard behind the drying sheds, where Renzo was practicing knots one-handed and Osvaldo sat sharpening a fishhook against a stone with terrible patience.

"Bastien Cadeau," Rafael said. No preamble. With the twins, preamble read as guilt.

Two heads came up at once, identical wariness.

"What about him?" said Renzo.

"He argued with the tally-master about hours, the week before he drowned. Everyone says so. Nobody says what the argument was about."

Osvaldo resumed sharpening. Renzo went back to his knot. For a full minute the only sounds were stone on steel and cord pulling tight.

Then Osvaldo said, without looking up, "Show us something first."

"Show you what?"

"Something true." The stone stopped. "You come here with a dead man's name and want things from us. Everybody wants things from us. Show us something true and we'll trade."

Rafael considered. Then he reached into his shirt and drew out the thing he'd been carrying for a week now, copied small and hidden flat against his ribs: a transcription of the ledger line for TOMAS MONTROSE, DIVER — DECEASED 14 MAR 1887 — DEBT OF COMPENSATION: NIL. He held it out.

Renzo took it. Read it. Passed it to Osvaldo, who read it twice, lips moving.

"Our father's line says November," Osvaldo said quietly. "1890. He drowned in February."

"We watched," Renzo added. "From the seawall. We were ten."

"Then you know what I know." Rafael took the paper back. "Now tell me what you have."

The twins exchanged the glance — the long one, the one that conducted entire conversations. Then Osvaldo reached into the heel of his boot and produced a folded square of oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth a scrap of paper gone brown with age: a fragment of a company requisition, torn across, bearing half a signature and three words in a cramped clerk's hand.

*"…per instruction of S.G., enter as…"*

"S.G.," Rafael breathed. "Silas Grebe."

"Found it stuck in the drain of the tally office when I was cleaning there two years ago," Renzo said. "Been waiting ever since for somebody to ask what it meant. Been afraid, too, the whole time. Both things at once. Is that how it is for you?"

"That's exactly how it is for me," Rafael said honestly.

Osvaldo nodded slowly, and something in his face unclenched — not trust, not yet, but the possibility of it, like ice going out of a harbor.

"There's one more thing," he said. "Our father wasn't quiet like yours. He talked everywhere. And the week before he drowned, he was saying the same sentence in every tavern on the front street." Osvaldo leaned close. "He was saying: *'Grebe writes men dead before they die.'*"

The words landed in Rafael's chest like a dropped anchor. Somewhere behind him, gulls screamed over the drying sheds, and the ordinary evening went on — but the sentence hung there, proof from a dead man's own mouth that the fraud hadn't been clerical error, hadn't been one bad foreman's greed. It had been *known*. Spoken aloud. Laughed off as a bitter joke.

"He told everyone," Renzo said, "and no one listened. So listen to us. Whatever you're building—" he nodded at Rafael's shirt, where the copied line hid against his ribs, "—build it well. We're in."

"Not in," Osvaldo corrected. "*Remembering.* Same thing, safer word."

* * *

That night Rafael sat on the end of his cot with a stub of pencil and a sheet of paper ruled into columns, and wrote down everything, small, in his neatest clerk's hand:

*Marta Q. — pay chit dated after recorded death. Father signed '88 petition.*
*Elio F. — heard Grebe: "Put him down for March. The March entry closes clean." Petition torn up.*
*O. & R. C. — requisition fragment: "per instruction of S.G., enter as…" Father said publicly: "Grebe writes men dead before they die."*

Five fragments. His own memory made six. Somewhere in the strongroom, if Tomás Iriarte hadn't lied to them, a seventh waited in ink: a letter of account, written by Grebe's own hand, confessing everything.

He looked at the column of names and felt something unfamiliar move in his chest. For sixteen years he had believed his silence was a wall that kept him safe. Tonight he understood it differently: it had been a cell. And these five others — the sorter with the chit, the singer with the sentence, the twins with their oilcloth — had been locked in cells of their own, all this time, along the same corridor, each one certain of being alone.

One lamp still burned at the dormitory's far end. Elio sat beneath it, ostensibly darning a glove, and when Rafael glanced over, Elio touched two fingers to his brow — the divers' salute, the one for men below, the one you give the drowned. Then he went back to his mending.

Rafael folded his list into his shirt, flat against his ribs beside the copied ledger line, and blew out his candle.

In the morning, he would carry six fragments to Adelina Cruz. And tomorrow, he knew — because the tally-master had posted it at dusk, chalked on the dormitory door — the historians would begin cataloguing the old compensation ledgers. All of them. Starting with 1889.

The truth had begun to gather itself. Now it was a race to see whether it could find its voice before the company found its pen.
