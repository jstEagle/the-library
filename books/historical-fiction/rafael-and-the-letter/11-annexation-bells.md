## 11. Annexation Bells

The bells began at dawn, and they did not stop.

Adelina Cruz lay in her narrow bed above the census office and counted them out of habit — the harbor chapel first, then the customs house, then the great cracked bell of the cathedral that had not rung for a festival since she was a child. Three bells, taking turns, as if the city itself were being wound up like one of Verrone's counting machines. Annexation was eleven days away. The whole port had been rehearsing its joy for a month, and now, with the historians' crates stacked on the quay and bunting going up between the customs house and the pearl exchange, the rehearsal was over. The performance had begun.

She dressed in the dark because her lamp oil had run out two days before her salary was due, which was the kind of arithmetic that told you exactly where you stood. In the office below, the morning shift would already be copying returns in their careful hands, and none of them would look up when she came down, and by noon there would be a paper on the supervisor's desk with her name on it, or perhaps it had already been written last night. She knew how these things moved. Paper moved faster than people, and quieter.

Her supervisor — Don Ferro, a soft man made entirely of caution — had summoned her the previous afternoon, and she had spent the evening turning the conversation over like a coin she suspected of being shaved.

"Señora Cruz." He hadn't looked at her. He'd looked at the window, where the workmen were hanging the annexation banners across the street. "There have been certain... irregularities flagged in your district returns."

"Irregularities," Adelina said.

"Discrepancies. Dates that do not reconcile. You understand that in a season like this one, the office cannot afford questions about the reliability of the rolls. The magistrates will be consulting them. The historians—" he said *historians* the way other men said *God* or *debt*— "the historians have requested certified copies of everything. Everything must be clean."

"Clean," Adelina said again, and watched him flinch. She had learned long ago that repeating a man's word back to him was more effective than arguing; it forced him to hear what he had actually said.

Don Ferro spread his hands, a gesture of beautiful helplessness. "I am instructed to place you on suspension. Administrative only. Pending review. It is not a dismissal, Adelina. I fought for you—" this from a man who had never fought for anything heavier than his lunch order— "but the census office is underwritten. You know whose money keeps the lamps lit here. There are interests."

"There are three families," Adelina said, "whose fathers died twice. Once on paper years too early, once in the water where everyone could see. And someone has erased the compensation owed to their children. That is what your interests call an irregularity."

"And I am telling you," Don Ferro said, finally looking at her, and his eyes were not cruel but they were afraid, which was worse, "that if you pursue it, the irregularity will be yours. Do you understand me? The record will close. The volume will be printed. And in fifty years, when someone pulls the official history from the shelf, it will say what Verrone paid for it to say, and no one — not you, not me, not the divers' ghosts — will be able to prove otherwise."

He had meant it as a warning. She had walked home through the banner-hung streets thinking: *no, old man, you have just given me a deadline.*

*

The suspension notice arrived with the morning post, typed on the office machine, signed in Ferro's apologetic hand. *Suspended pending administrative review, effective immediately. All official credentials to be surrendered.* They wanted her seal, her ledger key, her countersignature stamp. The little brass objects of her authority, laid out on her table like surgical instruments after a failed operation.

She surrendered them. What else was there to do? She packed them in the canvas bag and carried them down through streets gone mad with preparation — carpenters building a dais in front of the courthouse, a man on a ladder stringing lanterns along the fish market colonnade, boys sweeping the cobblestones with bundles of twigs, though the stones had been swept twice already. A printer's shop had its window full of commemorative broadsheets: *THE PEARL AND THE FLAG*, an engraving of a hand holding an oyster over a map of the coastline. Eleven days.

At the office door, the clerk who took her bag wouldn't meet her eyes either. Everyone in this city had learned the same skill, she thought. The whole port was indentured in its way — not to Verrone's ledgers but to its silence. They had all been taught, some young, some late, that the safest thing a person could be was unreadable.

Except that wasn't true, was it? That was the lie the company had spent thirty years teaching. Rafael Montrose believed it — she had watched him believe it, watched him fold himself smaller behind his counter while she asked her questions, watched him choose silence the way other men chose a coat against the cold. But silence didn't keep you safe. Silence just kept you *available*. A man erased from the record was a man anyone could write anything about. His father had drowned in Verrone's service, and Verrone's paper said otherwise, and so long as the paper stood unchallenged, the drowning had never happened and the debt was never owed and the boy belonged to the company forever. Silence was not a wall. It was an open account, and they held the pen.

She climbed the hill to her room and sat at her table with her private notebooks — the ones that had never borne the census seal, the ones no one could demand she surrender, because no one knew they existed. Three families. Montrose, Ocampo, Reyes. Deaths recorded years early. Compensation entries struck through in a clerk's neat hand, initialed S.G.

Silas Grebe. The foreman's name surfaced in file after file like a body rising in shallow water. And somewhere — she was almost sure of it now — somewhere in the pension files or the correspondence or the strongroom, there existed a letter. A letter of account. Grebe had been the kind of man who kept accounts even with his own conscience; men like that wrote things down. Men like that confessed on paper because paper couldn't look at them.

But a letter in a strongroom was not evidence. That was the lesson of today, the lesson Ferro had handed her wrapped in his apology: anything locked away was anything the company could keep locked forever. The strongroom answered to Bass. The archive answered to the historians, and the historians answered to the commission, and the commission answered to the same money that underwrote the census lamps. Every legitimate channel ran through Verrone the way every gutter in the lower town ran toward the harbor. You could not petition your way out of a system that owned the petitions.

Which left only one road, and it was the road Ferro had warned her off, the road that ran straight through the public square.

Evidence that stayed in a drawer could be buried. Evidence sworn aloud, before witnesses, before a notary, before the crowd itself — that became a different substance entirely. It stopped being paper and became *fact*. A hundred people who heard a thing read aloud could not all be undeceived. The magistrates would still be bought; she had no illusions about the bench. But bought men hated nothing so much as performing their purchase in front of an audience. Scandal was the one currency Verrone could not mint.

And there would never be a better audience than annexation day. Eleven days. Magistrates in their chains of office, the commission on the courthouse steps, half the harbor crowded into the square to hear the historians present their volume — a captive nation, gathered by the company's own expense, assembled to listen. If Grebe's letter existed, if it could be got out of whatever drawer or grave or memory held it, then annexation day was the one door that would never open again. After the volume was sealed and printed and distributed, the falsified dates would harden into history the way coral hardens into stone, and no voice raised afterward would be anything but a crank's complaint against the official past.

Before, then. It had to happen *before*.

Adelina took out a fresh sheet and began, in the disguised shorthand she used for her private notes, to plan. Not a protest — protests could be dispersed, and protesters arrested, and arrested women made poor witnesses. A *reading*. Lawful, formal, witnessed. A notary standing on the courthouse steps with his register. If the letter named names — Bass's predecessor, Grebe, the falsified drownings, the voided compensations — then it could be read as a document of record, a correction offered for public verification, and no magistrate could lawfully suppress a correction without making himself part of the fraud. She had spent nine years learning exactly how far official procedure could bend before it snapped and revealed the bone underneath. This was the place. Right there. The steps of the courthouse, the hour of the ceremony, the letter held up in front of God and the flag and the pearl exchange.

It was beautiful and it was probably impossible, and both of those things made her want it more.

*

She went to find Rafael that evening, and found the dormitory district strung with lanterns too, because even Verrone's barracks were expected to celebrate the flag that had come to bless the company's ledgers. The overseer let her into the common room on the strength of her old census credentials, produced reluctantly from her pocket like a relic whose saint had fallen from favor — and then she remembered she had surrendered them that morning, and realized the man had let her in on nothing but his own indifference, and filed that away as a small useful fact about the world.

Rafael was at the end of a long table, mending a net by lamplight, his fingers moving with the blind fluency of someone who had done a thing ten thousand times. Around him the other indentured youths played cards or slept sitting up. When he saw her he set the net down, and something crossed his face — not surprise; calculation. He had been expecting her, or expecting someone, or expecting trouble, and had prepared a face for each contingency.

"They suspended you," he said quietly.

"You heard."

"The overseer reads the notices aloud. He enjoys it." A pause. "He enjoyed it especially, I think, because of me. He believes we're conspiring."

"Are we?"

Rafael glanced down the table. Two card players had paused, watching. He picked the net back up, and spoke to it rather than to her, his voice pitched under the lamplight noise. "You shouldn't have come here. Not now. Bass gave me a post yesterday. With the historians. I start tomorrow."

The words went through her like cold water. *Inside*, she thought, and couldn't tell if it was hope or horror. "Bass gave you a post."

"He calls it a promotion." The shuttle moved through the mesh, quick and steady. "It's a leash. We both know it's a leash. He suspects something — maybe everything — and this way he can watch me while I sit in the room where the ledgers are."

"Then don't take—"

"If I refuse, he watches me anyway, and I stay outside the door." Now he did look up, and in the lamplight his face had changed since the day on the quay when he'd verified his family record with his jaw shut tight. The careful silence was still there — it might always be there, she suspected, worn smooth as a diver's stone — but something moved underneath it now. "You came here tonight to tell me something. So tell it fast, and then go, because the overseer is counting your minutes."

So she told it fast. The suspension. The deadline. The historians' volume that would seal the record within the month. Her plan — not fully formed, but formed enough to say aloud in front of a card game that had gone very quiet: a public reading, on the steps, on annexation day, before a notary, before the magistrates, before the crowd the company itself had gathered. *We need the letter. Whatever fragment, whatever copy, wherever it is. Without it we have discrepancies, and discrepancies can be explained away. With it we have a confession, and confessions have authors, and authors have graves.*

When she finished, the room seemed to lean in around them. One of the twins — she couldn't yet tell them apart — had turned fully around on his bench.

Rafael wound the last of the twine and tied it off. For a long moment he said nothing, and she recognized the silence for what it was: not refusal, not consent, but the sound of a man doing arithmetic with his life.

"Eleven days," he said finally. "Grebe is dead. His widow is in the hill villages, Marta says — the girl who scrubs the counting-house floor, she's from up there. And there's a clerk. Tomás Iriarte. He was ruined years ago; he drinks above the fishmonger's stall on Rope Lane. If the letter exists anywhere outside that strongroom, it passed through one of those two hands." He folded the repaired net with sudden decision, like a man closing an account. "I'll ask Iriarte. You get ready to read."

"And if there's no letter, Rafael? If we stand on those steps with nothing but my notebook full of discrepancies?"

He almost smiled. It was a small, dangerous thing, that almost-smile — the first she had ever seen on him.

"Then we read your notebook," he said. "Out loud. Twice."

Outside, the bells had started again — the harbor chapel answering the cathedral, the customs house joining in, the whole ringing city celebrating its own sale. Adelina walked down through it with her empty credential bag under her arm, stripped of her office, her seal, her standing, her salary, and lighter than she had felt in nine years, because for the first time since she had opened that first impossible ledger, she was not waiting on the institution to act.

The institution had spoken. Let it speak. It had eleven days left, and so did she.
