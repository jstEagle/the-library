## 12. Grebe's Grave

The road up from the port ended at a shrine to a saint nobody in the hills could name, and after that it was only mud and switchbacks and the smell of wet eucalyptus. Adelina walked ahead of me with her skirts pinned up and her ledger bag slung across her back like a soldier's pack, and she did not complain once, which I took as a kind of courtesy. I complained plenty, silently.

Three days we had been climbing. The widow Grebe lived — if the pension clerk at the company office could be believed, and he had been paid to believe very little himself — in a village called San Roque de Arriba, four hours past the last place with a church bell, where the houses were stone and slate and the people looked at strangers the way they looked at weather: carefully, and with an eye to what it might cost them.

"You knew him," Adelina said, for perhaps the sixth time since we'd left the harbor. Not accusing. Testing. She liked to test a thing until it gave her an answer or broke.

"I carried his boots," I said. "Down to the boat, every morning, for two years. He never once said my name right."

"Rafael."

"Raphael. Like the angel." I kicked a stone off the path and watched it tumble into the ferns. "He wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. A cruel man you can hate cleanly. Silas Grebe signed papers that drowned men twice over — first in the water, then on the page — and he handed out sweets to the divers' children at Christmas. I ate his sweets."

Adelina let that sit between us while the path narrowed along a ravine. Below us a stream ran brown with the week's rain.

"Guilt is a strange inheritance," she said finally. "My mother left me a rosary and a debt. I've spent twenty years deciding which one to pray with."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I said nothing, and she didn't seem to mind. We had learned that about each other on the road — that silence, between the two of us, had stopped being a wall and become something more like a room we both stood in.

* * *

San Roque de Arriba was eleven houses, a well, and a graveyard on the shoulder of the hill where the wind came off the sea even this far up, carrying salt like a rumor of home. The villagers watched us come with the flat patience of people who had buried too many children to be impressed by anything a stranger brought.

The widow lived at the end of the row, in a house with a slate roof patched in three places and a garden gone half to beans, half to weeds. She was small and straight-backed, maybe sixty, with hands that had done every kind of work hands could do, and when she opened the door she looked at me for a long moment before she looked at Adelina.

"You have his chin," she said. "The diver. Montrose. He dove the north banks with Silas before either of them had gray hairs."

I hadn't expected that. I hadn't expected to be recognized, and certainly not kindly. "You knew my father?"

"I knew everyone your father drank with, and he drank with everyone." She stepped back from the door. "Come in before the wind does. And don't touch the cat; she bites officials."

Inside was one room, clean and dim, smelling of woodsmoke and dried fish. A loom stood against the far wall, silent. On the mantel, among chipped saints and a brass clock that didn't run, sat a small framed photograph of a man in a foreman's coat, squinting into sun, holding a diving helmet under one arm like a melon.

"That's him," I said.

"That's him younger than he deserved to be." The widow — her name, she said, was Pilar — poured something cloudy and strong into three cups without asking whether we wanted it. "Now. You came three days up a mountain in the rain to see a dead man's wife. Either you want money, which I haven't got, or you want the truth, which I've got too much of. Which is it?"

Adelina set down her cup. "Señora, do you know what a letter of account is?"

Pilar Grebe's face did something complicated — a flinch folded inside a stillness, the way water goes glassy over a deep hole. "Silas talked in his sleep," she said. "Forty years married, you learn to listen without meaning to. Near the end he talked about a letter. Said he'd written it when his conscience got loud, and locked it away when his fear got louder. Said there were two copies. One went into the counting-house, and God help whoever trusted that building with the truth." Her eyes came to mine. "The other he kept. Divers keep their important things in their chests. It's a superstition older than the company. Papers, pay chits, a saint's medal, whatever you can't afford to lose — it goes in the chest, because a chest has been underwater with you and knows how to keep a secret."

"And his chest?" My mouth had gone dry. "After he died?"

She looked at the cold hearth for a while.

"They buried him with it," she said. "His own wish. The company wanted to buy it — offered me a month's pension for it, which tells you what they thought was inside. I told them a foreman's tools weren't worth a month of anything. They sent a man around twice more. The second time I put a boiling kettle in his hands instead of tea." Almost, she smiled. "So they stopped asking and started waiting. They're good at waiting, that company. They waited out your father's life, didn't they? They'll wait out mine."

"The grave," Adelina said quietly. "Where is he buried?"

* * *

The graveyard sat above the village in a ring of cypress, terraced into the hillside like everything else here, so that the dead lay in rows as neat as any ledger. Grebe's stone was easy to find — newer than its neighbors, granite instead of the local slate, because the company had paid for the stone even if it couldn't buy the chest. SILAS GREBE, FOREMAN, it read, and beneath that, in smaller letters someone had chosen with care: HE KEPT THE COUNTS.

"He kept the counts," Adelina read aloud. "That's almost honest. For a gravestone bought by the men he counted against."

I barely heard her. I was looking at the earth.

Grass grows slowly up here, and thinly. Around the edges of the stone the turf had knit itself closed over five years of weather. But along the left side of the grave, running head to foot, there was a seam — a line where the ground had settled differently, where newer grass, paler and thinner, stitched an old wound shut. Someone had dug. Not recently — months ago, maybe a year. But dug, all the same, deep enough to reach a coffin's depth, and then filled it in and let the rain and the seasons do the lying for them.

"Adelina." My voice came out strange. "Look at the turf."

She knelt. She was quiet a long time. When she rose, there was dirt on her knees and something worse than dirt in her face.

"This isn't animals," she said. "Badgers don't refill a hole. And they don't dig a straight line down the long axis of a coffin." She brushed her skirt off with sharp little strokes. "Someone opened the grave. Took what they came for. Closed it. And left it to heal."

The wind moved through the cypresses with a sound like the sea, and for a moment I was sixteen again on the night my father died, standing on the dock watching men carry up a helmet full of nothing, listening to a foreman say *the sea takes what the sea takes* in a voice that was reading from a paper.

"They knew," I said. "Before we ever climbed this mountain, they knew. Bass ordered the ledgers curated, the historians fed, the census smoothed over — and somewhere in all that housekeeping, somebody remembered Silas Grebe talked in his sleep too. Or remembered what a man keeps in his chest." I turned in a slow circle, looking at the cypress rows, the empty paths, the village below with its thread of chimney smoke. "Who? Who else could possibly—"

"Stop." Adelina caught my arm. "Don't do this. Don't stand in a graveyard and make a list of enemies, you'll make fifty and none of them true. Think like a clerk. Who knew the letter existed?"

"Tomás Iriarte. He told us it was in the strongroom — but he was ruined for asking about it. Ruined men talk. In taverns. For the price of a drink."

"Yes."

"Grebe himself. He wrote it. His wife knew. His drinking friends knew — my father may have known." My stomach turned. "And the company knew Grebe wrote it. Bass's predecessor. If they suspected a second copy—"

"Then they've had five years to find it, and they found it before we did." Adelina said it flatly, the way she recorded deaths in a ledger, because flatness was how she kept her hands steady. "Or—" She stopped. Frowned. Started again, slower. "Or they haven't. Rafael. Look at the seam again. One line. Head to foot, along the side. If the company's men came with shovels and lanterns and a supervisor, they'd have opened it properly, taken the whole lid, left a scar you could see from the village. This is one man's work. Careful. Ashamed, maybe. Or afraid."

"One man," I repeated. "Which one man?"

"I don't know." She looked west, where the light was going copper behind the ridgeline. "But I know someone who reads other people's archives the way I read census returns. Someone who's been pulling the same threads we have, and who had access to Tomás, and to the pension files, and to every scrap of paper this commission has touched." She didn't say the name. She didn't have to. Elias Wren hung in the air between us like the smell of salt off the sea.

"Wren," I said. "The historian."

"The second-chair historian who asked you about your father in the archive and watched your face while he did it." She pulled her ledger bag higher on her shoulder. "It could be him. It could be one of Bass's men working alone, keeping the copy for leverage instead of turning it in. It could be some thief who heard a story in a tavern and dug up a stranger's grave for nothing but a diving chest and disappointment." She sighed. "It could even be nobody — the ground settles strangely in these hills. But I don't believe in could-even-be-nobody. I've filed too many records that said nobody when somebody was standing right there holding the pen."

We stood a moment longer over the healed seam in the earth. I found I couldn't leave without doing something, so I crouched and pressed my palm flat against the grass over where his chest would have lain, under the stone, under the years. Cold came up through my hand like a message.

*You kept the counts,* the stone said. *Someone's kept the copy.*

"Señora Grebe will want to know," I said, standing.

"Señora Grebe has known since the day she saw that seam herself," Adelina said. "Why do you think she answered the door? Why do you think she told us about the chest at all?" She started down the terrace path toward the village, and the wind pushed at our backs like a hand hurrying us along. "She's been waiting three days for someone to climb up here and confirm she isn't mad. Now come on. We need to get down this mountain and find out what Elias Wren knows — and whether he's the man who robbed a grave, or the only man alive who can tell us who did."

Behind us the cypresses went on whispering, and the grave kept its new secret, and the light failed all along the ridge like a candle being pinched out room by room.
