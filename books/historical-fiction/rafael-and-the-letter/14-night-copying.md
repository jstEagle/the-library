## 14. Night Copying

The key was small and cold in my palm, no heavier than a coin, and I had carried it for two days like a stone swallowed whole.

Wren had pressed it into my hand behind the drying sheds on Thursday evening, without looking at me, the way men pass contraband when they don't yet trust themselves to be seen passing it. "The archive door," he'd said. "The iron one off the counting-house yard. The lock is older than the building; it turns hard, so turn it slow. And understand what you're holding, Montrose. If they find it on you, I never gave it to you, and you'll hang alone."

"And if they find it on you?"

He almost smiled. "Historians are permitted a certain forgetfulness. Clerks are not." Then he walked away toward the harbor lights, hands clasped behind his back like a man out for an evening stroll, and left me standing between the fish racks with a key that weighed more than it should.

Two days. I turned it over maybe four hundred times. In the dormitory at night I held it under the blanket and thought about my father's hands, which had been scarred white from years of salt water, and about how those hands had pulled up oysters worth more than his life, and how the company had written him dead four years before he actually drowned — dead on paper first, so that when his lungs filled with the real sea there would be nothing to pay. My father died twice. Once in ink, once in water. Only one of those deaths could be proven wrong.

Saturday night was the night because Saturday night the counting-house yard emptied by nine o'clock and did not refill until the sexton's bell rang for Sunday service. Saturday night Bass dined ashore with the magistrate's circle — Wren had confirmed this, sourly, as if it cost him something to know his patron's habits so well. And Saturday night the tide came in high, which mattered to me for a reason I couldn't have explained to anyone: it meant the sea was close, closer than usual, pressing against the pilings below the archive wall, and somehow that felt like company.

I waited until the last lamp in the overseers' quarters went dark. Then I went out through the laundry door, which I had loosened myself three nights earlier with a file borrowed and returned before anyone missed it, and crossed the yard in the smell of tar and old rain.

* * *

The iron door opened exactly as Wren promised: hard, then suddenly, with a groan of rust letting go all at once, loud enough that I froze with my heart slamming against my ribs. I counted to sixty. Nothing moved. The counting-house loomed above me, black windows, and somewhere inside it the ledgers slept in their cabinets like things buried alive.

Inside, the archive smelled of paper and camphor and mouse. I had been in this room forty times in daylight — clerks' hours, supervised hours, the historians' crates stacked against the north wall with their stenciled labels: *ANNEXATION COMMISSION — SOURCE MATERIALS — DO NOT REMOVE*. By day the room belonged to other people. At night it belonged to whoever was brave or stupid enough to be standing in it, and I stood very still in the dark until I decided which one I was.

The candle was the dangerous part. A lantern showed through shutters; a candle showed through everything. But I needed light to copy by, and copying was the whole point, and so after a while I lit it and cupped my hand around the flame and told myself that a hand around a flame is a small secret, and small secrets are the kind a man can keep.

The 1889 compensation files lived in the third cabinet from the window, drawer marked *DIVERS — INDENTURES & DEATHS*, and I had memorized its position the way I memorized everything now — foreman rotations, guard rounds, the hour the night watchman took his supper. I drew the drawer out slowly. Paper whispered. Somewhere in the walls a rat paused and resumed.

There it was. Folio 112. *Montrose, Esteban — diver, indentured — recorded deceased 14 March 1889.*

Fourteen March 1889. I put my finger on the date and pressed, as if pressure might change it, the way you press on a bruise to see if it still hurts.

I had been seven years old in the spring of 1889. My father was alive. I knew it the way you know your own name — not from documents but from the weight of his arm across my shoulders, the way he taught me to hold my breath by making a game of it in the shallows, the way he came home smelling of brine and cheap rum on evenings I could still count if I tried. He drowned in October of 1892. I had watched the boat come back without him. There were thirty witnesses on the quay. There was a body. They buried it.

But here was the ink, four years early, in a neat clerk's hand: *deceased 14 March 1889.* And beside it, in the margin, the notation I had already seen once and would see again every time I closed my eyes: *No claim outstanding.*

No claim outstanding. Because a dead man cannot be owed anything. Because a boy whose father died before some bureaucratic fiction could record it had no father to lose, and therefore no loss to compensate, and therefore no debt. The company hadn't erased my father. It had simply killed him in advance, tidily, in advance, so that the real death would cost nothing.

I opened my satchel. Paper — six sheets, good rag stock bought from the stationer on Rope Walk with coins I'd saved from mending nets after hours. Pen. Ink, a small bottle wrapped in cloth so it wouldn't clink. I laid the folio flat on the cabinet top, set the candle where its light fell clean across the page, and began to copy.

* * *

Copying is slower than you think, if you do it honestly. Anyone can forge a line. What I wanted was evidence — exact dates, exact entries, exact marginal notations, the clerk's initials, the folio numbers, the little wax stamps that proved these pages had passed through the counting-house proper and weren't some apprentice's doodling. So I copied everything. The header. The columns. The cross-references in the corners that pointed to other folios, because Adelina had taught me — without meaning to, back when she was only a census taker asking one question too many — that discrepancies live in the cross-references. A lie told once is easy. A lie told consistently across nine ledgers is nearly impossible, and the places where it frays are always in the margins.

Folio 112. Montrose. *Deceased 14 March 1889. No claim outstanding.*

Folio 114. *Okafor, Jonas — diver, indentured — recorded deceased 2 June 1889.* But Marta Okafor's father hadn't drowned in '89. Marta had told me so herself, in the net-loft, her voice flat as slate: she'd been eight, she remembered the year because it was the year of the big fire on the pier, and her father died two summers after that, dragged down by a fouled basket while the foreman watched from the deck and did nothing. Two summers after. Recorded four years before.

Folio 117. The Ferreira twins' father. Same pattern. *Deceased 11 November 1888. No claim outstanding.*

Three families. Three fathers killed twice. And I understood, copying them out in my careful clerk's hand, why Grebe's letter mattered — why Tomás Iriarte had drunk himself half-blind rather than keep quiet about it, why the company wanted the old ledgers sealed away with the historians before annexation made them permanent scripture. One falsified entry is an error. Three is a system. And a system has an author, and Grebe had signed his name to it in a letter that was never filed, and somewhere in this city that letter still existed.

My candle burned down past the first knuckle of wax. My wrist ached. I kept copying.

* * *

Somewhere in the second hour I stopped being afraid and started being something else. I don't have a better word for it than *alive*. Every sound — the rat, the wind worrying the shutters, the sea knocking at the pilings — stopped sounding like discovery and started sounding like accompaniment. I was alone in the dark with the truth about my father spread out under a candle flame, and I was taking it, page by page, out of their keeping and into mine. For sixteen years the Verrone Trading Company had owned my labor, my name, my silence. Tonight I was stealing back the only property they'd ever really held: the record of what they'd done.

I copied the 1890 supplementary register too, the one where the compensation column simply stops — where, year after year, divers die on schedule and the column marked *PAYABLE TO ESTATE OR HEIRS* reads *N/A, N/A, N/A*, in the same patient hand, folio after folio. Whoever wrote those entries had sat at a desk like mine, with a pen like mine, doing his work well. That frightened me more than anything else in the room. Not cruelty. Diligence.

Near the end, my hand slowed. Because there, at the bottom of the 1890 register, in the space where a verifying officer signs, was a signature I recognized.

*A. Bass.*

Not manager yet in 1890 — junior accounts, then. Young Aurelio Bass had signed off on eleven falsified death entries with the same confident flourish he now used on supply requisitions. It wasn't just the company, then. It wasn't some faceless machine. It was a man I passed in the corridor every morning, a man who had smiled at me last week and offered me a place among the historians, a gilded leash, and called it opportunity. He had signed my father into an early grave with the same hand he'd extended to shake mine.

I copied that signature too. Twice, so there'd be a spare.

Then the candle guttered, and I looked up, and the exhilaration curdled all at once, like milk left out in sun.

Because I saw the room as a stranger would see it. Cabinet drawer open. Candle stub on the floor where wax had dripped — dripped, and hardened, a little pale star of evidence on the boards that no amount of morning sweeping would explain. Six pages of fresh ink drying in my satchel. And the strongroom log — I turned and saw it hanging on its nail by the inner door, the leather-bound book where every entry to the archive was recorded, time and signature, and which I had walked past an hour ago without giving it a single thought, because I had been a thief full of purpose and thieves, I learned that night, are not careful people. Careful people are made, slowly, by fear. I had been too alive to be afraid.

Wren had said nothing about the log. Maybe he'd forgotten it. Maybe he hadn't. Maybe this was the shape of his caution — let the boy go in, let the log say he went in, and if the thing surfaced later, the historian's hands stayed clean and the clerk's neck took the weight. I didn't believe that. Or I told myself I didn't. With Wren you could never be certain which loyalty was the costume, and I had chosen to gamble anyway, and gamblers check the odds only after the dice are thrown.

I wet my thumb and rubbed at the dried wax on the floorboards. It flaked but didn't lift. I pushed the cabinet drawer shut until it clicked. I trimmed the wick dead between finger and thumb, and the dark came down on me complete and enormous, and I stood in it breathing, listening to my own heartbeat, doing sums.

It was a quarter past two by the chapel bell. The watchman made his yard round at three, and another near five. The log hung ten feet from where I stood. To sign it would be confession. To leave it blank was worse — an unsigned gap on a Saturday night, the one night everyone knew the counting-house stood empty, noticed first thing by whatever clerk opened up at dawn, reported upstairs before the bells finished ringing for service. Bass read that log with his morning coffee. Wren had told me that much, in his dry historian's voice, as though describing a custom in some foreign country: *Bass reads the log daily. It's the first thing he touches. Before his coffee, I think, even before his mirror.*

Bass would read it by morning. Whatever I did, Bass would read it by morning.

Unless.

I crossed to the log in the dark, found it by touch, and opened it to the current week. My fingers, trained by ten thousand ledger lines, read the entries by feel of the ink — Saturday's page still blank, Friday's filled in the watchman's round hand: *9:40 p.m., all secure, J. Okonkwo, watchman.* All secure. All week, all secure, round after round, because the watchman signed the whole week's page in advance and trusted the lock to do his remembering for him.

So I dipped the watchman's own pen — it hung on the same nail, of course it did, this room ran on habit the way the tides ran on the moon — and beneath Friday's final round I added, in a fair imitation of that round hand, a line for the small hours: *3:05 a.m., all secure, J. Okonkwo, watchman.* If the log must speak in the morning, let it speak in the watchman's voice, saying nothing happened. Let the discrepancy, when it came, fall on the man who had been too lazy to fill his own book honestly — and I hated myself a little for that, for spending a poor watchman's name the way the company spent my father's, and I did it anyway, because the alternative was spending mine.

Then I took my candle stub, my satchel, my six pages of stolen truth, and went out through the iron door into the yard, turning the lock slow, the way Wren said, so the rust groaned low instead of loud.

The sky over the harbor had gone the color of pewter. Gulls were already arguing somewhere along the breakwater. In less than two hours the city would wake, the archive would open, and somewhere upstairs Aurelio Bass would pour his coffee and reach for the log.

I slipped back through the laundry door and lay down in my bunk with the satchel's strap wrapped twice around my wrist, six pages of proof pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Around me the dormitory breathed — Marta in the girls' wing, Elio snoring, the twins tangled together as always — all of them sleeping on top of their own falsified deaths without knowing it. Tomorrow, or the day after, I would get these pages to Adelina, and we would find Grebe's letter, and we would stand on the courthouse steps and read my father's true death aloud to the whole harbor.

Tomorrow. If tomorrow came.

Because downstairs in the dark, on a nail by the inner door, a leather book lay open to a page bearing one entry too many, in one hand too many, and by the time the service bells rang, that book would be sitting open on the desk of the one man in this port who had signed my father's grave himself — and who read the log, every morning, before his coffee, before his mirror, first thing, without fail.

Sleep did not come. I counted the bells instead. Five o'clock. Six. Seven.

And at half past seven, through the dormitory window, I watched Aurelio Bass cross the counting-house yard with his morning papers under his arm, unhurried, punctual, climbing the steps to his office — where the log was waiting.
