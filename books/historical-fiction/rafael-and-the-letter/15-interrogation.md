## 15. Interrogation

Aurelio Bass had a rule about bad news: it was to be delivered standing up, in the first five minutes of the morning, before his coffee went cold. He had learned it from old Verrone himself, back when the founder still walked the counting-house floor and believed a manager who flinched at dawn deserved to be flinched at.

So when the strongroom log was laid on his desk at seven o'clock — opened by the night porter, annotated in red by the chief clerk — he read it standing, one hand flat on the ledger's spine as if it might bolt.

*Montrose, R. Entry 11:40 p.m. Exit 2:15 a.m. Archive wing. No requisition filed.*

Three hours and thirty-five minutes alone with the company's memory.

Bass set down the log and looked out the window at the harbor, where the annexation flags were going up along the customs house roofline, bright as wounds. Below, in the yard, the divers' children were hauling baskets the way they hauled them every morning of their indentured lives, small bodies bent under loads no magistrate ever weighed.

Rafael Montrose.

He should have known. He *had* known, in the way a man knows a splinter is working toward his heart — a low-grade ache he'd chosen not to examine. The boy was too quiet. Quiet was currency in this trade; Bass had spent thirty years buying it, pricing it, occasionally breaking it. But Montrose's quiet had always been the wrong kind. Not the empty quiet of a beaten thing. The full quiet of a held breath.

And now the breath had come out somewhere between eleven at night and two in the morning, three feet from the 1889 compensation ledgers.

"Send for him," Bass said.

"Alone, sir?"

"Alone. And clear my morning."

The chief clerk hesitated at the door. "The historians' commission arrives at nine. Wren will want—"

"Wren can want." Bass turned from the window. "Nothing goes near that vault until I say. Nothing moves, nothing burns, nothing gets catalogued. Is that understood?"

It was understood. It was also, he suspected, already too late to matter. A boy who walked into an archive at midnight wasn't gathering evidence for himself. Evidence for yourself you could find in daylight, in your own file, with permission granted to any indentured clerk with a family inquiry pending. No. A boy came at night because he wanted something that would not survive being asked for.

Which meant someone else wanted it more.

Bass poured his coffee, drank it cold, and began composing the conversation the way he composed all his conversations: as a net with only one shape of hole.

---

They brought Rafael in at eight sharp. He'd been given time to wash and change into his good shirt — Bass had insisted on that detail personally, over the objections of the yard overseer who'd wanted him dragged in wet from the oyster sheds. Fear made men stupid; dignity made them careful, and careful men said things worth hearing.

The boy stood in front of the desk with his hands loose at his sides and his eyes fixed on the middle distance just past Bass's left shoulder. Sixteen years old and built like a rope, salt-cured, with the diver's stillness that ran through all of them — the children of men who'd learned to hang weightless in green water and wait.

"You know why you're here," Bass said.

"No, sir."

"The strongroom log, Montrose. Don't insult us both."

Rafael's gaze came down to meet his, level and unbothered, and Bass felt a flicker of professional irritation. He'd interrogated smugglers, embezzlers, a foreman who'd sold dive charts to the Japanese fleet. They all had a tell in the first minute. This one had nothing but patience.

"I entered the archive last night, sir. That's true."

"At midnight. Without requisition. Without escort. Alone."

"Yes, sir."

"May I ask what you imagined you'd find there?"

"My father's record." The answer came fast, smooth as oiled rope. "His death entry. The 1889 file."

Bass let the silence stretch. Outside, a crane groaned; gulls argued over the fish offal barges; the ordinary machinery of the port ground on, indifferent. Inside, the boy stood like a fence post and gave him nothing.

"Your father drowned in company service," Bass said. "Eighteen eighty-nine. That's a matter of record."

"Yes, sir."

"A sad matter. The company compensated his debts against his indenture obligations. Standard practice."

Something moved behind the boy's eyes then — not a flinch, nothing so useful as a flinch. A settling. Like a man shifting his weight onto firmer ground.

"That's what I wanted to see written, sir. With my own eyes."

"Why?"

"Because a census taker told me the date didn't match. Last autumn. Señora Cruz, when she took the dormitory returns. She said his entry predated—" He stopped. Corrected course with visible care. "She noted a discrepancy. She asked me to confirm details. I couldn't. I've been trying to get into that file since. Permission requests take months. I stopped asking and just went."

It was good. Bass had to give it to him, it was very good — the humble clerk driven half-mad by bureaucracy, the boy who wanted nothing but a dead father's true date on paper. It explained the entry. It explained the timing. It even explained the secrecy, because what indentured clerk got prompt access to anything?

What it did not explain was Adelina Cruz sitting suspended in her rooms across town with her notes seized. What it did not explain was Elias Wren pulling 1889 files twice last week under cover of historiographical diligence. What it did not explain was Tomás Iriarte, drunk above the fishmonger's stall, suddenly sober enough last month to send Bass a note that said, in effect: *I know what you buried, and I know who's digging.*

"You spoke with Señora Cruz recently," Bass said. "After her suspension."

"I haven't seen her since the census, sir."

"She was seen at the harbor shrine. Speaking with Marta Ferro. Elio Sandoval. Your friends."

"They're not my friends, sir. We work the same yard."

"Ah." Bass smiled, and let the smile do its work. "Then you won't mind if I ask them the same questions I'm asking you."

That landed. He saw it land — a fractional tightening at the corner of the boy's jaw, the diver's reflex, the body sealing itself against pressure. But the face held. God, the face held.

"Ask them anything you like, sir. They'll tell you what I'm telling you. We're divers' children. We want our fathers' names spelled right. That's all any of this is."

*That's all any of this is.* Bass almost laughed. In thirty years he had never once heard the truth delivered so completely disguised as a lie.

He rose, crossed to the window, and gave the boy his back — an old trick, letting the silence do the interrogation while the subject stared at a pair of shoulders that owned his whole future.

"Here is where we are, Montrose," he said. "You entered a restricted archive at midnight. Under section eleven of your indenture terms, that's grounds for immediate dismissal and forfeiture of earned wages. Under section fourteen, it's grounds for referral to the magistrate as attempted theft of company property." He turned. "Do you follow me?"

"Yes, sir."

"But." He let the word hang. "You are sixteen. You have served ten years without a single mark against you — I checked. And the annexation celebrations begin in six weeks, at which time this company will be held up before the empire as a model of commercial stewardship. I have no interest in parading a weeping orphan across a magistrate's docket so the opposition papers can write headlines."

The boy said nothing. Waited. Bass felt the peculiar sensation, rare in his career, of playing cards against someone who had already seen his hand — and was politely pretending not to have.

"So here is what happens instead," Bass continued. "You are confined to quarters pending dismissal. Fourteen days. You'll take your meals in the dormitory. You'll speak to no historian, no clerk, no census official. At the end of that period, if the review finds nothing further — and I expect it will find nothing further, because there is *nothing further* — your indenture will be dissolved quietly and you'll be given passage money. Enough to start somewhere far from this port."

For the first time, something cracked. Not fear. Something worse. The boy looked at him with an expression Bass couldn't immediately name, and then could: it was pity. Thin, controlled, almost courteous — but unmistakably pity.

"Thank you, sir," Rafael said.

"Don't thank me. Thank the calendar." Bass sat, picked up a pen, and made a show of beginning the confinement order. "One more thing. The 1889 files you're so fond of — they'll be transferred today to the historians' sealed vault. Under commission seal. Whatever you think you saw or didn't see in that archive, the record is now beyond the reach of clerks, magistrates, and orphans alike. When the official history publishes, it will say what the record says. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I understand, sir."

"Say it back."

Rafael Montrose looked at him for a long moment, and Bass watched the boy decide — visibly, deliberately — to give him the obedience he was owed rather than the contempt he'd earned.

"The record will say what the record says," the boy repeated. "And no one can reach it."

"Good." Bass signed the order. "Escort him back."

At the door, the boy paused. Didn't turn around. Just stood a moment with his hand on the frame, and said, quietly, almost gently:

"My father held his breath four minutes and forty seconds, sir. Company tested him every season. It's in his file."

Then he was gone, and Bass sat alone with his cold coffee and the sudden, unwelcome certainty that four minutes and forty seconds underwater was exactly long enough to copy three pages by candlelight.

---

They locked him in the dormitory end room — the one with the seaward window and the broken shutter latch nobody had ever bothered to fix because nobody had ever been confined there before. The overseer posted a yard hand outside the door and confiscated his boots, which struck Rafael as the gesture of a man who had run out of ideas and reached for the nearest object.

Through the window he could see the harbor. The counting-house yard. The customs house roof, where the annexation flags snapped and glittered. And, at the far edge of the yard, the covered cart that arrived at noon and left at one, loaded with document crates under the eye of two commission men and a company guard.

The 1889 ledgers, going into the sealed vault. Exactly as promised. Exactly as planned.

Rafael sat on the bunk and made himself breathe slowly, the way his father had taught him on the beach when he was seven — *in for four, hold for four, out for four, and don't fight the water, boy, become boring to it.* His heart was hammering anyway. It had been hammering since Bass smiled at him, because Bass's smile was the smile of a man who thought he had won, and a man who thought he had won was a man who had stopped looking.

*You sought your own family record.* It had been almost too easy to say, because it was true — that was the beauty of the lie, it was built entirely out of truth, load-bearing truth, and Bass had probed it and found it solid and stopped digging. The old man wanted the story to end there. A grieving boy, a bureaucratic trespass, a tidy dismissal. Bass needed it to end there, because the alternative was a scandal six weeks before annexation, and Aurelio Bass would believe any comfortable lie rather than face that arithmetic.

But Bass had made mistakes. Small ones. Mistakes Rafael had counted on his fingers through the whole interview, keeping his face still, keeping his breathing slow.

First: Bass had confirmed the transfer to the historians' vault — aloud, on the record, before witnesses. Which meant the ledgers now sat under a commission seal that Elias Wren, second chair, had lawful access to. Wren, who had pressed a key into Rafael's palm three nights ago and said, *if they move the files, they move them to me.*

Second: Bass had ordered confinement, not the magistrate. Fourteen days of a locked door and confiscated boots, in a building whose laundry line ran directly beneath the dormitory window, where a folded paper weighted with a button could travel from the commission offices to a confined clerk faster than any courier.

Third — and this was the one that made Rafael's hands shake now, alone, with the door barred and the sea wind coming through the broken shutter — Bass had said *pending dismissal.* Not imprisonment. Not the magistrate. Dismissal meant the end of his indenture. Dismissal meant, in fourteen days or fewer, Rafael Montrose would walk out of the Verrone Trading Company a free man, whatever else happened.

Bass thought he was closing a trap. He was opening a cage door.

Rafael lay back on the bunk and looked at the ceiling beams, and did the arithmetic he'd been carrying since the night in the archive: three pages copied, folded inside his shirt and sewn into the mattress seam before dawn, before the porters came. Three entries. Three fathers. His own among them — *drowned October 1889, compensation debt of forty pesos erased March 1886,* three years before the water. Grebe's initials countersigning each erasure. Grebe, whose grave someone had disturbed, whose widow had gone silent, whose letter existed somewhere in the city like a lit fuse nobody had found yet.

Fourteen days. The ceremony was in six weeks. The historians would publish within the month after. Somewhere between those dates, Grebe's letter had to surface in front of a notary, or everything — the copying, the widow, Wren's key, Adelina's ruined career, Marta's scrap of pay chit and Elio's remembered phrase — would seal itself shut forever under commission wax.

He heard footsteps in the corridor, slow and deliberate, stopping outside his door. The laundry basket rattled. A pause. Then the footsteps moved on.

Rafael rolled off the bunk, knelt at the window, and looked down at the line strung between the dormitory wall and the wash shed, swaying in the harbor wind, empty except for one white shirt hung crooked and high — hung, he noticed, with the sleeves pinned in a way no laundress would ever pin them.

Left sleeve over right. Twice.

Wren's signal. *Tonight. Come ready.*

Rafael closed the shutter, lay back down, and became, once again, the most boring man in the building.
