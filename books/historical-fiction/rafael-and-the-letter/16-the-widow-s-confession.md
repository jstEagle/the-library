## 16. The Widow's Confession

Adelina climbed the hill road in the last grey light of afternoon, her boots slick with the first rain the coast had seen in weeks. Above her, the village of San Ferro stacked itself against the ridge like cargo lashed for a storm — whitewashed walls, rust-red roofs, laundry strung between windows that watched everything. She had been to villages like this before, on census rounds: places where the company's money arrived once a season and its silence stayed all year.

She knew which door she wanted before she reached the square. The fishmonger's boy had told her without being asked — he'd told everyone, she suspected, delighted to have something to sell that wasn't fish. *The Grebe woman. Third house past the chapel, the one with the blue shutters. She doesn't talk to anyone.*

Silas Grebe. Foreman of the Verrone diving crews for eleven years, dead six years now, buried up here among his own people because the company hadn't claimed him and no one else had wanted to. The man who had signed Rafael Montrose's father into the water and out of the ledger. The man whose letter existed somewhere — or didn't. Adelina had spent three nights turning Tomás Iriarte's drunken testimony over in her mind like a coin of doubtful metal, biting it, weighing it, finding no flaw and no comfort either.

*He wrote it down. Grebe kept accounts of everything — that was the joke of him, a foreman who wrote like a clerk. He wrote it and he locked it away, and then he died, and I asked one question too many about where.*

One question too many. It seemed to be the price of admission to this whole affair.

The house with the blue shutters stood at the lane's end, garden gone to seed, a diving chest visible through the open door of the front room — old salt-white wood, iron-banded, sitting under the window like an animal that had learned not to move. Adelina knocked.

For a long moment nothing. Then footsteps, unhurried, and the door opened on a woman perhaps sixty, tall and straight-backed, with forearms that spoke of decades of hauling nets and a face that gave away exactly as much as it intended to give.

"You're the one from the city," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm Adelina Cruz."

"I know what you are. The fish boy talks." She stepped back from the doorway — not quite an invitation, but not a refusal either. "You can come in if you're going to stand there dripping either way."

* * *

The room was clean and nearly bare. A lamp, a table, two chairs worn smooth by the same body over many years. On a shelf above the hearth: a photograph of a younger Silas Grebe in his foreman's coat, and beside it a small clay figure of the Harbor Mother, chipped at the shoulder, the kind divers' families keep so the sea knows them.

The widow — her name was Serafina; Adelina remembered it now from the burial rolls she'd pulled months ago, back when this was still a clerk's errand and not whatever it had become — set a cup of chicory coffee in front of her unasked.

"Rafael Montrose came here," Adelina said. "With a girl named Marta. They went to your husband's grave."

"I know they did. I was watching from the chapel path." Serafina sat across from her and folded her hands. "I've been watching people come looking for Silas since the day they nailed the box shut. Company men, twice. A lawyer with soft shoes. And now you."

"And what did you tell the company men?"

"That I hadn't seen my husband's papers. That a widow doesn't read what a widow isn't given." Something moved behind her eyes — not warmth, but its cousin. "They believed me. Men believe what a tired face tells them."

Adelina set down her cup. "Señora. I'm not going to pretend to be something I'm not. I was a census taker until the company decided my arithmetic was inconvenient. I'm suspended. I have no office, no seal, no authority. What I have is three families in the census returns whose fathers were drowned and erased, and a former clerk who says your husband wrote a letter admitting the falsifications, and a boy who was nine years old when he watched his father go under and has spent seven years being told it happened differently than he remembers." She paused. "I'm asking you not because I have power. I'm asking because I don't, and neither do they, and that may be the only reason it could still work."

Serafina was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence, ticking against the shutters.

"Marta," she said finally. "The dark girl with the quick mouth. She came back. Did you know that? Two days after the boy left. I sent the others off and she stayed, and I made her eat, and she talked about the dormitory and the baskets and how the overseer counts the spoons." Her mouth bent, almost a smile. "And then she said, very plainly, the way children say things when they've decided adults have wasted enough of their time: *My father is in the ledger as dead four years before he died. Someone wrote that. Someone chose to write that. Was it your husband?*"

"What did you tell her?"

"The truth. Yes. It was Silas." Serafina rose and went to the shelf, and her hand rested on the diving chest without opening it, the way you touch a door you've decided for years never to walk through. "You want to understand him. Everyone will want to understand him, after. So let me say it while I still can choose the words."

She turned. "Silas Grebe was not a cruel man. He was worse than a cruel man, in some ways — he was an obedient one. He came up from the water himself when he was young. Lungs ruined by thirty. He knew every diver by name, ate at their fires, carried Marta's father on his back when the bends took him in 'eighty-seven. And every year Bass's predecessor — Corvane, his name was, Aurelio Bass learned everything from Corvane including how to smile while doing it — every year Corvane would call him into the counting-house and show him the compensation schedule and explain, patiently, that the divers were insured at rates the company couldn't carry and the indentures of their children were worth more than the payouts, and that the deaths would be recorded per the schedule. Backdated. Reassigned. Drowned in years they hadn't drowned, in waters they'd never worked, voiding the debt entirely."

"He complied," Adelina said.

"For eleven years he complied. He signed the entries. He handed the widows their chits. And every year he hated himself more efficiently, because he was an efficient man in all things." Her voice didn't rise; it compressed, like rope taking load. "In 1891 he wrote a letter. A full accounting — dates, names, the actual death dates against the recorded ones, Corvane's instructions verbatim as he remembered them, the compensation figures that should have been paid and weren't. He addressed it to the magistrate and never sent it. He copied it once and hid both copies. He told me: *If I send it, Corvane destroys us and the paper ends up in a drawer. If I die holding it, someday someone comes asking, and someone honest might be holding the other end.* Then he got sick — the lungs, the same thing that emptied him out of the water — and he died angry at himself in a way I hope never to see again in any human face."

Adelina's hands had gone cold around the cup. "The grave. Rafael found the marker disturbed."

"Because I dug it up," Serafina said. "Six weeks after the funeral. I took the copy he'd buried with him — in the chest, under false boards — and I put stones back and told no one, and I have spent six years being afraid of a piece of paper." She laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know what fear does with time? It doesn't fade. It compounds. Like interest."

* * *

She opened the chest.

Inside: oilskins gone stiff, a brass compass, a coil of sounding line, the small ordinary wreckage of a working life. Serafina lifted the tray out whole — it hinged, Adelina saw, at a seam invisible unless you knew — and beneath it, in a cavity lined with oiled canvas, lay a packet wrapped in waxed linen and tied with tarred cord.

She set it on the table between them like something rescued from deep water.

"I read it once," she said. "The night I dug it up. Once, and then I wrapped it again, and I have not touched it since. I couldn't. If I read it twice, it becomes mine, and then I have to decide, and I am not brave. I want that understood. Whatever happens next — I am not brave. I'm just done."

"May I?"

Serafina nodded once and looked away, toward the photograph of her husband, as if giving him the privacy of not watching.

Adelina untied the cord. The linen fell open on a letter of four pages, closely written in a foreman's blunt hand, dated the ninth of March, 1891, and addressed *To whoever holds this honestly.* She read it by the lamp, slowly, and the room receded, and the rain stopped mattering.

It was all there. Names she knew — Rafael's father, *Emilio Montrose, drowned 14 October 1889, recorded as deceased March 1886, compensation owed: 340 pesos, compensation paid: nil.* Three more names beneath it, each with the same double entry, the true date against the lie. And then, explicit as a knife laid on a table:

*I make these entries myself, on the written instruction of Don Esteban Corvane, manager of the Verrone Trading Company, who states that the compensation schedules are a bookkeeping inconvenience and that the indenture of the divers' children resolves all obligation. I record this so that the lie cannot later be blamed on clerks or tides. The fraud is the policy of the house. I sign it knowing what I was.*

Signed. *Silas Grebe, foreman.* Signed, and witnessed beneath in a second hand Adelina didn't recognize — a second signature that meant the letter could stand as more than one dead man's confession.

Corvane. Not Bass. But Bass, who had inherited the ledgers, the strongroom, the historians, the whole polished machine of forgetting — Bass would be Corvane's letter come to life. And Corvane, she recalled from the pension files, was alive. Retired comfortably inland. Which meant this was not only history. This was a living man's crime on a living man's page.

Her eyes stung. She let them.

"It's real," she said, and her voice came out lower than she meant. "Señora — it's real, and it names him, and it names the second witness. With this, the magistrates can't hide behind the historians' volume. With this—"

"With this, they'll come for everyone who touched it," Serafina said. "Including the woman holding it. That's why it's yours now."

"Mine?"

"I'm old, my lungs are half-full of salt anyway, and I have no standing worth destroying. You have none either, but you have the habit of writing things down correctly, and that, in the end, is the only weapon anyone in this affair has ever held." She pushed the packet across the table. "Take it. Read it until you know it by heart. And then do what I couldn't: decide."

Adelina took it. The waxed linen was cool and heavier than paper had any right to be.

"There's a condition," Serafina added.

"Name it."

"When it's done — when it's public, sworn, witnessed, finished — you bring it back to me. Or you send word of where it rests. Silas waited his whole life to be honest in the right direction. He shouldn't end up filed in some magistrate's drawer next to the lies."

Adelina stood. At the door she paused, rain coming down beyond the threshold like a curtain between the hill and the harbor below — the harbor where the historians were sealing their volume, where the annexation bells were already being tuned, where a boy sat confined in a dormitory room waiting for news that proof existed.

"One more thing," Serafina said behind her. "The letter mentions a fifth name. A diver recorded dead who wasn't. Silas never learned what became of him." She met Adelina's eyes. "Whatever you're planning — plan fast. Because if the company learns this letter exists, the fifth man's silence is the first loose end they'll pull."

Below, through the rain, the harbor lights of the port were coming on one by one — and among them, Adelina knew, the counting-house was still burning its lamps late.
