## 17. Sealed Vault, Sealed Lips

Elias Wren had spent eleven years learning to read other men's archives, and in all that time he had never once been afraid of a filing cabinet. He was afraid of this one.

It was a small thing, the document that frightened him. A single typed sheet, laid face-down on the commission secretary's desk beneath a paperweight shaped like an anchor — the anchor of the annexation crest, gilded for the occasion, as everything in the city had been gilded for the occasion. Wren had come to the commission offices after hours to return a borrowed folio, and the building had been nearly empty, and the secretary's desk had been nearly tidy, and the corner of the sheet had been showing like a tongue caught between lips.

He should have walked past it. He told himself that later, standing in the dark of the corridor with his heart going like a diver's hammer against his ribs: you could have walked past it, Wren, no one would ever have known. But he had spent three weeks pulling threads he wasn't authorized to pull, and a man who has been pulling threads will always follow one more, especially when it lies glittering under an anchor made of gold.

The heading read: DISPOSITION OF SOURCE MATERIALS — SCHEDULE B.

Below it, in the flat administrative language that Wren knew better than his own prayers, was the list. The official history would be published in September, bound in blue leather, distributed to every magistrate's office and colonial bureau from here to the capital. And upon publication, per the agreement between the Verrone Trading Company and the Historical Commission, all source materials deemed redundant to the final text would be destroyed by incineration at the harbor works — date set, witnesses named, a fee already paid to the furnace operator.

Wren ran his finger down the column of items to be burned. Pay chits, 1881–1894. Dormitory ration books. Foremen's day-books. Correspondence, miscellaneous. And there, item fourteen, in its own line as if it were nothing:

Compensation ledgers, 1887–1892 (incl. 1889 volume). Redundant. Duplicate abstracts retained.

Redundant. He stood very still in the empty office while the word did its work on him.

*Redundant* meant that somewhere there existed clean copies — fair-handed abstracts, transcribed and tidied, the numbers lifted out of their context like teeth pulled from a jaw. The abstracts would say that so-and-so died in such a year. They would not say who wrote the entry, or when, or over what erasure. They would be beautiful. They would be enough to satisfy any magistrate for a hundred years.

And the originals — the smudged, corrected, crossed-out, human originals — would go into a furnace before the ink on the blue leather volumes had properly cured.

Wren put the sheet back exactly as he had found it, weight centered, corner aligned. Then he stood in the corridor of the commission offices, in the smell of paste and lamp oil, and understood that he had been complicit for months and had only now been handed the bill.

* * *

He had come to this port city eighteen months ago with a reputation for rigor and a hunger he'd mistaken for ambition. The commission had hired him as second chair — the man who verified footnotes, who chased citations, who made the great historian Aldous Prentiss's sweeping narrative honest at the edges. It was good work. It was, he had told himself, important work. Someone had to write down what happened to these pearl towns before annexation smoothed them into a footnote; better a commissioned history with compromises than no history at all.

That was the sentence he had carried like a coin in his pocket, turning it over whenever doubt surfaced. Better compromised truth than clean silence.

But compromise, he was learning, is a debt that compounds. First they had curated his access — Bass himself, smiling across the desk, unlocking cabinets with the graciousness of a man bestowing gifts. Then they had steered him away from certain volumes, gently, apologetically: *fire damage, water damage, we're so sorry, nothing there but arithmetic.* And Wren had accepted the steering, because the alternative was to make trouble, and second chairs do not make trouble if they want first chairs someday.

Then came the boy.

Rafael Montrose. Sixteen years old, assigned to the historians' staff as a records assistant — Bass's little gesture of generosity, a promotion plucked from the dormitory. Wren had noticed him the way you notice a well-made tool: quiet, precise, unobtrusive, hands that moved through ledgers like they belonged there. It took Wren ten days to notice something else, which was that the boy flinched — only slightly, only sometimes — at particular pages. Pages concerning 1889. Pages concerning drowned divers.

Wren had asked him about his family once, casually, over the sorting table. *Your father, was he a tradesman?* And the boy had said, *A diver,* in a voice so carefully level that it rang like struck glass, and had looked at Wren with eyes that were measuring the distance between them, weighing whether Wren was a door or a wall.

Wren had gone home that night unable to sleep. Because he had seen those eyes before. In the mirror, years ago, when he was a scholarship boy at the imperial academy being taught by men whose libraries had been built on fortunes like Verrone's. He had spent his whole life believing that archives were neutral — that paper kept safe was justice deferred but not denied. And here was a company preparing to burn the paper, and a boy who knew why, and a history with Wren's own name embossed on its acknowledgments page.

So he had started pulling threads. Quietly. After hours. He had pulled the 1889 compensation ledger itself from the sealed vault — signed out under pretext of citation-checking — and found what he expected to find: death entries dated years before the drownings the divers' families remembered, compensation debts marked *voided — no liability,* initials he did not recognize beside corrections in different inks. And once, on the verso of a flyleaf, in pencil, half-erased: a name. Grebe.

Now, holding the memory of Schedule B like a coal in his fist, Wren walked out of the commission offices into the annexation-decked streets and admitted to himself what he had been circling for weeks: he was not going to be able to stay inside this thing. He was either going to help the boy, or he was going to have to report him, and both of those acts would destroy the man he had spent eighteen months constructing — the reliable second chair, the rising man, the scholar whose career was finally, finally beginning to move.

* * *

He tried, for two days, to find a third path.

He went to Prentiss. He framed it carefully — a question of archival ethics, hypothetical, academic. Suppose, he said, over brandy in Prentiss's rented rooms above the customs house, suppose a commission discovered that its source material contained evidence contradicting its patron's interests. What obligation —

Prentiss waved the brandy glass and smiled the smile of a man who had survived four commissions and two scandals. "The obligation, my dear Wren, is to the reader. We give them the truest book we can get away with." He laughed at his own wit. "Get away with. That's the whole profession in a phrase. You'll learn."

"And if the sources are burned?"

"Sources burn everywhere, Elias. Empires run on bonfires. Write faster."

Wren sat with his untouched drink and felt the coin in his pocket turn over one final time and land. There was no third path. There was the book, and there was the truth, and the company intended to keep the first by destroying the second, and he had eighteen months left on his contract and one conscience, and only one of those things could be saved intact.

He thought about what it would cost. Not in money — he had little enough that losing it mattered — but in identity. Everything he called himself rested on the commission's regard. His letters of introduction. His membership. The faint, warm approval in Prentiss's voice when Wren's footnotes saved a chapter. To betray the commission was to betray the person he had built to survive the academy, the hiring committees, the long hungry years. It was, he understood with cold clarity, a smaller betrayal than the other kind. But it would hurt more, because he would feel it.

The other betrayal — reporting the boy, letting the furnaces take the ledgers, writing his graceful footnote in a clean book — that one wouldn't hurt at all. That was what made it unforgivable.

* * *

Getting word to a confined indentured clerk was, it turned out, absurdly simple, which told Wren something about how seriously Bass took the confinement.

Rafael had been restricted to quarters for six days — the dormitory wing, meals brought to him, duties suspended pending dismissal. Wren confirmed it by walking past the dormitory at dusk: a high window, a laundry line strung below it between the washhouse post and the wall, shirts and a uniform jacket hanging limp in the salt wind. No guard. Why would there be? The boy had no papers, no money, nowhere to go. Confinement for an indentured man was a formality, a door painted on a wall.

Wren waited until the washhouse emptied, then folded his note small and threaded it through the hem of a gray shirt hanging near the outer end of the line — a clerk's shirt, mended at the cuff, the kind of garment that would be collected by its owner eventually, because even confined men needed shirts.

The note said:

*Schedule B exists. On publication, all original source materials deemed "redundant" will be incinerated at the harbor works. The 1889 compensation ledgers are listed. Date of burning: within days of the September ceremony. I can give you the exact schedule and the vault rotation. Tell me how to reach you. — E.W.*

He hesitated over the signature and did not soften it. If the boy took the note to Bass — and the boy might, might well, survival taught strange loyalties — then Wren wanted his name on it. A traitor to the commission should at least be an honest traitor.

He hung the shirt back on the line, adjusted it so the fold hung natural, and walked away without looking up at the window. Looking up was how guilt got people caught.

* * *

Two nights passed. Nothing.

Wren sorted citations by day with his stomach knotted, smiling at Prentiss, initialing proofs, watching Bass tour the commission offices with magistrates in tow, gesturing at the sealed vault like a man displaying a jewel case. *Every document preserved, gentlemen. Our history will rest on the full record.* The lie was so fluent that Wren almost admired it, and hated himself for admiring it.

On the third evening he found a shirt waiting on his own lodgings' windowsill — impossible, unless the boy had a friend outside, which of course he did, they all did, the dormitory children moved through this city like water through stone. Inside the folded shirt was a scrap of pay-chit paper, written in a clerk's small upright hand:

*I know the vault. I need three things. The burning date in writing. The night the vault rotation changes guards. And someone outside the walls who can hold what I copy, because they will search me first. Can you be that someone?*

Beneath it, after a space, as if added after a struggle:

*My father is in that ledger. They wrote him dead four years before he died so the company wouldn't owe us. I watched him drown. I was nine.*

Wren sat on the edge of his narrow bed with the pay chit in his hand and read the last lines five times. He had known it, of course — had assembled it from flinches and page numbers and a penciled name. But knowing a thing in the abstract and having a drowning boy's handwriting tell it to you are different weathers entirely.

He thought: I am forty-one years old, and a sixteen-year-old indentured clerk is asking me to choose, and the shameful part is not that I'm afraid. The shameful part is how long I hoped I wouldn't have to answer.

He wrote back that night. Three things, as requested. The burning was scheduled for the fourth of October, eight days after the ceremony. The vault rotation changed on the last night of September — the old guard's shift ending at eleven, the new pair not arriving until midnight, one hour of thin coverage that Bass's own efficiency had created. And yes. Yes, he could be the one outside the walls. He had a locked trunk, a false bottom inherited from a smuggler uncle, and, he was beginning to understand, a talent for treachery that had lain dormant all his respectable life.

He sent the reply by the same route — a shirt, a hem, a line swaying in the dark — and then he sat at his desk and began, methodically, to write out the citation errors he had planted in his own recent memoranda. Small ones. Discoverable ones. If this ended badly, if the boy broke, if Bass came asking, Wren wanted a version of the story where his interest in the 1889 ledgers was scholarly sloppiness rather than conspiracy. He was not proud of the work. He did it anyway, and hated how good at it he was.

* * *

On the appointed evening he walked the long way past the dormitory, as agreed, carrying a portfolio of genuine citations so his presence had an innocent shape. The laundry line hung empty in the lamplight except for one white shirt, and pinned inside its collar where a starch-stiff fold would hide it was a slip of paper.

Wren unpinned it under cover of adjusting his coat, and read, and felt the ground tilt.

*Vault entered. Copies begun. The log book records my name and hour — I signed honestly, because a false signature would hang me faster. By morning Bass will know I was inside after hours. If I don't reach you tomorrow, take the copies to the widow Grebe in the hill villages, or to the census woman Cruz. Trust Cruz. Do not trust your own commission. — R.M.*

Wren stood in the lamplight with the paper trembling slightly in his fingers, doing the arithmetic he had been avoiding since Schedule B. The log book. Of course there was a log book; there was always a log book; he had spent his adult life among log books and had somehow imagined the boy could slip past the one habit of the entire institutional world. Signed honestly. God help them, signed honestly — the one virtue the boy had, and it was the rope.

By morning Bass would read the log. By morning there would be questions, and a search, and a sixteen-year-old alone in a room with a company's whole apparatus of polite menace bearing down on him. Wren had perhaps eight hours to decide what he was willing to be caught doing.

He thought of Prentiss laughing over the brandy: *sources burn everywhere.* He thought of the furnace fee, already paid. He thought of a boy at nine years old watching his father go under black water, and a foreman's pencil ghost on a flyleaf, and a ledger with four stolen years in it sitting in a vault eight days from fire.

Wren turned toward the commission offices, walking fast now, composing as he went the most dangerous document of his life: a memorandum requesting, for citation verification purposes, one further access to the 1889 compensation ledgers — tonight, before the rotation change, while his legitimate credentials still opened doors. Whatever the boy had copied, whatever happened at dawn, the originals had to be seen, touched, witnessed by a man whose name carried weight outside the company's walls. A witness, not a thief. It was thin. It might hold.

At the corner by the customs house he stopped, took out his watch, and gave himself until midnight to become, irrevocably, the kind of man who betrayed one loyalty for another.

Then he put the watch away and kept walking.
