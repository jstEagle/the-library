## 18. The Public Reading

**Rafael**

The bells began at dawn, and Rafael had not slept to hear them.

He lay in his narrow cot in the confinement quarters — they could hardly call it a cell, not with Bass's lawyers circling and the magistrate's inquiry hanging over everything like weather — and listened to the harbor ring itself hoarse. Annexation. The flags went up along the customs house at first light: bunting in the imperial colors, garlands of dried flowers over the fishmongers' stalls, children already in the streets with paper horns. By noon the whole city would be drunk on ceremony.

By noon it would be too late to stop what they meant to do.

He dressed slowly. His one good shirt, laundered by Marta's cousin through a chain of hands that would never be traced back to a confined man. His boots, mended twice. In the pocket of the shirt, folded into eighths until it was no bigger than a communion wafer, a scrap of paper in Wren's cramped historian's hand:

*Laundry line, east wall, after the second bell. The schedule is yours. God forgive us all.*

The burning was scheduled for the third day after publication. *Redundant originals*, the commission's memorandum called them — the compensation ledgers of 1889 among them, the very pages Rafael had copied by candlelight with his hands shaking so badly he'd blotted three entries. Copies could be disputed. Copies could be called forgeries, fabrications, the resentful inventions of a dismissed clerk. Only the originals, sworn before magistrates while they still existed, would stand.

Which meant today. Today, with half the harbor watching and the company at its most ceremonial and least able to act without an audience.

Someone knocked — two soft raps, then one, the signal Elio had invented and been so pleased with. Rafael opened the door. Elio stood in the corridor grinning like a boy at a fair, which in a sense he was. Behind him, filling the stairwell in a hush that was somehow louder than noise, stood the others: Marta with her jaw set the way their father's had when he refused a bad price; the twins in matching shirts, unnaturally solemn; old Señora Vidal from the net-loft who had lost two sons to the water and a third to Grebe's ledger; a dozen more from the dormitories and the shore rows, divers' children and divers' widows, people who had spent their lives being quiet.

"You look terrible," Elio said.

"I haven't slept."

"Neither has anyone." Marta held out her hand. In it lay the letter — Grebe's letter, surrendered by his widow three nights ago, read aloud once by candlelight in Adelina Cruz's rented room while the widow wept and the twins held each other. "Adelina says I should carry it. She says if anyone searches me, they'll search her first, and she's used to it."

"Where is she?"

"Courthouse steps already. She said someone respectable had to arrive early and stand there like she owned the place." Elio snorted. "She does own the place. She owns every place she stands in."

Rafael took a breath. It did not steady him, but he took it anyway.

"Remember the order," he said. "Nobody reads until the historians are done. Nobody moves until Adelina nods. And nobody — nobody — touches the letter but the reader. If they seize it, we lose everything."

"And if they seize it?" one of the widows asked quietly.

Marta answered before Rafael could. "Then we've all memorized it, haven't we? Every line. They can take the paper. They can't take the saying of it."

* * *

**Adelina**

Adelina had attended enough official ceremonies to know their grammar, and this one was fluent in its own language. The courthouse steps were draped. A canopy shaded the magistrates — three of them, the chief among them old Don Ferro, whose face gave away nothing and therefore gave away everything. The historians sat in a row beneath a banner reading THE PEARL CENTURY: A RECORD, and Elias Wren, second-chair, sat at the end of the row looking like a man attending his own funeral and trying to seem pleased about the catering.

Bass stood near the magistrates, gray-suited, gracious, devastatingly calm. He had shaken hands with Don Ferro. He had complimented the bunting. He had, Adelina noted, positioned himself where he could see the crowd's edges — where he could see her.

She stood where a suspended census taker with no standing whatsoever ought to stand: nowhere in particular, off to the side, beside a woman in widow's black whom no one looked at twice. Señora Grebe kept her eyes on her folded hands. Her knuckles were white as bone.

"The volume will now be presented," the master of ceremonies announced, and the historians rose in a rustle of good cloth.

Chairman Ossian Vey — silver-haired, sonorous, a man who had turned self-regard into a public service — accepted the great bound volume from its presentation case and held it up to the light like a relic. "Eleven years of labor," he proclaimed. "Every ledger consulted. Every testimony weighed. Herein lies the true and complete history of the pearl trade of this port, offered freely to the annexed administration and to posterity."

*Freely.* Adelina almost smiled. She had spent six weeks reading pension files in a cold office and knew precisely how free the consultation had been.

Vey read the dedication. He read the summary of findings — the noble founders, the benevolent management, the tragic but unavoidable losses at sea, "borne by the company with grief and compensated with honor." At the word *compensated*, somewhere down in the crowd, a man made a sound like a cough and strangled it. Adelina did not turn to see who. She knew everyone who had reason to cough at that word, and she knew every one of them was here.

Wren did not look at her. That was discipline. Last night, passing the laundry line, he had pressed the burning schedule into her hand and said only, "Third day after publication. Impound them or lose them," and walked on. Now he sat with his colleagues, a traitor to his commission in plain view of it, and gave nothing away.

Bass caught her eye across the steps. He inclined his head slightly — courteous, almost warm — the way a man acknowledges an opponent he believes he has already beaten. *Suspended census taker. No post, no standing, no proof admitted to the record.* His expression said all of it.

Adelina inclined her head in return, and let him believe it.

Vey concluded. Applause, organized and sincere in roughly equal measure. The magistrates rose to receive the volume. Don Ferro reached out his hands —

And Adelina stepped forward onto the open stone, into the space between the canopy and the crowd, and said in a voice trained by years of door-to-door enumeration to reach the deaf grandmother at the back of the house:

"Don Ferro. Before the record is sealed — a supplement."

* * *

**Marta**

Later, Marta would not remember walking forward. She would remember the letter against her ribs, heavy as a stone though it weighed less than a bird, and she would remember the silence — the whole plaza going still the way water goes still before something rises.

Adelina had moved first, drawing every eye, buying with her ruined career the ten seconds the rest of them needed. Then Rafael came up the other side of the steps with the twins behind him, and the widows, and old Señora Vidal, until the bottom of the courthouse stairs held a line of people in work clothes standing in front of the magistrates like a tide deciding to come in.

"What is this?" Chairman Vey demanded. "This is a sealed ceremony—"

"It is a public ceremony," Adelina said, "on public steps, before public magistrates. Article Nine of the annexation charter guarantees any subject of the new administration the right to present sworn testimony to a sitting magistrate, publicly, and to have it entered in the record of the day." She produced a folded paper — the citation, copied in Wren's hand from the charter itself. "I have brought the article. I have brought a notary." She nodded to the small anxious man she'd retained with her last month's salary, who raised his stamp bag like a man surrendering. "And I have brought witnesses. More witnesses than your volume has pages."

Don Ferro looked at Bass. Bass looked at Don Ferro. For one long moment the entire annexation hung on whatever passed between them — and Marta watched the chief magistrate make the calculation she would think about for the rest of her life. He could refuse. He could call the guard. But half the harbor was watching, and the charter was the charter, and a magistrate who broke it on the anniversary of the annexation, in front of the annexation crowds, would answer to the colonial office, which hated Verrone almost as much as it loved procedure.

"Article Nine is clear," Don Ferro said at last, heavily. "Present your testimony. Briefly."

"Briefly," Adelina agreed, and stepped aside, and nodded.

And Marta Montrose — no, she corrected herself, standing on the step below the magistrates, feeling the sun on her face and the paper trembling in her hands — Marta, daughter of Esteban the diver, unfolded Silas Grebe's letter and began to read.

Her voice shook on the first line. She hated that. So she stopped, and breathed, and started again, and by the third line it didn't shake anymore, because the letter was doing the work now, and the letter was a hammer:

*"To whom it may concern, and to God, who I intend shortly to see: I, Silas Grebe, foreman of the Verrone Trading Company diving fleet, do set down what I have done and seen done..."*

The plaza went quieter than she had ever heard it. Fishermen stood with their caps in their hands. Women on balconies leaned out over the railings. Somewhere a paper horn fell silent mid-squeak.

*"...that in the year 1889 and the year 1890, at the direction of Mr. Aldous Renwick, manager, I did enter deaths in the fleet ledger before they occurred, and cause debts of compensation to be struck, and did witness men sent down in rotten gear because the insurance on the season outweighed the insurance on the men..."*

A sound went through the crowd like wind through wheat. Señora Vidal, beside Marta, whispered her sons' names — both of them, both dated 1889, both *entered early*, both compensated with honor, meaning not at all.

Marta read on. The names. All the names, in Grebe's careful clerk's hand — the men he had written into death ahead of schedule, and the ones whose drownings he had falsified outright to void the company's obligations. When she reached *Esteban Montrose*, she heard Rafael, somewhere behind her, breathe in and hold it, and hold it, and let it go.

*"...and I write this because my soul cannot carry it and my wife should not inherit it. Let any man who finds this letter weigh it against the official books, and let him notice which one tells the truth."*

She finished. The last word seemed to hang in the air over the harbor like a gull that couldn't decide where to land.

Then Rafael stepped up beside her, and the notary climbed the steps with his stamp bag, and the world changed gears.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren watched the notary press seal after seal and thought: *It's working. It's actually working.*

The magistrates listened. That was the miracle of it — Don Ferro sat through the reading of the falsified entries, cross-checked against the certified copies Adelina presented (copied from the originals, which existed, which were impounded pending inquiry, which could not now be burned without the burning itself becoming a confession), and his face never moved, but his clerks wrote. Everything went into the record of the day. Every name. Every date. Article Nine, invoked lawfully on the courthouse steps before the annexation crowds, could not be un-invoked.

Bass had gone very still. That frightened Wren more than shouting would have. He watched the manager confer in a low voice with his lawyer, watched the lawyer shake his head — *no provable irregularity, no grounds, the charter binds us too* — and saw Bass absorb it the way stone absorbs rain.

When the reading ended, Don Ferro addressed the plaza. His voice was dry as ledger paper. "Testimony having been received and witnessed, the matter is referred for judicial determination. The documents described are hereby placed under protective impoundment." A pause. "This court notes for the record that the official history, as presented, will require revision."

Chairman Vey made a small sound like a man swallowing his own monument.

The crowd did not cheer. Wren had expected cheering, and understood its absence immediately, and found it more moving than any roar: the divers and their children simply stood there, in the sun, some of them crying without noise, the way people cry at graves. Old Señora Vidal crossed herself twice and said her sons' names aloud at last, in daylight, in front of everyone, dates and all.

Then Elio broke it. Elio, sixteen and incapable of solemnity past a hard limit, threw his cap in the air and whooped, and the twins whooped, and half the harbor's children whooped with them, and the sound rolled across the water and came back off the headland like the harbor itself answering.

Wren looked for Adelina. She stood at the edge of the steps, spent, hollow-eyed, holding nothing — the letter had gone to the notary, the copies to the court — and utterly upright. Their eyes met across the crowd.

He mouthed it before he could stop himself: *Thank you.*

She shook her head once, and looked toward the magistrates, and Wren followed her gaze and understood: she wasn't thanking anyone yet. Not until the ruling. Nothing was won until the ruling.

And neither of them saw Bass leave. Neither of them noticed the manager slip down the side stairs with his lawyer and his private secretary, unhurried, a man with nothing left to prove and everything left to arrange — into the offices where the magistrates' clerks had brothers, and the brothers had debts, and the debts had owners.

Only Rafael saw him go. And Rafael, who had grown up reading the faces of powerful men the way other children read weather, felt the celebration around him go suddenly cold, and knew — with the certainty of a diver feeling the current shift above him, pressure changing in his ears before his mind knows why — that the ceremony was over and the trial had begun.
