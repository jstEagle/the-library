## 1. The Weight of Paper

The Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs had been built in a more confident century, and it wore its confidence like an old uniform that no longer fit at the shoulders. Ulyana Almeida climbed the marble staircase past a mural of winged figures delivering messages to a grateful nation — the paint gone chalky now, one angel's face water-stained into anonymity — and told herself, as she told herself every morning, that she was only going to work.

That was the trick of it. The words *only* and *work*. She had learned them from her mother, who had said them over mending baskets through two wars, and from her first handler, Voss, who had said them over dead men's photographs until his heart gave out at his desk with a pen still inked in his hand. History happened elsewhere, Voss used to say. It happened to other people, in other rooms, and our job is merely to carry paper across the distance between here and there. Paper is light. Remember that. Whatever they tell you about what you're carrying, paper is light.

She was forty minutes early for the appointment, which was deliberate. Punctuality could be read as eagerness; lateness as reluctance; earliness as nothing at all, because no one ever noticed the person already waiting. So she sat on the bench beneath the mural with her gloved hands folded and watched the clerks come up the stairs with their collars turned against the cold, and she let herself be furniture.

Outside, the capital was performing its winter pantomime. Snow had fallen overnight in fat, indifferent flakes that had turned by morning to grey slurry under the tram wheels. Bread lines had formed before dawn along Kirov Street, and the newspaper kiosks were selling a special edition whose headlines she had not needed to read, because she had written half of them herself, or rather, she had carried the dispatches that became them: the eastern front folding like wet cardboard, the southern garrisons negotiating their own surrender, the ministries burning their files in copper boilers that smoked all night above the courtyards and made the snow fall yellow.

The government she served had perhaps six weeks left. Everyone knew this the way one knows a diagnosis. And everyone behaved accordingly — which is to say, most people panicked, and a small number did not, and it was the small number who kept receiving assignments.

"Almeida."

The voice came from the top of the stairs, and she rose without hurrying. Her contact this time was a woman named Grebe — or a woman who used the name Grebe, which amounted to the same thing — small, silver-haired, dressed in the kind of coat that had been good once and had since been let out twice at the seams. She wore spectacles with plain glass in them. Ulyana had never determined whether the plainness was disguise or affectation, and had decided long ago that not knowing was part of her own protection.

They walked together down the east corridor without speaking, past offices where typewriters clattered with the desperate industry of people typing things no one would ever read. Grebe unlocked a room at the end that contained a table, three chairs, a map of the republic pinned at the corners, and nothing else — no filing cabinet, no safe, because anything worth stealing was carried out again each evening in someone's memory or lining.

"You'll take the southern route," Grebe said, when the door was shut. "Through the river towns. Vetrov, then up the valley to the pass."

"The bridge at Vetrov closes with first ice," Ulyana said. "It closed on the fourteenth last year. It's the ninth today."

"It closes with first ice," Grebe repeated, as though Ulyana had offered a weather report rather than an objection. "The seasonal crossing is the safest point on the border precisely because everyone knows when it shuts. The patrols on both sides relax once the chains are on. A woman walking the valley road in December is nobody. A woman crossing in November is a question." She took off her spectacles, breathed on the plain glass, polished it with a handkerchief that had seen better decades. "You'll have eleven days of margin if the ice holds its usual temper. If it comes early—"

"Then I'm stranded in a fruit town for the winter."

"Then you're stranded in a fruit town for the winter," Grebe agreed pleasantly, "with a full coat and a warm bed somewhere above a cider press, and my sincere envy. There are worse fates than apples, Almeida."

Ulyana looked at the map. The republic sprawled across it in faded ink, its borders drawn in a confident red that had begun, over the years, to look less like territory and more like a wound that refused to close cleanly. Vetrov was a smudge on the river line, too small for its name to fit inside the circle. She had passed through such places before. Places where history was something discussed in the market by men reading weeks-old newspapers, where the revolution arrived not as an army but as a rumor, then as a requisition notice, then as empty shelves. Places where the war happened elsewhere, to other people — until the day it didn't.

"And the material?" she asked.

Grebe set a parcel on the table. It was wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, and it weighed almost nothing, and both of them regarded it for a moment with the particular reverence the weightless inspire.

"Coat first."

Ulyana removed her coat — good wool, dark grey, chosen because dark grey coats belonged to thousands of women and none of them — and spread it across the table. Grebe produced from her handbag a curved needle and a spool of thread the color of the lining, and worked in silence, slitting the seam along the left side below the arm, inserting flat oilcloth packets one by one into the channel between fabric and lining, and closing the stitches so finely that when she finished and ran her palm along the seam, Ulyana could feel nothing but the ordinary ridges of tailoring.

Eleven packets. Ulyana knew the count without being told, because eleven was the number she'd been briefed on, but knowing and seeing sewn shut against your own ribs were different arithmetics. Eleven dispatches. Coded throughout, keyed to tables she would receive separately, memorized tonight and burned tomorrow. Names, numbers, terms, timetables — the skeleton of a government's final correspondence with whatever parts of itself expected to survive.

"What are they?" she asked, though she had stopped asking that question years ago, and asked it now mostly to hear what answer Grebe would choose.

"Continuation orders," Grebe said, threading the needle a second time for a reinforcement stitch along the shoulder. "Terms of transfer. Lists of assets to be moved or liquidated. The usual testamentary paperwork of a state that intends to die with its affairs in order." She smiled without looking up. "You're carrying the will, Almeida. Try not to think of yourself as a mourner."

"I wasn't."

"You were thinking of it exactly that way. You do every time. It's why you're good." Grebe tied off the thread, bit it clean, and smoothed the lining flat. "Voss told me once that you never ask what's in the packets. He said it was the most restful quality he'd ever encountered in a courier."

"He said that to my face?"

"He said it to anyone who'd listen. He was proud of you." Grebe held the coat up and examined it against the window's grey light. "He also said restful people frighten him, eventually. But he was old and superstitious by then."

Ulyana took the coat and put it on. It settled onto her shoulders with the same familiar weight it always had, and this was the strange thing, the thing she noted each time and filed away unexamined: the coat did not feel heavier. Eleven dispatches, a government's dying words distributed through oilcloth and wool across her body, and the scales registered nothing. The weight of paper was a lie Voss had told her, or a kindness, or both.

The keying tables came next, printed on rice paper thin enough to dissolve on the tongue — and she did not need Grebe to instruct her in that; it was the traditional courtesy, the last page of every briefing edible so that even capture ended in blankness. Grebe laid them in a folder, and beneath them a travel permit bearing a name Ulyana had not used before: Marta Reznik, widow, dressmaker, traveling south to sell her late husband's tools.

"A dressmaker."

"Widows travel unquestioned. Grief is the best document in any country." Grebe closed the folder. "There's one more thing."

There was always one more thing. Ulyana waited.

"The man you may need at Vetrov, if you're stranded — there's a workshop near the river landing. Fruit packing, orchard storage. The owner takes in quiet lodgers in the winter months." Grebe wrote nothing down; she recited it, and Ulyana understood the address was meant to live only in memory, dissolved like everything else if the river took her. "Ostrov is the name on the sign. He was ministry staff, before. Signals section. He retired."

"Retired," Ulyana repeated. In the current dialect of the collapsing republic, *retired* covered a range of conditions from comfortable exile to shallow burial.

"He kept his hands clean and his mouth shut and his trees pruned, and when the change came he traded something — I've never known what, and I've never asked — for permission to go on trading apples. If the bridge closes and you need a transmission sent, he's the only working set of hands between here and the pass." Grebe replaced her plain-glass spectacles. "But understand me: you go to him only if the ice traps you. Otherwise you walk past that workshop as if it sells nothing but cider, because it does sell nothing but cider, and the fewer people who know what else has passed through it, the longer it goes on existing."

"And if he refuses?"

"He won't refuse." Grebe paused, and something moved behind the plain glass — not quite hesitation, more like the shadow of a calculation she declined to share. "Rafael Ostrov has never refused a packet. Ask him why sometime, if you're the sort who asks. Most couriers aren't."

* * *

They parted at the Ministry steps just after noon, Grebe turning west toward the tram lines with her handbag held close, Ulyana standing a moment in the falling sleet with the folder under her arm and the coat sitting light as air upon her shoulders. Then she walked. She always walked after a briefing; it burned off the peculiar static the meetings left behind, the sense of having been handled herself, measured and threaded and stitched shut.

The city received her indifferently. Trams groaned past trailing blue sparks. A squad of militiamen, barely older than the republic itself, huddled around a brazier outside the telegraph office and warmed their hands with the sullen energy of boys who had enlisted for boots and found themselves guarding wires. A woman sold roasted chestnuts from a cart and called out prices that changed, Ulyana noticed, depending on the customer's coat. Outside the Hotel Metropol, a limousine idled with diplomatic plates from a country that had recognized the government in March and was widely reported to be reconsidering by Christmas.

History, she thought, watching the chestnut seller cheat a soldier. Happening everywhere and nowhere. Happening to the militia boy and the chestnut seller and the diplomats behind their tinted windows, and passing her by entirely, because she was only going to work, only carrying paper, only a dressmaker's widow bound south with her late husband's tools.

She bought chestnuts. She ate them walking, hot through the paper cone, scalding her thumb, and the small pain was oddly steadying — proof of a body, proof of a present tense. Around her the city went on making its arrangements: shopkeepers boarding windows, families queueing for coal ration cards, a priest arguing with a policeman about the use of a churchyard for firewood. Six weeks, the newspapers said. Six weeks, and then some other flag, some other boiler smoke rising over the courtyards, some other clerks climbing these same marble stairs beneath the same chalky angels.

None of it, she told herself firmly, was hers. She would walk south. She would cross at Vetrov before the chains went on the gates. She would deliver eleven packets weighing nothing at all, and history would continue happening to other people, in other rooms, and she would remain — as she had remained through everything since Voss died with his pen still inked — a person through whom events passed without leaving a stain.

Except.

Except that walking home through the early dusk, she became aware of a sensation she declined to examine, the way one declines to probe a tooth. It sat low in her chest, below the sternum, beneath the eleven packets. Not fear exactly. Fear had a shape, and this had none; it was more like the sound a house makes at night, settling, the timbers adjusting to cold — a low hum of dread that belonged perhaps not to her but to the season, to the year, to the whole groaning structure of the world taking on ice.

She hung the coat on the back of her chair. She lit the stove. She sat across from it and studied the garment hanging there, dark grey, ordinary, its seams holding secrets the way a river holds currents — invisibly, entirely, all the way down.

*Light as air,* she thought. *Paper is light.*

Then, because she was disciplined and disciplined people sleep, she memorized the keying tables until the characters swam, fed the pages to the stove one at a time and stirred the ash until no corner survived, packed her bag with a dressmaker's modest tools she did not know how to use, and lay down in the dark to listen to the radiators tick.

In the morning she would take the southern train as far as it ran, and after that the roads, and after the roads the river. Somewhere ahead of her was a town called Vetrov that she had never seen, a bridge that would shut like a trap at the first hard frost, a workshop that smelled of cider and lamp oil, and a man who had traded something unspeakable for the right to grow apples in peace.

She did not know yet that she would miss the bridge by four hours. She did not know that she would stand before chained gates in falling snow, hearing the lock click home like a period at the end of a sentence, understanding for the first time in her careful life that history does not happen elsewhere — it waits, patient as winter, in exactly the place you cannot leave.

That night she dreamed of nothing at all, which was the closest thing to peace her trade allowed.
