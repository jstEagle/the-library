## 2. Roads Out

The train gave out at Kholmsk, and after that the world was wheels and mud.

Ulyana had expected this. The timetable in her coat pocket — the same coat whose lining now held her sealed dispatches, sewn in flat against her ribs by a tailor who had asked no questions and been paid not to — showed a rail line that ran as far as Kholmsk and then dissolved into dotted lines, the cartographer's confession that beyond here the republic was held together by roads nobody had resurfaced since the war before the last one. She had memorized the connections. She had memorized everything. That was what she did: she took chaos into herself and returned it ordered, folded, filed. It was the only prayer she knew.

What she had not expected was the silence of the villages.

She hired a cart from a man with a face like wet paper and a horse too old to be worth stealing, and for two days they rolled north through country that looked, at first glance, like any winter country: bare fields, black woods, smoke rising from chimneys in thin obedient columns. Then she began to notice what the smoke was hiding. A village would appear on the horizon whole — roofs, fences, a church spire or its stump — and as they drew closer it would empty out, the way a stage empties when the play is over. Doors stood open to the cold, swinging or nailed shut. Windows were stuffed with rags against the wind rather than glassed against it. In the third village they passed, she counted four people: an old woman splitting kindling with the economy of someone who could not afford a single wasted motion, two children dragging a sled loaded not with toys but with firewood stolen from somebody's fence, and a dog so thin it moved like a shadow learning to walk.

"Conscription," the carter said, when he saw where her eyes went. He said it the way other men said *weather*. "First they took the young men. Then the horses. Then the grain, which was worse than the horses, because a horse you can eat but grain is bread." He flicked his whip without touching the horse; it was a gesture, not a command, and even the animal seemed to understand the difference. "Then they came back for the boys of fifteen. Then the ones who ran. Now there's nobody left to come back for."

"And the families?" Ulyana asked. "Do they follow? Go east?"

The carter shrugged, a slow continental movement starting at his shoulders and ending somewhere below the seat boards. "Some follow. Some freeze. Some wait." He glanced at her sidelong, and she saw him doing the arithmetic travelers do — her fine boots under the travel-stained skirt, her accent that was capital-precise no matter how she roughened it, her hands, which had never peeled a potato in their lives. She let him do the arithmetic. A courier who looked like nothing was a courier who had already failed somewhere upstream.

"They're waiting for the government to fall," he said at last, conversationally, as though remarking on a late harvest. "They figure whichever side wins, somebody will need apples eventually."

*Apples.* The word snagged on something in her mind and would not come loose. Her briefing had mentioned the route — south along the river road to Vetrov, the last crossing point before the frontier marshes, where a seasonal bridge closed with first ice — and the briefing had mentioned, in a footnote she'd skimmed, that Vetrov's principal industry was fruit: orchards, a press house, winter storage cellars dug deep into the riverbank. She had noted it the way she noted everything, filed it under geography, and forgotten it. Now the word came back to her with the smell of cider attached, faint and absurd, like a memory borrowed from someone else's life.

*History happens elsewhere,* she reminded herself. *To other people. You are the person who carries the envelope between the places history happens. You are the hinge, not the door.*

It was a sentence she had constructed for herself years ago, in another life, and she reached for it now the way a smoker reaches for a case that isn't there.

* * *

On the third day the road bent down toward the river, and they began to overtake other travelers, which meant the road was converging with theirs, which meant everyone was going where she was going: to Vetrov, to the bridge, to the crossing that stood between the falling republic and whatever lay on the far bank.

They traveled in twos and threes mostly, on foot or in carts, bundles tied in blankets, children wrapped until they were only eyes. There was a family of six walking behind a handcart stacked with a wardrobe too heavy to carry and too precious to abandon; the father pushed it with his head bowed like a penitent. There was a pair of old men leading a goat. There was a woman alone, young, with a soldier's greatcoat cut down and re-sewn to fit her, and Ulyana watched the way she scanned the treeline at intervals — left, right, left again, never twice the same rhythm — and recognized the habit the way one recognizes a scar. Somewhere ahead, everyone believed, was safety. The bridge. The far bank. The arithmetic of survival reduced to planks and chains.

Ulyana did not look at them longer than necessary. This was discipline, not cruelty, she told herself. A courier who made eye contact with every refugee on the road became memorable, and memorable was a synonym for dead. But discipline has a cost that accrues quietly, like interest, and by evening of the third day she found she had stopped humming to herself in the cart — she hadn't known she'd been humming until she stopped — and that the ordered world inside her head had begun, very slightly, to slip its files.

At dusk they put up at a posting inn that was really just a farmhouse with ambitions, and the common room held eleven travelers, one stove, and a smell of boiled cabbage and wet wool that had soaked into the beams over generations. Ulyana paid for soup she didn't taste and a corner near the fire, and it was there, in that cabbage-smelling room, that she met the schoolteacher.

His name was Pavel Ilyich, and he announced it himself within minutes of sitting down beside her, uninvited, with the confidence of a man whose loneliness had passed through shyness long ago and come out the other side as talkativeness. He was perhaps fifty-five, round-shouldered, with ink stains permanently embedded in the creases of his knuckles, and he carried a leather satchel he touched every few minutes the way other men touch a rosary. He had been a schoolmaster in a town whose name he pronounced with such elaborate neutrality that Ulyana understood it had probably been burned.

"You're going south," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going wherever the road goes."

"The road goes to Vetrov," Pavel Ilyich said. "Everything goes to Vetrov now. And from Vetrov, across the bridge, and then—" he made a small gesture with his cup, a gesture that contained all of Europe, all of elsewhere, all of every place that was not here. "You know about the bridge?"

"That it closes with first ice."

"Everyone knows it closes with first ice. What nobody tells you—" he leaned in, and lowered his voice, and Ulyana registered with professional detachment that he smelled of chalk dust and cheap tobacco and fear — "is that it closes early. Every year the garrison commander closes it early, because closing it early makes him look decisive, and opening it late costs him nothing. Last year it shut nine days before the ice. The year before, twelve. And this year—" he spread his hands, palms up, presenting the evidence of the entire visible world: the emptied villages, the refugees, the cold coming down off the steppe like something with intent. "This year the colonel up there is frightened. Frightened men close bridges."

Ulyana kept her face still. Inside, a small cold mechanism began to turn. *How many days am I from Vetrov? Three, if the weather holds. Four if it doesn't. When does the ice come?* She had known these numbers once, in the capital, in the warm, where numbers behaved themselves. Here on the road they had begun to move around like livestock.

"I'm not in a hurry," she said.

"Nobody is in a hurry," Pavel Ilyich agreed pleasantly. "That's what hurry is for other people to have." He drank, wiped his mustache, and studied her with eyes that were, beneath the chatter, quicker than she'd credited. "You're not a merchant. Merchants count things. You're not a nurse; nurses look at hands. And you're not running, because running people don't sit with their backs to the wall and their face to the room." He smiled apologetically. "Thirty-one years teaching children. You learn to read faces or you learn nothing at all."

"What do my face say, then?"

"It says you've already decided what you'll do in every room you'll ever enter," he said, "and that you're waiting to find out whether the rooms will agree." He laughed at his own observation, a dry rustling laugh, and waved away her non-answer before she could construct one. "No matter, no matter. A stranger's business is a stranger's. I'll tell you mine instead, since I'm paying for the telling: I'm carrying the register books. My school's registers. Forty years of names." He patted the satchel. "Dates of birth, dates of leaving, marks in arithmetic. Half those boys are dead and half the rest are scattered from here to Odessa, and I suppose some part of me thinks that if the books cross the bridge, then it wasn't all—"

He stopped. He looked into his cup. Outside, the wind found a gap in the shutter and played one long note on it, low and patient, like a man whistling while he worked.

"It wasn't for nothing," Ulyana finished for him, quietly, because someone had to say it or the sentence would hang there forever.

"No," Pavel Ilyich said, recovering his cheerfulness like a man pulling a coat back onto his shoulders. "Exactly. Though between you and me, madam — between us educated people — I don't believe in causes anymore. I believe in registers. A cause is an opinion wearing a uniform. A register is just the truth, written down while anyone still bothered to write anything down." He raised his cup slightly, a toast to truth, and drained it.

Later, when the room had gone quiet except for breathing and the stove ticking, Ulyana lay on the straw mattress she'd paid for and stared at the ceiling beams and turned his words over like stones. *Weeks, not months* — he hadn't quite said it, but it had been underneath everything he said, the way water is underneath ice. The republic had weeks. Perhaps fewer. And her orders assumed months: safe houses strung along the southern road like beads, relays timed to a calendar drawn up in an office where the windows faced a boulevard and the boulevard was calm.

She got up, checked the coat — the dispatches were there, flat and patient against her ribs, warm from her body, indifferent as seeds — and told herself the familiar sentence. *History happens elsewhere.*

But the sentence sounded different tonight. On the road, in the dark, in a farmhouse full of sleeping strangers, it sounded less like a shield and more like a lie she had been telling so long she'd mistaken it for a personality.

* * *

The snow came on the fourth day, fat and windless, the kind that falls straight down as if the sky had simply given up holding it, and with the snow came the soldiers.

They met them at a crossroads hamlet called Stary Brod — a name meaning Old Ford, though the ford itself had been mined in the autumn and closed ever since, funneling every living thing toward Vetrov like blood through a narrowing artery. There were eight of them, maybe ten, posted at the junction with a machine gun on a sledge and a table dragged out into the falling snow, and behind the table sat a lieutenant so young his belt looked borrowed, checking papers with the furious thoroughness of someone performing diligence for an audience.

The queue moved slowly. Ulyana used the time the way she used all time: observing. The lieutenant was checking papers and confiscating certain ones — she watched a man ahead of her surrender a document with a red stamp and receive in exchange a slip of paper and a look that stripped five years off his life. Deserters, then, or suspected deserters. They were pulling males between roughly sixteen and forty out of the line, and the pulled ones walked to the sledge and stood beside it with the particular stillness of animals that have scented the slaughterhouse and decided, for now, not to run.

Her own papers were flawless — that was the one mercy of her trade, that the forgers in the capital were better than the checkers on the road — and her story was simple: a widow traveling south to her sister's household outside Vetrov for the winter, letters enclosed. The lieutenant barely glanced at her. Women were not yet being taken; the republic was bleeding from one arm and hadn't thought to bleed from both. He waved her through, and she walked past the machine gun with her heartbeat steady and her breath making little clouds, and only fifty meters past the checkpoint did she permit herself to hear what the queue had sounded like: the shuffling, the coughing, the small wet sounds of people trying not to cry in front of strangers.

The schoolteacher was behind her in the line. She saw him, when she looked back, standing very straight with his satchel hugged to his chest, watching the lieutenant with an expression she couldn't read at that distance and didn't try to.

* * *

They sighted the river on the sixth day, and Ulyana understood immediately why every mapmaker and every garrison commander in history had cared about this stretch of water.

The Vetr flowed wide and slow through a valley it had cut itself, dark between snowy banks, carrying plates of ice downstream with a soft grinding patience. And there, where the valley narrowed, stood Vetrov: a town of grey roofs climbing the northern slope, a stone quay, warehouses with hoisting cranes frozen mid-gesture, and — unmistakable even at three kilometers, even through snow-haze — the bridge. Iron spans on stone piers, a gate-house at each end, and on the far tower a flag she did not recognize, which meant either that the flag was new or that her knowledge of the frontier was older than she liked to admit.

Below the town, terraced down the south-facing bank in long geometric ranks, bare and black and beautiful in the snow, lay the orchards. Thousands of trees in rows, pruned into shapes that spoke of decades of patient hands, each row ending in a white-painted marker post. Even leafless, even asleep under snow, they looked less planted than *arranged* — an argument in favor of order made by people who had never lived to see whether the argument convinced anyone.

"Pretty, aren't they," the carter said, following her gaze. He had grown more talkative as they neared the town, the way men do when a journey's end makes conversation feel cheap again. "Ostrov's trees, most of them. Rafael Ostrov. Owns half the orchards and the packing works both. Good employer, they say. Pays through the winter when other growers let people starve till pruning season." He shrugged. "Strange sort, they say, too. Keeps to himself. Never married." A pause, and then, with the mild malice of small-town knowledge: "Used to be somebody important, before. Nobody says what. Around here you learn not to ask what people were."

Ulyana filed it. *Ostrov. Packing works. Used to be somebody.* The files inside her head were getting heavier; she could feel them, a physical weight behind the sternum, and she wondered briefly when filing had started to feel like labor instead of relief.

The town took them in slowly, through a checkpoint that was merely a bored sergeant counting carts, up streets packed hard with ice, past the smell of woodsmoke and — yes, unmistakably — cider, sweet and fermented, drifting from the warehouse district like the ghost of October haunting January. The inn she'd been given in her instructions was shuttered; the innkeeper's wife, leaning out a window, said the inn had been requisitioned for officers a fortnight ago and suggested, with a shrug that apologized for the entire war, that she try the boarding houses near the market. Everything near the market was full. Of course it was full. Everyone on the road had converged here like water finding a drain, and the town was a cup that had overflowed weeks ago.

It was nearly dark when she found the room — a narrow bed under a slanted roof above a chandler's shop, costing triple what it should, smelling of tallow and frost. From its one window she could see down over the roofs to the river, and to the bridge, where lanterns were being lit along the spans one by one, and to the gate-house on the near end, where — she leaned closer, breath fogging the glass — soldiers were unrolling something long and pale from a drum.

Chain. They were unloading chain.

She stood at that window a long time, watching the lanterns bloom and the chain pile up in gleaming coils, and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for six days. First ice was due within the week; the ice was already running in sheets. Pavel Ilyich's voice, chalk-dusted and dry: *frightened men close bridges.* Twelve days early last year. Nine the year before.

And her dispatches, sewn against her ribs, needed a crossing. Not today, not tomorrow — the transmission point was on the far side, the contact, the schedule, all of it calibrated to a calendar that had been drafted by optimistic men in a warm room — but soon. Weeks, the plan said. The plan had budgeted weeks.

The republic, by every witness she had met on the road, had weeks too.

Two clocks, then, ticking at each other across a dark river, and hers was the slower one.

Ulyana turned from the window, unpacked her brush and comb with the deliberate motions of a woman establishing normalcy, and caught her own reflection in the spotted glass: tired, sharp-eyed, ordinary. *History happens elsewhere,* she told the reflection.

The reflection did not answer, but downstairs the chandler's shop bell rang, and a voice — a customer asking after candles — carried up through the floorboards, low and level and oddly distinct, and something about its cadence snagged in her ear the way the word *apples* had snagged days ago. She couldn't have said why. Only that it belonged to someone accustomed to being listened to.

She would meet that voice tomorrow, though she didn't know it yet. For now she banked the stove, checked the coat one last time, and slept badly in a strange town while the river ground its ice and the chain waited in coils by the gate-house, patient as everything else in Vetrov, patient as winter itself.
