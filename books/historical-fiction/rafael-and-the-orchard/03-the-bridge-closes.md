## 3. The Bridge Closes

She saw the smoke before she saw the river — a single gray column rising from the far bank, thin as a plumb line dropped from heaven. For a foolish moment she thought it was the chimneys of a working town, bakeries and smithies going about their morning, and her heart lifted stupidly toward it like a moth. Then she crested the last hill above Vetrov and understood.

The smoke came from soldiers' cookfires. They were burning green wood on the bridge itself, and they had been burning it for hours.

Ulyana stopped at the crest and let the wind push against her, taking inventory the way she had been trained to take inventory: not with alarm but with arithmetic. The road descended in a long gray ribbon through bare fields. The town lay along the south bank of the Vetr, low-roofed and huddled, its church spire leaning slightly eastward as if listening for something across the water. And the bridge —

The bridge was already dying. She could see the gates at both ends, iron-framed timber barriers that had stood open every winter of her childhood travels, waiting now behind coils of chain. Soldiers moved along its length with the unhurried purpose of men doing a job that would not be undone until spring. As she watched, two of them swung a sledge together, once, twice, and something in the bridge's decking gave up with a crack she felt in her own chest at this distance.

She checked her watch. Eleven minutes past nine.

The schedule said first ice. The schedule said the crossing remained open until first ice, and the ice on the shallows along the bank was barely a skin, gray lace over black water, nothing any quartermaster would call navigable or even walkable. The closing should have been days away. Someone upstream had decided to close it early, and no one downstream had thought it worth a notice posted on the road.

*Hours,* she thought. *I am hours late.* And beneath that, quieter, in the voice she kept locked below the floorboards of herself: *You are stranded.*

She did not let the second voice finish. She put it away with the practiced motion of a woman sliding a letter back into its envelope, and she began to walk down into Vetrov.

* * *

The town had the look of a place holding its breath. Not empty — that would have been simpler — but half-empty, shuttered in patches, a laundry line still swaying between two houses whose windows were boarded. A dog watched her pass from a doorstep with the flat professional gaze of an animal that had learned strangers meant either bread or trouble and had stopped caring which. Near the market square, an old man was stacking firewood with tremendous slowness, each log placed as though he were composing a sentence he did not want to finish.

"Which way to the ferry office?" she asked him.

He looked at her boots first, then her coat, then, last and briefly, her face. "No ferry office."

"The bridge, then. When does it reopen?"

"When it opens." He set another log. "Spring. Maybe April. Maybe later, if the colonel's men decide the ice isn't honest yet."

"Colonel?"

He finally looked at her face properly then, and whatever he saw made him go back to his woodpile with the finality of a man closing a shutters. "Ask at the tavern," he said. "If you're asking."

The tavern was open, which surprised her until she went inside and understood that everything about it — the heat, the smell of boiled cabbage and cheap tobacco, the four men playing cards with the grim concentration of conscripts — existed precisely because there was nowhere else to be. Winter had closed around Vetrov like a fist, and the tavern was the space between two fingers where people gathered to breathe.

She ordered tea she didn't want and listened.

It took less than an hour to assemble the shape of things. The bridge had closed at dawn, three days ahead of the posted date, on direct order from the garrison at Krayev — the same garrison that had, according to the card players, doubled its patrols along the south road all week. Nobody knew why. Everybody had a theory, and the theories circled the same drain: the war upriver, the government in the capital, the men coming out of the north with their armbands and their lists. The republic was a body already cooling; the arguments were only about which organ would fail first.

"They say weeks," one of the card players told her, not unkindly, when she asked how long the town expected peace. He turned over a card, frowned at it. "Weeks, not months. Then everyone who wants to be across the water will need to swim."

She drank her tea. Her hands were steady. She noted this fact about her hands the way a surgeon notes a pulse — professionally, without warmth — because somewhere beneath her sternum something was beating very fast against a door she had no intention of opening.

*Assess,* she told herself, and the word worked like a key.

One: the dispatches were intact, sewn flat against her left side, undetectable, unremarkable. Two: the mission's timetable assumed a crossing by midwinter; that assumption was now dead. Three: the instructions contained no contingency for a closed bridge, because the men who wrote them had never imagined standing on the wrong side of one. Four: she could not go back. Back was through territory where her face had been seen, where the coachman remembered her, where the word *courier* traveled faster than any courier.

Five. Five was the problem. Five was the blank page where the plan should have been.

She paid for the tea and stepped out into the white light of late morning, and for one full minute she allowed herself to stand still in the middle of the street and feel it — the walls of the season coming up around her, the river at her back running cold and open under its lace of ice, the roads out sealed, the capital receding day by day into a country that might not exist by thaw. Panic rose in her like water filling a boot.

Then, by habit older than thought, she converted it.

Panic was fuel that burned too fast. Planning was the same fuel, fed to the flame a stick at a time. She had learned this at nineteen from a handler who smelled of ink and never once used her first name: *Fear is information, Almeida. It tells you what you haven't accounted for. Write it down, account for it, and it becomes a checklist.* So she stood in the street and wrote it down, invisibly, item by item, until the water in her boot found its level.

She needed lodging — not the tavern, where travelers were noticed and remembered. She needed work, or the appearance of work, because a woman with no trade and no errand in a small town through a whole winter was a question people eventually answered for themselves, badly. She needed to learn who held authority here, how often it changed hands, and what it looked at. And she needed, above all, time — weeks of unremarkable, forgettable time — because the dispatches were not perishable, but she might be.

A town lived by its commerce. What commerce did Vetrov have?

She walked the streets until the answer presented itself the way answers do in river towns: by smell. It came faintly on the wind off the water — sweet, cidery, with a darker note underneath, resinous and warm. Lamp oil and apples. She followed it east along the bank, past a row of net-drying sheds, to where the ground rose slightly and a long building stood with its back to the river: timber, broad-eaved, its yard stacked with crates stenciled in faded paint. An orchard climbed the slope behind it, rows of bare trees marching up the hillside in disciplined lines, their branches black and patient against the sky.

Above the packing hall, she saw, there were rooms. Windows under the eaves, a separate stair down the building's flank, a curtain in one window that moved when the wind moved and otherwise hung still. Somebody lived there. Somebody who owned crates and trees and, apparently, silence — because the yard was immaculate, the tools racked, the whole establishment wearing the closed, self-contained air of a house that had drawn its shutters inward years ago and liked it that way.

There was a sign. It was small, hand-painted, and old enough that the letters had gone soft-edged: **OSTROV — WINTER APPLES, PRESERVES, PACKING BY ARRANGEMENT.**

Ulyana stood in the lane and considered the sign for longer than a woman buying apples would consider it.

*Packing by arrangement.* Everything about the phrase was ordinary and yet it sat oddly in her ear, the way a familiar word will suddenly look misspelled. She filed the feeling without examining it. She was tired, she told herself. Cold makes the mind superstitious.

But she noticed, too, the details she could not help noticing, because noticing was the one appetite she had never managed to starve: that the crate stacks formed clean sight-lines down both sides of the yard, so that anyone approaching the building was visible for thirty meters in every direction; that the cellar doors facing the river were newer than the rest of the woodwork; that the curtain upstairs hung perfectly straight, weighted at its hem.

None of it meant anything. All of it might.

The wind came off the river again, carrying the smell of cider and oil, and beneath it, fainter, a sound from the bridge — chains shifting, iron settling against iron, the sound of a door being locked from the outside of her life.

She smoothed her coat, arranged her face into the mild, slightly weary expression of a traveler asking after lodgings, and walked up the packed snow of the yard toward the packing hall, her boots crunching in rhythm, her heart doing its caged thing behind the door where she kept it.

Inside, through the tall doorway, lamplight was already burning against the early dusk. Racks of apples glowed amber in it, thousands of them, ranked in wooden slats like sleeping lanterns. And moving among them — she saw only the silhouette at first, a man's shape bent over a sorting table, hands moving with unhurried precision — someone was awake in the closed world, and had perhaps been watching the lane for some time.

The man straightened. Turned. Looked at her across forty feet of winter apples and lamp oil with eyes she couldn't yet read, and said, before she could speak a single rehearsed word:

"You took the south road. Nobody takes the south road anymore unless they have to."

Not a greeting. Not a question, exactly. Ulyana felt the checklist in her mind shiver, pages stirring as if a draft had gotten in, and she understood that whatever she had planned to be when she walked into this room, she was about to be interviewed instead.
