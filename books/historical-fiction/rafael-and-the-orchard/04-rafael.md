## 4. Rafael

The woman who ran the lodging house above the bakery — a widow named Marta with flour in the creases of her knuckles and a way of looking through people rather than at them — told Ulyana that the workshop took in boarders only when it suited them, and that it never suited them for long.

"You want to ask at the packing hall," she said, jerking her chin toward the window, where the river fog was lifting off Vetrov's crooked streets in long gray ribbons. "The Ostrov man. He keeps winter workers sometimes, when the crates come down from the hill orchards. But he'll look at you first. He looks at everyone first."

"And if he doesn't like what he sees?"

Marta shrugged, and the gesture seemed to contain the whole philosophy of the town: things went as they went, and ice closed when ice closed. "Then you sleep in my attic another night and pay me for it, and we both pretend that's a fine arrangement."

Ulyana paid her for one more night anyway, out of caution rather than gratitude, and went down into the streets with her coat buttoned to the throat and the dispatches sewn flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She had spent the previous evening taking inventory of herself, the way she had been trained to take inventory of a room. The bridge was chained. The road east was a rumor of mud and soldiers. The road west led back toward a capital that had stopped answering its telegraphs. Which left Vetrov: four hundred houses, two churches of equal and competing authority, a market square where an old man sold frozen pears by weight, and one fruit-packing workshop whose chimney had been breathing steadily since dawn.

She could wait here. That was the sensible arithmetic — sit in Marta's attic until the thaw, spend her coin carefully, keep her face forgettable. But waiting had its own signature, and Ulyana had learned in three years of courier work that idleness drew eyes faster than motion did. A woman who did nothing in a small town became a question. A woman who worked became furniture.

So: work.

The workshop stood at the bottom of Kuznechny Lane, where the street gave up on cobblestones and surrendered to packed earth, close enough to the river that she could hear the ice ticking inside itself, a sound like a clock wound too tight. It was larger than she'd expected from the outside — a long timber building with a slate roof furred white with frost, a loading dock facing the lane, and behind it, rising up the slope, row upon row of bare apple trees pruned into shapes so disciplined they looked less planted than stationed. Beyond the trees, a smaller stone building with a heavy door: the press house, by the smell of it, cider pressed in October still haunting the air.

She smelled the workshop before she reached the door. Cold apples, wood shavings, lamp oil, and beneath it all something faintly chemical and sweet that she couldn't name and didn't try to — her training had taught her that curiosity was a muscle best exercised selectively.

The loading dock doors stood half open against the cold. Inside, lamplight fell in amber pools across trestle tables stacked with crates, and among the crates moved perhaps a dozen figures, mostly women, their hands quick and red with cold, sorting fruit from bushel baskets into graded piles. The sound was soft and constant: the knock of apple against apple, the whisper of straw.

Nobody looked up. That in itself was information. In a town this size, a stranger on the doorstep would ordinarily draw at least a glance; a workforce trained not to glance had either excellent discipline or excellent fear.

"You're standing in the light."

The voice came from the shadow between two stacks of crates, and Ulyana kept herself from turning too quickly, which cost her something, because every instinct she owned insisted on turning immediately. She let her gaze travel instead — table, crates, hands — and found him the way one finds the second soldier in a room: by elimination.

He was younger than she expected and older than he looked, which she understood later was a thing the war had done to a whole generation, stamping thirty-year-olds with fifty-year-old eyes. Tall without being broad, dressed in a worker's canvas apron over a wool coat too good for the wearing, his dark hair cut short and going gray at the temples in a way that suggested haste rather than age. He held a ledger under one arm and a pencil behind his ear, and he stood with the particular stillness of a man who has learned that movement attracts fire.

"I'm sorry?" she said, in the flat provincial accent she'd put on somewhere outside Novo-Karev and worn ever since like borrowed boots.

"The doorway." He nodded past her. "You're backlit. Anyone in the lane sees your whole outline and none of your face. Come in or move along."

It was not a welcome. It was an instruction delivered with the mildness of a man discussing weather, and Ulyana noted — filed, actually, in the cold little drawer at the back of her mind where useful things went — that he had assessed the tactical geometry of his own doorway before assessing her.

"I'm looking for work," she said. "I was told you take winter hands."

"Who told you?"

"Marta, above the bakery."

"Marta tells everyone that. Marta wants her attic empty and her rent paid." He came forward into the lamplight then, and looked at her.

She had been looked at before — by border guards, by officers of three different governments, once by a man in a café in the capital whom she'd later learned was counting the seconds until he could report her — and she had survived all of it by becoming, under inspection, precisely what the inspector expected: dull, tired, slightly stupid about money. She began assembling that woman now, letting her shoulders drop, letting her mouth go slack with the fatigue of someone who had walked too many roads.

Rafael Ostrov watched the transformation happen.

She felt it happen — felt him watch it — the way one feels a hand pass over one's belongings in the dark, checking each item without moving it. His gaze went from her boots (road-worn, resoled twice, the left one newer than the right) to her hands (she had remembered to roughen them with cold water and lye before leaving Marta's, but he lingered there a moment too long) to her collar, her jaw, the set of her eyes. And then he did the thing almost no one did: he stopped looking at her entirely, turned to the nearest crate of apples, and picked one up, examining its skin as though she had already gone.

"Where were you working before?"

"Karev. A textile house. The looms stopped in September."

"Family?"

"My mother's in Karev. My father's dead."

"Can you lift forty kilos?"

"Yes."

"It's not a strength question," he said mildly, still studying the apple. "It's an honesty question. Most people say yes because they want the wage, and then I find out in a week they can't, and then I've lost a week." He set the apple down and looked at her again, briefly, and there was something in the brevity that was more unnerving than any sustained stare — the economy of a man rationing even his attention. "Forty kilos is a full crate of winters off the hill orchards. We're short-handed because the men who used to do this are—" He paused, chose a word. "Elsewhere. Can you start now?"

"Now?"

"Wages are twenty kopecks a day and dinner. You sleep upstairs over the drying loft, alone, which suits most of my workers since they're local and have homes. Board is separate from wage." He said it all without inflection, terms laid out like coins on a counter. "One condition."

Here it is, she thought. Here is the price, whatever it is.

"No questions about the crates," he said. "What goes in them, what comes out of them, why some of them are heavier than they ought to be. You'll see things in the course of honest work. You saw nothing. Not because I'm ashamed of it — most of it's smuggling brandy past the customs post at Sarny, and the customs officer drinks half of it himself — but because questions are how towns get burned. Someone asks a question, someone answers it wrong, and six weeks later there are soldiers in the square asking better ones." He tilted his head, and for the first time something moved in his face that wasn't calculation. It might have been humor. It might have been its funeral mask. "You seem like a woman who understands that trade. Silence for wages."

"I understand it," she said.

"I thought you might."

There it was — the hook under the words, set gently, almost courteously. *I thought you might.* She kept her face still and her heartbeat disciplined, and made herself ask, in the voice of a tired textile worker with no imagination: "Why? Because I'm not from here?"

"Because you walked into my doorway and checked the sightlines before you checked the room." He picked up his ledger. "Start tomorrow at dawn. Marta will tell you where the drying loft stair is. Don't be late; the hill carts come early and the frost doesn't wait."

* * *

That night she lay in the narrow bed above the drying loft, wrapped in two blankets and the smell of apples slowly dehydrating below her floorboards, and conducted the interview again in memory, line by line, the way she'd been taught to reconstruct interrogations — hers and other people's.

He had seen through her in under a minute. That was the fact she circled, unable to leave it alone. Three years of tradecraft, a dozen crossings, and a fruit merchant in a river town had read her posture like a telegram. Either he was unusually perceptive — possible; such men existed, usually in police services — or he knew what to look for because he had been the thing he was looking at. People fluent in concealment recognize the accent. It was like hearing a fellow native speaker in a foreign country: the grammar gave them away to each other while remaining invisible to everyone else.

And he had hired her anyway.

That was the part she kept returning to as the frost feathered the little window and the river groaned distantly in the dark. A cautious man — and everything about him was cautious, the rationed glances, the pre-assessed doorway, the ledger held like a shield — a cautious man does not hire the stranger he has identified as dangerous. Unless the hiring is itself the caution. Unless he wanted her where he could see her, sorting his apples inside his walls, on the theory that a watched stranger is a known quantity and an unwatched one is a hole in the map.

Or unless he needed something, and had recognized in her bearing exactly what he needed.

She turned onto her side. Below her, the drying loft breathed its warm apple breath up through the boards. Somewhere across the town a dog barked twice and stopped, and the ice on the river said something long and low that was not quite a word.

She had come here carrying a dead government's secrets sewn into her coat, planning to hide in plain sight until the water opened. Instead she had walked into a workshop run by a man who read strangers like correspondence, who employed women who didn't glance up, who smuggled brandy and possibly other things in double-bottomed crates, and who had offered her silence for wages in a tone that suggested he'd made the same offer before, or received it, or lived his entire life inside it.

Tomorrow at dawn, then. Apples. Crates. Forty kilos, honestly lifted.

She closed her eyes and, being who she was, began planning the layout of the packing hall: the position of the tables relative to the loading doors, the sightline from the drying loft stair, the location — she'd noticed it on her way in and made herself not look twice — of the small iron-banded door set low in the press house wall, padlocked, swept clean of snow in a radius where everything else wore its frost undisturbed.

Sleep took her eventually, but it went around the workshop first, walking its perimeter, checking its doors.

* * *

Dawn at the workshop smelled of paraffin and cold iron. The hill carts arrived as promised, sledges drawn by shaggy ponies, laden with crates of winter varieties — russets, a dark red apple the locals called *krovavka*, blood-apple, for the color of its flesh when cut — and the day resolved into rhythm: unload, grade, sort, pack. Ulyana learned the grading system from a wide-hipped woman called Danya, who spoke to her only in instructions and otherwise ignored her with a thoroughness that felt deliberate, communal, rehearsed. *Winters to the left. Bruised to the middle bin. Anything with a break in the skin goes to the press, don't argue with me, rot travels.*

By midmorning her shoulders had found the ache they'd carry for a week, and she had counted — without appearing to count — eleven crates whose bottoms sat too high by two fingers' width, and one sledge driver who received a sealed parcel from the office at the back with the deference of a man handling something other than fruit.

At noon they broke for dinner in the packing hall, black bread and salted cabbage and tea from an urn, eaten standing, and Ulyana was reaching for her cup when the hall went quiet — not gradually, but all at once, the way a room quiets for a person rather than a sound.

Rafael Ostrov had come in from the office, and he was not alone. With him, talking, was the customs officer from Sarny, a fat man in a green coat with a face like risen dough, and the contrast between them — the dough and the blade, she thought, watching Ostrov's easy smile, his open hands, his laughter pitched to carry just far enough — was instructive. She ate her bread and watched the performance sideways, the way Danya did, the way all of them did: the officer complaining, Ostrov agreeing, a crate produced, a bottle produced from the crate, the officer's mood visibly sweetening, the transaction dissolving into the ordinary commerce of a border town where everyone stole from everyone at agreed rates.

But once — only once, for less than a second, while the officer's attention was on the bottle — Ostrov's eyes crossed the room and rested on her.

Not on the room. On her. A look of brief, level assessment, like a man confirming that a piece placed on the board yesterday was still standing where he'd left it.

Then the officer laughed at something, and the look was gone, and Ostrov was pouring brandy with the genial weariness of a small businessman humoring a large official, and the hall resumed its noise, and the afternoon came on, and the crates got packed.

Ulyana sorted blood-apples until her fingers went numb, and thought about the iron-banded door in the press house wall, and the two-fingered false bottoms, and the columnar figures she'd glimpsed in the ledger he carried under his arm — neat vertical strokes, grouped in fours, that no fruit merchant had any earthly reason to keep.

And she thought: *he knows what I am, or he knows what I might be, and he has put me inside his walls on purpose.*

She should have been afraid. Some disciplined compartment of her registered that she should be afraid, the way a thermometer registers temperature without feeling it. What she felt instead, standing in the lamplight among the winter apples while the river ticked toward spring outside, was something she hadn't permitted herself since the capital: the low, unwelcome thrill of a puzzle beginning to open under her hands.

Across the hall, Rafael Ostrov closed his ledger, and the click of it sounded, in the quiet after the day's last cart, very much like a lock turning.
