## 5. The Orchard in Winter

The frost came down from the hills on the first of December, and Rafael Ostrov walked his rows before sunrise because that was when he could pretend the orchard belonged to him alone.

He moved slowly, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a man inspecting rather than a man grieving, though the two postures had long since become identical. Two hundred and forty trees, if you counted the eleven young ones he'd put in along the south wall three seasons past — and he did count them, always, because counting was a habit the old bureau had burned into him so deeply that not even seven years of apples and cider had scoured it out. Two hundred and forty. He knew each trunk by its scar. The Cox's Orange Pippen with lightning damage on its north face, healed into a knot like a closed fist. The Bramley that leaned, patient as a tired clerk, toward the river. The row of Winter Bananas nobody local would plant anymore because they ripened too late and froze too early, and which Rafael kept anyway for reasons he had never once said aloud: they had been his mother's.

The ice was coming downriver. He could feel it before he saw it — a change in the air's grain, a thinness at the edge of sound. In another week, perhaps ten days, the Vetrov bridge would close its gates with a rattle of chain and the town would fold itself up like a letter no one would open until spring. He had watched this happen twenty-two times now. Twenty-two winters since he had come to this ground with nothing but a forged agricultural certificate, a set of grafting knives, and a memory full of numbers that could still, if he let them, kill men whose faces he had never seen.

He stopped at the third row and put his bare hand against an apple still hanging low on a bough, forgotten by the pickers or deliberately left. It had frozen and thawed through four cycles now; the skin was translucent as wax, the flesh beneath gone to something soft and brown and sweet-smelling. Bletted, the English called it. Rotten in the gentlest possible way. He pressed his thumb into it and felt it give, warm-cold, yielding, and thought: there is a word for fruit kept past its season until sweetness and decay are indistinguishable, and I have never found it, and I suspect it is my name.

* * *

He had been good at ciphers. That was the thing people never understood about those years, the thing that made the confessions of lesser men sound like boasting when he tried to explain it even to himself in the dark hours before the stove caught. He had been *good*. Not competent, not adequate — good, in the way that some men are good with horses or good with grief. Numbers arranged themselves for him. Where others saw noise, he saw the seam where the noise had been sewn, and he could pick it open with his fingernails. At twenty-six he had run the section's traffic analysis for the whole western corridor. At twenty-eight he had designed the columnar system himself — the one with the double transposition keyed to the date and the day's shipping manifests, unbreakable in practice, elegant almost to the point of vanity — and men had died on schedule because his schedules were correct, and other men had lived on schedule because his corrections arrived in time. He had told himself then, as everyone in that building told themselves, that he was only the instrument. That the hands holding him belonged to the republic, and the republic was a body worth keeping alive.

It had been true enough, probably. Truths were cheap in those years. What had never been true was the corollary he had built his whole self upon: that being an instrument kept you clean. Instruments got cleaned. Instruments got put away. And when the rot came up through the ministry the way blight comes up through rootstock — invisible, systemic, everywhere at once — the instruments were the first things cut out and burned, because instruments remembered everything and could prove nothing and were, above all, replaceable.

He had not been replaceable. That had been his second mistake. The first had been believing in the work.

* * *

The winter of his fall — the winter of the purges, the town still called it, though the word was too grand for what had actually happened, which was a series of quiet arrests conducted between lunch and dinner — Rafael had spent six weeks doing what he did best, which was reading traffic. He could see the shape of it coming through the wires: the new committees interrogating the old ministries, the old ministries denouncing their own subsections, names moving through the cipher channels like stones rolling downhill, gaining weight and speed. He had watched his own section chief's name arrive in a dispatch one Tuesday morning, flagged for review, and had spent that entire day at his desk transcribing routine freight manifests while somewhere across the city men were asking his chief questions in a room with a drain in the floor.

On Wednesday his own name came through. Not flagged — *pre-flagged*, which was worse, which meant someone upstream had already decided the answer and was merely waiting for the paperwork to arrive at its destination. He remembered the moment precisely: the lamplight, the tea going cold beside his elbow, the small scratch of his pen as he finished the line he was working on before allowing himself to stop. Finishing what you start. He would meet a woman someday, though he didn't know it yet, who believed exactly that, and the recognition would go through him like a blade finding an old wound.

He had forty-eight hours. He knew how these things moved; he had helped design the machinery of their movement. Forty-eight hours before his name completed its journey and became an instruction.

And here was the part he had never told anyone, the part that sat in him like the bletted apple sits in its skin — sweet, rotten, preserved. He had not spent those forty-eight hours destroying files, or warning colleagues, or running. He had spent them negotiating.

There was a man named Kessler — not his real name, no one's real name in those rooms — who handled the practical end of the committees' housekeeping. Rafael went to him not with money, of which he had little, and not with pleas, which were worthless, but with inventory. He laid it out the way he laid out everything: columns, dates, cross-references. He knew the locations of three dead drops still active from the corridor network. He knew the real names behind six operational pseudonyms, including two men in his own subsection who had done nothing worse than move papers with too much care. He knew the route by which certain funds had traveled from the arms bureau into certain private accounts — knowledge that was dangerous not to the regime but to particular men now sitting on the committees, men who had been enthusiastic participants in the looting and had no desire to see the ledger of it published.

He offered all of it. And in exchange he asked for one thing only, which was why the bargain had taken Kessler three days to believe: not escape abroad, not money, not a position. A parcel of land outside a river town called Vetrov, and a certificate declaring one Rafael Ostrov to be a fruit-grower of no political record whatsoever. A grave, essentially. A place to be buried alive among trees.

Kessler had looked at the list of names Rafael slid across the table — six names, two of whom would be shot within the year, none of whom had ever harmed Rafael or anyone else except by being careful — and had said, with something almost like professional respect, *"You've costed this very well."*

*"I've costed everything very well,"* Rafael had said. *"That's what I'm for."*

They shook hands. He could still feel it sometimes, in cold weather: Kessler's grip, dry and soft as paper.

* * *

The sun was fully up now, gray and low, laying long shadows westward from every trunk so that the orchard seemed to march away from him, row after row, an army of the pruned and patient. He took out his pruning knife more from ritual than necessity — the serious cutting waited for deep dormancy — and turned a dead spur off the Pippen with his thumb.

Two of the six names had haunted him in ways the others hadn't. Marta Ilves, who had taught him the columnar system's ancestor when he was nineteen and hungry and brilliant and grateful. She had been arrested in March of that same winter, eight weeks after his signature — not his signature, his *information* — put her in a drawer somewhere. She had sent him a card once, years before, when he passed his examinations: a picture of an orchard in blossom, of all things, with a single line on the back. *Plant things, Rafael. It's the only work that doesn't lie.* He had kept the card through every search and seizure and move of his old life, folded into a book of nursery catalogues, and had burned it the night before he signed the certificate for this ground, because keeping it felt like a kind of theft — stealing her forgiveness in advance for spending her.

The other name was a boy, really. Tomas Reval, twenty-three, who ran the courier schedules for the northern leg and who had once covered for Rafael's own arithmetic error at a cost to himself he never mentioned and Rafael never acknowledged. When Rafael's list reached the right desk, Tomas had been reassigned to a garrison posting on the eastern marshes, which was the committee's idea of mercy, and had died there inside eighteen months — not shot, not tried, just swallowed, the way the marshes swallowed men. Rafael had learned of it from a newspaper two years late, standing in this very orchard with a crate of Windfalls in his arms, and had put the crate down and walked to the river and stood there until dark, and had come back and finished the pressing, because that was what he did now. He finished things. It was the only virtue left to him and it was not a virtue at all; it was a sentence being served in installments.

Guilt, he had learned, did not burn off like shame did. Shame you could sweat out through work, through drink, through the ordinary abrasions of a life. Guilt preserved itself. It sat in you the way fruit sat in syrup in the jars lined along his cellar wall — sealed against the air, sweetened past honesty, darkening a shade every year, perfectly edible and faintly wrong. You could live on it. He had. Seven years of living on it, and the jars multiplied every autumn, and he never gave any away.

* * *

He heard the gate below the slope — the workshop gate, iron on stone — and did not turn around immediately. There was only one person it could be at this hour besides himself, and he wanted another moment, one more moment, of being a man alone with his trees instead of a man being observed among them.

The woman. Almeida. Four days under his roof now, sleeping in the loft above the packing hall, sorting apples with hands that were too precise for the work and pretending, competently, that they weren't. He had known within the first hour what she was — the bearing gave it away, the way she entered a room last and stood where she could see the door, the way her eyes went to his ledgers lying open on the hall table with a hunger she thought she'd hidden. Couriers, field officers, the handlers of small deadly documents: they all carried themselves the same way, weight forward, listening to the floor. He had trained half of them once. He could have taught her herself, in another life, in the building with the drains.

She appeared at the head of the row now, breath steaming, wrapped in the wool coat she never quite stopped wearing indoors, and stopped a few paces off with the courtesy of someone used to not startling armed men — or men she suspected might be armed, which amounted to the same discipline.

"The ice is talking," she said. Her accent placed her a hundred miles east and educated; her caution placed her anywhere at all.

"It does that at night first," Rafael said. "Groaning. Like an old door. By tomorrow you'll hear it from the loft."

"Is it dangerous? The crossing?"

"The crossing is dangerous in every season. In thaw it's suicide." He looked at her — the direct look he permitted himself rarely, the one that stripped the fruit-grower off him like bark — and saw her register it, and file it, and decide not to react. Good instincts. Bad luck, arriving here. Or good luck. With her sort it was never clear which until afterward, and sometimes not even then.

"You're pruning," she said. "In December."

"I'm thinking. The knife is an excuse." He held it up briefly, blade catching the flat light, and watched her watch it. "Come. If you're going to stand in my orchard, you'll hold the ladder."

She fell into step beside him, and he found himself aware of her the way he was aware of weather systems — pressure changing, something gathering beyond the hills. He had spent seven years keeping this ground clean by keeping it empty: no visitors, no correspondence, no patterns for anyone to read. And in four days this woman had made it a place where things happened again. He should have turned her out into the snow on the first night. He told himself he hadn't because turning out a stranger in a town crawling with patrols was its own kind of exposure, and that was even true. But underneath the truth lay the older truth, sweeter and browner by the year: that he was tired of preserving himself in syrup. That part of him — the nineteen-year-old who had wept over a cipher's elegance, the boy Marta Ilves had told to plant things — had looked at her competent, frightened, determined face and recognized the work arriving at his door at last, the bill come due after seven years of installments.

They reached the end of the row where the ladder stood against the Bramley. He steadied it while she climbed, and from below he watched her reach into the high branches with those too-precise hands, lifting the frozen windfalls down one by one, and he thought about the list in the false bottom of his writing desk. Six names, two decades old, written in his own hand in the ink of survival. And beneath it, newer, drafted twice and torn up twice and drafted a third time in October: another dispatch, unfinished, addressed to authorities who did not yet exist, confessing everything, surrendering everything, signing himself over at last to whatever justice sprouted from the ruins. He had not been able to finish it. Some instrument in him still refused the final entry.

Below them, faint through the bare branches, the river groaned — a long, grinding note, like a door opening somewhere far underground.

Ulyana Almeida went still on the ladder, an apple in her hand, listening.

"That," Rafael said quietly, "is the sound of your road closing."
