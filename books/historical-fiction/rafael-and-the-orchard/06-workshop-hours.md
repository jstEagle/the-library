## 6. Workshop Hours

The days in Vetrov arranged themselves around the workshop the way water arranges itself around a stone.

Ulyana learned its rhythm within a week: Rafael came down before dawn to fire the boiler, and by the time the light through the packing hall's tall windows had turned from grey to gold, the smell of apples and lamp oil was already thick enough to taste. The hall itself was long and low, built of blackened timber that had once been a granary or a chapel — no one would say which — with a sawdust floor packed hard as clay and a row of sorting tables running its length like pews. Above them, strung on wires, hung dried apple rings in pale constellations, turning slowly whenever the door opened, as if the whole room breathed.

Her hands were supposed to be learning this work. Her mind was doing something else entirely.

She had told herself, in those first days, that she was only being careful. A courier stranded behind a closing border did not survive by relaxing her attention; she survived by noticing everything and appearing to notice nothing. So she noticed. That was all. She noticed how the crates were built.

They were handsome crates — that was the first thing anyone would say about them. Pine slats planed smooth, corners dovetailed without a nail showing, stenciled on their flanks with the workshop's mark: an orchard tree bent under fruit, and beneath it, in neat block letters, OSTROV & SONS, EST. 1894. There were no sons. Ulyana had counted the people who worked the hall — four men, two women, old Marta who ran the press house, and Rafael himself — and none of them answered to *son*. The stencil was a fiction, like so much else here, but it was a well-aged fiction, faded into respectability the way a forged signature fades when it has been signing things for twenty years.

She discovered the double bottoms on her ninth morning, lifting a crate of Winesaps from the stacking rack near the loading doors. It should have weighed more than it did. That was the thought that snagged her — not suspicion, not yet, just the small arithmetic of a body that had carried luggage across six borders and knew what weight was supposed to feel like. She set the crate down, ran her thumb along the inside seam, and felt it: a hairline gap where there should have been none, a second floor sitting perhaps three centimeters above the true bottom, invisible unless you knew to look for the way the grain of the inner boards ran crosswise to the outer ones.

Three centimeters. Enough for paper. Enough for a great deal of paper.

She put the crate down as if she had noticed nothing, because she had noticed nothing, officially, and spent the rest of that morning sorting bruised Gravensteins into the cull barrel while her pulse performed quiet gymnastics behind her sternum.

*It's smuggling*, she told herself. *Of course it's smuggling.* Every river town on a contested border lived off smuggling the way a mill lived off its stream; double-bottomed crates were practically the municipal coat of arms. Brandy, tobacco, letters from emigres to their mothers, ammunition if the times were bad enough — and these times were bad enough. There was nothing in it. Nothing at all.

Except that the crates went out marked as fruit, and fruit was weighed at customs, and a man as careful as Rafael Ostrov plainly was — a man who planed his own crate slats and kept his stove swept and his ledgers squared — would know exactly how much a false bottom could add before a weighmaster's eyebrow lifted. He would pack the top layer heavy. He would account for every gram. And the space between the floors would hold whatever mattered, and whatever mattered in a town like this, in a winter like this one, was very rarely brandy.

She caught herself smiling over the cull barrel and stopped immediately, horrified at herself. This was the trouble with people like her — people who had been trained, however briefly, however reluctantly, to see the architecture underneath ordinary life. Once you started seeing it, you could not stop, and worse, you *enjoyed* it. There was a pleasure in it that she distrusted profoundly, the same pleasure she had felt at nineteen, cracking her first practice cipher in a government classroom while outside the window students marched and were dispersed, and she sat warm and fed and absorbed in a puzzle that had nothing to do with the blood on the cobblestones below.

History happened elsewhere, she had always believed. To other people. Her job — the job she had accepted in the capital with a handshake and a coat whose lining now lay against her skin day and night like a second nervous system — was merely to carry paper from one place to another. She was a bridge, and bridges did not ask what crossed them.

But bridges did not lie awake counting the ways a crate might be built.

* * *

The ledgers were worse. Or better. She could not decide which word frightened her more.

Rafael kept the accounts himself, in a back corner of the hall at a desk so cluttered with pruning knives, twine spools, and jars of grafting wax that it looked less like an office than an altar to minor domestic gods. Twice a week he brought out the ledger books — fat, canvas-bound volumes with marbled covers gone soft at the corners — and entered the week's sales and shipments in a hand so regular it might have been printed.

Ulyana was sweeping near the desk on a Thursday afternoon when he was called away to settle a dispute about freight rates with a barge man who had come up from the landing ice. He left the ledger open. She noted later that she could not remember whether he had left it open deliberately, and the fact that she could not remember bothered her more than anything on the page itself.

What was on the page was this: columns of figures, impeccably neat. Bushels sold. Kilograms pressed. Crates shipped, numbered sequentially. All perfectly plausible, all perfectly boring — except that the numbers were wrong.

Not arithmetically wrong. Structurally wrong. She understood it only after reading the page three times, her broom propped forgotten against the wall, her breath shallow. The figures obeyed rules that accounting does not follow. Certain digits appeared only in certain positions. Certain rows ended in patterns — a five, then a two, then nothing, then a five again — with a frequency that chance would have blushed to produce. The totals column did not always equal the sum of its parts; sometimes it exceeded them by amounts too small to matter commercially and too consistent to be accidental. And every few entries, a figure was written slightly heavier than the rest, the nib pressed harder, as though marking it — *this one, pay attention* — in a language made of ink pressure rather than words.

She knew that language. Not fluently — never fluently; she had been a student of the discipline, not a practitioner, and the war had eaten most of the people who were fluent — but she recognized its bones the way one recognizes the skeleton of a face once loved. Columnar substitution, keyed to position, with emphasis-marks carrying secondary meaning. It was the method used by the old Foreign Ministry's field bureaus before the collapse, taught in a room in the capital where Ulyana had sat for eleven months learning to build such tables and, more importantly, to break them. They had called the training exercise *bookkeeping*. The instructors had laughed at their own joke until they weren't laughing anymore, in the last autumn, when the bookkeepers began disappearing.

She swept the same square meter of floor for four minutes. Then Rafael came back in, still arguing cheerfully about freight rates, and closed the ledger, and looked at her — just for a moment, just long enough — with an expression she could not read at all.

"Floor won't clean itself twice," he said mildly.

"No," she agreed. "It won't."

* * *

That night she lay in the narrow bed above the packing hall and listened to the building talk to itself.

Old timber structures spoke after dark — joists ticking as they cooled, the boiler muttering in its iron throat, wind working at the shutters like a patient thief. Below her floor she could hear, occasionally, the sound of Rafael moving: footsteps crossing the hall, the scrape of the press house door, once — she checked her watch against the church bells — the unmistakable metallic click of a lock turned at half past one in the morning. What kind of orchard needed locking at half past one in the morning? Fruit did not escape. Cider did not conspire.

She got up, wrapped the blanket around her shoulders, and stood at the window. Below, the yard lay blue under a waning moon, the stacked crates throwing shadows like a little city, and beyond the wall the orchard itself, leafless and rigid, row upon row of trees standing in the frost like an audience waiting for something to begin. The river showed as a pale band beyond it, already white at the edges, holding its breath.

Somewhere out there, she reminded herself, patrols were searching towns along this stretch of the border for smugglers, deserters, and — according to the last rumor Marta had carried up from the market with the flour — a woman courier carrying sealed dispatches, description circulated, reward offered. Somewhere out there, the republic she served was dying on schedule, and the papers sewn into her coat were rotting by inches in the damp, and every day she stayed in Vetrov was a day closer to the thaw, when the waterways would open and her hunters could move freely again.

She should be planning. Escape routes. Cover stories. The weight of the ice on the river and the number of nights until it might bear her. That was the work. That was the only work.

Instead she found herself lying back down and thinking about the ledgers.

There was a thrill in it that she refused to dignify with analysis. For eight days she had been a pair of hands among other pairs of hands, sorting fruit, invisible, purposeless, a courier with nowhere to deliver — and purposelessness, she discovered, corroded her faster than fear ever had. Fear gave you edges. Purposelessness dissolved them. But now there was a puzzle in the walls of her refuge, a locked door inside the house that sheltered her, and her mind had seized on it with an appetite that shamed her. Because the puzzle was not hers. The puzzle belonged to a man who had offered her silence and wages and a warm corner by the boiler, and prying at his secrets from inside his own roof was not investigation; it was ingratitude wearing a clever mask.

And yet.

The crates were double-bottomed. The books were cipher practice — no, more than practice; practice grows stale, but those figures had the living irregularity of real traffic, real messages dressed as bushels and kilograms, going out weekly with the fruit shipments to buyers downstream who were perhaps not buyers of fruit at all. Which meant Rafael Ostrov, keeper of apples, planer of pine slats, was either a smuggler with literary pretensions or a functioning intelligence asset operating out of a packing shed on the edge of a drowning republic.

And if the latter — then he had equipment. Methods. Contacts. Perhaps even a transmitter, though the thought of that seemed too much to hope for, and she made herself stop hoping it, because hope was a luxury that couriers could not afford and she had already spent too much of it staring at the ceiling imagining lamplight and brass keys and her dispatches finally, finally singing southward over the frost.

She turned her face to the wall. *You are here to hide, not to investigate,* she told herself firmly. *Every hour you spend decoding his books is an hour you don't spend surviving.*

The thought was correct and completely ineffective. She fell asleep composing key-tables against the dark.

* * *

By the second week she had stopped pretending, even to herself, that the sweeping was incidental.

She engineered her hours in the hall so that her route with the broom passed the desk at predictable moments. She volunteered for crate-stacking near the office corner. She memorized, across four stolen glances, the layout of the current ledger's left-hand pages, and reconstructed from memory each evening, in pencil, on the backs of apple labels, the figures she'd seen — building, line by line, a private facsimile of the book. It was slow work. It was delicious work. It was, she admitted somewhere around the tenth night, hunched over her label-scraps by the light of a shielded candle, exactly the kind of work she had sworn off forever when she took the courier's oath: the oath had said *carry, do not open; move, do not linger; observe, do not analyze* — and here she was analyzing, lingering, opening everything in sight except the one thing sewn into her coat.

Perhaps that was why. She considered this honestly, because dishonesty with oneself was a luxury for people with futures. Perhaps she dug at Rafael's secrets because they were not hers — because his ledger held no lists of names destined for executioners, no orders she would have to carry or refuse, only clean abstract figures that asked nothing of her conscience. His cipher was a holiday from her cargo. She could solve puzzles all night as long as none of them was the puzzle strapped against her ribs.

The reconstruction was going well. Too well. On the eleventh evening she laid out her label-scraps in sequence on the bedspread and saw, with a cold bloom of triumph, that three weeks of Rafael's "sales" resolved cleanly under a positional key she had half-remembered from her training — and the plaintext fragments, where they emerged, were not invoices at all. One fragment read, in the clipped shorthand of bureau traffic: *...landing ice unsafe after the 14th... second channel preferred...* Another: *...no movement expected from the capital before thaw, advise patience...*

Advice. Advising whom? About what? Patience toward what?

She sat very still, the candle guttering, the fragments arranged before her like a hand of cards she had cheated to win, and felt the two halves of her situation grind against each other like the plates of the freezing river. If Rafael was transmitting intelligence — whose? To whom? The old regime's remnant networks still existed, ghost-handed, scattered; the new authorities had their own web; third powers fished these waters with long lines. And she, Ulyana Almeida, courier of a falling government, was living in the rafters of a station that belonged to someone, working for him, eating his bread, warming herself at his fire, while she picked the locks of his correspondence by candlelight in his own guest room.

If he discovered her — when he discovered her; men like Rafael discovered things, it was clearly his profession —

The candle fluttered in a draft from the shutter. She cupped it, watched the flame steady, and understood with sudden clarity what the next step had to be. Not more copying. More copying was cowardice dressed as diligence. The fragments she had were enough to prove she knew; anything further would only deepen the crime without changing its shape. No — the next step was to stop hiding in the margins and walk straight into the middle of it. To go down tomorrow, or the day after, when the hall was empty, and stand at his desk, and say the thing that could not be unsaid: that she had read his books, that she knew what they were, and that she had something of her own that might make them useful to each other.

Her heart beat fast and unpleasantly at the thought, part terror, part — there was no honest word for the other part except *hunger*. Purpose, arriving uninvited, tasting of danger and apples.

She blew out the candle. In the dark she could hear the river, faintly, a long groan traveling upstream through the forming ice like a word trying to become a sentence.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I stop being a ghost.

Below her, at half past one, the lock clicked.
