## 8. Terms

Rafael Ostrov had learned long ago that the hardest bargains were not the ones struck with enemies. Enemies could be read like weather — you watched their fronts, counted their men, estimated the damage and paid it or ran. The difficult negotiations were the ones conducted across a kitchen table with someone who spoke your own language of concealment, because then every silence carried freight, and every honesty might be a coin minted for the occasion.

Ulyana Almeida sat at his kitchen table now, in the small room above the press house where he took his meals alone, and she was doing to him exactly what he would have done to her: waiting.

The lamp between them burned low. He had trimmed the wick twice out of habit, though neither of them had asked for more light. On the table lay the ledger pages she had copied — his ledger pages, his columnar figures, his dead bureau's private arithmetic — transcribed in her neat courier's hand onto three sheets of paper that might as well have been warrants. She had folded them once, lengthwise, and set them down with the fold toward him, the way one returns a borrowed knife. Not accusing. Just returning it, handle first, so that whatever happened next would be understood to be his own act.

"You caught me," she said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become its own argument. "I'd rather you said so than pretended otherwise."

"I haven't decided what I'm pretending yet." He kept his voice even. Under the table, where she couldn't see, his thumb pressed against the knuckle of his forefinger, an old habit from the cipher rooms in the capital, where a man under interrogation learned to give his hands something honest to do. "It's been eleven years since anyone sat in this house and knew what those columns were. I'd gotten fond of being only an orchardist."

"Are you?"

"Ask the apples."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile; a recognition, maybe, that they were both going to keep fencing until the terms were spoken aloud. She had good eyes. That was the trouble with couriers; the state selected for people who could hold a stranger's gaze while lying about their destination, and then the state taught them nothing about what to do afterward, in kitchens, in winters, with other survivors.

"The dispatches," she said. "You know why I'm here."

"I know you came over the pass after the bridge closed. I know your boots were resoled in the capital — the stitching is a military pattern. And I know that a woman who spends her days sorting apples by lamplight is spending her nights copying a dead man's ledgers." He leaned back. The chair creaked, a domestic sound, absurd against the weight of what sat between them. "What I don't know is whether you're here alone, or whether 'alone' is the story you were given."

"I'm alone." A pause, precise as a cut. "That part's true."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is sealed."

He almost laughed. Eleven years, and the regime's habits still fit her like a tailored coat. *Sealed.* As if paper could protect anyone anymore. As if seals had ever protected anything but the careers of the men who applied them. He had spent four years in the old cipher bureau watching sealed things travel — sealed orders, sealed lists, sealed apologies from ministries that would be corpses within the decade — and he had learned the only lesson the work offered: the seal protects the sender. The carrier carries. The recipient reads. And everyone whose name is inside the paper learns about the seal's protection far too late and entirely without gratitude.

"There's a patrol in Vetrov," he said instead. "Two gendarmes billeted at the inn since November, plus the customs man who drinks. They're bored. Bored men read strangers the way priests read sinners — closely, and hoping to find something. If they find you—"

"They won't."

"That's not an answer. That's a hope wearing an answer's clothes."

Her chin lifted a degree. He watched her decide how much contempt she could afford, and settle on a measured portion. "I've crossed three borders in two years, Mr. Ostrov. I've been searched twice and questioned once. I am still here. Whatever you think my chances are, I've already beaten them."

"In open country, yes. With roads and darkness and distance to spend. This is a town of four hundred people who will all have memorized your face by Sunday. You cannot buy distance here. There isn't any." He rose, went to the window, looked down at the dark shape of the packing hall, the pale rectangles of the orchard beyond it where the snow held the rows of trees like lines of text no one had learned to read yet. "Which means we come to the actual conversation. Because there are only two futures available to you in Vetrov, madam. In one of them, you sit in my cellar until the thaw and pray, and I watch patrols walk past my gate and grow old very quickly. In the other—"

"You help me."

"—we make your problem smaller by making it shared."

He heard her chair shift. When he turned back she was standing too, hands flat on the table, and for the first time since she had arrived at his workshop some six weeks ago, all the way back in the season of early ice, something unguarded had broken through the courier's surface — not fear exactly, and not relief, but the thing underneath both, the raw nerve that professionals carry wrapped in procedure. He recognized it because he had worn it himself for years. It was the look of a person who has been alone with a task so long that the offer of help feels like a trap.

"Why?" she said. "Why would you risk this? Don't tell me it's the old regime's memory. You kept these ledgers dormant for a decade. You let the whole apparatus rot. If you wanted to serve, you had ten winters of opportunities."

*Because I named men,* he thought. *Because I bought this ground with other people's blood and I have been paying interest ever since, and there is a debt in this house that no amount of cider can discharge.* He did not say it. Some confessions must be delivered in writing or not at all — he had learned that too, in another life, drafting denunciations by lamplight in a warm office while cold men waited outside.

"Say instead," he told her, "that I remember how to do it. Say that a man can leave the trade and find, some nights, that the trade has not left him. Say that when you copied those figures you woke something up, and I would rather aim it than let it rattle around this house unsupervised."

"That's not a reason either."

"No," he agreed. "It's a reason-shaped object. We're both carrying several. Mine stay mine."

For a moment he thought she would push. He could see the question forming behind her eyes — *what are you hiding, orchardist? what does a man like you owe and to whom?* — and he braced himself the way one braces for a wave already committed to the shore. But she was a professional, and professionals bank their questions. He watched her bank this one, saw it go into storage behind her face, and felt something that was nearly respect and nearly pity.

"All right," she said. "Terms."

* * *

They negotiated the way he imagined two merchants in a famine year would negotiate over the last sack of grain: politely, precisely, each aware that the price of failure was not merely loss but ruin.

His conditions first, because he had rehearsed them and rehearsal showed.

One: nothing transmitted from Vetrov until the new moon, when the patrol rotations thinned and the customs man's drinking reached its monthly crescendo. She bridled at the delay — he saw her calculate the days, weigh them against whatever clock ticked in her coat lining — but she took the point. Speed killed couriers more often than caution did. He had buried enough of them, professionally speaking, to know.

Two: no transmission from the workshop itself. The attic of the press house held equipment he would not describe to her yet, and the press house had walls thick enough to swallow a signal, but the antenna — the wire, the whole visible anatomy of the thing — would live somewhere else, somewhere deniable, and she would not ask where. She didn't ask. Another point in her favor. Curiosity was a luxury; she rationed hers like everything else.

Three: she would do nothing — nothing — that touched the town's people. No favors asked, no cover sought, no neighbor drawn into her orbit, however innocent the errand appeared. He said this one harder than the others and watched it land wrong, watched her stiffen with the particular pride of someone who has survived by her own wits and does not enjoy being managed.

"People helped me get here," she said.

"People elsewhere. People who've already spent themselves. Here—" and here his voice did something he hadn't authorized; it roughened. "Here the ledger is full. Do you understand me? Every name in this valley that can be spent has been spent, some by me, and I will not add to it for the convenience of your timetable. You work alone or you don't work."

She studied him for a long moment. He had the uncomfortable sensation of a man whose coat has been read through — not the lining, not the sewn pockets, but the body underneath, the old wound under the ribs. She couldn't have known. There was nothing in the room to tell her. And yet her gaze rested on him for a beat too long, the way a doctor's hand pauses over the place that hurts before the patient says a word.

"Agreed," she said quietly. "Alone."

Then her terms, and he should have known she would have them ready, drafted and folded, because this woman wasted nothing — not paper, not movement, not words.

First: he would transmit her dispatches entire. Unread if possible, unread if not — no, she corrected herself, she was not naive, he could read what he must to work the key, but he would alter nothing, strike nothing, append nothing. The content was not his to judge. He was a road, not a gatekeeper.

"Easily given," he said, "because it was never in question."

Second: she required a route home. When the transmissions were done — assuming they were done, assuming the river cooperated, assuming the patrols stayed stupid — she would need passage south, and his orchard, his crates, his double-bottomed boxes bound for markets over the mountains, would provide it. Apples had crossed that border all winter under papers he would not describe. One more crate with a breathing passenger was an extension of an existing trade, not a new enterprise.

He gave her this one slowly, letting her see him weigh it, because a bargain conceded too fast teaches the other party to ask for more. But privately the concession cost less than she believed. He had already decided, weeks ago, in some cellar of himself he didn't visit often, that if she turned out to be what he suspected, she would leave this valley alive. The decision predated her terms. Bargains, he had found, were mostly ceremonies performed over conclusions already reached.

Third—

And here she stopped. He watched her actually hesitate, this woman who had negotiated her way across a continent, and he understood before she spoke that the third term was the real one, the one the first two existed to disguise.

"One dispatch of mine goes with yours," she said. "Not through your hands. Through the same channel, the same night, but sealed from you. My seal, my key. You transmit it blind."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind had come up off the river, and somewhere down in the dark the ice made a sound — not the groan of breaking, not yet, just a settling, a giant shifting its weight in sleep — and the sound seemed to fill the pause the way water fills a footprint.

"A blind transmission," he said. "From my transmitter. Carrying I don't know what, to I don't know whom."

"Yes."

"And if it's a betrayal? If you're selling me even as I sell myself? If that sealed paper names this house, this valley, this foolish orchardist who trusted a courier's smile?"

"Then you'll have been paid in kind," she said, "for whatever you're hiding from me. Which, by the weight of it, is considerable."

There it was. He had wondered when she'd say it plainly. She had been circling his silences for weeks the way a crow circles a frozen field — patient, unhurried, certain that winter gives up everything eventually. And she was right, God help him. The weight of what he hid was considerable. He could feel it nightly, lying awake above the press house: the list of names, eleven years old and perfectly preserved, that lived in his head the way the syrupped fruit lived in his cellar — sealed against time, sweetened against decay, rotting nonetheless at some deep level no sugar could reach.

"Everyone in this business carries one letter they'd burn rather than send," he said.

"Then I'm asking you to trust that mine isn't it. Or rather—" and here she did something he found genuinely unsettling, which was to stop performing. Her voice dropped its courier polish, its diplomatic grain, and became simply tired and young and human. "Or rather, I'm telling you I don't have the luxury of trusting you either, Mr. Ostrov. I have sealed paper in my coat that could end me in nine jurisdictions. I've handed my life to a stranger who runs ciphers out of a fruit-packing shed and won't say why. We are not negotiating trust. Trust left this table an hour ago. We're negotiating terms. Give me mine, or give me back my copies and I'll find another road."

*Another road.* There was no other road. They both knew it; that was the engine under the whole evening. Two survivors at a table in a dying republic, each holding a knife the other needed, each convinced — he could see it in her, absolutely certain of it, the way he was certain in himself — that they were the more honest liar in the room.

He went to the cupboard and took down the bottle of late-harvest cider he kept for occasions he had never had, and poured two measures into mismatched cups, and set one before her.

"My terms stand," he said. "Yours are accepted. All three."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "Just like that?"

"Just like that." He raised his cup slightly, not quite a toast; he wasn't sure his arm could carry the hypocrisy of a toast. "With one addition. One document of mine travels with yours. Sealed from you, keyed to my own system, transmitted blind. You may weigh it, count the pages, curse my name over it — but you don't read it, and you don't refuse it. It rides in the same crate."

"What is it?"

"A confession," he said, and drank, and let the sweetness sit on his tongue the way he let everything sit nowadays — fully tasted and somehow nourishing nothing.

"To whom?"

"To whoever is listening on the other end of the wire. Which is, if my information holds, the same government you serve." He set down the cup. "It concerns events of eleven years ago. It concerns names. Some of them you'd recognize, if the capital still bothers to teach its couriers history. It says what I did to earn this orchard. It says it plainly, in my own hand, under my own name — which is a thing, madam, that I would wager everything in this house you have never once done."

He expected anger. He had prepared for anger, had his defenses laid out like tools. What he got instead was worse: she simply looked at him, and in her looking there was no judgment at all, only the flat professional appraisal of a courier estimating cargo. Weight, bulk, risk. His confession, weighed like fruit.

"It stays sealed," she said at last. "But if it compromises my mission—"

"It doesn't. It compromises only me. That is rather the point of a confession."

"And when it arrives — when your government reads what you did — what happens to you?"

He looked out the window again, at the black orchard, at the rows he had planted the first spring after his pardon, one tree for each name, though he had never told anyone that and never would, except perhaps on paper, soon, in cipher, to strangers with headphones in a distant room.

"Nothing happens to me," he said. "That's what I'm confessing. Nothing ever happened to me. That's the crime the document describes — the acquittal, the pardon, the ground, the trees, the eleven quiet years. I'm sending them the receipt." He smiled, and knew even as he did it that the smile was one of the false ones, the kind he'd perfected in the old bureau, and that she saw through it instantly, and that she filed it away without comment, because she was fluent too.

"So we're agreed," she said. "Your confession and my dispatches, riding together. Each of us carrying the other's worst truth blind."

"Each of us certain we're getting the better of the arrangement."

"Are we?"

"We always believe we are," he said. "That's what makes us survivable."

She gathered her copied pages, folded them back into wherever such things lived on her body, a geography he was beginning — dangerously, he noted, and shelved the note — to find interesting. At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and stood a moment in the lamplight with the wind pressing at the boards behind her, and for one unguarded instant she looked less like a courier than like a young woman standing at the edge of a very long winter, and he felt the old treacherous ache of wanting to warn people about things they would not thank him for knowing.

"New moon," she said. "How many days?"

"Nine."

"Nine days." She nodded, once, the nod of a soldier accepting a date she'd have preferred to argue with, and went down the stairs into the cold.

Rafael stood alone in the kitchen a long while. Then he took the lamp down to the cellar, past the racks of syrupped fruit glowing amber in the dark like preserved summers, to the corner where a loose board covered a floor safe he had sworn never to open again. Inside lay a single sheet of heavy paper, folded twice, written in his own hand in the old bureau's cipher — a system so obsolete that only two living people could read it, and one of them was dead.

Eleven names. Eleven springs of trees. One receipt.

He put his palm flat on the folded page, feeling nothing through the paper, which was the nature of paper — it held everything and weighed almost nothing — and upstairs, faintly, he heard Ulyana's door close, and below the floorboards, beneath the frost, beneath the roots of everything he had planted, the river shifted its great shoulder in the dark and settled again, counting down without knowing it.

Nine days. He blew out the lamp and climbed back toward the light, and began, in the privacy of his own skull, to compose the opening line of the only honest sentence he had ever intended to send.
