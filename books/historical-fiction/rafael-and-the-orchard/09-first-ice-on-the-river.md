## 9. First Ice on the River

The first hard frost came on a Thursday, and with it the sound Ulyana had been waiting for without admitting she was listening: the river, groaning.

She heard it from the packing hall at dawn, where she stood with her hands buried in a crate of Winesaps, and the noise rolled up through the floorboards like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the town. Not cracking yet — not breaking — just settling, the water contracting under its new lid of ice, testing the weight of winter against itself. The men loading the morning cart paused. One of them spat into his glove and said what they all knew: the river had taken its shape for the season. Nothing would cross now until spring.

Ulyana kept sorting apples. Her hands did it without her, which was fortunate, because the rest of her had gone very still and very cold, colder than the frost on the windows, and somewhere behind her ribs a clock that had been ticking slowly since the bridge closed began to tick faster.

The ice was in. Which meant the patrols would stop waiting for her to try to leave, and start looking for where she'd decided to stay.

* * *

That evening Rafael drew the shutters in the workshop's back room earlier than usual, and lit only one lamp, turned low, its shade wrapped in sacking so the glow fell in a yellow coin no wider than the table between them. The room smelled of lamp oil and dried apple peel and the faint metallic breath of the stove. He had moved the ledger shelf to cover the window. He had done this, Ulyana noticed, days ago — before she'd asked, before they'd agreed on anything — as though he had always known how the winter would be spent.

He set her coat on the table between them like a patient on an operating slab.

"Show me again," he said. "Slowly."

She unpicked the seam along the left lining with a needle she'd kept threaded since the capital, and drew out the first dispatch: a strip of paper no wider than two fingers, folded eight times, dense with figures in a hand so small it might have been printed. She had carried it against her body through three hundred kilometers of emptied country. It had absorbed her warmth so thoroughly that when she laid it flat under the lamp, the paper seemed almost alive, faintly damp, breathing.

Rafael did not touch it at first. He bent over it the way he bent over a suspect crate of fruit — close, unhurried, reading the skin of the thing before trusting the flesh.

"This is a running key," he said finally. "Book cipher underneath, but the key shifts by column. Whoever built this wanted it readable even if you lost half your instructions." He glanced at her. "They trusted you to lose things."

"They trusted me to be searched," Ulyana said. "It's different."

"Is it?" Something dry moved at the corner of his mouth. "I spent eleven years being searched. I never decided it was a compliment."

She let that go. There was a version of their evenings together in which they talked — about the orchard, about the war's long tail, about whether the ice on the Vetrov bend would hold a cart or merely a man — and there was this other version, in which they spoke only in the narrow language of work, and every ordinary word carried freight neither of them would name. Tonight was the second kind. She preferred it. She distrusted how much she preferred it.

"The key book," she said. "You have anything printed before '09? Poetry, scripture, a school primer?"

"I have everything printed before '09." He rose and went to the press house door at the room's far end — the heavy oak door he kept locked and oiled and, she now understood, watched — and came back with four volumes: a hymnal, an almanac, a slim book of pastoral verse, and a child's arithmetic primer with a cracked spine. He dealt them onto the table like cards. "Which one?"

Ulyana looked at the first line of figures. 4–12–7. Page, line, letter, if the convention held. She tried the hymnal. Nonsense. The almanac gave her a fragment — *the* — then collapsed. The primer, absurdly, opened the whole first sentence like a lock: **THE SEASON IS LATE AND THE FRUIT WILL NOT WAIT FOR THE HAND THAT PROMISED IT.**

She read it twice. Then she read it aloud, because some part of her needed to hear the words exist outside her own head, spoken in a warm room, in lamplight, by a voice that sounded like hers.

Rafael listened without expression. When she finished he said, quietly, "So it begins," and reached for the pen.

* * *

They worked the way besieged people eat: quickly, silently, in the intervals allowed them.

By day the workshop ran as a workshop. Crates went out to the garrison at Kostin Ford; ledgers were entered; Rafael's two hired men, old Tomas and the boy Anselm, packed fruit and complained about the price of straw and knew nothing except that the master had taken to keeping late hours and that the woman from the east sorted apples better than any seasonal hand he'd ever employed. Ulyana learned to make herself unremarkable in daylight — sleeves rolled, hair pinned flat, hands red with cold and cider — and then, after ten o'clock, when the town lamps died one by one and the patrol boots could be heard making their rounds along the embankment road, she learned to become remarkable again, hour by stolen hour, in a circle of light small enough to hide under a coat.

The dispatches came apart slowly. There were nine strips sewn into her lining; the first took them two nights. The second, three, because its key was keyed to the first — a chain, each document unlocking the next — and because Rafael refused, on the second night, to continue past midnight.

"You're falling asleep over the figures," he said. "A tired decoder makes confident errors. I've seen whole networks die of confidence."

"I've seen whole republics die of caution," she answered, and pulled the strip back toward her, and saw him decide not to fight her on it — saw, too, the flicker of respect and irritation that decision cost him, and filed both away in the growing drawer of things she knew about Rafael Ostrov that he hadn't told her.

Because she was learning him, the way she'd been trained to learn a cipher: by frequency, by repetition, by what recurred under pressure. He touched his left cuff when he was thinking about the old regime — straightening it, as if a uniform still hung there. He hummed, barely audibly, three notes of some harvest song when a decryption resolved, and stopped himself each time as though the habit were contraband. He never sat with his back to the press house door. And once — only once, on the fourth night, when she laughed at something trivial, the way the primer misspelled *orchard* — he looked at her with such naked, startled attention that she understood two things simultaneously: that he was lonely in a way he had made structural, built into the architecture of his life like the locked door and the shuttered window; and that she was becoming, for him, a breach in a wall he had spent years mortaring.

She should have found it useful. She did find it useful. That was the trouble — it was also something else, and the something else grew fastest precisely during the hours she most needed to be only a courier.

* * *

The patrols changed character in the second week.

Before, they had been weather: boots on the embankment, a lantern swinging, voices arguing about rations. Now they had a purpose, and the purpose had a description. Ulyana heard it from Anselm, who heard everything, who came in from the market white-faced with the pleasure of carrying bad news. A woman, traveling alone, last seen on the north road before the bridge closed. Possibly carrying documents. The garrison at Kostin Ford was offering a bounty — a real one, a month's wages — for information leading to her detention. House-to-house searches had begun at the river end of town and were moving street by street toward the square.

"They'll reach us within four days," Rafael said that night, laying the map of the search pattern out in his head the way he laid out grafting schedules, season by season, tree by tree. "Not because they're clever. Because they're thorough, and thoroughness doesn't need to be clever."

"Then we need four nights of work done in two."

"We need two nights of work done correctly." He turned the lamp wick down another notch. The circle of light shrank; the dark pressed closer, warmer somehow than the light. "And we need a story for the day they knock. You've been here since October as a widow from the east, hired for the winter pack. Your name is Marta Ilves. Your husband sold timber and drowned. You don't discuss politics because grief doesn't discuss politics. Can you be her?"

Ulyana considered. It was strange, how easy it had become to consider it — the erasure of herself, the wearing of another woman like a borrowed coat. Three months ago in the capital she had believed history happened elsewhere, to other people, while people like her carried its paperwork. Now she lived inside its machinery, and the machinery had a sound like ice groaning, and she had stopped flinching at it.

"Marta Ilves," she said, trying the name on. "Did she drown her husband, or did the river?"

"The river. But in your telling, you'll say it happened quickly, and you'll say it as though you wish it hadn't, and you will not cry, because women who cry are remembered and widows who don't are forgotten." He said it without cruelty — as craft, the way he might explain how to prune a tree so it bore rather than bled. "Do you understand?"

"I understand that you've hidden people before."

The silence that followed had texture. She felt him weigh it, decide, and set the decision aside unopened — a man putting a letter back in a drawer.

"I've hidden things," he said. "People are heavier."

* * *

On the fifth night they decoded the third dispatch, and it was the third dispatch that told her the mission was real.

Not the words themselves — the words were logistics, rendezvous designations, a schedule of confirmations dressed in the language of grain shipments. What told her was the key change. Halfway through the strip the running key shifted to a new book, and Rafael, working the columns, went still over the primer, then reached past it to the hymnal, and the hymnal failed, and he sat back and rubbed his eyes and said, almost to himself, "They rotated on you."

"What does that mean?"

"It means whoever built your key expected the journey to take longer than it did. Or shorter. Either way—" He tapped the unfinished line of figures. "Half of this strip decodes against a book I may not own."

"Then I'll reconstruct it. Give me the plaintext conventions and I'll solve it as a substitution. It'll take all night."

"It'll take all night and it will make noise. Paper, pages, movement. On the night after a search passes our street." He looked at her across the small yellow coin of lamplight, and she saw the argument assembling behind his eyes, and she saw him abandon it — not because he conceded, but because he had looked at her face and concluded, correctly, that the argument would cost more than the risk.

"All night," he said. "But we do it here, at this table, with the lamp like this, and if I say the word *apples*, you stop, instantly, whatever the column. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"*Apples*, Ulyana."

Something in hearing him say her name — her true name, in that room, in that voice, the syllables handled carefully like documents — went through her with a warmth entirely disproportionate to the moment. She kept her face still. She was fluent in concealment. But she marked the moment anyway, put it in the drawer with the rest, in the section that was getting crowded, the section she did not audit.

They worked until the church bell counted three. She solved the rotation at twenty minutes past two — the key book was the almanac after all, but read backward, from the last page, which was either paranoia or wit depending on who had designed it — and the remainder of the strip opened like frost melting off glass. Rafael copied the plaintext into his small, disguised ledger-hand while she held the lamp, and when he finished they sat for a moment without speaking, two people looking at a page of secrets in a room above a river full of trapped water.

Outside, distantly, a dog began to bark, and did not stop, and was joined by another.

Rafael raised his hand. They both froze. The barking moved — along the embankment, toward the mill, away from them — and faded into the ordinary night sounds of a river town pretending to sleep. He lowered his hand slowly.

"Four days," he murmured. "Perhaps three."

* * *

It was near dawn when she caught herself watching him instead of the page.

He had fallen asleep sitting up, briefly — thirty seconds, no more, his chin dropping toward his chest, the pen still in his fingers, ink drying on the nib. The lamp had burned so low that the light lay across him like a sheet drawn to the shoulders. He looked, asleep, like a man standing at attention in some interior parade ground no one else could see. Even unconscious, he did not quite trust the room.

She thought: *he was going to tell me something, the first night, and didn't.* She thought: *everyone in this country has paid something, and everyone pays in a different currency, and I don't know his denomination yet.* She thought, less usefully, that the grey coming into his beard was concentrated at the temples in a way she found — she searched for the professional term and could not find one, and settled for the truth — beautiful.

Then his eyes opened, all at once, fully awake, the way soldiers wake, and found her looking at him, and neither of them moved for a moment longer than accident allows.

"You should sleep," he said. His voice was roughened.

"So should you."

"I'll sleep when the ice breaks." He capped the ink, squared the papers, slid them beneath the loose brick by the stove that had apparently always been a loose brick. "Three more strips. Two nights. Then we transmit, and then—"

He stopped. The sentence had been heading somewhere neither of them had mapped — *then you leave, then spring comes, then this room goes back to being only a room* — and he declined to finish it, and she declined to help him, and the unfinished thing sat between them on the table like a ninth dispatch, encoded, waiting for a key.

She gathered her coat and climbed the stairs to her room under the eaves. Through the frost-ferned window she could see the river, black and white under a sky going grey, holding its new shape. Groaning again, softly, as the temperature fell — the sound of a thing bearing weight it had not chosen.

Below her, faintly, she heard Rafael moving through the dark workshop, checking the shutters, the locks, the door. Checking everything, twice, the way a man checks a life he expects to lose.

Three nights later, the knock would come.
