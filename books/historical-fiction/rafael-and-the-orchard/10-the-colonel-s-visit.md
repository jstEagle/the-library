## 10. The Colonel's Visit

The knock came on a Tuesday, though Ulyana would remember it afterward as arriving without any day at all, out of sequence with the calendar, the way disasters do.

She was in the packing hall, up to her elbows in winter apples — Winesaps and Black Ben Davis, their skins waxy under the lamplight, cold as river stones — when she heard the boots in the yard first. Not Rafael's boots. Not the soft scuff of the two hired women who came for the sorting days. These were military heels, deliberate, spaced like punctuation.

"Company," she said to no one, and reached for the rag to wipe her hands, because that was what a woman did when strangers arrived: she made herself tidy, made herself ordinary, made herself nothing worth a second glance.

Rafael was already crossing the hall from the press room. He moved quickly but not hurriedly — there was a difference, and she had learned to read it in him the way she'd learned to read weather in the sky over the orchard. He caught her eye for half a second, and something passed between them that had no words in either of their vocabularies yet, though they would invent them later, in the dark, by lamplight, over cipher pages: *be dull. Be fruit.*

The door opened on a man built like a door himself — broad, flat-faced, uniformed in the grey-green of the revolutionary garrison with a red armband worn slightly askew, as if he'd put it on in a hurry or didn't care about it much. Two soldiers stayed outside by the gate, visible through the frost-furred window glass, standing with the patient insolence of men who had been told to wait somewhere warm later.

"Lieutenant-Colonel Vess," the man said. His voice was unhurried, almost gentle, which was worse than shouting. "Requisition order." He held out a paper without looking at it, the way a man holds out a hand he expects to be kissed. "The town is short forty barrels of preserved stores. Your workshop has been assessed at twelve."

Rafael took the paper. He read it thoroughly, top to bottom, turning it as if checking for a watermark — and Ulyana understood, watching him, that the thoroughness itself was a performance, a small theater of an honest tradesman who knew his rights and expected to be cheated anyway. It was beautiful. It was terrifying.

"Twelve barrels," Rafael said. "That's most of our winter stock."

"The town's need is greater than yours."

"I don't dispute it, Colonel. I only note it." He said it mildly, with a shrug that invited sympathy rather than argument. "Twelve barrels, then. You'll want the Winesaps — they keep better than the Ben Davis this time of year. I'll have my girls pack them properly. Loose apples travel badly; you'll lose a third of them before they reach the garrison kitchen."

Something flickered across Vess's flat face — surprise, perhaps, or its cousin, interest. Men like him, Ulyana thought, expected resistance or groveling and had scripts for both. They had no script for a man who offered to improve their confiscation.

"You're cooperative," Vess said.

"I'm an orchardist. Apples rot whether I argue or not." Rafael smiled, and it was a good smile, a local smile, the smile of a man who had sold fruit at market stalls for twenty years and haggled with grandmothers and drunk with carters. Ulyana had seen him use pieces of it before, but never the whole apparatus, never at full power. Watching it now, she felt the hair rise on her forearms beneath her sleeves. She had known he was fluent in concealment. She hadn't known how *fluent*.

Vess stepped inside.

He did it slowly, and Ulyana realized that everything so far had been preamble. The requisition was real — the paper, the soldiers, the barrels, all real — but the requisition was also a key, and the key had turned, and now the door stood open and the Colonel intended to walk through every room it opened onto.

* * *

She kept sorting.

It was the hardest physical work of her life, harder than the mountains, harder than the border crossings, harder even than the night on the ice that still lay ahead of her in a future she couldn't see: to stand with her head bowed over a crate of apples while a hunter's eyes moved across the room looking for something, knowing that the something might be her, and to sort apples. To turn each one, check each skin, set each down with the small wooden sound of fruit on wood, at an unvarying rhythm, as if the world contained nothing more remarkable than apples.

Her hands knew the work now. Three weeks of it. That was the mercy and the trap of it both — the work had gone into her body deep enough that her body could perform it without her mind, leaving her mind free to scream quietly behind her sternum where nobody could hear.

Vess walked the length of the hall. He looked at the presses, the cider vats, the racks of drying trays. He picked up a ledger from the side table — *the* ledger, the columnar one, and Ulyana's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth — and flipped three pages with his thumb.

"Good figures," Vess said. "Most orchardists can't add."

"My father couldn't either," Rafael said pleasantly, taking the ledger back with the ease of a man retrieving a hat, not a weapon. "I hired an accountant once, after a bad year. Learned more watching him than I meant to."

"Mm." Vess let him have it. Let him — that was the phrase Ulyana's mind chose, and she distrusted it immediately, because *letting* implied choice, and choice implied purpose, and purpose implied that the Colonel was not here primarily for apples.

His eyes found her then.

She felt it land between her shoulder blades like a dropped coin — the specific weight of being looked at by a man trained to look. She did not turn around. She sorted apples. Left hand, right hand, turn, inspect, bin or discard. Her pulse hammered in her throat and she sent it downward, downward into her hands, into the rhythm, until the hammering became the rhythm became the apples.

"You're new," Vess said to her back.

"She's my sister's girl," Rafael said.

Ulyana turned then, because a niece who didn't turn around was a niece with something to hide, and she gave Vess the look she had rehearsed in her own head a hundred times without ever admitting she was rehearsing it: wary, tired, faintly resentful, the look of a woman from the provinces whose uncle had taken her in when her husband went off to the war and didn't come back. A woman inconvenienced by officers. A woman who wanted to get back to her apples.

"From Kolvary," she added, naming a town two hundred versts north that had been shelled in the spring, because a dead town could not be telegraphed to, and a dead town's widows could not easily be checked on. It was the first lie she had spoken aloud in the workshop, and it sat in her mouth like a coin.

"Kolvary," Vess repeated. "Bad business there."

"Yes," she said, and let the word carry all its freight, and looked down again.

But he kept studying her. That was the thing she would remember — not cruelty, not suspicion even, exactly, but *study*, the patient professional attention of a man cataloguing. His gaze went from her hands — too smooth for three weeks of orchard work, damn it, she should have been rubbing lye into them since October — to her posture, to the way she held her chin, and she saw the arithmetic happening behind his flat face and could not know what sum he was arriving at.

"You have educated hands," Vess said mildly.

"Typist, before," she said. "Before the war I worked in a solicitor's office. Figs don't care if your penmanship's pretty, but it's a hard habit to break." And she flexed her fingers, deliberately, a typist's gesture, a clerk's little vanity, and prayed to a God she had stopped believing in sometime during the retreat that the gesture was right, that clerks really did that, that she had guessed correctly about the small grammar of another class of life.

Rafael laughed — a short, easy laugh, the laugh of family teasing — and clapped her on the shoulder as he passed with the first barrel. "She types our invoices now. Better than I deserve." The touch steadied her more than any words could have; it was a rope thrown across a gap. *You are mine to explain,* the touch said. *Let me.*

* * *

They got the twelve barrels loaded by early afternoon. Vess supervised it personally, which was wrong — a lieutenant-colonel did not supervise apple loading, he signed papers about apple loading — and while the soldiers rolled the casks out to the cart, he stood in the yard with his hands clasped behind him, looking at things.

At the press house. At the loft windows above the packing hall. At the orchard rows marching away toward the river, black-limbed and patient under the snow.

"Fine ground," he said.

"It's been in the family a long time," Rafael said.

"That's becoming rare." Vess turned. There was something in his voice now that had shed the administrative boredom entirely, something almost conversational, almost intimate, and Ulyana, standing in the doorway with her arms crossed against the cold, understood that they had arrived at whatever the visit had actually been for. "Ground stays. Men go. Men get shot, or they run, or they change their names and start packing fruit somewhere quiet." He smiled. It did not reach anything. "The republic's falling apart, Ostrov. Everyone knows it. The question people are asking themselves these days is — what will they have been, when it falls? Loyalists? Opportunists? Dead men?" He shrugged. "Me, I keep lists. It's a habit from the old days. Lists are honest. A list doesn't care what you tell yourself you are."

"A useful habit," Rafael said, in the tone of a man discussing drainage.

"It is." Vess put on his gloves, one finger at a time, unhurried. "Your requisition receipt. Twelve barrels, fair weight, no complaints registered. You've been well-treated today, orchardist." He settled the armband straight with a tug, finally caring about it, and Ulyana thought: *there, that's the uniform going back on, the performance ending, the animal returning to its shape.* "If anyone comes asking about your household — your workers, your visitors, your *niece* — you'll find me reasonable. Reasonable men make better neighbors than suspicious ones. For everyone concerned."

"I'll remember it, Colonel," Rafael said warmly, and shook his hand, and stood in the yard waving like a farmer seeing off a customer until the cart cleared the gate and the sound of the wheels faded down the frozen road toward town.

Then he stood not waving.

Ulyana watched his back in the doorway. The shoulders she had come to know — broad, steady, the shoulders of a man who carried things — were very still, and the stillness frightened her more than Vess had. Snow ticked softly against the windowpanes. Somewhere beyond the orchard, far off, the river made a sound under its ice, a long groaning note like a struck cable, and neither of them remarked on it, because neither of them needed to anymore; they had both begun keeping count of the river's moods.

"He took your ledger," she said quietly.

"He read it. I gave him back the wrong one."

Her breath caught. "There's a *wrong* one?"

"There's always a wrong one, Ulyana. There have been wrong ones since before you were carrying dispatches." He turned around then, and his face had reassembled itself into calm, but she could see the seams of it now, the places where the calm was stitched on. "He wasn't here for apples."

"I know."

"He was here to see if we flinched. Requisitions are how they take a man's temperature." He came up the steps past her into the hall, and the warmth of the room closed around them, cider-smell and lamp-oil-smell, the smell she had already come to think of, treacherously, as safety. "And he liked you."

"*Liked* me?"

"Noticed you. Noticed is worse." Rafael hung his coat on its peg with exaggerated care, each motion precise, and she recognized the ritual — she had her own versions of it, smoothing paper, squaring ink bottles, the small ceremonies that kept the terror compartmentalized. "Educated hands. He wrote that sentence on some list in his head. Educated hands, northern vowels under the provincial ones, a niece nobody in this town remembers arriving." He looked at her then, directly, and for once made no attempt at charm. "How soon can we finish the third dispatch?"

"Two nights. Maybe three, if the key keeps slipping."

"Make it two." He pulled the ledger from under his arm — the wrong ledger, the decoy — and set it on the table between them like a card played face-down. Outside, the light was already failing; the winter days were short and getting shorter, the dark coming earlier every afternoon like a creditor. "Because that man is going to stand in his office tonight and enjoy being reasonable, and then tomorrow, or the day after, he's going to wonder why being reasonable didn't make him feel safer. And when he wonders that—"

"—he'll come back without the paperwork," Ulyana finished.

Rafael nodded once. Above them, through the floorboards, the loft creaked in the wind off the river, and beneath everything, faint and rhythmic, the ice went on talking to itself in the language of things about to give way.

"Two nights," she said, and reached for the lamp.
