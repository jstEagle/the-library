## 11. Half a Message

The key died on a Tuesday, in the ninth minute past midnight.

Rafael knew it the way a man knows his own heart has skipped — not by evidence at first but by a wrongness in the rhythm of things. The lamp was turned low, its shade draped with a folded blanket so that only a coin of amber light fell on the table between them. Ulyana's hands moved over the cipher wheel with the confidence of someone who had been born to small precise mechanisms, and for six nights now they had worked like this: the packing hall empty above them, the winter apples breathing their slow sweetness through the floorboards, the river muttering under its new ice like an old man talking in his sleep.

The transmitter itself was nothing — a hand-built thing of brass contacts and salvaged coils, cased in what looked like an ordinary fruit-press crank housing, keyed through a telegraph sounder Rafael had bought legitimately, in daylight, from a railway surplus office seven years ago. Its antenna ran up inside the wall cavity of the press house and out along the fence wire, disguised as a lightning conductor. It had sent perhaps forty transmissions in its life, all of them short, all of them timed to dead frequencies where no one listened except the one ear that mattered.

Tonight it stuttered.

Ulyana heard it before he did. Her hand stopped on the wheel. "That's wrong," she said. "The dash is breaking up."

He leaned in. The signal they were sending — the third of her dispatches, half delivered into the frozen dark — came back through the sounder as something ragged, a voice with a stone in its throat. He touched the case of the transmitter and felt it: heat where there should have been warmth, warmth where there should have been nothing. The smell arrived a moment later, faint and unmistakable, hot varnish and copper.

"Stop," he said. "Key open. Now."

She opened the circuit. For a moment they sat in the silence, listening to the building settle, to the ice groan somewhere downriver with a sound like a door being forced. Then a thin gray ribbon of smoke curled up out of the vent slit in the transmitter's housing, delicate as thread from a seamstress's needle, and Rafael's stomach dropped through the floor and kept falling.

"The primary coil," he said. His voice came out flat, and he was grateful for that, because underneath the flatness something was climbing his spine with cold fingers. "Windings have gone. It happens. It happened last winter too — I nursed it through February on reduced power."

"And we are not in February," Ulyana said. "We're in January, and I have two dispatches left, and this one is only—" she tapped the log sheet, where her neat columnar figures recorded the transmission's progress like a physician's chart, "—half out of my hands."

Half a message. The phrase sat in the room like a third person. Half a message meant the receiving station would hold a fragment, meaningless alone, tantalizing to anyone who intercepted it. Half a message meant the colonel's signals men — and Rafael had seen the aerial strung on the customs house roof, had watched the young operator climb it twice in the past week — would have a puzzle worth solving. Half a message meant time, and time was the one commodity the river's ice was spending faster than any of them could earn.

"There's a reserve set," he said.

Ulyana looked up. In the coin of lamplight her face was all angles and patience, and he saw her decide not to ask why a fruit-packing workshop owned by a man who had supposedly retired from everything kept a reserve transmitter. She had decided that weeks ago, he suspected. People like her — people like him — didn't accumulate questions; they banked them, against a day when the answers might be useful or might be fatal, whichever came first.

"Where?"

"Press house. Under the floor." He was already moving, snuffing the lamp, pulling the blanket off the shade, folding the failed transmitter's leads with hands that wanted very much to shake and were forbidden to. "Not tonight. Tonight we let the air go quiet. If anyone was listening to our half-message, let them hear nothing follow it. Tomorrow night, after the patrol passes the mill — they pass at eleven-forty, they take twelve minutes to clear the bridge quarter — we move."

"You've done this before," she said. Not a question either.

"I've done many things before." He heard how it came out of him — clipped, closed, a door swung shut on its own hinge — and he saw her register it, saw the small stillness come over her like frost forming on glass. He hadn't meant to wound. Or perhaps he had. That was the trouble with shame after enough years: it stopped feeling like guilt and started feeling like weather, ambient, directionless, and it made a man snap at the nearest warm thing without knowing why.

* * *

The press house stood thirty paces behind the workshop, across a yard where the pruned apple whips lay stacked like kindling waiting to become firewood. It was a long low building of stone and timber, older than the workshop, older than the town's memory of why it was called a press house at all — some ancestor enterprise, cider or flax or printing, no one living could say. Rafael had bought the property in the first spring of his exile here, walking its grounds with the agent while the orchard stood in blossom so thick the air seemed solid with it, and he had chosen the place for reasons he never spoke aloud: the deep stone footing, the dry cellar, the sight-line to the river road, and the fact that a building with history in it attracted no curiosity about what was buried in its own.

They went out at eleven-fifty-three, moving through the yard in the blue-black light of a moon three days from full, their breath pluming. The cold had teeth tonight; it found every gap in wool and leather and worried at the skin beneath. Ulyana carried the crowbar. He carried the lamp, unlit, and a bundle of oilcloth.

The press house floor was oak over joists over rubble, and beneath the third-floorboard east of the cider press — the board with the iron ring set flush into it, painted over four times until it looked like a knot in the wood — there was a cavity two feet square, lined with zinc against the damp, and in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed paper and wool, a transmitter that had never once been used.

It was a better machine than the first. He had built it in the year of his pardon, in the months when sleep avoided him and work was the only honest company available, and he had built it with the particular ferocity of a man constructing his own future alibi. Every joint soldered twice. Every wire run redundant. He had told himself it was insurance against failure. He had known, even then, in the wordless way one knows things, that it was insurance against himself — against the day his nerve broke, as it had broken before, and he needed to buy his way out of a second corner.

He knelt at the edge of the opened floor and lifted the machine out into the moonlight falling through the high window, and Ulyana knelt beside him, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The zinc lining caught the light dully. The cavity below it was empty now, and would stay empty, and somehow that felt like a sentence being read out.

"It's clean," he said. "Fresh coils. I tested it once, three years ago, into a dummy load. It will carry your dispatches."

"Rafael." She said his name quietly, and he braced himself, because he had learned to hear what came after quiet names. "Why do you keep it under a press house instead of in the workshop?"

Because I built it the winter I signed the list. Because I couldn't sleep in the same building with it. Because a man can live above his instruments but not above his crimes, and I needed forty paces of frozen ground between me and what I'd done, and even that wasn't enough, not on nights like this, not when the ice talks.

"Redundancy," he said. "A bureau keeps its reserves separate from its working rooms. Standard practice."

It was standard practice. That was the obscenity of it — that every lie he told her was true.

* * *

Later — the reserve set installed in the packing hall's false wall, the floorboard replaced, the yard swept of their footprints by the wind that was rising ahead of the weather — Rafael sat alone in his rooms above the workshop and could not sleep, which surprised him not at all.

The dispatches were going out again tomorrow. Two remained after the broken one, and then the confirmation protocol, and then — if the river cooperated, if the patrols stayed stupid, if luck held its shape another fortnight — Ulyana would walk out of his life across the ice and take with her whatever it was that had grown between them in these weeks, this thing he refused to name because naming it would make it a debt, and his life was already mortgaged past recovery.

And he owed her more than the transmission. He knew that. He had known it since the night he'd set his terms — carry mine alongside yours — and watched her agree without hesitation, because she was the kind of person who honored bargains the way other people honored saints. She thought his dispatch was a confession of old service, perhaps a warning to colleagues scattered abroad. She deserved the truth. And the truth was a thing he had never once given anyone whole, not the tribunal that pardoned him, not the neighbors who sheltered him, not the trees he planted with his own hands in ground bought with betrayal.

The truth was this:

In the autumn of 1917, when the republic's ministries were already burning through their secretaries faster than they could print loyalty oaths, Rafael Ostrov had run the western cipher desk. Eleven years old, the desk. He had inherited it from a woman named Marta Vey who taught him columnar transposition the way his mother had taught him grafting — patiently, with dirt under both their nails — and when the end came it did not come as a storm. It came as an audit.

Three men in civilian coats sat across a table from him in a requisitioned schoolhouse outside the capital, and one of them — the youngest, the one with the ink-stained fingers who did all the talking — laid a folder on the table and opened it, and inside was everything. The workshop's true ledgers. The frequency schedules. A photograph of Marta Vey taken through a window, her face turned toward the light, unaware.

"We don't need you to talk," the ink-stained man said. "We need you to write. Names, locations, methods. Write them well and you keep your orchard. Write them badly and you share the folder's fate." He tapped the photograph. "Everyone in this book shares the folder's fate anyway. You're only deciding whether you're in it."

Rafael had asked for a pen. That was the detail that returned to him on nights like this, lying awake above his own workshop with the river groaning: not the fear, not the bargaining, not even the moment of decision, but the small obscene civility of asking for a pen, and the man sliding one across the table as though passing the salt.

Fourteen names. He wrote fourteen names, and each one cost exactly as much as the last, because the tribunal had priced them in advance and the price was uniform: one name, one life, one entry in a ledger that would outlive every tree he ever planted. Marta Vey went first on the list because she would have gone first on theirs regardless, and telling himself that was the first self-forgiveness, and the thousandth self-forgiveness, and none of them ever took. He had stood in the blossom-filled yard the following spring and understood that the orchard grew out of graves, that every apple it gave him would be sweet and rotting at once, syrup over corruption, preserved guilt, and that he had chosen this with a pen he'd asked for politely.

He had never transmitted his confession. That was the final cowardice — he had written it, sealed it, addressed it to the new authorities' records office in the capital, and for five years the envelope had sat in the zinc cavity under the press house floor, beside the transmitter, two weights on the same scale that never balanced. Some nights he told himself he was waiting for the right moment. Most nights he knew he was simply unable to lift it.

Now the transmitter was out of the cavity. Only the envelope remained, buried in the dark under thirty paces of frozen yard, waiting for the bargain he had already struck with a woman who trusted him, whose dispatches would travel under his name and his confession together, and who did not yet know what cargo she had agreed to carry.

Rafael got up, dressed, and went down through the cold workshop into the packing hall, where Ulyana slept in the narrow room behind the grading tables. He stood in the dark among the crates and listened to her breathe, and hated himself with the clean efficient hatred of long practice, because tomorrow he would help her finish her work and send his own poison riding beside it, and she would learn what she carried only when carrying it became irreversible — or never, if he found the courage he had failed to find for five years.

Through the window, far downstream, the ice boomed — a long cracking report, like artillery very far away, like a countdown begun in a language only rivers speak.

The thaw was coming early this year.
