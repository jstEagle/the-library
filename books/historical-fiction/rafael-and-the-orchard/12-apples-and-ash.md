## 12. Apples and Ash

The cold came down from the hills on the third of February like a verdict, and for two days neither of them spoke much at all. The transmission work had gone quiet — the colonel's patrols had thickened after the seizure, the key changed, the air itself seemingly full of listening — and so there was nothing to do but the ordinary work, which Rafael insisted upon with a stubbornness that Ulyana had come to understand was its own kind of discipline. *A man who stops pruning his trees in winter,* he said, *has already decided not to be here in spring.*

She did not tell him that she had made no such decision either way. She did not tell him that she thought about it every night now, lying under the eaves above the packing hall, listening to the river groan in its sleep.

On the morning of the pruning they went out before dawn. Rafael carried the long-handled shears across his shoulder like a soldier's rifle, an echo he perhaps did not notice and she certainly did. The orchard lay white and silent under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, four hundred trees in ranks down the slope toward the water, their branches bare and black and patient. Frost had built itself into strange coral formations along the south-facing limbs. When the wind moved through, the trees creaked against each other like old men shifting in a meeting.

"Antonovka," Rafael said, stopping at the first row. "My grandfather planted these. They say an Antonovka doesn't ripen properly until it has survived ten winters." He turned a branch over in his gloved hand, examining the bud scars with an attention Ulyana recognized from the lamplight, from the ledgers, from the nights when he bent over cipher columns as though reading scripture. "This one is thirty-one."

"You count them like soldiers."

"I count them like the only things I have ever finished." He handed her the small shears, the ones for close work, and showed her where to cut — just outside the collar, angled so the wound would shed water, never tearing the bark. "A tree heals what you cut cleanly. It carries what you don't."

* * *

They worked in the rhythm that labor makes possible between people who cannot yet afford speech. Snip, pull, drop. The pruned wood went onto a sledge to be burned later; Rafael called the pile *the year's sins*. By midmorning the frost had begun to sweat off the southern branches and the whole hillside smelled faintly green, impossibly, under all that white — the smell of apples still hanging shriveled on a few forgotten trees, blackened fruit that the birds hadn't found. Ulyana reached up and took one down. It was frozen through, wrinkled like a grandmother's hand, and when she bit into it beside the trunk, out of the wind, it tasted of cider and of something almost like wine, dark and concentrated and a little past saving.

Rafael watched her eat it with an expression she couldn't read.

"Frosted fruit is sweeter," he said. "The cold drives the sugar inward. Everything the tree can't spare, it keeps."

"That's not how I would have put it."

"How would you put it?"

She considered the apple, the bite mark going brown already in the cold air. "That everything it couldn't keep, it loses."

He laughed — a short, real laugh, the first she'd heard from him in days, since the neighbor Vasilisa's arrest, since the notices went up on the telegraph office door with their careful ink descriptions: *a woman, mid-twenties, foreign bearing; a man, forties, lame in the left knee.* He laughed and then stopped laughing, and looked at her too long, and turned back to the tree.

They worked until the light went flat and gray at noon, and then sat on the sledge among the cut branches, sharing bread and hard cheese from a cloth, and a thermos of coffee that had gone lukewarm hours before. Below them the river ran under its ice lid, and even at this distance they could hear it — that deep, arrhythmic knocking, as though something enormous were trying doors in the basement of the world.

"It's early," Ulyana said.

"The thaw? No. Weeks yet."

"The ice is moving anyway. You've heard it."

He didn't answer immediately. He poured the last of the coffee into her cup rather than his own, an old habit of hospitality so ingrained it seemed involuntary. "Ice talks all winter," he said finally. "It's the silence afterward you watch for."

* * *

She did not plan the confession. Later she would tell herself it was the landscape that loosened her — the enormous patient whiteness, the smell of green under frost, the fact that they were surrounded by four hundred living things that survived by losing exactly what they could not carry. Or she would tell herself it was strategy, that tenderness was a currency and she was spending it deliberately, buying the alliance she would need for whatever the final dispatch demanded. Both explanations were true. Neither was the truth.

"Can I ask you something honest?" she said.

"I would prefer it to the alternative."

"When you were in the service — before — did you believe in it?"

Rafael looked out at the river. His profile against the snow was all sharp planes and the particular stillness of a man who has practiced not reacting. "Which service?"

"Don't do that."

"All right." He set down his cup with great care, as if it were evidence. "No. Not the way they wanted. I believed in the work. There is a difference, and the difference kept me alive, and it also—" he paused— "it also made me useful to men I should have despised. Belief makes you predictable. I was never predictable, so they gave me the ugly tasks instead."

"And you did them."

"And I did them." He said it without flinching, and she understood that this was the point — that he had learned to say it plainly because saying it plainly was the only penance available that cost anything.

Ulyana pulled her knees up inside her coat — the coat, with its sewn lining, empty now except for the ghost-shape of the dispatches that lived in the press house below, in oilcloth, waiting. "I never believed in it at all," she said. "Not once. Not in the capital, not in the training, not on the road. Everyone around me spoke as if the republic were a cathedral and we were carrying relics, and I stood there nodding, and the whole time I felt nothing. Do you understand? Nothing. I used to wait for it to arrive — the conviction, the thing they all clearly had. I thought maybe it was like faith, that it would find me late, or in a crisis." She turned the cup in her hands. "It never came."

"What did come?"

"The work." She heard herself use his word and did not take it back. "I finish things. That's all I am. A woman who finishes things. Someone gives me paper and says carry it, and I carry it. They say decode, transmit, and I decode and transmit. And I told myself that was humility — that history is made by believers and carried by everyone else, and being carried-by was its own honest station." She shook her head slowly. "But that's a lie too, isn't it? It's not humility. It's just another way of never having to decide whether any of it was worth doing."

The words hung in the frozen air. Somewhere downslope, ice cracked and settled, a sound like a rifle shot muffled by wool, and both of them flinched in the same instant — an instinct they shared now, calibrated by the same weeks of fear, and she saw him register that too.

"No," Rafael said quietly. "It isn't humility. But it isn't nothing either." He picked up a length of pruned applewood and turned it in his fingers, stripping the bark with his thumbnail. "Do you know why I prune in winter, really? Not for the trees' health, though that's the excuse. Because cutting is the only part of this work I'm good at that doesn't lie. Every cut is a decision. This branch lives, this one dies, and the tree will tell you within a season whether you chose well, and you cannot argue with it. No committee, no doctrine. Just the wound and the healing." He looked at her. "You're a cutter, Ulyana. Whatever else you are. You make clean cuts and you live with them. In my experience that is rarer than belief, and worth more."

"And if the cuts are wrong?"

"Then next winter you prune differently." He said it simply, without consolation, and somehow that was more consoling than comfort could have been. "You're assuming the finishing matters because of what it finishes. Maybe it matters because of what it makes of the finisher."

* * *

The afternoon light came low and gold through the western gap in the hills, striking sideways across the snow so that every tree threw a shadow three times its height, a forest of blue-black crosses laid over the white. They went back to work. Her hands ached inside her gloves; her shoulders burned; and she found, with a kind of shame, that she was happy. Not the bright happiness of safety — she had not been safe since the bridge chained shut behind her — but the low, banked happiness of a body fully used, of a day with a shape, of company that required nothing performed.

At some point their work converged on the same tree, an old Grushovka leaning away from the prevailing wind, and they ended up beneath its canopy together, reaching into the crown from opposite sides, their arms crossing sometimes in the tangle of branches. He steadied a limb for her. She took his shears when hers wouldn't bite. It was choreography without discussion, and when she came down out of the branches and stood close to him in the tree's narrow shade, neither of them stepped back.

"We'll be gone by spring," she said. It was not a question. It was arithmetic — patrols, thaw, the dispatches, the confession he'd asked her to carry, the list with names on it that she had read and reread until the letters swam.

"Yes."

"You know that. I know that. We're standing here pruning trees we'll never see fruit from."

Rafael reached up and touched a branch above her head — not touching her, touching the branch — and followed it down with his eyes to the bud scar, the tight knot of next summer folded inside the dead of winter, invisible unless you knew to look. "Everything I have ever loved," he said, "I have tended on borrowed time. You get so you can tell the difference between wasting time and spending it." He lowered his hand, and now it rested very lightly on her shoulder, snow-damp wool under his palm, and she let it stay. "This is spending it."

She turned her face up. For a moment neither moved, the way two people stand at the edge of cold water, each waiting to see who goes in first. Then she kissed him, or he kissed her — afterward neither could have said — and it tasted of the frozen apple, sweet and dark and a little past saving, and the orchard stood witness around them, four hundred trees holding their buds like held breath.

When they broke apart the light had failed to violet and the temperature was falling fast, and far down the valley, where the road entered town, she saw the lanterns of a patrol swing off the highway and start up the lane toward the workshop — three lights, moving unhurried, in no direction but theirs.

Rafael saw them too. His hand closed briefly around hers, then released.

"Inside," he said. "Take the back path. And Ulyana—" he was already turning, gathering the sledge rope, becoming someone harder and older before her eyes, the tender morning folding itself away like a letter— "whatever happens tonight, remember the cut. Clean angle. Outside the collar."

The lanterns came up the lane, and the ice on the river knocked, and knocked.
