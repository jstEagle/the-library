## 13. Intercepted

The morning the notice went up, frost had painted the workshop windows in ferns, and Ulyana was elbow-deep in a crate of bruised Winesaps, learning again how much of survival was repetition. Sort, wrap, pack. Sort, wrap, pack. The apples came down the chute from the loft in their wooden slats and she took them one at a time into her hands, turning each for rot the way she had once turned cipher blocks for weakness — looking for the soft place, the brown spreading under skin that still looked whole.

She heard the boots before she saw them. Not soldiers' boots; those she knew by now, the heavy nailed rhythm that announced itself a street away. These were lighter, quicker, the boots of a boy running errands, and they stopped outside the packing hall's side door with the particular hesitation of someone who did not want to be seen stopping.

Marta Voss put her head through the door. She was the cooper's widow two houses down, sixty years old, with hands like tree bark and a voice that carried across the market square without effort. In the weeks since Ulyana had come to Vetrov, Marta had taken to buying apples she did not need, lingering over the scales, asking after Rafael's health in tones pitched to be overheard. It was cover, and both women knew it was cover, and neither had ever said so aloud. That was how it worked here. The whole town performed a play in which nothing unusual was happening, and the price of admission was simply never breaking character.

"Paper on the customs house," Marta said, low, not looking at Ulyana but at the crate between them, as if discussing the fruit. "And on the church door. And on the ferry office." Her thumb moved along the crate's edge, worrying a splinter. "You'll want to see it before you go anywhere."

Ulyana wiped her hands on her apron with a deliberateness she did not feel. "Bad news?"

"News," Marta said. "The kind that walks around wearing a uniform."

* * *

The notice was pasted at eye level, four copies along the customs house wall, the paste still glistening, the colonel's seal stamped in violet wax above each signature line. Ulyana approached it the way she had learned to approach everything now — as part of her performance, a woman out for morning bread who happened to pause, as everyone paused, to read what the authorities wished read.

It was written in the flat administrative language that made terror sound like bookkeeping.

*On the night of the 14th, an unauthorized radio transmission was intercepted originating within the township of Vetrov. The transmission was incomplete. Persons with knowledge of its origin are instructed to report to the military commandant. Failure to report constitutes complicity.*

Below this, two descriptions.

*A man, approximately fifty years, tall, gray at the temples, known to frequent the river district. Walks with no limp but favors his right knee in cold weather.*

*A woman, approximately thirty years, foreign accent imperfectly concealed, dark hair worn pinned, works with her hands. May present as a laborer or shop worker.*

Ulyana read it twice. The first time her eyes went where anyone's would — to the words *unauthorized*, *incomplete*, *complicity*. The second time she read it as a professional, and the professional reading was worse. *Imperfectly concealed.* Someone had heard her speak. Someone had stood close enough, long enough, for the vowels of her capital training to snag on their ear like a nail catching wool. And the description of the man — *favors his right knee in cold weather* — was so precisely Rafael that she understood, with a cold unspooling beneath her ribs, that whoever had talked had not talked in generalities. They had talked from acquaintance.

Around her, the small morning crowd shifted and murmured. An old man spat carefully to the side. A woman pulled her child closer by the sleeve. Nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that could not be repeated later under questioning, and everyone in Vetrov had learned the arithmetic of repetition: whatever you said might be said back to you someday by a man with a ledger and no reason to be kind.

Ulyana bought her bread. She walked home the long way, past the frozen fountain, past the shuttered ferry office where a fifth copy of the notice flapped in the wind off the ice, and she let her face do nothing at all, because a face doing nothing was the only currency that spent safely here.

But inside, where the performance could not reach, something had begun to scream in a very quiet voice.

They had been careful. God, they had been so careful — the transmitter buried under the press house floor, the sessions kept short, the antenna disguised as a drying line, the transmissions timed to the patrol rotations Rafael had memorized until he could recite them like prayers. And still. *Incomplete*, the notice said, which meant they had caught the middle of a burst, the carrier wave bleeding through the winter air like light under a door. Somewhere in the hills east of town a listening post had turned its ear toward Vetrov, and the colonel had come down from his headquarters with his violet wax and his printing block, and now the whole town wore the search like weather.

She thought about the woman in the crowd, pulling her child close. She thought: *she will remember my face from this morning. I stood next to her. I fit the description.* Every person she passed became a potential witness, every witness a thread leading back through the snow to the workshop door. She had spent her career believing herself a needle moving through fabric, invisible among threads. Now she could feel herself as the fabric felt a needle: a small wrongness, a pull, a place where the weave no longer lay flat.

* * *

Rafael took the news standing at the window of his upstairs room, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a man inspecting his orchard, though the orchard was only white rows and black branches and the river beyond, groaning faintly under its load of new ice.

"Incomplete," he repeated. He said the word the way a doctor repeats a term from a lab report — wanting to hear it again to be sure of its weight. "How much did they take?"

"The middle third, by the timing. We were ninety seconds into the burst when you cut power." Ulyana sat on the edge of his bed because there was nowhere else to sit and standing felt too much like waiting for a blow. "Ninety seconds of our traffic. Encrypted, but —"

"But encryption delays a good analyst by days, not months." He turned from the window. His face in the lamplight had gone the color of the paper notices downstairs, and older. "And they have the rhythm of it. Frequency, schedule, duration. Enough to know we exist. Enough to know we are amateurs enough to be caught."

"We are not amateurs."

"No." Something moved through his expression — not quite a smile, more like the ghost of one visiting a house it used to live in. "We are worse. We are professionals pretending, which means when we fail, we fail expensively."

He crossed to the stove and fed it a stick of wood, and for a moment the two of them watched the flame catch, the way people watch fires when the alternative is looking at each other. The room smelled of lamp oil and dried apple peel and, beneath it, the iron scent of the press house below, where the reserve transmitter waited under the floorboards like a body in a shallow grave.

"They posted descriptions," Ulyana said. "Mine is accurate. Rafael — *imperfectly concealed accent*. Someone has heard me talk. At the market, at church, somewhere. And yours—" She stopped.

"Favors my right knee." He flexed the leg in question, grimacing slightly at the cold-locked joint. "That would be the ferryman. Petar. He watched me walk the ice road all January." Rafael's voice stayed level, but Ulyana had learned to hear the floors under his floors, the way she had learned to hear the double bottoms in his crates. Underneath the levelness was fury, and underneath the fury was something colder and more patient that she suspected had lived in him for years.

"What happens to Petar?"

"He hasn't been arrested yet, or the notice would say so. They prefer to post first, arrest second. It makes the town feel the posting is a courtesy rather than a threat." He straightened. "Which means we have perhaps two days before the courtesy expires."

"And then?"

"And then Petar tells them what he knows, or doesn't. He's an old man. He ferried contraband tobacco for twenty years and never gave up a customer, but tobacco customers don't hang you for naming them." Rafael looked at the fire. "If he talks, they learn the gray-haired man crosses the ice regularly toward the press house. If he doesn't talk, they learn it anyway, because they'll follow him instead of asking him."

Ulyana felt the trap closing with a clarity that was almost physical — walls of arithmetic, each number another soldier's route, another hour of the patrol rotation reassigned, another degree of certainty accumulating on the far side of the wall like snow load on a roof. "Then we stop transmitting."

"We transmit *once more*. The final dispatch." He said it flatly, and she heard in the flatness that he had already done the same arithmetic and arrived at the same terrible sum. "Everything else can burn. That cannot."

* * *

They arrested Marta Voss on the third evening.

Ulyana learned of it from the boy with the quick boots, the same one who had brought word of the notices, a cooper's apprentice named Tomas who ran messages through the town's quiet nervous system for coins and for the pleasure of mattering. He came to the side door after dark, breath smoking, and delivered the news in the clipped whisper of someone who has rehearsed brevity as a defense.

"Took her from her house at suppertime. Four men. Didn't let her fetch her shawl." He swallowed. "Her granddaughter run to my master's house after. Says the soldiers turned over everything. The flour, the mattress, the pickle barrels. Looking for papers." He glanced past Ulyana into the dark of the packing hall, where crates stood stacked like sleeping animals, and lowered his voice further. "She'd told the granddaughter, if ever — if ever anything happened — tell the apple man the ice is thick this year."

Then he was gone, boots fading down the frozen lane, and Ulyana stood in the doorway holding the cold like a thing handed to her, because it was.

The ice is thick this year. She turned the phrase over. It meant, presumably, *go now, cross while you can.* It also meant nothing, officially, that any informer could hang a meaning onto. Marta Voss had built her warning the way Rafael built his ledgers — every surface innocent, the truth hidden in the structure. And now she was sitting in the colonel's cells without her shawl, and the question was not whether she would talk but what talking would cost her, and whether an old woman's endurance was a resource Ulyana had already spent without ever counting it.

She found Rafael in the press house, standing over the loose floorboard with a lantern turned low, and told him.

He received it the way stone receives rain. Nothing showed. But his hand, resting on the edge of the workbench, closed slowly around a wooden spacer until she heard the fibers creak, and when he finally spoke, his voice had dropped into a register she had not heard from him before — very quiet, very controlled, the voice of a man holding a door shut against something enormous.

"Marta Voss covered for us for eleven weeks. Bought apples she couldn't eat. Told the requisition officer her cousin was visiting when it was you crossing her yard at night." He set the crushed spacer down. "Eleven weeks. For what? So we could send ninety seconds of traffic that half of it got intercepted anyway?"

"You don't mean that."

"Don't I?" He turned on her, and the lantern threw his shadow huge against the pressing beams overhead. "Tell me what you mean, then. Tell me what her arrest buys. Tell me the exchange rate — one old woman per dispatch? Because I've seen that ledger before. I kept it. I know exactly what column her name goes in, and I know who writes the entry, and it isn't the colonel. The colonel is just the ink. We are the hand."

The words landed in Ulyana like cold water finding a crack. Because he was right, and she had known he was right since the notices went up, since she had stood in the market crowd calculating witness lines and escape routes while Marta Voss stood beside her buying bread she would never cook. Abstraction had been her armor all her working life — the cause, the mission, the greater arithmetic in which individual names dissolved into categories. But abstraction did not sit in a cell without her shawl. Abstraction did not have a granddaughter running through the snow with a coded message about ice.

"She knew," Ulyana said. Her own voice surprised her — rough, unsteady. "When she started covering for us, she knew what covering costs. She's lived in Vetrov her whole life. She's buried a husband and two sons, one to the war and one to the purges. Nobody in this town covers for strangers out of innocence." She made herself meet Rafael's eyes. "She chose. That doesn't make it bearable. But it makes it hers, and I won't insult her by deciding she didn't understand the price."

Rafael held her gaze for a long moment. Then he looked away, at the floorboards, at the buried transmitter, at all the machinery of secrets that had cost a widow her freedom, and his jaw worked once.

"When I named my colleagues," he said quietly, "I told myself the same thing. That they understood the world they'd entered. That they chose." He picked up the lantern. "I was lying. It's possible to choose and still be betrayed, Ulyana. Both things are true at once, and the second one doesn't dissolve because you admire the first."

He climbed the stairs toward the workshop, and she stood alone in the press house with the dead press and the living secret under the floor, and outside, far off across the fields, the river spoke in its sleep — a long, deep groan rolling under the ice like something turning over, restless, beginning to wake.

Above her, through the floorboards, she heard Rafael begin to move — drawers opening, papers gathering, the sounds of a man preparing a fire.

The final transmission would happen tomorrow night. And Marta Voss, in a cell across town, had already sent them her last message: the ice is thick this year.

Ulyana pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars, and made herself go up and help him pack.
