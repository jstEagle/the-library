## 17. Ice Groaning

The river spoke first.

Ulyana heard it in the dark before dawn on the fourth of February — a long, groaning exhalation that traveled under the ice from the far bend to the pilings below the press house, as if the water beneath had turned over in its sleep and found the season wrong. She lay awake above the packing hall with her breath making small clouds toward the ceiling beams and listened to the river say what neither she nor Rafael had permitted themselves to say aloud: *early. It is early this year.*

The thaw was not due for six weeks. The almanac nailed to the workshop wall said so; the gray hairs of the market women said so; the ice itself, a foot thick and road-gray along the shore, had said so every morning since December. But the ice did not consult almanacs. Somewhere upstream, in country she would never see, warm rain had fallen, or a dam had been opened, or the winter had simply decided — the way wars decided, the way governments decided — to end ahead of schedule. And now the river was counting down for them, one groan at a time.

She dressed without lighting the lamp. In three months of hiding inside another woman's life she had learned to move through Vetrov's hours like a hand moving through water — no splash, no ripple. Down the narrow stair, past the racks of empty winter crates, into the packing hall where the cold kept its own ledger of everything they'd left undone.

Rafael was already there. He stood at the long sorting table with the reserve transmitter disassembled before him, its components laid out on oilcloth like instruments before an operation: the brass key, the coiled aerial wire wound around a bottle, the battery cells wrapped in sacking against the damp, the frequency chart folded to the page he'd need. A single lamp burned low behind him, and his shadow made a second man on the wall who worked when he worked and stopped when he stopped.

"You heard it," he said. Not a question.

"At four o'clock. Twice more since."

He nodded once and went on checking connections, his fingers sure even in the cold, though she could see the tremor he refused to acknowledge living somewhere deeper than the hands — in the set of his jaw, in the way his eyes moved over the table as if memorizing it. As if he were already saying goodbye to the objects.

"Three nights," he said. "If the pattern holds, we have three nights of solid ice before the patrols stop trusting it, and maybe five before the ferry crossing opens and they have something better to do than walk the streets looking for you." He looked up. "We send tomorrow night. Everything."

"Tomorrow is two days sooner than the schedule—"

"The schedule assumed February." His voice stayed level, but there was iron under it now, the tone of a man who had run ciphers through worse winters than this one and buried colleagues who'd trusted schedules. "The river has rewritten it. We don't argue with the river."

* * *

By daylight the town confirmed everything the river had promised at night.

The patrols doubled. Ulyana counted them from the loft window with the practiced patience of someone who had once done this work for a living and had never entirely stopped doing it in her head: pairs of soldiers where there had been singles, an extra circuit of the embankment at the hour the lamplighter made his rounds, a new checkpoint at the bridge road that had not existed the week before. The colonel's men were not searching anymore. They were *waiting* — arranged like a net around the town, their faces turned toward the water, because everyone in authority understood what the thaw meant. The river was a door, and doors were for guarding.

At the well, Marta the cooper's wife caught Ulyana's sleeve and drew her close, her breath smelling of onion and fear.

"They took Petar's boy for questioning yesterday. Just questioning, they say." Her eyes flicked toward the workshop, then away, quick as a bird avoiding a shutter. "And there are soldiers measuring the ice at the shallows. Measuring it, do you understand? With augers. Like men planning to cross it themselves."

"Then they believe someone means to leave by it," Ulyana said carefully.

"Someone means to leave by it, or someone means to arrive." Marta pressed her hand once, hard, and let go. "Either way, girl, whatever you're not doing here — don't be doing it much longer."

Ulyana carried the water back up the hill slowly, letting her shoulders curve into the shape of a tired packing-house worker, while inside the straight spine of her the countdown ran like a second pulse. Two days. Perhaps less. Every face she passed might be cataloguing her; every soldier at the corner was a hinge on which the whole remaining weight of the mission could swing. She had crossed contested borders before with codes sewn into her coat, but she had never done it knowing the names of the people those codes would deliver, never carried paper whose weight she felt not in grams but in lives. The coat lining seemed heavier every day. She had begun, in unguarded moments, to imagine she could hear the dispatches rustling against her ribs like something alive.

* * *

That evening Rafael spread the final pages across the sorting table and they worked by the light of two lamps shielded with tin, the shutters closed, the stove fed just enough to keep the ink from freezing in its pot.

The last dispatch was the longest and the most heavily encoded — she had known that from the capital, had felt it even then as a wrongness she couldn't name, the way one feels a false floorboard before ever stepping on it. Now, after everything, after the colonel's visit and the neighbor arrested down the lane, after the ledger of names and the truth she had dragged out of Rafael by lamplight, the encoding read to her like what it was: a document written by men who expected it to survive longer than the people it described.

She transcribed. He checked. The work had the rhythm of all their shared weeks — her pencil moving in columns, his eyes following, occasionally his hand reaching to turn a page before she asked — but tonight the rhythm had a metronome behind it that neither of them acknowledged. Between strokes of the pencil, both of them listened. To the wind working at the shutters. To boots passing in the street, slowing near the workshop door, moving on. And underneath everything, fainter than footsteps, patient as digestion: the river, talking to itself.

*Groan.* A long note, rising. Falling silent.

Rafael's pencil paused. Resumed.

"Tell me again how you'll go," he said, some time past midnight. He didn't look up from the cipher sheet.

"The same way I told you. Downstream past the tannery, onto the ice at the willow stand where the current runs shallow. Cross at the widest point, where the freeze is oldest, and come up the far bank north of the customs post." She recited it flatly, a route rehearsed until it had worn smooth as a stone. "Then nine kilometers through the pine belt to the relay house."

"And if the ice gives?"

"Then I swim to the edge, break out, and keep going wet. I've been cold before." She said it lightly, and hated herself for it, because she saw him not accept the lightness — saw it land on him like a debt he intended to collect.

"There's a better crossing," he said. "Two hundred meters downstream of the willows. My grandfather used it for hauling timber. The gravel bar underneath makes the ice double-thick, but only along a line as wide as this table. Miss the line and you're on four inches over deep water." He pulled a stub of pencil from his pocket and drew it on the margin of the frequency chart: the shore, the line, two crosses marking the safe entry and exit. "I'll draw it larger for you tomorrow. You'll carry it here—" he tapped his own temple "—not on paper. Paper sinks. Memory floats."

"You've thought about this a great deal."

"I've thought about very little else since January." He said it simply, without performance, which was how he always said the truest things, and she had learned to receive them that way — without flinching, because flinching would make them harder for both of them to hold.

They worked on. The stove ticked. Somewhere above the town the clouds were breaking, and a blade of moonlight slid between the shutter slats and lay across the table like a drawn line — the line of the crossing, she thought absurdly, projected here into the warm room, waiting for her.

* * *

It was nearly two when the patrol came to the door.

Not the colonel himself this time — a sergeant and two men, their lantern throwing a wobbling coin of light against the workshop's front boards, their voices carrying the particular false courtesy of soldiers who had been told to search politely and resented it.

"Ostrov! Open up. Inventory check."

Ulyana was on her feet with the transmitter's brass key in her hand before she had consciously decided to move. Rafael was faster. One motion of his arm swept the frequency chart off the table into the drawer; the other killed both lamps; and in the sudden dark his hand found her wrist and pressed it — *down, still, breathe* — guiding her into the space behind the apple racks where the crates stood stacked to the ceiling and the smell of stored fruit was thick enough to hide in.

He opened the door himself, unhurried, buttoning his coat as though he'd been asleep these last minutes. Lamplight from the soldiers' lantern fell into the hall and found exactly what it should find: a cold room, a sleeping man, nothing more.

"Inventory," the sergeant repeated, pushing past him. "New orders. All stores of lamp oil and wax sealed for requisition. And a count of persons resident."

"Persons resident," Rafael echoed mildly. "You can count me. I'm the whole census."

The soldiers moved through the packing hall. Ulyana stood among the crates with her heart beating so hard she was certain it struck the wood beside her like a knuckle on a door. Through a finger-wide gap between two boxes she watched the lantern light travel the walls, watched it pause at the sorting table — at the oilcloth, still spread, still bearing the innocent geometry of a disassembled pressing machine, because Rafael had rebuilt the disguise around the transmitter the way a crab wears its shell — watched the sergeant lean over it, grunt, and move on.

"In the back room," one of the soldiers said. "There's a woman's shawl on a chair."

"My packer's," Rafael said. "She left it when she went home to her mother's in Zalavany for the winter. Women leave things. It's how they mark territory."

A silence, in which Ulyana heard the sergeant deciding. She had spent her professional life reading such silences from the outside — from the safety of capitals, from behind desks — and had never imagined how long a silence could stretch, how many deaths could fit inside ten seconds of a lantern guttering.

"Zalavany," the sergeant said at last. "That's through the checkpoint. Your packer will need papers to come back through."

"She knows the arrangement."

"Hm." Boots on floorboards, retreating. At the door the sergeant turned, and even from her hiding place Ulyana felt the weight of his glance sweep the room one last time, slow, thorough, a man memorizing a room he suspected he'd see again under different orders. "Thaw's coming early, Ostrov. Command says the river'll be open in days. When it opens, this town gets busy. You'd want your accounts settled before then."

"I'm a careful bookkeeper," Rafael said.

The door closed. The lantern light withdrew down the street, shrinking to a spark, vanishing.

Ulyana came out from behind the crates. Her legs felt strange under her, too light, as if some portion of her body had already left. Rafael stood with his back against the closed door, and in the dark she could not see his face, only hear his breathing — controlled, deliberate, the breathing of a man managing himself the way he managed everything, with an accountant's precision.

"A woman's shawl," she whispered. "When did you—"

"November. Before I knew your name." He pushed off the door. "I keep a decoy life in this building the way other men keep savings. A shawl. A hair ribbon in the wash basin. A second pair of women's boots, one size too large, muddied on purpose. If anyone ever searched, there would be evidence of a woman — a vague one, gone, deniable." He relit the lamp, and his face emerged from the dark wearing an expression she had never seen on him before: exhaustion stripped of all its architecture, plain as a field after harvest. "Tonight it bought us a night. It won't buy a second one. They'll ask in Zalavany about your packer. There is no packer. Within two days that thread pulls through."

"So we send tomorrow night."

"We send tomorrow night." He crossed to the table and put both hands flat on the oilcloth, over the brass key, as if taking an oath upon it. "One transmission. Full text. And then—" he stopped.

"And then," she prompted, though she knew. She had known since the ledger, since the confession, since the night he'd shown her the reserve transmitter under the press house and the price of his pardon had lain between them like a third person in the room.

"And then you go to the willows," he finished, "and I stay."

* * *

They did not sleep. There was no pretense of sleeping. They sat at either end of the sorting table with the completed cipher pages squared and bundled in oilskin, and the lamps burned low, and outside the wind changed direction twice, and each time the wind shifted the river answered it — louder now, less patient, the groans coming in sequences rather than singly, as though the ice were practicing the sound it would make when it finally gave way.

Once, near four in the morning, a crack rang out sharp as rifle fire from the direction of the shallows, and they both froze, and then both exhaled, recognizing it for what it was — not gunfire, only the river rehearsing.

"It's early," she said. "Everyone keeps saying the thaw is early, but it isn't the thaw, is it? The thaw is a season. This is something else. This ice was never going to hold to the calendar. It was only ever going to hold as long as it pleased."

"Like everything else this winter," Rafael said.

She looked at him across the table, across the bundle that held the last dispatches of a dying republic and the confession of a man who had sold his colleagues to save an orchard, and felt the strangeness of the hour settle over her — that tenderness which arrives precisely when there is no time left to be tender in. Tomorrow night one of them would climb the bank at the willow stand and one of them would not. Neither of them said it. The river was saying enough for both.

"Read me the route again," she said instead. "The gravel bar."

He read it. Entry at the split willow, bearing on the broken chimney of the tannery. Hold the line of the bar — no more than a table's width — exit at the leaning birch. Nine kilometers north through pines. Relay house at the old forestry station. She closed her eyes and walked it once, twice, a third time, laying it into her memory like bricks into a wall.

Outside, the sky was turning the color of ash, and under the groaning of the river, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots — many boots — moving down toward the embankment, where the soldiers with their augers would soon be measuring the ice again, learning it, learning them.

Rafael blew out the lamps. "Get your things ready," he said quietly. "Tonight we finish it."
