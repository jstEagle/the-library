## 18. The Last Transmission

The river began to speak at dusk, and by midnight it was shouting.

Ulyana heard it through the floorboards of the packing hall, a deep irregular groaning that traveled up through the joists and into the soles of her feet like the voice of some enormous animal turning over in its sleep. The ice on the Vetrov was failing early this year — three weeks ahead of any thaw Rafael could remember, he'd said, though she suspected he said it the way men say anything when they want to believe the world is simply out of joint rather than closing around them. The patrols had doubled. Lanterns moved along the embankment all night now, and twice since Tuesday she had seen soldiers walking out onto the ice itself, testing it with iron bars, as if they too understood that the river was the last door and everyone wanted to know how soon it would open.

She sat beneath the press house with the key drum against her knee and waited for Rafael to come down.

Above her, through the trapdoor, she could hear him moving — the deliberate sounds of a man putting his affairs in order without admitting that was what he was doing. A drawer opened. Closed. Something heavy shifted across the boards: crates, perhaps, dragged over the trapdoor's seam, or only the weight of him kneeling there a moment before he descended into the last night of everything they had built between them. She did not call up to hurry him. There were no more hours to save by spending them impatiently.

The lamp between her feet burned low and steady, turned nearly to its smallest flame. In its circle of light the apparatus looked almost domestic — the reserve transmitter Rafael had built from salvaged parts in the years after his pardon, its brass fittings dull with disuse until this week, the coiled antenna wire he had strung along the ceiling beams among the drying apple racks where no inspector would think to look for copper among the fruit. Beside it lay the dispatch folder: her final transmission, decoded two nights ago in this same lamplight while gunfire cracked somewhere past the mill district and Rafael read each line over her shoulder with an expression she could not then name and could now, finally, translate.

It was not surprise on his face. It was recognition. He had known what she carried. Perhaps he had known longer than she had.

Three names struck from the list of officials to be surrendered to the executioners. That was what the dispatch held — not rescue terms, not funds, not the lifeline she had crossed a frozen republic believing in, but a ledger of human beings written out in cipher and addressed to men who would read it with their boots on someone's neck. She had spent the days since learning that truth carrying it the way one carries a hot stone: hand to hand, unable to set it down, unwilling to drop it where others might step.

But tonight she would not carry it unchanged.

In her coat pocket, folded into quarters so small they felt like a seed, were the pages she had copied by candlelight over the past two evenings while Rafael slept badly upstairs — or pretended to sleep; she no longer flattered herself that either of them deceived the other about much. His confession, transcribed into the bureau's own columnar figures. And below it, in her own hand, the falsified entry: three names removed, the checksum adjusted, the arithmetic made clean. She had done the work four times to be sure. She had checked it against every table in the workshop's hidden archive. If anyone in the capital audited that transmission — and they might; the new masters of the republic audited everything — the entry would hold. It would hold because she had learned her craft in rooms like this one, from people like the man above her, and because a lie built properly is indistinguishable from a fact.

She had not told him. That was the part she kept returning to, turning it over like a coin whose face kept changing. She owed him the truth — they had traded truths across this winter like smugglers trading contraband, and the currency had become something neither of them called love aloud because naming it would have been a kind of theft, taking the thing for themselves when the world had already claimed both their futures. But if she told him, he would argue. He would insist the confession go unwritten, that his name stay off the wire, that she keep her hands clean of his particular penance. And she had decided — lying awake with the river groaning beneath her — that this was the one decision in this whole frozen season that belonged to her alone.

He had named colleagues once to buy his ground. He meant to name himself now to pay it back. Both acts were his alone, and she understood them, and she refused to let the second stand unaccompanied. Three strangers on a list would live or die because a courier in Vetrov had decided, in the space between one groan of the river ice and the next, that history could be edited by whoever held the pen. Let it cost what it cost. She had watched enough this winter to know the price was already being paid everywhere, by everyone, in currencies no one had agreed upon.

The trapdoor lifted. Cold air fell down the ladder ahead of Rafael like water.

He came down slowly, favoring the hip that had ached since the night he'd waded the shallows to check the anchor point for the antenna's counterweight. He wore his oldest coat, the one with the burned cuff, and he carried two things: a pistol wrapped in sacking, and a loaf of black bread with something sewn inside the crust that she chose not to ask about.

"Patrol crossed the mill bridge twenty minutes ago," he said. "They'll work the embankment to the ferry stairs and back. When they reach the willows they'll be directly overhead." He crouched beside the lamp and unwrapped the pistol with the calm of a man laying out tools for a repair. "We transmit during the gap. Eleven minutes, maybe twelve, if the sergeant stops for tobacco the way he has every night this week."

"You've been timing him."

"I've been timing everything," Rafael said, "since October." He looked at her then, and in the low light his face was all planes and shadows, older than it had been in autumn, and yet she thought — with a clarity that hurt — that it was also more at rest than she had ever seen it. Whatever he had decided about tonight, he had decided it completely. "Ulyana. Before we begin. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"It may. Say it anyway."

She folded her hands in her lap to keep them still. "If it goes wrong," she said, "you burn the archives and I cross the ice, and one of us finishes it. That was always the shape of it."

"And the other thing? The thing you're not saying?"

She met his eyes. For a moment she considered telling him — here, now, with eleven minutes of safety left in the world — and then she saw how he would look, how he would take the pages gently from her hands and feed them to the lamp and tell her that his debts were his own, and she knew she could not survive watching him erase the only good thing she had ever done with the machinery of her trade.

"The thing I'm not saying," she said, "is that the bread smells wonderful, and I haven't eaten since noon."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly; suspicion they had spent long ago. A man fluent in concealment knows when another person is concealing, but he also knows when to permit it. Rafael nodded slowly and broke the loaf apart, and they ate standing up among the presses like thieves sharing a stolen meal, and the river groaned beneath them, and above them the patrol lanterns swung nearer along the embankment road.

* * *

The sergeant stopped for tobacco at the willows, as he always did.

Rafael counted it under his breath — she watched his lips move, marking seconds the way monks mark prayers — and at the count of forty he threw the main switch. The transmitter woke with a hum that Ulyana felt in her teeth, and the needle on the ammeter trembled upright like a small creature roused from hibernation.

"Antenna," Rafael said quietly.

She pulled the cord. Somewhere above them, among the apple racks, the concealed wire rose on its counterweight through a vent cut into the eaves years ago and never used until this winter. Rafael set his fingers to the key.

What followed was the strangest music Ulyana had ever helped make: the dry chatter of the transmitter spelling out the surrender of a republic in five-character groups, punctuated by the long silences between bursts while they listened for footsteps on the floorboards overhead. Rafael sent with a rhythm that betrayed his years at the old regime's central bureau — fast, clean, unhurried even now — and between bursts he fed her the decoded text line by line so she could verify against her copy, and she verified, and the list of names went out into the freezing air one group at a time toward an operator she would never see, in a capital she would never return to, addressed to men whose faces appeared in newsprint with their collars up.

Halfway through the handover list, while Rafael's attention was fixed on the key and the clock and the ceiling, Ulyana reached into her coat.

The pages came out soundless. She had practiced that too — drawing them without rustle, the way she had once practiced lifting documents from a desk in the Foreign Ministry while the minister himself talked to her about the weather. She laid them beside the log sheet. Rafael's confession first: thirty-one groups, already keyed and ready, timed to follow the dispatch with a plausible interval. Then her insertion, tucked into the stream at the point where the checksum recalculation made room for it — three names gone, the arithmetic corrected, nothing else disturbed. She had written the substitution into the transmission sequence an hour ago, while Rafael was upstairs dragging crates over the trapdoor, and now there was nothing left to do but watch it happen.

"Next block," Rafael murmured, and sent it.

Her block. Her altered block. The needle chattered out the names of three officials who would wake tomorrow morning in a city falling apart, never knowing that somewhere under a press house on the Vetrov, a woman who had never believed in the cause she served had reached into the machinery of history and subtracted them from its sentence. Their fates passed through her hands and out along the wire, and she felt — not triumph, nothing so simple — but a fierce, cold clarity, the same feeling she imagined a person has setting a broken bone: agony and rightness fused into a single act that cannot be undone and should not be.

The river groaned so loudly just then that both of them froze. Above, boots crunched on frost. The sergeant coughed, struck a match — the smell of it drifted down through the vent, tobacco and phosphorus — and Rafael, without looking away from the clock, sent the next group.

Then the confession. Thirty-one groups of a man naming himself to his enemies, transmitted faithfully by the man himself, who believed he was sending his soul across the wire alone. Ulyana watched each character leave his fingers and thought: *I am the only witness. I am the only one who knows you are not alone.* It was unbearable and it was necessary, and she found that she could bear it precisely because it was necessary, which was, she supposed, the whole of her creed distilled at last.

The last block went out at 3:14 by Rafael's pocket watch. He closed the contact one final time, held it a breath longer than the protocol required — a period, a signature, a full stop at the end of a life — and opened the circuit. The hum died. The needle settled. The silence afterward was enormous, filled entirely by the river and by their own breathing.

"Acknowledgment window in nine minutes," Rafael said, already reaching to collapse the antenna. "If they confirm receipt—"

Boots on the boards above them. Not the sergeant's unhurried tread. Many boots, moving fast, and a voice — the colonel's voice, hoarse and certain, calling orders into the dark.

Rafael's head came up. For a single instant their eyes met in the lamplight, and Ulyana watched a lifetime of calculation run through his face and arrive, with terrible speed, at its conclusion.

"The front stair," he said. "Now. Take the folder and the bread and don't stop for anything, not me, not—" The rest of the sentence dissolved into the sound of the trapdoor being thrown open above them, and lantern light pouring down the shaft like something spilled.

* * *

They ran.

There was no plan after the first thirty seconds; there was only the building coming apart around them and their bodies obeying the oldest instructions. Ulyana went up the back ladder through the fruit cellar as the front doors gave way below, wood splintering, shouts echoing through the packing hall where she had spent a winter sorting apples into crates with false bottoms. Rafael was somewhere behind her — she heard him, not following, doing something else, something deliberate — and then the smell reached her.

Lamp oil. Everywhere, it seemed, all at once, and beneath it the sweet rot-sugar smell of stored fruit beginning to cook.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, and climbed faster.

She came out through the cellar hatch into the orchard's edge, into air so cold it flayed, and turned to see the press house windows already glowing orange from within. Figures moved along the embankment road — the patrol, drawn by fire now instead of hunting it, and somewhere among them the colonel's voice rising in fury and confusion, ordering men toward a blaze that had chosen its own hour. A rifle shot cracked from the trees near the ferry stairs — wild, or aimed at shadows, impossible to know. Another answered it. The night filled with noise and light, and none of it, none of it, was aimed at her.

Because he had made sure of it. Because the fire was the message and the diversion and the penance, all three, priced exactly.

"Rafael!" She screamed it into the heat, into the roar of the packing hall catching — the dry apple racks going up like tinder, decades of paper ledgers feeding flames that climbed the walls with a sound like applause. She got as far as the side door. Heat pushed her back the way a hand pushes, flat and absolute, and through the burning window glass she saw him — one glimpse, one — standing in the middle of the hall with the oil can still in his hand, looking not at the door but upward, at the beams he had raised and the rafters where the antenna wire hung glowing like a filament, at the whole architecture of the life he had bought and was now spending to its final coin.

He saw her. She was certain of it, though the smoke stood between them like a congregation. He did not wave. He did not mouth anything. He only inclined his head — slightly, formally, the acknowledgment of one professional to another at the close of business — and turned back to the fire as if resuming a conversation already in progress.

Ulyana ran.

Behind her the workshop became a torch visible from the far hills, and the patrols became rescuers fighting it, and the colonel's hunt drowned in the general catastrophe of light. Ahead of her the river waited, black and groaning, its ice flexing visibly now in the starlight, plates of it rising and settling along cracks that glowed faintly white. She reached the bank with the folder against her ribs and the confirmation slip sewn inside her boot and her lungs full of smoke that tasted like apples, like cider, like every winter hour she would now carry forever.

Out on the ice, a crack ran the width of the river with a report like artillery.

She stepped down onto it anyway.
