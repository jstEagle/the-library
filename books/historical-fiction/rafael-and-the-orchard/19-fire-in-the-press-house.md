## 19. Fire in the Press House

The knock came at half past eleven, and Rafael knew before he opened it that this was the last night of the workshop.

He had known it the way a man knows a storm is coming — not from any single sign but from the whole weight of the air, the pressure behind his teeth, the way the horses in the colonel's convoy had screamed all afternoon for no reason anyone could name. The knock itself was ordinary: three raps, unhurried, gloved. It was the silence afterward that told him. No shuffling of boots. No second knock. Whoever stood on the step was content to wait, because waiting cost them nothing and him everything.

He set down the ledger he had been pretending to read. The lamp threw his shadow long across the packing-hall floor, over the empty crates with their double bottoms, over the press that had not pressed fruit in six weeks but had transmitted three dispatches into the frozen dark. He looked at all of it the way a man looks at a room he is leaving — not sentimentally, but taking inventory.

Then he went to the door and opened it.

Colonel Vasen stood on the step with snow in the shoulders of his coat and two soldiers behind him, their breath rising like small fires of their own. He was younger than Rafael had first taken him for; up close, in the lamplight spilling out past Rafael's body, you could see the youth still living in his face, fighting the uniform for possession of it.

"Ostrov." The colonel's voice was pleasant. It had been pleasant every time, and Rafael had learned that the pleasantness was not a mask so much as a habit — the manner of a man who had discovered early that courtesy frightened people more than shouting did. "You're awake."

"I'm always awake at this hour," Rafael said. "Fruit doesn't keep itself."

"No," Vasen said. "I imagine it doesn't." He turned his head slightly, and one of the soldiers stepped forward, past Rafael, into the hall, uninvited, unhurried. The soldier carried a lantern of his own and lifted it as he walked, and the light moved across the crates, the presses, the stairs going up to Ulyana's dark room, and Rafael felt the whole scene with terrible clarity, the way one feels a tooth before the dentist touches it.

"We found something interesting today," Vasen said. "Down by the mill race. A wire. Buried under the ice shelf where the water runs shallow — someone ran it poorly, I'll grant, but someone ran it. It comes ashore, do you know where? Within four hundred meters of your property line."

"Wire," Rafael said. "There's wire everywhere in this town. Half the fences are strung with telegraph scrap since the army pulled out."

"Mm." Vasen smiled. He took off one glove, then the other, finger by finger, folding them together with the deliberation of a man preparing to handle something delicate. "May I come in, Ostrov? It's cold, and what I have to say is better said sitting down. You have cider, I think. Everyone says you have cider."

*Everyone says.* Rafael understood then how long the net had been drawing closed — weeks, perhaps months, longer than he had allowed himself to believe. Every neighbor who had mentioned the cider, every friendly word traded across the fence, had been an entry in some clerk's neat hand. He thought of Marta Kovic, arrested nine days ago, and wondered if her testimony had been extracted or invented or simply guessed by men who were very good at guessing. It didn't matter which. All roads led here, tonight, to this doorstep, and the only variable left in the world was whether Ulyana Almeida was upstairs asleep or already gone.

She was gone. He had put her across the river path at ten o'clock, wrapped in the grey coat she'd worn since the capital, carrying the confirmation slip sewn into her boot heel and nothing else worth losing. She had wanted to argue — she had argued, briefly, beautifully, with that ferocity he had come to love the way one loves weather, knowing it cannot be argued with — and then she had stopped arguing, because they both knew the arithmetic. One could cross the ice. Two could not. A man carrying a lantern draws every eye on the far shore; a woman alone in the dark might pass for a shadow, if the lantern stayed behind.

The lantern would stay behind. Everything would stay behind.

"You may come in," Rafael said now, stepping aside. "But I'll warn you the cider's gone sharp. Winter does that."

"I know," said Colonel Vasen. "Winter does a great many things."

* * *

They sat in the little office behind the press house where the ledgers lived, and the soldier — there was only the one inside; the other had circled back, Rafael noted, to stand at the corner of the building nearest the river path, which meant they had thought about escape routes too, which meant they had thought about everything — the soldier searched while Vasen talked. He talked about apples. About the orchard's reputation in the district, about how his mother had once bought Vetrov preserves in another life, in another country that no longer existed. He talked the way ice talks when it begins to move: pleasantly, enormously, promising ruin.

Rafael answered in kind. He poured the sharp cider. He spoke of grafts and rootstock and the late frost of 1911 that had nearly ended him. Two men performing normalcy at each other across a desk while a third man went through their lives drawer by drawer.

It was the soldier who ended it. He came back into the office holding the false-bottomed crate lid, turning it over in his hands, and then he reached into the cavity and drew out the spare key cylinder — the reserve transmitter's tuning key, which Rafael had hidden there three nights ago in the exhausted stupidity of a man running out of places — and held it up without a word.

Vasen looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at Rafael, and the pleasantness drained out of his face all at once, like water from a cracked jar, and what remained underneath was younger and harder and much more dangerous.

"Well," he said. "So it's true."

"What's true," Rafael said, "depends entirely on who's asking, and I find these days that everyone asking works for someone I didn't vote for."

"Don't." Vasen set the key down on the desk between them, gently, almost tenderly. "Don't perform for me. I've read your file, Ostrov. The old file, the one they kept in the ministry cellar. Cipher section, third bureau, eleven years. And then 1916, when the list came out — the list of names — and suddenly you're a fruit merchant in a river town with a pardon stamped by men who are all dead now." He leaned forward. "You know what strikes me? Not that you informed. Half the bureau informed; it was practically a pension plan. What strikes me is that you *stayed*. Everyone else with your history ran — south, west, over the water. You stayed in the republic you helped sell, and you planted trees."

"They grow well here," Rafael said. "The river soil."

"Why?"

And there it was — the question Rafael had been answering for himself, in one form or another, for three years among his frozen rows, and had never once managed to answer aloud. He looked at the colonel's young face and understood, with a strange and sudden pity, that the man genuinely wanted to know.

"Because running is a debt too," Rafael said. "You carry it. It compounds. I've watched men who ran — they arrive somewhere safe and they spend the rest of their lives flinching at mail. Here, at least, I knew exactly what I owed and to whom. There's a kind of peace in it. You wouldn't understand."

"Try me."

"No," Rafael said. "I don't think I will."

Vasen sat back. Outside, the wind had risen off the river, and somewhere in the dark the ice groaned — that long, deep complaint the crossing made at night, the sound Ulyana had once described as the river clearing its throat before it spoke. Rafael heard it and felt it in his chest like a second heartbeat. She would be at the far bank by now. Or near it. Or on it. Every minute he kept this man talking was a minute of dark water between her and the dogs.

"The woman," Vasen said. "Your packer. The one who arrived in November and never appears in any register, whose hands are wrong for fruit work, who has ink stains on her right middle finger that no apple-sorter earns. My corporal saw a figure on the river path two hours ago. Small. Hooded. Heading east."

"Then you should send riders," Rafael said. "The ice won't hold a horse, but I hear the new government has good boots."

Something flickered in the colonel's face — annoyance, or its cousin, respect. He rose, buttoned his coat, and stood looking down at Rafael with the expression of a man closing a ledger of his own.

"I'm going to say one thing plainly, Ostrov, because whatever else you are, you were the bureau once, and the bureau deserves plain speech. We have the wire. We have the key. We have Marta Kovic's statement and my corporal's eyes and a file older than most of my soldiers. In the morning this place belongs to the Provisional Authority, and you belong to a cell in the fortress, and the woman belongs to whichever interrogation officer draws the assignment. That is what happens if we do this by the book." He paused. "I am offering you the chance to make it simpler. Names. Frequencies. Who she reports to, and what she sent. Give me those tonight and I'll see the record reads the way records can be made to read. You've done it before. You know the craft."

Rafael looked up at him. And what he felt, rising through the fear like warmth through frozen ground, was not defiance and not despair but something almost like gratitude — because the moment had finally arrived, the moment he had seen coming since the day he signed his own name on another man's death warrant and walked out of the ministry cellar into the sunlight with his life in his hands like stolen fruit. He had known even then that the bill would come due again. Debts like that don't discharge. They transfer. They wait. And he had spent three years planting trees above the debt, tending it, pruning it, letting it ripen, and now here it was at last, heavy on the branch, ready for the hand.

He had only ever hoped it would fall on him alone. He had hoped that right up until ten o'clock tonight, standing on the river path watching a hooded figure go east into the dark. Now he hoped for one thing more, and it was this: that the fire, when it came, would burn wide enough and bright enough and fast enough to blind everyone who might otherwise look east.

"There is one thing I'll give you freely," Rafael said. "Since we're being plain."

Vasen waited.

"The cider really is too sharp this year. I'd have it looked at." He smiled, and it cost him nothing, because smiling had become the easiest thing he did. "Everything else, Colonel, is between me and the morning."

Vasen studied him for a long moment. Whatever he saw — and he was not a stupid man, only a young one wearing an old government's habits — he evidently decided it did not matter. He had the wire, the key, the file. He had time. Time was the one thing Rafael could not manufacture, and they both knew it.

"Burn a lamp for me, Ostrov," the colonel said at the door. "I'll want you awake when I come back with my men. An hour. Perhaps two. Think about which future you'd rather write down."

The door shut. The soldiers' boots crunched away toward the barracks road, all but the one at the corner of the building, whose silhouette stayed fixed against the snowglow like a nail driven through the night.

An hour. Perhaps two.

Rafael stood alone in the office among the ledgers, and began, calmly, to count what had to die.

* * *

The lamp oil was the easy part. There were forty liters of it in the storeroom — winter stock, laid in against short days — and kerosene forgives a man his hesitations. He worked without hurry because hurry was a luxury the watchman at the corner made expensive, and without haste because haste would have dishonored the work. This was, after all, the last thing the workshop would ever ask of him, and he intended to do it the way he had done everything here: thoroughly, and with his hands.

He started in the archive room, where the real cargo slept. Twenty-two years of ledgers, every one of them double-lived — columns of weights and measures on the surface, cipher practice beneath, and beneath *that*, in the locked drawer of the iron cabinet, the true archive: the bureau's residual files, the ones he had been meant to destroy in 1916 and had instead smuggled home in a crate of quinces because some cowardly archivist in his soul could not bear the burning. Names. Frequencies. The grammar of a dead state's secrets. For three years they had sat under the floor like a tumor he refused to have cut out, and he had told himself he kept them as insurance, as leverage, as history — and all of it lies, all of it syrup-sweet rot, because the truth was that he kept them the way guilty men keep anything: as proof that the past was real, and therefore that his guilt was real, and therefore that he still existed somewhere outside the orchard's beautiful forgetting.

He poured oil over all of it now. Over the ledgers and the loose pages and the drawer of names, over the columnar figures that had taught Ulyana's fingers their old dance again, over the page — her page, the last one, the one she had copied out in her quick slanted hand — and he stood in the reeking dark of the archive room for a moment with the lamp raised, feeling the strangeness of the act settle over him.

He had spent twenty-two years keeping things. Records, trees, promises, debts. Keeping was his nature, his craft, his disease. And now the whole art of his life came down to destroying well — to burning cleanly enough, completely enough, that nothing he had ever touched could be used to touch anyone else. Somewhere across the black river a woman was walking on ice with his confession sewn against her skin, and every sheet that burned in this room was a sheet that would never be read against her, or against Marta Kovic, or against the boy who swept the mill, or against any of the small lives that had sheltered theirs without knowing it.

He struck the match on the second try. His hands were steady. He noticed that they were steady and was quietly surprised, and then not surprised at all.

The archive caught with a soft rushing sound, almost gentle, the way snow slides off a bough. He closed the iron door on it — let it eat its meal in private — and moved on through the building with the oil can, laying his trail backward through his own life. The press house last, because the press house held the transmitter and the transmitter had to die hardest; the packing hall before it; the stairs; the office where two men had drunk bad cider and spoken plainly about nothing. Oil on the floors, oil in the crates, oil over the ledgers on the open shelves, the honest ledgers and the dishonest alike — it seemed important, suddenly, that none of it survive, not even the innocent arithmetic. Let the fire take the whole doubled life at once. Let there be nothing left for the colonel to read except heat.

Outside, the cold hit him like clean water. The sky was moving apart in the east; there would be moonless dark for another few hours yet, but the stars over the far shore had that hard winter brilliance that makes distance feel like a lie. He looked at the river and could see nothing of her, of course. She had an hour's start and the sense God gives a courier, and the crossing would take her out along the ice shelf where the willows broke the wind, and then onto the white spine of the river itself, and the ice would hold or it would not, and he would never know which, and he had made his peace with never knowing, mostly, most days.

The groaning came again, longer this time, a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat, and he thought: *early*. The thaw was coming early this year. The river had begun to speak a full month ahead of its habit, and everyone in Vetrov had remarked upon it, and no one had understood what it meant — that the season of hunters on solid ground was ending, and the season of hunters in boats beginning, and that the window between them was narrowing to the width of a night.

Tonight was that width. Tonight was the whole window, and she was in it, and he was the lamp left burning on this shore to fill every eye in town.

From the corner of the building, the watchman's silhouette had shifted. Rafael heard him call out — a young voice, uncertain — *"Ostrov? Is that you?"* — and Rafael turned, and raised the oil can slightly in salute, and said, in the voice he had used for twenty-two years on customers and inspectors and colonels:

"Checking my stores. Go back to your post, son. Cold night to be standing around."

The boy hesitated. Rafael could feel the hesitation from thirty feet — the whole fragile machinery of authority and obedience grinding against the simple fact of a familiar neighbor in a familiar yard — and then, from inside the press house, the fire found the transmitter's wiring insulation and made a sound like a whipcrack, and the boy forgot about neighbors forever and began to shout for the barracks.

Rafael walked, unhurried, into the orchard.

* * *

He went up the row he always went up, the long central alley between the Antonovkas, the trees he had planted the first spring after his pardon, whips no thicker than his thumb, planted in ground he did not yet trust himself to deserve. They were old soldiers now, those trees, broad-crowned and scarred with every pruning knife year of his penance, and he walked among them in the growing orange light and put his hand against the nearest trunk the way one touches the shoulder of a friend one is about to leave at a border.

Behind him, the workshop went up properly.

It did not explode; it *arrived*. Heat rolled out across the snow and the flames stood up off the roof like a congregation rising, and the sound was enormous and intimate at once — the roar of the burning and underneath it, distinct, the small voices of things he knew dying: glass going in the office windows, the press frame groaning as its timbers let go, the iron cabinet door ringing like a bell as the archive inside it reached the temperature where paper becomes light. Sparks went up in thousands. The smoke bent eastward on the river wind, a black banner unfurling over the town, over the barracks road where boots were already hammering, over the ice shelf, over the river itself — and Rafael watched it go with a craftsman's eye and a sinner's heart, and judged it good. The smoke would hang over the east bank till dawn. No lantern, no figure, no small hooded shape would be visible through it or suspected beneath it. He had built the blindfold himself, board by board and tree by tree, over twenty-two years, and tonight it fit the world's face perfectly.

Men were coming. He could hear them — the bell at the barracks, shouts, the crackle of the pump wagon's wheels on frozen ruts — and he knew what they would find when they arrived: a burning cipher house, and in the yard beside it, seated on his own chopping block with his hands open on his knees, the owner, waiting to be taken. He had considered running. For perhaps a full minute, standing in the oil-reeking dark, he had honestly considered it — the river path, the willows, the ice, the far shore, the road south where a grey coat was moving through the dark with his confession against her ankle. He had weighed it the way he weighed everything now, in hours and meters and the price of bread.

But a man who runs leaves questions behind him, and questions travel faster than men. If Rafael Ostrov vanished the same night his workshop burned, the colonel would not chase smoke — he would chase the shape of the absence, and the shape of the absence was a woman on the ice. Whereas if Rafael Ostrov sat in his yard and let himself be taken, then the story wrote itself, clean and closed: a former regime cipher-man, exposed, destroying his archives, taken in the act. Full stop. The period at the end of the sentence that kept every other sentence from being written.

It was, he reflected, the last cipher he would ever compose: himself. Plaintext, at last, after a lifetime of columns and keys. There was a relief in it that bordered on joy.

The firelight reached the orchard now, orange on the black trunks, orange on the snow, and the heat came off the burning building in waves that made the winter air feel, for the first time all year, warm. He looked down the rows — his rows, forty of them, sleeping under their wool of frost — and he thought about what would happen to them. Someone would tend them, probably. Fruit was fruit; the town would not let the trees go wild. Some neighbor would prune them badly and harvest them generously and the cider would be sharp for a few years and then fine again, and in ten summers the orchard would not remember him at all, and this seemed to him not a tragedy but the entire point. Trees outlast their planters. That is why one plants them. That is the only honest answer to the question the colonel had asked and he had not given: *Why stay?* Because staying is how you leave something behind that isn't a debt.

The soldiers came up the lane at a run, halberds and lanterns and breath like steam engines, and the colonel behind them with his coat unbuttoned and his young face lit red by the ruin he had come to catalog. They stopped when they saw him sitting there. For a moment nobody moved at all — six armed men and one unarmed one in a burning garden, and the only sound the fire eating the roof beam by beam, patient as bookkeeping.

"Ostrov." The colonel's voice had lost its pleasantness entirely. He looked at the flames, then at Rafael's empty hands, then at the orchard, doing sums. "Where is she."

"Gone," Rafael said. It felt extraordinary to say it — the first true word he had spoken aloud in years. "Gone before you knocked. You're a day late and a river short, Colonel, and everything you needed is in that fire, and everything you'll get from me is ash with opinions."

They took his arms and brought him to his feet, more roughly than necessary, less roughly than he'd expected. The chains were cold through his sleeves. As they turned him toward the lane he looked back once — just once — at the workshop, fully surrendered to the fire now, its windows pouring light like a festival, and beyond it the dark rows of trees standing witness, and beyond *them* the river, invisible, enormous, already carrying its one small passenger eastward under the smoke.

"March," someone said, and Rafael Ostrov, who had sold men with ink and bought his life with names and buried his guilt under forty rows of apple trees, straightened his back between his captors and walked, and found — this was the thing he would carry with him into whatever cell they gave him, the thing no interrogation would burn out of him — that his heart was quiet. Quiet as a winter orchard. Quiet as ground that has finally, at last, paid what it owes.

Above the river, unseen by anyone still on this shore, the smoke drifted east, and under it, far out on the black-and-white water, the ice groaned once more — long, deep, and moving — as if the whole frozen river had turned over in its sleep and begun, at last, to let someone go.
