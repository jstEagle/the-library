## 20. Crossing

The fire was still visible from the riverbank, a bruised orange glow above the rooftops of Vetrov, and Ulyana made herself stop looking at it.

She stood at the edge of the willows where the towpath ended, and behind her the town she had entered as a stranger and was leaving as something else — something with no name yet, because names were for people who survived to be called them — burned in sections. She could smell it even here: smoke and scorched apple wood, the sweet char of a press house full of paper. Somewhere in that light Rafael Ostrov was either walking away or he wasn't, and she had agreed — had *promised*, with a grip on his wrist hard enough to leave marks — not to turn back.

The river lay before her like a road that had been left out all winter for someone to ruin.

It should have been solid by now. In a normal year the Vetrovka froze in November into a slab you could drive a cart across, and the old ferryman's grandson had told her so, laughing, back when laughter had seemed like a thing the world still permitted. But this year the thaw had come early and then retreated, and what remained was a month of contradictions: ice thick at the banks, thin in the channels, thick again over the shallows, thin where the current remembered itself. The river groaned constantly now — long, tectonic sighs that traveled through her boot soles and up into her teeth, as if the whole landscape were turning over in a sleep it would soon wake from.

She checked her gear one last time, by feel, in the dark. The dispatches themselves no longer rode in her coat; they lived now in an oilcloth packet strapped flat against her chest beneath two shirts, tied with the same knot Rafael had used on his crates, the knot that swelled rather than slipped when wet. The confirmation slips — the transmission receipts, the only proof any of it had ever been sent — were sewn into her right boot, inside the heel counter, wrapped in waxed paper. Three slips, each bearing the acknowledgment codes of three receiving stations, each worth more than her life and priced accordingly.

Her coat she would lose. She already knew this the way one knows weather. The coat held nothing but its own history: the lining she'd unpicked in the capital, the ghost-seams where the dispatches had once slept, the collar worn soft by a hundred days of her own chin. It was the last object that belonged to the woman who had accepted the assignment, who believed that history happened elsewhere, to other people. She would have liked to keep it. That was precisely why she wouldn't risk it.

*Every step forward costs something irreplaceable.* She had thought that phrase was about objects.

The crossing point Rafael had chosen was two kilometers downstream of the bridge — past the pilings, where the channel braided around a gravel bar and the ice, he said, froze in plates rather than sheets, so that a failure took one plate and not the whole floor. He had drawn it for her on the back of a ledger page, in pencil, in the columnar figures she had learned to read, and then crossed the drawing out with a single line and handed it to her anyway. *If I'm wrong about the plates,* he'd said, *you'll be wrong quickly, and near the bank, which is the best place to be wrong.*

He had also said: *Don't look back at the fire. Fire pulls the eye and the eye pulls the feet.*

So she didn't look back. She stepped off the bank onto the ice.

* * *

The first kilometer was almost kind.

The snow on the surface had been scoured by wind into long grey ripples, and her boots — Rafael's boots, too big, stuffed with straw at the toe — found purchase where her own would have failed. The plates under her creaked and ticked but held, each step announced by a small percussion that she learned to hear as conversation rather than warning. *I'm here,* said the ice. *Still here. Still here.*

She moved the way he had taught her, without ever teaching her — or perhaps he had taught her, those evenings in the packing hall, in the language of apples: how weight distributes, how a load carried low is safer than a load carried high, how you listen through your feet. Low stance. Weight on the ball of the foot, never the heel. Arms out from the body like a tightrope walker's, though there was nothing to walk toward but the far shore and nothing below but the black suggestion of water between the white seams.

The glow of Vetrov shrank behind her. Ahead, the far bank was a wall of unlit forest, which frightened her more than the fire did. The fire was known. The forest was not.

Halfway out, at the gravel bar where the channel divided, she stopped to breathe, and in the pause the river spoke plainly for the first time.

It was not the ticking anymore. It was a deep, rolling groan that began somewhere upstream and passed beneath her like a train under a station, and the vibration came up through the plates and into her hips, and every hair on her arms lifted under four layers of wool. Then a sound like a rifle shot — one crack, then another, off to her left, a seam opening somewhere in the dark with a blue-white flash she felt rather than saw.

She went down to her knees without deciding to. Instinct pressed her low, spread her weight, put her palms flat against the cold. Her breath came out in fast white flags. *Listen through your feet.* She listened. Under the ringing after-silence, the river resumed its ordinary groaning, and the plate she knelt upon did not move.

*Get up,* she told herself, and her body, which had been trained by two governments' worth of handlers to obey instruction before fear, got up.

The second channel was wider and the ice over it was younger — darker, glassier, wind-scoured down to bare black surface in places so that she could see, absurdly, the reflection of stars sliding beneath her boots. Walking on the sky. She fixed her eyes on the far bank and refused the metaphor. Metaphors were a luxury of the safe. Out here there was only physics: her weight, the ice's patience, the water underneath running at a speed that meant any failure would not be a hole but a *departure*.

Twenty meters from the far edge of the channel, the river decided otherwise.

There was no crack this time, no announcement. One moment the plate was holding her, conversing its small litany of *still here*, and the next it tilted, gently, courteously, like a hand withdrawing from under a saucer — and the black water came up around her legs.

The cold did not hurt. That was the first lie of drowning, and she understood it instantly: the cold was a kindness, an anesthesia, a voice saying *stop struggling, sleep, we'll take it from here*. She felt her boots fill. She felt the current take her ankles and begin, with terrible patience, to pull her off the broken plate and under the intact one ahead.

Later she could never reconstruct the next ninety seconds in order. They existed in her memory as a single clenched fist of sensation: the oilcloth packet slamming against her sternum as she threw herself forward; her elbows landing on sound ice with a force that cracked something in the plate behind her rather than beneath her; the water releasing her boots with a reluctance she heard as a long suck and swallow; the straw packing squelching; her own voice making a sound she did not recognize as hers — not a scream, something smaller and more animal, a syllable with no meaning in any language.

She crawled. There was no dignity available and she did not seek it. She crawled on forearms and knees across twenty meters of black glass until her hands found snow, and then she crawled further, up onto the shelving ice of the shallows, and only then did the shaking start — the great democratic shaking that takes the body whether or not the mind has consented, jaw and spine and fingers, everything rattling like the crates in the packing hall.

She assessed herself the way Rafael had taught her, in the language of inventory. Boots: full of water, ruined for warmth but functional. Dispatch packet: present, dry — she could feel the oilcloth's waxy stiffness against her breastbone, undisturbed, the knot swollen tight exactly as designed. Confirmation slips: she pressed her thumb against her right heel and felt the small rigid rectangle through leather and wool. Present. Dry. Hers.

Coat.

She looked back.

The coat was gone. It had gone into the water with her and the current had taken it — she saw it, or imagined she saw it, a dark shape rolling under the starlit skin of the channel, sleeves rising and falling like a swimmer's arms, like someone waving. The woman who had accepted the assignment in the capital, who had let a handler sew secrets into her lining and had believed, sincerely, that history happened elsewhere — that woman was being carried downstream toward the bridge pilings at this very moment, and would fetch up somewhere in the shallows by morning, and some patrolman would find her, and read her seams, and find them empty, and never know what he had missed.

Ulyana laughed. It came out as steam and a cough, and then it came out properly, ragged and uncontrollable, grief wearing laughter's clothes because grief knew no other way to travel tonight. She laughed at the coat waving goodbye. She laughed because the alternative was the other thing, and the other thing would freeze her faster than the water had.

Then she stopped, because the fire was visible again from out here — smaller now, or farther, a red smear along the tree line behind Vetrov — and looking at it, she broke the promise anyway, one minute, thirty seconds, whatever the account allowed. She let herself believe, for exactly that long, that the figure standing silhouetted at the edge of his own burning orchard was real and not constructed, that he had walked out, that atonement could be priced in anything less than the whole coin. Then she turned her back on the light, because fire pulls the eye and the eye pulls the feet, and she had a bank to reach and a border beyond it and three names in her heel that had to survive the night.

*Get up.* Her body obeyed. It was, by now, the only government she trusted.

* * *

The last stretch to the far shore took forty minutes that felt like a season.

The shallows were safer and crueler: thinner ice over gravel, cracking at every step but never failing, so that she crossed the final hundred meters on a surface that shattered in small white flowers around her boots and re-froze behind her, leaving a trail of blooms no one would ever see. Her wet boots froze progressively solid. By the time her hands touched the roots of the first pine on the southern bank, she could no longer feel her toes, and the straw in the boot toes had frozen into a packed plug that made each step sound like knocking on a door.

She did not knock. There was no door. She pulled herself up the root-mass and into the trees and stood there a long time, forehead against bark, breathing, letting the forest hold her up because nothing else would.

Under the pines there was no wind. The river's groaning receded to a rumor. Somewhere overhead a branch shed its snow with a soft whump, and the silence afterward was so complete she could hear her own heartbeat in her ears and, faintly, absurdly, the memory of cider and lamp oil, the smell of the packing hall, as if her body had begun archiving already, filing the senses away in the cellar where she kept things she could not afford to carry and could not bear to burn.

Rafael had given her one last instruction, at the willows, gripping her shoulders with both hands — the closest he had ever come to touching her face. *When you get across, don't think about any of this yet. Thinking is for later. Later is a country too, and it has a border, and you can stand at that border as long as you need. But not on the ice, and not in the trees. Walk first. Think later.*

So she walked.

She walked south through the pines with her boots knocking, following the compass bearing she had memorized in the capital a lifetime ago, and the forest received her without comment. Twice she heard dogs, far off, and stopped among the trunks until the sound resolved into wind. Once she passed a boundary stone half-swallowed by moss, its imperial eagle worn to a blind ghost, and understood that she had crossed the border itself sometime in the last hour without ceremony, without a gate, without anyone to chain it for the season. All that weight — the dispatches, the slips, the dead man's confession riding folded against her heart, the three names she had struck from a list with her own hand and the one name she had added — all of it had passed out of one country and into another on the feet of a woman who couldn't feel her toes, and the border had not even noticed.

That was the joke, she supposed. That was the joke she would tell no one, ever.

Near dawn she found the forester's hut Rafael had marked on the crossed-out map — a low roofline of snow among the trees, a chimney breathing a thread of smoke. She stood at its edge and performed the signal: three knocks, a pause, two knocks. A shutter opened. An old woman's eyes, unreadable, took her measure in the grey light — the frozen boots, the missing coat, the posture of someone carrying weight that showed nowhere.

The door opened. Warmth came out like a blow.

Inside, the old woman asked no questions. She pointed to the stove, set down bread and a pot of something, and turned away with the practiced blindness of people who had spent their lives on this border and understood exactly which sights preserved them. Ulyana sat by the stove and began, methodically, to work her boots off — peeling frozen leather from frozen flesh, unwinding the waxed paper from her right heel, checking the three slips by stove-light until she had confirmed each code, each station, each small typed acknowledgment that the messages had been *received*.

Received. Not read. Not acted upon. Received.

She dried the slips, resewed them into the boot with thread from the old woman's basket, and sat watching the fire while the feeling she had been refusing since the willows finally arrived and sat down beside her like a third traveler. It had no name she wanted to give it. It was grief and survival fused into a single substance, the way sap becomes syrup, the way fruit in syrup keeps its shape long after the fruit has gone — sweet and rotting at once, and kept, and kept, and kept.

*Walk first,* she reminded herself. *Think later. Later is a country too.*

But the old woman, clearing the pot away, paused at the window and looked south — toward the river, toward the smear of smoke still rising above the distant tree line — and then looked back at Ulyana with an expression that was not quite pity and not quite question.

"The bridge," the old woman said. "They're saying the garrison burned the orchard quarter last night. Hunting couriers." A beat. "They're saying nobody came out."

Ulyana met her eyes across the warm room, across the border, across everything.

"Then they weren't looking," she said, "in the right direction," — and outside, far off, the river groaned again, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep, and she understood that what she carried now would have to be delivered before anyone woke.
