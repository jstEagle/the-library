## 1. The Last Crossing

The river had a sound that morning, and everyone in Verensk heard it.

Not the usual sound — the slap of hulls against pilings, the cries of fishwives, the creak of the great chain ferry hauling itself across the gray water. No. It was the absence beneath all those sounds, the way a held breath has its own weight. The city was listening to itself. And under the listening, somewhere far off toward the granary quarter, a drum kept time with no march attached to it, just beating, beating, as though someone had forgotten to stop.

Bram Thackeray heard it through the open window of the print shop and did not let her hands falter. Her graver moved along the face of a piece of type with the small, sure bite of a mouse gnawing through a wall. Lead shavings curled away like pale eyelashes. She blew them off, squinted at the letter in the lamplight — it was not yet dawn enough for daylight — and set it into the composing stick beside its brothers.

*Order*, her father always said. *The order is everything. A page is a sentence is a word is a letter, and every one of them knows its place. Lose the order and you have printed nothing but noise.*

She had been cutting type since she was nine years old, standing on a stool so her chin cleared the workbench. Five years now since she had come down from that stool as Bram and never gone back up again. In this quarter, in this shop, she was a thin young man with ink-stained cuffs and a voice she kept low on purpose, and the lie fit her like an old glove fits a hand — worn smooth, warm inside, hiding nothing because it hid everything.

Outside, the last carts were crossing the bridge.

She could hear them even over the drums: the iron complaint of wheels on the bridge plates, one after another after another, a procession that had begun before midnight and showed no sign of ending. All day yesterday the rumor had run the length of the river road like fire along a fuse — *the bridge closes tomorrow, the regiment is coming, get your papers in order, get your people across.* Some had gone. Most had stayed, the way people stay, telling themselves that whatever came across a bridge could also be waited out behind a door.

Bram did not believe in waiting things out. She believed in lead, in paper, in the order of things. But belief, that morning, sat in her chest like swallowed river stone.

* * *

Her father came down the stairs at half past six, which was wrong. Aldous Thackeray was a man who rose with the light like a rooster and had done for forty years; to hear his boots on the stair while the sky was still the color of dirty wool meant something in the house's clockwork had slipped a tooth.

He looked older than he had at supper. Not ill — emptied. As if during the night something had been poured out of him.

"You're up early," Bram said, and made it sound ordinary.

"Couldn't sleep." He went to the stove, knelt, opened the little iron door. "Cold morning."

It was not a cold morning. It was the mildest week they'd had since thaw, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so. He fed letters into the stove — actual letters, folded papers tied with string, a bundle of them, and then another bundle from somewhere Bram hadn't seen him fetch. The paper caught with a soft yellow bloom of flame. She watched the corner of one envelope curl and blacken and saw, before it went, a wax seal, deep red, broken.

"Father."

"Hm?"

"That's correspondence."

"That," he said mildly, feeding in another handful, "was correspondence." The flames licked higher. He closed the stove door and sat back on his heels, and for a moment he looked at the iron door the way other men looked at graves.

Bram set down her graver very carefully, the way one sets down a thing when one's hands are not entirely one's own. "Who wrote to you?"

"People who trusted me." He stood, brushed ash from his knees. His eyes, when they found hers, were steady and tired and full of something he had decided, long ago or perhaps only hours ago, not to tell her. "People whose trust I intend to keep. That's all you need carry today, Bram. Carry the type. Mind the shop. Be dull. Dullness is armor."

"I've been dull for five years."

Something crossed his face then — a flicker, quickly banked, like the reflection of the stove fire in a dark window. "I know you have," he said quietly. "Better than you think."

And she wanted — God, how she wanted — to say *what do you mean, better than I think, what do you see when you look at me, what have you known all this time* — but the bell above the shop door chimed, and the morning came in wearing gray uniforms, and there was no more time in the world for questions.

* * *

They were polite. That was the worst of it. Four soldiers, rifles slung, mud to the knee, and a fifth man in a better coat with a leather satchel and the flat, patient manner of a clerk. They announced themselves as the Provisional Authority for Public Order, Quarter Three, and asked to inspect the premises for seditious material, and they said *please*, and they smiled, and their boots left wet prints on Bram's clean floor like accusations.

The clerk — his name was Vossel, he told them, historian by appointment, here to assist with the *correction of the public record* — ran a gloved finger along the cases of sorted type and pronounced the establishment exemplary. He admired the press. He complimented the sharpness of the father's matrices. He asked, idly, whether the shop had lately produced any pamphlets, broadsides, declarations, petitions.

"Wedding announcements," Aldous Thackeray said. "Funeral bills. An invoice for a flour merchant. The usual honest dust of trade."

"And the granary?" Vossel asked, still smiling, still touching nothing. "There are stories going about regarding the granary. Unfortunate exaggerations. When we publish the official account next week — and we shall, in every quarter, in every tongue spoken in this city — we would hope respectable printers will assist us in laying the exaggerations to rest."

"The dead don't exaggerate," Bram said, before she could stop herself.

The room went very quiet. The four soldiers shifted like weather. Vossel turned to look at her fully for the first time, and his gaze moved over her with the unhurried thoroughness of a man reading a difficult manuscript — the narrow shoulders, the ink-stained fingers, the jaw held just slightly wrong, and Bram felt the glove of her lie pull tight against every seam at once.

"A poet in the family," Vossel said at last, pleasantly. "How fortunate. Poets make excellent correctors. They understand how much depends on which words are chosen." He tipped his hat to her father. "We'll be closing the bridge at sundown. After that, gentlemen, the quarter keeps its own counsel — and we keep ours. Good morning."

They went out. The wet prints dried slowly, gray shadows of gray men.

Aldous Thackeray did not speak until their footsteps had faded entirely, and then what he said was: "Sundown."

"Yes."

"Yes," he repeated, softly, as though confirming a sum. He looked at the press, at the cases, at the stove where the letters' ashes now lay cold among the coals. Then he looked at Bram, and whatever he had been carrying since dawn seemed suddenly too heavy to keep carrying alone, and too dangerous to share whole.

"Bram," he said. "Go to the cellar. Bring up the case with the brass lock. The small one. Do not open it."

* * *

The case was cold from the cellar and heavier than its size, the way secrets are. Her father set it on the workbench between them, in the lamplight, among the innocent trays of e's and t's and the long silence of the em-quads, and he put both his hands flat upon its lid as though taking an oath upon it.

"What's in it?" Bram asked.

"An account," he said. "Set, proofed, true. Every name. Every hour of that night at the granary. Who gave the order. Who carried it out. Who stood in the doorway and let the grain burn rather than feed people." His voice did not rise; it went quieter, which frightened her more. "One proof short of a full run. That proof was to be pulled tonight."

"Then pull it tonight. I'll help—"

"No." The word came down like a type-case dropped. He gripped her shoulders, and his hands were trembling, and that frightened her more than anything the soldiers had done. "Listen to me. If they take me — *when* they take me, because Vossel will be back, and he already knows, Bram, he read this shop the way I taught you to read a galley — they must find nothing but wedding announcements. Nothing but dullness. You will not open this case. You will not finish the run. You will give them a son who knows nothing, wants nothing, *is* nothing—"

"I am not nothing."

His grip tightened. For one naked moment her father's eyes were not her father's eyes but a man's, desperate, drowning, begging her to understand a thing he could not say aloud in a house that might already have ears in its walls.

"Remember the order," he whispered.

Then the bells of the cathedral began to ring — not the hour, not a service, but the alarm peal, ragged and urgent, and through the window they saw the first smoke rising beyond the rooftops toward the bridge, and the drums, closer now, changing rhythm, becoming a march.

Her father released her, snuffed the lamp, and went upstairs to dress as though for church.

Bram stood alone in the dark shop with the locked case against her ribs and understood, with terrible clarity, that every silence she had ever mastered — five years of them, layered like coats — had not been armor at all.

They were a cage. And the lock, she realized, turning the small brass key her father had pressed into her palm without a word, had just been placed in her own hand.
