## 2. Lead and Ash

The knocking came before dawn, three blows with the butt of a rifle, and Bram knew the sound the way she knew the ring of good type against a composing stick — by its weight, its certainty.

Her father was already dressed. He had slept in his clothes again, she realized now, all week; he had been waiting for this knock as a man waits for weather he has read in his bones. When she came down the stairs he was standing at the stove, feeding it the last of the letters — not type but correspondence, years of it, bundles tied with string that curled and blackened and gave themselves up to flame. The smell of burning ink filled the shop like a confession.

"Papa—"

"Mind the door, Bram." His voice was level. "Let them in when they break it down. It saves the wood."

They did break it down. Four soldiers in the gray-green of the Provisional Authority, and behind them an officer with a paper that trembled slightly in his gloved hand, though from cold or eagerness Bram could not tell. The officer read the warrant aloud: *sedition, dissemination of false accounts, conspiracy against the restored peace of Verensk.* The granary massacre had a new name already, then. The restored peace. She watched her father listen to his own death sentence delivered in bureaucratic cadence, and she kept her face still, because stillness was the first thing he had ever taught her — stillness of hand, stillness of countenance, the printer's discipline of showing nothing until the form was locked.

They took him between them like a man being carried out of floodwater. At the threshold he turned, and for one moment his eyes found hers over the soldiers' shoulders, and he did not say remember the order again. He didn't have to. The locked case sat under the floorboard beneath the proofing press, exactly where he'd told her, exactly where she had knelt last night while he burned his papers above, and the knowledge passed between them the way ink passes into dampened paper: completely, silently, permanently.

Then the door hung open on gray morning light, and the shop was hers, and the city outside belonged to men who shot people at granaries and called it restoration.

* * *

She waited an hour before lifting the board. Long enough for the patrols to pass twice, their boots ringing on the cobbles in the rhythm of ownership. Long enough for the fear to settle into her stomach like wet sand. Then she pried it up and drew out the case — iron-bound, heavier than any case of ordinary type, locked with a key she found sewn into the lining of her father's spare coat where a button should have been.

Inside lay not loose type but locked formes. Galley after galley, composed and corrected and ready for the press: a broadside, complete, titled in letters two inches tall —

**THE TRUTH OF THE GRANARY — SET DOWN BY THOSE WHO SAW**

Bram's hands shook as she lifted the first galley to the lamp. Her father's composition was flawless, of course; forty years at the case had given him a spacing so even it looked machine-made. But it was the content that made her breath go thin. Names. Forty-one names of the dead, set line upon line, each with age and trade — *millers' daughters, a carter, two boys who had gone only for flour* — and below them an account of the morning of the shooting, witnessed and sworn, the number of rounds fired, the order given, the officer's name. Everything the victors' historians would leave out. Everything they were at this very moment, somewhere across the river, setting into their own clean and lying type.

One proof short of a full run. That was what her father had meant, or half of it: there was paper enough downstairs, ink enough, a press that only needed hands. What there wasn't — what there had never been, in five years of her passing — was anyone who knew. Anyone she could ask without asking for everything else too.

She stood in the lamplight holding the galley of the dead and understood that the silences she had mastered had been training for this. All those years of keeping her voice low, her history folded away like a forme laid safely in the stone. She had learned to hide in plain sight. Now hiding was the work itself.

* * *

The historian came at midday, and he came politely, which was worse than rifles.

He arrived alone, in civilian wool despite the cold, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic air of a clergyman calling on the bereaved. He introduced himself as Master Ovrel, historiographer to the Provisional Authority, and he admired the shop sincerely — ran his fingers along the cases, tipped his hat to the press as if greeting a colleague.

"Your father's reputation reached even our offices," he said. "A craftsman. Precision in every character. Which is precisely why I hoped to find you reasonable, Miss Thackeray."

The name landed on her like a dropped chase. She had heard herself called Thackeray a thousand times — *the boy*, the shopfolk said, *Thackeray's lad* — never *Miss*. He saw her flinch, and something flickered behind his spectacles: not triumph, exactly. Recognition. He had come knowing more than he said.

"The record must be corrected," Ovrel went on, drifting toward the window, watching the empty street. "History is a compositional art, you understand. Someone must choose what stands and what is spaced out. The granary affair — regrettable. A crowd, a panic, a few shots. The official account will say eleven deaths. Eleven is a defensible number. Forty-one is an accusation." He turned back to her. "You are young. You have a trade. Cooperate — confirm that no dissident materials were printed here, surrender any suspicious type — and the Authority will forget this shop exists. There may even be a commission in it. Official work. Steady pay."

Bram looked at him for a long moment. She thought about how easy it would be. How the word yes would cost her nothing but everything.

"You've made an error, Master Ovrel," she said, and her voice came out lower and steadier than she expected. "You think the record belongs to whoever holds the press. But a press only holds what hands give it. And these hands—" she held them up, ink in the creases, calluses from five years of cutting and casting, "—these hands belong to the truth."

Something like sorrow crossed his face. "That is a very expensive sentiment."

"It's set in permanent type," she said. "I can't take it back."

He bowed — actually bowed, a small courteous bow that chilled her more than any threat — and left his card on the counter. "I will return in two days," he said. "I do hope to find only type here."

* * *

She went first to the apprentices, because they were closest and because fear shared is fear halved.

Petra was seventeen, all elbows and grease-stained cuffs, sleeping in the loft above the shop since her family's house by the tannery burned in the autumn riots. Joss was older, quiet, a former seminarian who had traded Latin for letterforms and never explained why. Bram laid the galley of the dead on the imposing stone between them and watched their faces as they read — Petra's jaw tightening, Joss's lips moving silently through the names as if in prayer.

"They'll hang us," Petra said at last. Not a refusal. An arithmetic.

"They'll erase them," Bram answered. "There's a difference between dying and never having mattered."

Joss touched the edge of the galley gently, the way he touched damaged type. "My brother was at the granary," he said. "We never found his name in any list." He looked up. "Tell me what you need."

What she needed, next, was Mara Venn — the widowed bookbinder two streets over, sixty years old, forearms like a stevedore, who had bound half the respectable books in Verensk and had buried a husband the Authority's predecessor government had shot at a dockworkers' strike twenty years past. Bram found her sewing signatures by candlelight and put the broadside in her hands without preamble. Mara read it standing, unmoving, for the length of ten pages. Then she set it down and began pulling canvas and paste from her shelves without a word.

"Mara—"

"My husband's name was on a list once," the old woman said, not turning around. "A true one. Nobody printed it. I swore if ever I could put lead against a lie, I'd spend my whole stock doing it." She glanced back, eyes bright and hard as type metal. "Paper, thread, glue, my cellar for drying sheets, and these hands. Now tell me your plan, girl, before I die of old age waiting."

By nightfall the four of them stood in the shuttered shop around the press like conspirators around an altar. Outside, boots rang on the cobbles; the patrol schedule was tighter tonight, everyone knew it, the quarter sealed like a fist. Bram inked the forme with her own hands, laid the first dampened sheet, and pulled the bar.

The press spoke — that great wooden groan and sigh, the heartbeat her father had loved — and the first true sheet of the massacre came off the cylinder, ink still shining, forty-one names rising black out of white.

"Print fast," Bram said. "We run till dawn."
