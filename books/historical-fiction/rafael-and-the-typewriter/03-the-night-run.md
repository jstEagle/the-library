## 3. The Night Run

The press began its breathing at midnight.

Bram had heard that sound her whole life — the great iron lungs of the Albion press inhaling and exhaling in the back room of Thackeray & Son — but tonight it sounded different. Tonight it sounded like a heartbeat that would not be quieted. She stood at the stone with ink on her wrists to the elbow, black as river mud, while Tomas fed sheets and Petre worked the lever, and between them the broadside came into the world one page at a time: *THE TRUTH OF THE GRANARY*, set in twelve-point Caslon, every letter cut or chosen by hands that shook only a little.

Two hundred copies. That was what they needed. The occupiers' historian had promised the official record would be sealed by week's end, and paper was the only weapon fast enough to outrun him.

"Again," Bram said, and the press exhaled.

Outside, the quarter lay closed. Soldiers at both ends of Binders' Lane, boots on cobblestones in a rhythm she'd learned to read through the floorboards. Long pause. Two steps. Pause. A man standing still was safer than a man walking; a man walking was safer than a man running. She had catalogued the patrol like type — sorted, measured, ready for composition.

The stove ticked as it cooled. Her father's chair sat empty behind it, his spectacles folded on the seat, and Bram could not look at them without her throat closing.

"Ink's thinning," Petre murmured.

"Then we make it stretch." She drew the brayer across the stone and thought of her father's voice: *Remember the order.* The locked case had held more than the broadside. It had held the order of operations, written in his cramped hand on a slip inside the lid — which pages first, how many proofs, where the distribution points lay, marked with the small symbols of a printer's code they'd used for family letters since she was nine years old. He had known. All those years of silence, he had been composing this the way she composed a line of type, one deliberate character at a time.

* * *

*They think I am too old to be afraid,* Mara wrote that night, by candlestub, in the ledger she kept of everything the soldiers burned. *I am seventy-one years old and I am afraid down to the marrow. But I have bound books for forty years, and I know this: a book is not finished when it is printed. It is finished when it is read.*

*So I will carry the thread and needle. If they come, I am only an old widow stitching covers in my shop next door. Old women are invisible; I have been invisible so long I could teach ghosts. Let them look past me. Let them always look past me.*

*I have watched the girl all night. She moves like someone who has spent five years practicing stillness, and there is a grief in that I recognize. But when she sets her hands on the press, she stops being afraid. I understand that too. The work is larger than the fear. That is the whole secret of surviving anything.*

* * *

By three o'clock they had one hundred and forty sheets, drying in ranks along the walls like a paper garrison, when the knock came.

Not the patrol rhythm. Three sharp blows, then silence, then three again.

Bram killed the lamp with her palm. In the sudden dark she heard Tomas swallow hard and Petre's breath go ragged, and beneath it all the press ticking as it cooled, ticking ticking, loud as a confession.

"Through the coal cellar," she whispered. "Mara's shop. Take the sheets — the dry ones, leave the rest."

"The press—"

"They won't take the press. They'll take us." She pressed the bundle into Petre's arms, still warm from the machine. "Go. Now. Both of you. I'll follow."

Boots on the stairs outside — too many boots, moving too fast for the rhythm she knew. Someone had reported the smell of ink, or the light, or simply the fact that Thackeray's windows had been dark in a suspicious, deliberate way.

Bram did not go to the cellar. She went to the door instead, because if they broke it down and found an empty room with two hundred seditious broadsides drying on the walls, there would be nothing left of any of them. She opened it herself, one lamp relit and held high, wearing her father's leather apron and her most bored expression.

"You're late," she said to the lieutenant on the step. "We sent word hours ago about the broken forme. Sedition work, you said you wanted examples. Well. Come see."

It was madness. It was the only play she had. For one suspended second the lieutenant stared at the boy in the doorway — narrow, ink-stained, insolent — and Bram watched suspicion move across his face like weather.

Then Mara's voice rang out from the adjoining shop, high and cracked and furious: "Lieutenant! Lieutenant, in here — the old woman's fallen, she's hurt herself bad, come quick—"

And because old women falling is a truth no soldier doubts, he went.

* * *

*He went to me. God forgive me for the theater of it.* Mara's hand was steadier than her words. *I had knocked the stool over and lain down among my paste pots like a felled tree, and when he bent to haul me up I saw past his shoulder, through the connecting door, the girl slipping into the cellar dark with her arms full of our night's work.*

*But there were two soldiers behind him, and one of them was not stupid. He looked at the overturned stool. He looked at me, sitting up too quickly, too nimbly for a woman of my years. And he looked toward the cellar door, which stood open six inches, breathing coal dust into the room.*

*"Check below," he said.*

*I did the arithmetic faster than I have ever done anything in my life. There was a soldering iron in the brazier beside me, hot since evening, meant for trimming brass corners off book boards.*

*I picked it up and fell against him with it. Not his throat. His arm. I am old, but I am not a murderer, and besides — a wounded soldier makes noise, and noise brings the whole garrison, and the whole garrison would find the girl in the coal cellar with two hundred copies of the massacre in her arms.*

*He screamed. I screamed louder, clutching my hip where I'd struck the floor, weeping, apologizing, a foolish blind old creature flailing in the dark. And in the uproar — I heard it even through my own wailing — the cellar grate clinked shut, and the lane beyond grew quiet.*

*They took the iron from me and asked questions. I told them my eyes are failing, which is nearly true, and that I smelled smoke, which is also true, though the smoke was only the brazier. They believed me. Or they pretended to. Either way they left, carrying their howling corporal, and I sat alone among my ruined paste pots and laughed until the laughing turned into something else entirely.*

*My arm will scar. Good. Let it be a ledger entry too.*

* * *

Bram came up out of the coal chute into the freezing dark of Binders' Lane with the bundle strapped beneath her coat, and the cold felt like mercy after the furnace-dark of the cellar.

Distribution was the part her father's coded note had planned most carefully, and she understood now why: printing was a crew's work, but delivery must be solitary. One person, unremarkable, moving through the closed quarter like a stitch through cloth. The baker's shutter — tuck ten sheets inside the flour sacks. The chapel door — under the loose stone by the step. The well, the wash-house, the seamstress's window with its broken latch. Every place the people of Verensk touched in their ordinary mornings, so that tomorrow the broadside would seem to have risen from the ground itself, like water, like spring.

She had delivered half the bundle when she turned onto Tanner's Row and stopped dead.

A soldier stood beneath the guttering lamp at the corner, and he was not looking at the street. He was looking at her.

She knew the shape of him before she knew the face — the set of the shoulders, the way he hooked his thumb in his belt — and then the lamplight found his features and five years collapsed like wet paper. Joren Vale. Joren, who had mended nets beside her on the docks when she was eleven and barefoot and still called by her true name; Joren, who had taught her to gut fish and skip stones and lie convincingly to harbor masters. Joren, who had gone into the army the winter her father first cut her hair short and handed her the shears himself, saying *be whoever you must be, little bird, just be safe.*

He knew her walk. He knew her laugh. He knew the way she stood right now, weight on her left foot, chin slightly down — the stance of a girl who had grown up beside him.

"Bram," he said quietly. Not *boy*. Not *you there*. Just the name, soft, in the voice of someone testing ice. "Bram Thackeray."

Her hand was inside her coat, on the remaining hundred sheets, on the whole truth of the granary. Down the row, a patrol's boots were coming — long pause, two steps — and somewhere behind her, Mara was binding a wound by candlelight, and her father was in a cell whose location she didn't know, and the historian's record would be sealed in three days.

Joren stepped out of the lamplight, close enough to touch, and reached toward her coat.

"Don't," he said. "Don't run. Just tell me one thing first."
