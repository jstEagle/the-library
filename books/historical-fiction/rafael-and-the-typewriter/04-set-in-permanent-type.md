## 4. Set in Permanent Type

The broadside went out like water goes out — not all at once, but finding every crack, every gutter, every low place in the city, until there was nowhere it had not seeped.

Bram heard it first as sound. On the third morning after the night run, standing at the shop's darkened window with a cup of cold tea going bitter in her hand, she heard a boy on the corner reading aloud. Not singing, not hawking bread or bootlaces — reading. The granary account in her father's type, set by her own hands, coming off the boy's tongue in the flat bright voice of someone who had learned his letters recently and loved them. *Eleven souls, of whom three were children.* He read the numbers twice because the crowd kept asking him to.

By noon, copies were nailed to the granary doors themselves. By evening, they were pasted over the victors' proclamations, sheet over sheet, so that the official account of the massacre — *an unavoidable response to armed insurrection* — wore its rebuttal like a second skin. Soldiers tore them down and new ones appeared by dawn, and Bram understood something she had only half known before: you could burn paper, but you could not burn the memory of having read it.

Mara's shoulder was bound and splinted, the bayonet wound stitched with thread from her own workbasket, and she sat in the back room directing operations from a chair like a general too wounded for the field but never for the war. "They'll come for us," she said, not unhappily. "Men who lose an argument always come for the press."

"Let them come," said Joss, the elder apprentice, ink-stained to the elbow and grinning like a man drunk on something better than drink.

"No," Bram said quietly. She was setting type again, small pieces this time, a single-column notice. "Let them *ask*. We want them asking. A raid burns paper. An inquiry puts it on the record — sworn, signed, witnessed." She slid the composing stick level. "My father taught me that. Ink is cheap. Attention is what costs."

The summons came on the fifth day, delivered not by soldiers but by a clerk in civilian gray who would not meet anyone's eyes. The Provisional Commission on Public Order would hear testimony regarding the events at the granary. The printers of Thackeray & Son were invited — the word *invited* doing labor no honest word should have to do — to appear and answer questions concerning the production of seditious materials.

"Seditious," Mara repeated, tasting it. "The truth is sedition now. Good. Let them say it in front of witnesses."

Her father was brought to the shop two days later under guard, thin, unshaven, his coat missing its buttons — confiscated, he explained, because buttons could be sharpened, which told Bram everything about how frightened the victors had become. They were given one hour, supervised, a soldier at the door studying the ceiling with tremendous diligence.

Her father crossed the shop floor slowly, touching the frames, the stone, the cases, as a blind man reads a face. Then he stopped at the locked case — still open on the bench where she had left it, its compartments emptied, its secret spent — and put his hand flat upon the wood.

"You found the order," he said.

"I remembered it." Her voice cracked on the second word. "Every night for five years I watched you lock it and I thought — I thought it was just another silence. Another thing we didn't say in this house."

"It was never a silence, Bram." He turned and looked at her fully, and for the first time since the arrest she saw that his eyes were clear, almost gentle. "It was a letter I was writing you. I only hadn't finished it yet. You finished it yourself. That's what I hoped — that if it ever mattered, you'd know enough to read me."

She wanted to tell him about the nights, the fear, Mara bleeding in the alley, the soldier on the bridge whose eyes had gone through her like an awl through damp paper. There was no time. The hour burned down around them like a short wick. So she took his hands instead — ink-creased, nail-bitten, trembling slightly — and held them the way she used to when she was small and the thunder shook the type cases.

"The inquiry," she said. "They'll ask who printed it."

"They will."

"And when they ask about me—"

"My daughter," he said, without hesitation, without softening, "will answer however she pleases. I have spent five years letting silences stand in for love. I'm done with it. Whatever name you give them, give it whole."

* * *

The commission met in the customs hall, a high cold room where grain had once been weighed and men had once been taxed, and now truth was to be measured out by the pound. Three commissioners sat behind a table scarred with old cargo stamps. Behind Bram stood Mara with her arm slung in linen, Joss and little Wren the younger apprentice, a dozen other printers from across Verensk who had arrived without being asked — the guild's old men, the widow Ostrava from the bindery row, boys with ink under their nails who had walked miles to stand in a room and be counted.

The middle commissioner read the charges in a bored voice: production of unauthorized publications, dissemination of false accounts, sedition against the Provisional Authority. Her father, questioned first, answered plainly and without apology: yes, he had set the broadside; yes, he had printed it; yes, every word was true, and he could prove it, and had already deposited proofs with three foreign consuls, because he was an old printer and old printers hedge their runs.

Then the questions turned toward the shop. Who else worked there. Who else knew. Who had carried the sheets through the closed quarter on the night of the patrol.

And into the pause that followed, the door opened, and a soldier came in.

Bram's heart stopped and started again, badly, like a press with a slipped gear. She knew him before she raised her eyes — she had known him on the bridge, had known him in the half-second before recognition completed itself, the way you know a chord is wrong before you can name the note. Corporal Dain Vesey. From the old years. From before. The one person alive who had seen her at fourteen, who had carried her books, who had called her by the name she had buried with her mother's sewing box at the bottom of a locked drawer.

He gave his statement to the commission in a voice like gravel poured slowly. He had been on patrol on the night in question. He had encountered an unknown figure distributing printed sheets near the tanners' walk. He had pursued. The figure had escaped in the fog.

"Did you see this person clearly?" asked the commissioner.

A silence. Long enough to hang a man. Bram felt Mara go rigid beside her, felt the whole room lean.

"I saw a printer," Vesey said at last. "That's all I saw. Whoever it was moved like someone carrying something worth more than their life. I'd know that walk anywhere. My mother was a printer's daughter." He looked then — only then — at Bram, and his eyes did what they had done on the bridge: passed over her, paused, and chose, deliberately, to see nothing. "No further identification possible."

He saluted the commission and left, and Bram sat very still and let her heartbeat come back down like a compositor easing a full stick into the chase.

Then the senior commissioner turned to her. "And you are? The record requires the names of all persons employed at the establishment."

The room waited. Her father waited. Somewhere outside, a boy was probably reading the numbers again — *eleven souls, of whom three were children* — and the river ran brown and patient under the closed bridge.

Five years of *boy*, of lowered eyes, of *Thackeray's lad*, of binding herself flat as a sheet in the press so the world could run smoothly over her. Five years of a name worn like a locked case.

She stood up.

"Bram Thackeray," she said, and her voice carried to the rafters where the grain dust still hung like weather. "Daughter of Edvard Thackeray. Cutter of type. Setter of the true account. I set the broadside myself, every line of it, and I would set it again tomorrow, and the day after, for as long as there is lead in this city and breath in me to breathe on the type to keep it from fogging." She laid her hands flat on the rail before her, palms up, ink still in the creases. "Write it down exactly as I've said it. You people claim to care about accurate records. Start with mine."

The scratching of the clerk's pen was the loudest thing in the room.

* * *

They released her father that afternoon — the commission, faced with consular proofs and a public that had already memorized the truth, preferring to lose gracefully rather than be dragged. The charges dissolved into a phrase about *extenuating circumstances* that no one believed and everyone repeated with a smile.

The shop reopened within the week. Bram took down the old sign and repainted it herself, standing on a ladder in the cold morning while the apprentices shouted directions none of them intended her to follow: THACKERAY & DAUGHTER, in letters tall enough to read from the far bank.

Mara claimed the back room permanently, her bandages replaced by a sling she refused to retire, ruling the bindery like a small fierce empire. Wren learned to cast type and sang while he worked, badly. Joss was teaching himself to draw, badly, for the illustrations the next broadsides would need — because there would be next ones; the quarter had tasted print like a starving thing tastes bread, and it was not going back to hunger quietly.

On the first evening, her father lit the stove and warmed the room the way he had when Bram was a child, and they all sat among the cases and the stone and the smell of oil and lead, six people who were not born family choosing it instead, the way you choose a font, deliberately, knowing its weight, and set it in permanent type.

"Tomorrow," her father said, tapping the empty composing stone, "we begin the full history. Everything the victors' historians got wrong. Everything they tried to burn. A proper book, bound and signed."

Bram looked around the lamplit shop — at Mara's raised cup, at Wren's smudged grin, at the window where the bridge stood open at last, lanterns crossing it freely in both directions — and reached for the first case.

"What shall we call it?" Wren asked.

Bram sorted a handful of type by touch alone, the letters clicking softly together like people settling into seats.

"*Set in Permanent Type,*" she said. "We start with the granary. And this time—" she lifted the first letter to the lamp, turning it so its mirrored face caught the light, "—this time, everyone's name goes in."
