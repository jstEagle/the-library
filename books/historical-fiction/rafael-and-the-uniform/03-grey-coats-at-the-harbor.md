## 3. Grey Coats at the Harbor

Piet had read the harbor for fifty years, and the harbor had never once lied to him.

Fog told him things. The color of the water told him things. The way gulls sat on the breakwater instead of circling the fish market told him there were no guts to be had, which meant the boats had come in empty, which meant the herring had moved north, which meant lean weeks ahead. He could stand at the top of the ferry ramp with his bad knee aching and tell you what the day would cost before the sun cleared the headland.

So when he came down the ramp that morning and saw three grey transports riding low in the outer roads, he knew the weather of it before he knew anything else.

Not weather like rain. Weather like the pressure drop before a storm that hasn't decided where to land.

He stopped. Set down his crate of rope. Counted.

Three transports. Troop ships — he'd carried soldiers in the last war and knew the silhouette, the high freeboard stuffed with men, the landing davits hung with boats too small for comfort. Not supply ships; supply ships sat deeper and uglier. These were troop ships, and they were not coming in yet. They were standing off, waiting for something.

Waiting for permission, Piet thought. Armies always waited for permission. That was what made them armies.

* * *

By noon the first launches came ashore, and the town learned what kind of army this was going to be, because it was not the kind that fired first.

They came in grey coats, forty of them in the first wave, and they did not loot, did not shout, did not kick in a single door. They formed up on the quay with the precision of men who had done this in eleven towns already, and an officer with a ledger under his arm walked up to the harbormaster's office and requisitioned it. Politely. There was even paper involved — an official form, stamped twice, handed over with both hands like a gift.

Piet watched from the ferry ramp, close enough to hear the officer's voice through the open window. Young. Flat. Patient in the way of a man reading terms aloud to someone who has already signed.

"The warehouses on the east quay will be inventoried by Friday. The customs shed is now a billeting office. All civilian traffic out of Vellsmark harbor requires a port authority permit, available at the customs shed, effective tomorrow."

The harbormaster, fat old Dekker, said something about existing contracts. The officer turned a page.

"Existing contracts are noted," he said. "They are also suspended."

That was all. No threat in it. The threat was the page turning.

Piet had seen occupiers before — he was old enough to have seen two flavors of them — and the shouting kind had always struck him as honest, in their way. You knew where you stood with a man who broke your window. It was these quiet ones that taught you new things about fear. They didn't take your town away from you. They filed it.

* * *

Through the afternoon the notices went up, and Piet read every one of them, because reading was the one weapon left to a man with a bad knee and a ferry nobody rode anymore.

NOTICE THE FIRST: All inhabitants will present themselves for registration of residence. Heads of household to report within ten days. Penalties for false declaration.

NOTICE THE SECOND: Curfew, dusk to dawn.

NOTICE THE THIRD: The harbor is closed to civilian departures until further notice. Permits required.

He stood in front of the third one a long time. The ink was barely dry and already the words had the smell of permanence, that particular bureaucratic stink that outlasted empires. *Closed until further notice.* In his experience, further notice never came. Harbors didn't reopen. They just changed whose flag the cranes flew under.

Ten days. Every soul in the town to be written down in a book kept by men in grey coats.

Piet thought of Bess's table, and how many chairs went around it now, and how few of those chairs would want their names in any book.

He took up his crate and walked home fast, knee be damned.

* * *

Aunt Bess's boarding house stood on Kelp Lane, four streets up from the water, a tall narrow house that leaned slightly toward the sea as if listening for it. Piet had lived in the ground-floor back room for six years, ever since the ferry company folded and left him with a boat, a pension of nothing, and hands too ruined for nets but good enough for mending other people's gear.

The house smelled, as always, of bread and lamp oil and Ilse's sewing machine oil, three smells that should not have mixed and somehow did. He found Bess in the kitchen, kneading dough with the ferocity she brought to everything, her sleeves rolled, flour to the elbows.

"Troops in the harbor," he said.

"I know." Flour dust hung in the light. "Tomas came running half an hour ago, white as a candle. Three ships, he said."

"Troop ships. And the harbor's closed. Notices on the quay." He set the notice he'd torn down — carefully, whole, evidence — on the table. "Registration in ten days. Every name in the house goes in a book."

Bess's hands stopped moving.

It was a small thing, the stopping. Most people wouldn't have caught it. Piet had known her thirty years, since she was a girl selling fish off her father's cart, and he knew that her hands only stopped when the arithmetic underneath got loud.

"Every name," she said.

"Every name." He pulled out a chair and sat, because his knees had opinions and the news wasn't going to improve with him standing. "Bess. I counted heads this morning while I was counting ships. Eleven people sleep under this roof. How many of them exist?"

She resumed kneading, slower now. Pushing the dough away and pulling it back, away and back, like she was trying to wear out the question.

"That depends," she said, "on who's asking."

"Men with ledgers are asking. That's who's asking."

"And we'll answer them," she said, "the way we've answered everyone for six years. Carefully."

Piet looked at the notice on the table. *Penalties for false declaration.* He'd been ferryman long enough to know that penalties were the word governments used when they hadn't yet decided whether the penalty was a fine or a wall.

"Bess. This isn't a tax assessor. This isn't some clerk you can charm with coffee and a sad story about your late husband. This is an occupation administration. They'll cross-check. They talk to neighbors. They count chimneys."

"We've survived worse—"

"No," Piet said, and heard his own voice come out flat, the voice he used in weather. "We haven't. We've survived neglect. Neglect is easy. Nobody cared enough to look hard. This is different. This is attention." He tapped the notice. "And attention is the one storm you can't outrun in a leaky boat."

Bess wiped her hands on her apron, very slowly, and looked at him, and he saw her decide something — saw the exact moment the mother in her stepped aside and let the quartermaster step forward, the woman who had fed eleven people on money that officially belonged to six.

"You think we run," she said.

"I think we think about running. Tonight. While thinking's still legal."

* * *

The argument started at supper, because Bess called it at supper, which was where all the real business of the house happened anyway, conducted between the passing of potatoes so the children — Tomas, and Ilse, though Ilse was twenty-three and hated being counted as a child — wouldn't hear the worst of it.

Except nobody in that house was a child, not really, and everyone heard everything. That was the nature of the place. Strays heard everything. It was how they'd survived becoming strays.

"There are permits," Bess said, keeping her voice at the register she used for discussing the roof or the price of coal. "Departure permits, from the customs shed. We apply, we travel, we're a widow relocating her household. It's clean."

"It's a queue," Piet said. "Eleven people, one queue, and every clerk in that shed looking for reasons to say no."

"Then we don't all go at once—"

"Splitting us up," Ilse said quietly, and her needle paused above the mending she did at the table the way other people smoked. She never stopped sewing during arguments. It was her way of pointing out that she'd heard worse. "You mean splitting us up. Sending us off in twos and threes like parcels."

"I mean keeping you alive," Bess said, sharper than she meant, and Piet saw Ilse flinch and cover it by biting her thread.

"It worked for my sister," Tomas offered, into the silence. His stutter always worsened when he was frightened, and tonight it was bad. "G-g-going separately. In-in-in the war. People left in groups and got — got noticed. People who left alone mostly — mostly arrived."

"People who left alone mostly disappeared," Anselm said.

He sat at the end of the table where he always sat, near the door, which Piet had privately understood for a year now was not an accident. The deserter ate little, spoke less, and listened the way a man listens when he has trained himself to sleep through artillery but never through footsteps. When he spoke, the table went quiet, the way water goes still around a stone.

"You want to know what happens," Anselm said, "when a town gets registered? I've watched it happen twice. First registration, they write down names. Second visit, they check names against other lists. Third visit, the names that don't match get visited at night." He turned his cup a quarter-turn, precisely. "There's no fourth visit worth staying for."

"So we run," Bess said.

"On what?" Piet asked. "The harbor's closed. The road south has checkpoints already — Dekker saw the wire going up past the mill. And the fishing boats—" he shook his head. "Greycoats are walking the east quay with a list of every hull. They're inventorying *boats*, Bess. Boats."

"They can't inventory the sea," Ilse said.

No one answered that. It was the kind of thing that sounded like hope and was actually just a true statement about geography, and everyone at the table was smart enough to know the difference.

Tomas pushed his food around his plate. "Could we — could we not d-decide tonight? Could we watch? One day. See how they — how they b-behave."

"They behave well," Anselm said. "That's the trap. They'll fix the streetlamps. They'll pay fair prices at the market. They'll be polite right up until the night of the third visit." He looked down the table, and his eyes stopped on Bess. "I've seen the polite ones. The polite ones are worse. Cruel men you can predict. Administrators you can't."

* * *

The argument wore itself out around nine, the way household arguments do — not resolved, just exhausted, adjourned to be refought tomorrow with the same pieces. Bess decreed that no one would do anything foolish, that everyone would stay indoors after dusk, and that she herself would go look at the customs shed in the morning, because if there were permits to be had she wanted to be early in the line and ordinary in her manner.

Piet helped her bank the stove, and when the others had gone up — the creak of floorboards overhead, doors closing one by one, eleven people folding themselves into the house like a letter into an envelope — he stayed in the kitchen a moment longer.

"Bess," he said. "I've been reading that harbor since before you were born. Let me tell you what I read today."

She waited, arms folded, flour still in the creases of her knuckles.

"Those weren't garrison ships. Garrison comes in one transport and unloads and stays. Those three were the first wave of a rotation. There'll be more behind them. And troop rotations need a working port, which means the civilian berths go first, then the civilian traffic, then the civilians who are inconvenient." He rubbed his knee, which ached the way it did before weather he couldn't yet see. "The port doesn't close someday, Bess. The port closed today. Everything after this is just the lid screwing down."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then: "If it comes to leaving, we leave together."

"Eleven people don't leave together. Not through a checkpoint, not on a permit, not in one boat."

"Then we don't leave."

"That's not courage, that's arithmetic with the numbers hidden."

"And yours isn't?" she said, not unkindly. "Piet. Six years ago I took in a stammering boy because nobody else would sign for him. Then a girl running from a man with a ring of keys. Then a ferryman with no ferry. If I start doing sums on human beings, I stop being the thing this house is. And if this house stops being that thing—" she looked around the kitchen, at the worn table, the seven mismatched chairs, "—then there's nothing here worth saving anyway."

Piet nodded slowly, because he agreed with her and thought she was wrong, and had discovered over sixty years that both things could be true at once.

He climbed to his room, lay down on his narrow bed, and listened to the house settle. Through the wall he could hear Bess pacing her room, the particular slow tread of a woman counting. He knew what she was counting. He was doing the same sum himself against the ceiling in the dark.

Eleven people.

One harbor, closing.

Ten days on a notice nailed to a post, and somewhere upstairs in this house — he didn't know exactly where, he'd never asked and never wanted to know — an atlas full of coastlines drawn by a dead man, and whatever else a dead mapmaker keeps between his pages.

The gulls were quiet. Even the gulls knew.

Tomorrow the line at the customs shed would stretch down the quay, and Bess would stand in it with her widow's face and her honest eyes, and Piet would mend nets in plain view of the east quay and count grey coats, and the house on Kelp Lane would begin learning what ten days felt like when each one was a plank being pried loose.

But tonight, lying in the dark, Piet kept returning to one detail from the quay, the one he hadn't mentioned at supper because supper was frightened enough.

The officer with the ledger — the young, flat, patient one — had spent twenty minutes at the harbormaster's desk, and Piet had watched him through the window, and what the man had asked for, page after page, stamp after stamp, was not the tax rolls and not the militia lists and not the grain accounts.

He had asked for the old coastal charts.

Every survey, every soundings book, every chart of the approaches to Vellsmark that the harbormaster's office held — collected, stacked, tied with cord, and carried out under the officer's arm like something precious.

An army that fixes your streetlamps does not need your charts, Piet thought, staring at the ceiling.

An army that wants to own the sea asks for the maps first.
