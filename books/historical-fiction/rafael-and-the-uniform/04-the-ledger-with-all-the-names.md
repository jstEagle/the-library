## 4. The Ledger With All the Names

The notice went up on the harbor board at seven in the morning, and by eight Piet had already read it aloud to the whole street in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as Aunt Bess's kitchen window.

Noor heard it through the glass while she was grinding ink—she had learned to grind ink at dawn because dawn was when her hands were steadiest—and she did not stop grinding when she understood what Piet was saying. She kept the pestle moving in slow circles. That was the trick of fear. You gave your hands work and let your head do its arithmetic behind them.

*By order of Commandant R. Voss, Military Governor of Vellsmark and District: all heads of household shall present themselves to the requisition office within ten days to register the full names, ages, trades, and places of origin of every person residing under their roof. Failure to register constitutes obstruction of military administration. Obstruction will be dealt with accordingly.*

*A census*, she thought. Not soldiers kicking down doors. Not arrests in the night. Paper. They wanted paper.

She almost laughed at that—the army had come all this way with grey coats and gunboats and what they wanted first was a ledger. But the laugh died somewhere under her ribs, because Noor Crane knew better than anyone alive what a ledger could do. A ledger was a map of people. And a map of people, in the wrong hands, was a hunting chart.

The pestle kept turning. Outside, Piet's voice rolled on, reading the notice again for the latecomers, adding his own commentary like weather warnings stitched into a forecast.

* * *

Aunt Bess called a household meeting for that evening, which meant she cooked enough for a siege and let everyone argue over the food, because Bess had learned long ago that people say truer things with bread in their hands.

They gathered in the kitchen the way they always did: Ilse at the end of the bench nearest the door, which was where she always sat, three years in Vellsmark and still positioned for flight; Tomas folded into the corner with his bowl held close, stammering less now than he used to but still choosing silence when silence would serve; old Piet by the fire with his boots off, an honor he extended to no other room in town; Anselm last, coming in from the woodshed where he slept, where he had slept for fourteen months, officially because the attic was full and unofficially because a deserter learns to keep walls between himself and windows.

And Anselm was one of the reasons for this meeting. He and two others. Three people in this house who did not exist.

Bess stood at the head of the table with the notice flattened under her palm as if she could pin it there like a moth.

"You've all heard it," she said. "Ten days. Every name in every house in Vellsmark goes into the commandant's book." She looked around the table. "So we're going to talk about names."

Nobody touched their bread.

"Ilse," Bess said gently, "you came to us with nothing. No papers, no family name anyone in this province has ever heard, and a story about a seamstress's apprenticeship in Marlowe that I have never once believed."

"It's mostly true," Ilse said. Her chin came up, the way it always did before a lie or a truth, and even after three years Noor couldn't always tell which. "Mostly true is what I have."

"Anselm," Bess went on, "is not my cousin's son visiting from the hills. He is a deserter from the Fourth Regiment who walked out of a camp up the coast with a musket and a grudge and knocked on my door in the rain because his mother once sold my mother eggs." Anselm didn't move, but his knuckles whitened on the table edge. "And Tomas—"

"No," Tomas said quickly. "I'm—I'm fine. I'm nobody. Nobody writes down nobodies."

"You are somebody," Bess said, "which is precisely the problem."

Noor sat at the table's side and said nothing yet, because she had learned from Aldous that you let the room finish its argument before you offered the thing everyone actually needed. Her mind was elsewhere anyway. It was upstairs, in the attic, in the flat cedar box under the floorboards where the atlas slept. It was doing the arithmetic Piet had started that morning and Bess was finishing now.

Eleven people lived at 9 Lantern Row. Eleven ate at this table, slept under this roof, drew water from this well. The ledger Bess kept—for taxes, back when taxes were the worst thing a ledger could be—listed five. Herself. Bess. Tomas, as a nephew. Two lodgers whose names were real but whose rooms were rented to ghosts. Five on paper. Eleven in fact. Six names that existed nowhere except in the memory of the people sitting around this table.

Three of those six were dangerous. Ilse, who had run from a master who owned her debt and would call her theft instead of escape. Anselm, who had run from something worse, though he'd never said what, and deserters were shot, not fined. And Piet—old Piet, who wasn't hiding from anything except being counted, because twenty years ago he'd lost his ferry license in a dispute with a harbormaster and had simply refused ever since to appear in any record anywhere, a man so thoroughly unregistered that he had begun to suspect, half-jokingly, that if any official document acknowledged him he might evaporate.

"We register honestly," Bess said. "Me, Noor, Tomas, the lodgers. We say the rest left. Seasonal workers gone up the coast. It's autumn; people leave in autumn."

"And when they ask where to?" Noor said.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak yet, but the question had been waiting in her chest since morning and it came out level, which surprised her, because underneath the levelness was something cold and climbing.

"They won't ask where," Bess said. "They'll write *departed* and move to the next house."

"This commandant won't." Noor laid her palms flat on the table, mirroring Bess without meaning to. "Piet read me the notice twice. Listen to how it's written. *Full names, ages, trades, places of origin.* That's not a tax roll. Tax rolls want numbers. This wants lives. A man who orders a census like that is building something, and men who build things check their foundations. Someone will ask where the seasonal workers went. Someone will notice that Ilse's bed is warm and her thread is in the sewing basket. Someone—" she caught herself, softened, "—I'm sorry, Bess. I'm not saying don't try. I'm saying a partial lie is the most fragile kind. One loose thread and the whole cloth comes apart."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them the house settled, the way old houses do, creaking through its joints like a tired animal.

"So what's your answer?" Ilse asked. There was no accusation in it. That was the strange mercy of Ilse—she asked things plainly, as if the answer were a garment she intended to wear whatever it looked like. "You're the one who's good at paper. What do we put in the book?"

And there it was. The whole household looking at her, because that was the arrangement none of them had ever spoken aloud: Bess fed them and defended them and loved them loudly, but when the world demanded documents, they turned to the quiet woman in the attic who could make ink behave.

Noor looked down at the table. The grain of it, worn pale in front of each habitual seat. A map of the family, drawn by years of elbows.

"The honest answer," she said slowly, "is that I don't know yet. Ten days is time. Time is material. Let me work with it."

* * *

After the others drifted to bed, she climbed to the attic and took out the ledger—not the atlas, not tonight; she couldn't bear to look at Aldous's coastlines while her own hands shook—but the plain household book, the dull domestic one, columns of rents paid and candles used. In the back pages, in her own cramped hand, was the true list. She had written it two years ago on a bad night, after a rumor swept the town that records were being collected for conscription lists, and she had never shown it to anyone.

Eleven names. She read them like coordinates.

*Bess Crane.* Safe. Real, documented, boring—a widow of modest means running a boarding house, the most legible woman in Vellsmark.

*Noor Crane.* Safe, technically. Apprentice cartographer, ward of the late Master Aldous Crane, papers in order. Though she noticed, as she did every time she wrote it, that her own name was the least certain thing on the page. Ward. Apprentice. Niece, sometimes, when the situation required. She had been so many relations to so many clerks that she sometimes felt like a coastline redrawn at every survey.

*Tomas Ellery.* Half-safe. His papers existed; his nerve didn't. A boy whose stutter got worse whenever a uniform entered the room, registered as a nephew at sea more often than as himself, so that even his real name had begun to feel like a costume.

*Ilse Brenner.* Not safe. The name itself was probably invented; the girl behind it definitely hunted.

*Anselm Voigt.* Not safe at all. The name on his regiment's rolls, the name on a warrant that was surely gathering dust in some provost's drawer, waiting.

*Piet Jansen.* Officially dead, insofar as he existed at all. Unlicensed, unrecorded, uncounted for two decades.

Plus the four lodgers, harmless, real, and forgettable, and old Marta who took her meals here but slept at the chapel, a border case Noor had never resolved.

She sat back on the stool and listened to the house breathe. Floorboards. Wind in the chimney. Ilse turning over in the room below, the particular sigh of the third stair when Piet went up to bed despite having a perfectly good mattress in the loft he preferred not to use because, he said, beds made him feel observed.

Six names that existed only here. Six people whose entire legal existence fit inside one page in one attic, guarded by one woman with ink-stained fingers.

Aldous had taught her that a map is a promise. *Every line you draw says: this is here, this is real, someone should be able to find it.* He'd said it about harbors and shoals, but Noor had understood even then, at nineteen, that he meant it about everything. A census was a map too. And the army was asking her to hand them a chart of her family with six marks missing—and if she handed them a perfect one instead, six marks that led straight to a gallows.

Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the middle of the list. She noticed it now with a kind of clinical interest. Fear, she thought, was like tide: it came in, and if you stood in it long enough it went out again and left the ground firmer.

Ten days.

She pulled a fresh sheet toward her, dipped her pen, and began—not forging, not yet, just sketching, the way Aldous sketched a coastline before committing to it. Rough lines. Plausible shapes. A seasonal worker named Hendrik who had gone north in September to his brother's herring crew. A niece returned to Marlowe to nurse an aunt. Deaths, maybe—one, small and distant and unverifiable, a fever in a village three days' ride away where no clerk would ever ride to check.

It felt terrible. That was the thing no one told you about lying in ink: your hand knew. Her hand wanted to make clean lines, true ones. Every false name she set down felt like drawing a reef where there was open water, and some part of her—the part Aldous trained—screamed at the falsification of the chart.

But another part, the part that had buried him and stayed in his house and learned to love a table of strays, looked at the six names and understood that this was the same craft. Maps existed so that no one got lost. So did lies, sometimes. Probably. She would have to decide later whether that was wisdom or just the elegant way liars talked to themselves.

She worked until the candle guttered, building the fiction the way you built a seawall: not in one grand stroke but course by course, stone by stone, checking each against the questions a thorough man would ask.

And then she stopped, pen lifted, because a thought surfaced that chilled her worse than the notice had.

Commandant Voss had come to a port town with troop transports and requisitioned warehouses and posted a census order in a hand so neat it could only belong to a meticulous man. Meticulous men did not collect names for the pleasure of the list. They collected names because names were the beginning of a search, and searches ended in attics.

And she was, by every account that mattered, the finest cartographer in Vellsmark—the heir of a master whose unfinished atlas had been the talk of two provinces before illness and quiet had closed around him. If the new governor was the sort of man who wanted to know every soul in his district, he was also, almost certainly, the sort of man who had already heard about the dead mapmaker on Lantern Row.

Noor blew out the candle and sat in the dark with her true list in her lap, and understood that the ten days were not going to be spent only on the ledger.

Somewhere across the town, a meticulous man was making his own lists. She only hoped hers would be finished first.
