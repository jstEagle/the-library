## 5. What Aldous Drew in the Margins

Tomas had carried heavy things before. Sacks of herring up from Piet's boat, crates of Bess's preserves down from the attic, a man once—drunk, twice his weight, singing—out of the Anchor and Chain. His arms knew work. It was the other kind of carrying that made him stammer, and tonight he was carrying both.

The atlas lay wrapped in oilcloth on Noor's bed like something sick.

"It's heavier than it looks," he said. Then, because the words came out wrong-shaped as always: "Heavier. Than it looks." He winced at himself. Three years in this house and the stammer still ambushed him whenever the stakes went up, as if fear squeezed the words through too narrow a door.

Noor didn't look at him funny. She never did. She just knelt and tucked the cloth tighter around the corners, the way she did everything—precise, unhurried, hands that seemed to know what they were about even when her face said nothing at all.

"We're not carrying it," she said. "We're hiding it."

"Where?"

"The floor under your room. The boards by the chimney don't match the others. I checked years ago."

"You *checked*?" He hadn't known that. He hadn't known she'd surveyed the house the way she surveyed coastlines, marking depths and hazards, deciding where things could be sunk safely. "When—when was that?"

"The first month you were here. You talked in your sleep for a week and I needed somewhere to put the ledger if soldiers ever came asking." She said it plainly, without apology, and Tomas felt the strange doubled warmth he always felt around Noor: grateful to be protected, unsettled to learn how thoroughly he was watched over. "Grey coats in the harbor change the arithmetic. If Voss searches the house, the attic's the first place they'll look. Books get found in attics. Things get found under floors only if someone's pulling them up board by board, and nobody does that unless they already know."

"Unless they al-already know," he echoed, and hated the echo, and kept lifting anyway.

* * *

The space between Noor's room and his own was a narrow landing with a window that had fogged permanently sometime before the war, so that the harbor beyond it existed only as a grey suggestion: masts, smoke, the shapes of ships being counted by men who counted everything now. The notices had gone up on the customs house two days ago. REGISTER ALL OCCUPANTS. TEN DAYS. And beneath it, smaller, the signature that made Piet spit: R. VOSS, COMMANDANT.

Ten days. Tomas turned the number over the way he turned everything, slow and complete, looking for the flaw in it. Ten days was enough time to paint a boat. Not enough time to become invisible. Ilse had been invisible for four years and it took work every single day—the right name, the right story, the right flinch of nothing when her old life was mentioned.

"Careful on the stairs," Noor said. "Third step groans."

He stepped wide over the third step out of habit—he always did—and realized she'd mapped the stairs too. The whole house was charted in her head, soundings taken, safe channels marked. He wondered what it cost a person to live like that, holding every route in mind, trusting no floorboard.

In his room the light was going amber at the edges. He'd lit no lamp; lamplight in windows was another thing the grey coats noticed. So they worked by the last of the day, Noor kneeling at the chimney corner while he pried at the boards with the flat of a chisel, and it was going fine, it was nearly done, until the oilcloth slipped.

It wasn't a drop. The atlas didn't fall. It just shifted as he adjusted his grip, and the wrapping loosened at one corner, and the leather cover fell open against his forearm the way books do, eager, and the page that showed was not a coastline at all but the margin of one—a stretch of sea north of the Vellsmark shoals that Tomas knew like his own hands, because Piet had taught him those waters before his voice finished breaking.

There was nothing there. That was the thing. Open water, clean as a hymn. But inked along the margin, small and patient, ran a line of symbols: a circle with a tail, a triangle pierced by a stroke, three dots descending like birds going down, a hook, a hook again, then a mark like a half-sunken moon.

"Penny for them," Noor said, coming to see why he'd stopped.

"I don't—these aren't—" He turned the page toward her. "What are these?"

She looked. And Tomas, who had spent three years learning to read faces because his own mouth couldn't be trusted, saw something cross hers that he'd never seen there before. Not fear exactly. Something older. The look of a person who has walked past a locked door in their own house for years and just heard, faintly, a sound behind it.

"They're everywhere," she said quietly.

"What are they?"

"Aldous's hand. Marginal marks. I thought they were—" Her fingers hovered over the circle-with-a-tail without touching it. "Surveyors' shorthand, maybe. Draftsman's ticks. I've been inking over some of them for three years when they cluttered a plate. Cleaning them up. Tidying him."

"Tidying him," Tomas repeated softly. It sounded terrible when she said it like that.

"He was particular. Everything on the page had to earn its place. If he drew something, it meant something." She sat back on her heels. "I never decoded them because I never thought they needed decoding. A master doesn't leave noise in his margins. I assumed I understood the system well enough to know which marks mattered."

"But these aren't noise."

"No," she said. "These aren't noise."

* * *

They brought the atlas back into the light instead of hiding it, which later would seem like the strangest decision of that whole strange week, but neither of them thought of it that way then. They spread it open across Tomas's bed with the oilcloth thrown back, and Noor fetched her good magnifier and a sheet of paper, and Tomas sat cross-legged at the foot of the bed feeling like a boy again, like an apprentice, like someone permitted near the grown-up mystery.

The symbols ran through the whole book once you started seeing them. Along the northern shoals. Down the estuary. In the margin of the harbor chart itself, so close to the customs anchorage that it seemed like audacity rather than accident: the half-sunken moon, drawn small as a flea.

"There," Tomas said, tapping the harbor page. "That's—that's off the point. Where the old pilot beacon used to be."

"You know it?"

"Piet took me past it a hundred times. Beacon came down in the storm year. Nothing there now but rocks." He paused. The thought arrived slowly, the way his best thoughts always did, walking the long way round. "Except boats that don't want to be seen go close to rocks. Because patrol boats don't."

Noor looked at him with an expression he decided privately to call respect.

"And the hooks," he went on, gaining speed, words coming easier now that they had cargo. "Two hooks together, here and here. Piet calls those tide gates. Slack water between currents—you can cross a channel at slack that'll kill you any other hour. He times his ferry by them. Everyone local does."

"So Aldous drew the tides."

"Aldous drew the *windows*." Tomas heard his own voice go quiet and steady, the stammer gone entirely, which happened only when he forgot to be afraid of speaking. "Not where the water is. When."

They worked until full dark, Noor refusing the lamp, matching symbol to symbol, and the pattern assembled itself the way patterns do—all resistance and then suddenly all at once. The circles-with-tails were coves and landings. The pierced triangles were houses—safehouses, though neither of them said the word aloud yet. The descending dots were signal points or watch posts. And threaded through all of it, linking rock to rock, cove to window to door, ran routes. Whole routes. From the border marshes down the coast, through Vellsmark's back channels, ending at the harbor margin with its little drowned moon.

Fugitive roads, drawn in the margins of a masterpiece, hidden in plain sight inside the most beautiful atlas anyone on this coast had ever produced.

"He moved people," Noor said at last. Her voice was very level, the way it got when level was costing her. "That's what this is. He moved people who needed moving."

Tomas thought of his own arrival. Rain, night, a knock, Bess opening the door before he'd finished knocking because Bess always knew. He'd told no one how he'd come, and no one had asked. That was the house's law: your story stays yours.

"How many people do you think walked those lines?" he asked.

"Enough that someone drew them carefully enough to last." Noor closed the atlas gently, both hands, like closing a coffin—or like closing a door on a sleeping child. "Enough that whoever used them trusted the map more than their own eyes."

* * *

They sat in the dark a while after that. Downstairs the household sounds rose through the floorboards—Bess arguing cheerfully with the stove, Piet's boots, Ilse humming something in a minor key. Eleven people in a house that officially held six. Ten days to account for five ghosts. And now this other thing underneath everything, this second map under the first.

"We still have to hide it," Tomas said finally. "More than before."

"Yes."

"Not because of the census."

"No."

"Because if Voss finds these margins, he doesn't find a boarding house's secrets. He finds—" Tomas worked it through, saying each piece separately so it couldn't trip him. "He finds proof Aldous helped fugitives. Treason. And everyone connected to it. His apprentice. His—his household. Anyone who ever—"

"Anyone who ever knocked on our door at night," Noor finished. "Yes."

The stove downstairs banged; Bess swore at it fondly. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that had no idea what was lying wrapped on the bed above them.

Tomas looked at the atlas—at the beautiful, careful, deadly thing Master Crane had spent his life making—and understood suddenly that the dead man had left them more than a legacy. He'd left them a liability with gilt edges. Every coastline true. Every margin a confession.

"Noor," he said. "Do you think anyone else knows? Whoever he moved them *for*—the people at the other end of the routes. Do you think they're still out there? Still reading these same symbols somewhere?"

She was quiet long enough that the fogged window fully gave up its grey light.

"I've been wondering the same thing," she said. "And Tomas—I think we're about to find out. Because a man like Voss requisitions a port town for one of two reasons. Supplies—or what moves through it."

Downstairs, a knock came at the front door. Two knocks, a pause, then one more.

Nobody in the house knocked like that.

Noor's hand closed over the atlas, and in the dark her face was unreadable as open water—but her voice, when it came, was steady as a charted channel.

"That," she said, "is either the answer to my question—or the beginning of it."
