## 6. Aunt Bess Counts Heads

Bess Crane had run the boarding house at 14 Salt Lane for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once needed to count the people in it.

You knew a household the way you knew your own hands. Piet came up from the harbor smelling of tar and cold water. Tomas came down from the loft when he heard the kettle and not one minute before. Ilse took her bread standing at the window because she still flinched at doors opening behind her, though three years had softened that to only a stiffening of the shoulders. Anselm ate like a man apologizing for it. The twins from the ropewalk — Marta and Joren — were gone most days and loud most nights. Old Sanna paid nothing and swept everything. And Noor was either in the attic or she wasn't, and when she wasn't she was somewhere between the attic and wherever Aldous's ghost had asked her to go.

Eleven. Bess counted them on her fingers at the kitchen table on the morning after the grey coats arrived, and the number sat there like a stone she'd swallowed.

Eleven people. One ledger page. A commandant whose notices used words like *mandatory* and *under penalty* without ever once using a word like *please*.

She set the kettle on out of habit and then stood looking at it as if it might tell her something useful. It didn't. Kettles never did. That was why she'd liked them better than people for most of her widowhood.

The notice was still pinned to the door where Joren had tacked it up last night, because nobody in this house could bear to bring it inside and nobody could bear to throw it away. Bess had read it four times now, and each reading made the arithmetic worse, which she hadn't thought possible.

*All heads of household shall register the names, ages, trades, and places of origin of every person residing under their roof within ten days. False registration is an offense against the occupation administration.*

Every person residing under their roof.

Not every citizen. Not every name on some old tax roll. Every person. The word *residing* did the work, and Bess understood with a cold clarity that this Voss — she'd already heard the name twice at the fish market, spoken the way you speak about weather that's coming — had chosen it deliberately. He wasn't asking who belonged to Vellsmark. He was asking who slept under Bess Crane's roof, and he would count beds if he had to.

She'd spent the night doing exactly that. Counting beds. Eleven sleepers, nine beds, two pallets, and a dumbwaiter shaft that Ilse could fold herself into if it came to it, though the girl was twenty-three now and her shoulders were wider than they'd been at nineteen and the shaft was not getting any bigger.

One forged ledger page's worth of lies. That was what it came down to. She'd said it aloud to the empty kitchen, and the kitchen, being a kitchen, had agreed.

* * *

She gathered them after supper, when the light through the salt-fogged windows went the color of dishwater and even the harbor sounds outside seemed to hold their breath. Piet by the stove, because his knees and the stove had an understanding. Tomas in the corner chair with his hands folded like a schoolboy awaiting a caning. Ilse on the window bench, sewing something she wasn't sewing. Anselm standing, because Anselm never sat when the conversation turned serious — Bess had noticed that months ago; sitting was surrender to him, maybe, or sitting meant staying, and staying was a thing he didn't trust himself to want. Sanna dozed in her rocker, which was how Sanna attended everything important. Noor came down last, ink on her fingers as always, and took the seat across from Bess, and looked at her with those steady grey eyes that always made Bess feel the conversation had started before she'd opened her mouth.

"You've all seen the notice," Bess said. "Ten days to register everyone under this roof. Names, ages, trades, origins." She let that sit. "So let's say it plain. There are eleven of us. There are—"

"Three," Noor said quietly, "who don't officially exist."

Bess nodded. She'd expected Noor to have the count ready. Noor always had the count ready; it was one of the things about her that alternately reassured and unnerved.

"Ilse," Bess said. "Anselm. And—" she hesitated over the third, because saying it aloud gave it weight, "Tomas."

Tomas's head came up. "I'm on the tax rolls."

"You're on the tax rolls as Tomas Verlaine, aged fourteen, nephew of the late Gerrit Verlaine of Draymark." Bess kept her voice gentle. "You're seventeen now, love, and Gerrit Verlaine never existed, and Draymark burned two winters back so there's no parish left to ask. You're a boy I invented on a form six years ago and fed ever since." She reached over and touched his wrist. "It doesn't make you less real. It makes you less findable. Those are different things."

"I know," Tomas said. His voice did its stammering thing around the edges but held. "I just — hearing it."

"Aye. Well. We're all going to hear worse before this is done."

Piet grunted from the stove. "Say it, then."

"Here's my thinking." Bess laced her fingers on the table, the way she did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. "We register truthfully — near enough truthfully — for eight of us. Myself, Piet, Sanna, Marta, Joren, Noor, and the boys' trades are honest enough to survive questions. Real names, real ages, real work. Eight names that any assessor, any soldier, any neighbor asked to gossip can confirm without trying. That part has to be clean, because that part is what buys credibility for everything else."

"And the other three?" Ilse asked. Her needle had stopped moving.

"The other three we register as departed. Ilse — you went south to your cousin in Osterhavn when the seamstress shop closed, everyone knows the shop closed, half the street saw you carrying boxes. Anselm—" she glanced at him and he met her look without blinking, "—signed onto a coastal trader out of the harbor last spring. Piet can swear to it; Piet swears to things at the harbor tavern weekly whether they're true or not."

"They're usually true," Piet said mildly.

"They're usually *believable*, which is better." Bess pressed on before anyone could object, because if she paused she'd lose the momentum and the momentum was the only thing keeping her voice level. "And Tomas stays Tomas Verlaine, aged fourteen, because fourteen-year-olds attract no questions and seventeen-year-old boys get pulled for labor details. We shave nothing off your height, we add nothing to your age, and if anyone asks, you're my sister's grandson and grief-stupid about dates."

She sat back. For a moment the room held still, the way rooms do when everyone is deciding whether to be relieved or afraid.

"No," Noor said.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing about Noor — she didn't do dramatic, she did precise, and precise was somehow worse. She said it the way she'd say *that coastline is wrong*, and Bess felt her neck prickle.

"Excuse me?"

"It won't hold." Noor turned her teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit Bess had watched her inherit from Aldous without either of them knowing it. "Aunt Bess, listen to me. Partial truth is the most fragile lie there is. A whole forgery you can defend — every detail consistent, every answer prepared. A whole truth needs no defense. But eight truths and three inventions? That's a wall with three doors in it, and Commandant Voss strikes me as a man who walks rooms testing doors."

"He's thorough," Piet said. "That's the word from the harbor. Thorough and patient. Worst combination there is."

"Then think what thoroughness does with our list." Noor leaned forward. "He registers us. Fine. Then he cross-checks. Tax rolls against harbor manifests against parish books against the letters other households file — because people inform, Aunt Bess, not from malice, mostly, but from carelessness. Someone remembers Ilse buying thread last month. Someone recalls a young man chopping firewood here who doesn't match any registered occupant. Every true fact we register becomes a pin holding the false map flat, and one loose thread anywhere pulls the whole cloth apart. If we're going to lie, we should lie completely or not at all."

"Not at all isn't available," Bess said. "I'm not putting eleven invented people on an occupation census. Eight true names is the floor under us. If it collapses, it collapses on solid ground."

"If it collapses, it collapses everywhere." Noor's voice stayed level but something underneath it had begun to hum. "Don't you see? The moment Voss finds one inconsistency — one — he stops trusting the ledger entirely. Then eight true names don't protect eight people. They give him a complete list of exactly who to question first. Truthfulness doesn't buy mercy. It buys efficiency. His efficiency."

"Tomas is fourteen," Bess said, and heard her own voice harden. "Fourteen-year-olds don't get questioned, they get believed. That's the whole point of—"

"That's the point until someone asks him where in Draymark he was born and he says the name of a parish that's ash, or he can't remember his invented mother's name because he's frightened, and Tomas frightens loudly." Noor caught herself, flushed, and looked at Tomas directly. "I'm sorry. But I'd rather plan around the truth of you than around the story of you."

For a heartbeat Bess thought Tomas would crumple. Instead he straightened in his chair, and when he spoke the stammer had burned off entirely, which happened perhaps twice a year and always startled everyone including himself.

"She's right," he said. "About me, anyway. Under questioning I'd break the story in a day. I break stories in an hour." He almost smiled. "Ask Piet about the time I tried to cover for the broken skylight."

"The skylight," Piet confirmed solemnly, "confessed before Tomas finished inventing it."

Despite everything, the room breathed a small laugh, and Bess hated how much she needed it.

* * *

Later, when the others had drifted to their corners and their beds, Noor stayed at the table and Bess found herself unable to go up. The lamp between them burned low. Outside, somewhere along the quay, boots — many boots, in step — passed in the dark, and both women listened until the sound faded.

"You think I'm being soft," Bess said.

"I think you're being a mother." Noor said it without accusation, which somehow landed harder. "There's a difference."

"Don't patronize me, girl. I buried a husband and raised you and ran this house through two shortages and a quarantine. I know arithmetic." Bess rubbed her eyes. "But here's what I know that your maps don't show. Voss's men will knock on doors and ask neighbors simple questions. Simple questions get simple answers. 'Does Bess Crane board people?' Yes. 'How many?' Four or five, says the neighbor, counting what they see at market. So I register seven, eight — close enough to what the street believes that no one bothers correcting us. But if I register eleven, and the street says five, then the ledger itself is the discrepancy. Do you understand? The bigger the lie, the louder it echoes."

Noor was quiet a long moment. "Or," she said finally, "the street says five because the street has only ever been allowed to see five. Which means the street's testimony is worth exactly as much as we've taught it to be worth. Three years of practice, Aunt Bess. Ilse as my cousin visiting. Tomas as yours. Anselm as Piet's deckhand who sleeps aboard. We've done the rehearsal. I'm only saying we shouldn't abandon the play in the second act because the theater got new management."

"The new management hangs people."

"Yes," Noor said. "Which is why the acting has to be perfect."

Bess looked at her — really looked, across the lamplight, at this woman she'd taken in at eleven years old, all elbows and silence and a dead man's name, who had grown into someone who counted risks the way other people counted spoons. Aldous had left more than an atlas in that attic. He'd left a mind that couldn't leave a coastline unexamined, and right now that mind was aimed at keeping eleven people breathing, and Bess was torn clean down the middle between gratitude and terror.

Because she could see Noor was right. That was the unbearable part. Partial truth *was* fragile. She'd known it since she said it aloud to the empty kitchen, known it the way you know a storm is coming off the water before the sky admits it. Eight true names and three ghosts was not safety. It was a bet dressed as caution.

But Noor's way — full forgery, total invention, eleven consistent lives built out of paper — that required a forger. That required weeks of work and nerves like piano wire and a hand steady enough to fake parish records and harbor manifests and the small ugly handwriting of clerks. That required—

Bess stopped. Looked at Noor's fingers, still faintly ink-stained even now, even at rest.

"You've already started," she said. "Haven't you. On the ledger."

Noor didn't flinch. "I've started thinking about it."

"Noor."

"There are ten days," Noor said. "I need all ten."

They sat with that. The lamp hissed. Down the hall, faintly, Sanna's rocker creaked its slow insomnia rhythm, and Bess thought: eleven people under my roof, and not one of them asleep, probably, and every one of them pretending to be, and that was the household too, that had always been the household — a roof held up by mutual pretense, load-bearing lies, and love doing the work of mortar.

"Truthful except for Ilse and Anselm," Bess said at last. "That's my offer. Tomas goes on the books as himself — real name, real age, whatever mess of origins we have to build around him, we build. But I won't erase that boy twice. Once was enough."

Noor studied her, and Bess watched her weigh it — actually weigh it, the way Aldous must have weighed currents and cargo and risk, and Bess realized with a strange lurch that she was negotiating with her own niece as with an equal, and that some part of her had been doing so for years.

"All right," Noor said softly. "Truthful except for Ilse and Anselm. And Tomas built from scratch, properly, so deep no question reaches bottom."

"Properly," Bess agreed.

They shook on it, absurdly, formally, two women over a dying lamp, and Bess felt the strangest grief in the handshake — the sense of crossing some line she couldn't name, from keeping secrets into manufacturing them, from hiding people to erasing them, and not being sure the distance between those two things was as wide as she'd always believed.

She was blowing out the lamp when Noor spoke again, quiet, almost to herself.

"There's something else. In the atlas. Marginal markings I've never been able to read. Tomas spotted them today while we moved it." A pause. "I think Aldous drew routes, Aunt Bess. Routes for people who needed to disappear."

Bess's hand froze on the lamp key.

The boots passed again on the quay below, distant now, in step, in step, in step — and she understood that whatever her niece had found in the margins of a dead man's life's work, the ten days had just gotten much shorter, and much darker, than any ledger could measure.
