## 7. The Commandant's Cartographer

Reiner Voss kept his maps in the cellar of the customs house, which had seemed to him the only honest room in Vellsmark. The upper floors smelled of requisitioned wine and other men's cigars; the harbor offices leaked salt into every ledger; the billets assigned to him had been a chandler's parlor and still smelled of tallow and herring. But the cellar was dry, cool, and dark, and in it he had installed the one luxury his rank permitted: a long table of planed pine, lit by two lamps with green shades, where the town's charts lay flat and unrolled like patients awaiting diagnosis.

He stood over them each evening after the day's paperwork, because he believed that a man who did not look at maps would eventually believe anything.

What he saw tonight he did not like.

The coastal survey—the army's own, commissioned three years ago by a staff colonel who had since been promoted somewhere far from water—showed Vellsmark's approaches as clean, confident lines. Depth soundings in tidy columns. A shoal marked east of the breakwater, another off the point, hazards neatly contained, the sea behaving itself. It was a map made for men who would never sail these waters.

And yet in eleven days of occupation, three supply vessels had touched ground coming in. Not wrecks—Vellsmark's pilots were competent enough at gunpoint—but groundings, delays, a grain barge sitting on a sandbar for six hours while the tide decided whether to be merciful. The third incident had cost him a dressing-down from the regional command that he had accepted standing up, hands behind his back, face arranged into the expression of a man absorbing instruction. He did not make the same face twice for anyone.

Voss was not a vain man, but he was an exact one, and exactness had taught him the difference between being wrong and being lied to. The survey was not wrong the way incompetence is wrong—drifting, sloppy, asymmetric. It was wrong the way a forged signature is wrong: too correct in all the places a forger thinks matter, and quietly false everywhere else. Someone, sometime, had drawn this coast as they wished it to be rather than as it was.

Or—as he allowed himself, turning the lamp lower—the coast itself had changed. Bars migrate. Channels silt. A war keeps a harbor busy and no one surveys.

Either explanation required the same remedy. New soundings. New eyes. A new chart.

He had already ordered the naval launch sounded the outer roads, and the results had come back worse than useless: the young officer assigned to the task could not tell a lead line's marks apart from a fisherman's knots, and his figures contradicted the old pilotage books by fathoms in both directions. Voss had sent him back out with instructions to do it again and had spent the intervening afternoon doing what he always did when a problem resisted him. He read files.

It was in the files that he found Aldous Crane.

* * *

The name appeared first in a shipping register, fourteen years dead now, listed as cartographer of record for the harbor improvements. Then again in a guild roll. Then in a letter of complaint from a hydrographic office in the capital, preserved in triplicate by some clerk with a genius for retention: *Master Crane's corrections to Chart 114 are noted but declined, as they imply error in the original survey.* Declined—and yet, Voss noticed, someone at the admiralty had penciled in the margin, in a hand smaller and more careful than the clerk's: *check the bar at Kessel Point.* No date. No follow-up. Just that one anxious little sentence, filed and forgotten by everyone except whoever had written it.

Voss sat back in his chair. He liked finding sentences like that. They were the fingerprints of intelligent people inside bureaucratic mud.

He pulled the personnel and residency records next, and here the trail thickened. Aldous Crane, master cartographer, resident of Vellsmark for thirty-one years. Deceased three years past, cause recorded as illness of the lungs, attended by Dr. Halloran, who had since moved inland. Household: none registered. Apprentice: one, entered in the guild rolls as Noor Crane—no relation indicated, though she carried his name. Female, age at registration twenty-one. The guild entry ended there, unsigned by any journeyman examiner, which meant either she had never tested or no examiner had ever come.

An unfinished atlas. The phrase arrived in his mind unbidden, and he examined it the way he examined everything: coldly, turning it over, looking for flaws. A master mapmaker dies leaving an apprentice. The apprentice does not test, does not travel, does not appear in any subsequent trade record for three years. She simply stays—in a boarding house, of all places, run by one Bess Crane, née Vasseur, widowed, tax record thin as paper gets. A woman of twenty-four, trained by a master good enough to annoy the admiralty, spending three years in a dying port town doing—what?

Nothing, said the file.

Files that said nothing were, in Voss's experience, the ones worth reading twice.

He summoned his adjutant at nine the next morning—a punctual, humorless lieutenant named Brandt whom Voss had selected precisely because he reported facts without garnish—and gave him three instructions. First, find out who had sold provisions or materials to the Crane household in the past three years; paper, ink, and vellum leave receipts even when people imagine themselves invisible. Second, determine whether any vessel out of Vellsmark had logged unusual courses since the death of Aldous Crane. Third, bring the apprentice to him at four o'clock, politely, with a written summons citing her guild status, so that she came as a professional and not as a suspect.

Brandt hesitated at the door. "If she's just a boarder, sir, drawing pictures in an attic—"

"Then I have lost an hour." Voss did not look up from the survey chart. "If she isn't, I will have gained the only honest map on this coast. Go."

* * *

She arrived at four o'clock precisely, which told him something before she had said a word. People summoned by occupying armies came early, to show deference, or late, to show courage. Coming exactly on time meant a person who had calculated that neither gesture would help, and had chosen instead to give him nothing at all.

She was small, dark-haired, ink-stained at the right middle finger and nowhere else—a working stain, not a decorative one. She wore a grey dress that had once been good and had been mended in ways designed not to be noticed. Her hands stayed folded. Her eyes went, first and briefly, to the maps on the table behind him—not to his face, not to the soldiers at the door, not to the window. To the charts. And then back to him, composed, as if she had caught herself touching something hot.

Yes, thought Voss. There you are.

"Miss Crane. Thank you for coming. Sit, please." He gestured to the chair across the table, deliberately placed among the maps rather than before his desk. He watched her take it, watched her note the placement, understood that she understood it was deliberate, and felt the particular pleasure he took in meeting a worthy counterpart. It happened rarely. It never lasted. That was the tragedy of his profession: everyone interesting was either promoted away, shot, or lying to him.

"You knew my master," she said. Not a question. She had read the summons, then; she had done her own research in whatever hours he had given her.

"I know his work," Voss said. "I know a complaint he lodged with the admiralty fourteen years ago regarding the bar at Kessel Point. I know they declined it. I also know that in the margin of the file, someone wrote *check the bar at Kessel Point*, and I very much doubt that someone was Master Crane."

Noor Crane said nothing. Her stillness was not the stillness of fear. He had interrogated frightened men by the hundred; they breathed wrong, their hands migrated, their eyes asked permission. This woman breathed like someone waiting out weather.

"Three ships have grounded in eleven days," he continued. "Your master warned the admiralty about this coast years ago and was ignored. I am not going to ignore him. I'm told he left an atlas unfinished. I want it finished."

"There is no atlas."

The answer came without hesitation, which was itself informative. Innocent people say *what atlas?* They ask questions. Guilty people deny. She had done neither, quite—she had stated a fact with the calm of a woman reciting her own name, and Voss recognized the technique because he used it himself. Deny nothing loudly. Simply decline the premise.

"No atlas," he repeated pleasantly. "A master cartographer spends his last decades on a port town's coastline, takes an apprentice, teaches her for years, and produces nothing? Miss Crane, I collect maps. Have done since I was a boy. My father was a customs surveyor on the Danube and I learned before I could ride a horse that a map is the only document that tells the truth by refusing to say anything else. So when I hear that the finest mapmaker this coast ever produced died with his life's work incomplete, and his apprentice remains in the town where that work was done—" he spread his hands, an open, reasonable gesture— "you must understand that I don't disbelieve you. I simply believe you less than I believe arithmetic."

For the first time something moved behind her eyes. Not fear. Calculation. He let the silence sit, because silence was the tool he trusted most, and because he had learned that the moment you filled your own quiet was the moment you stopped learning anything.

"The atlas, if it exists," she said at last, slowly, "is unfinished. Unfinished work has no value. It couldn't serve anyone."

"Everything has value to someone willing to finish it." He rose, crossed to the table, and drew out a drawer. From it he took a small leather case and opened it on the table between them: coins, gold, imperial mint, more money than a boarding-house apprentice would see in five years. Beside it he laid a folded paper—his own draft, prepared that morning, the ink still smelling faintly of the good iron-gall he imported. *Commission for services to the Army Survey Office. Protection of person and property extended to the signatory and all members of her household.*

He watched her read that last clause. Watched her stop breathing for half a second, resume, and control it. There it is, he thought. There's the household. Whatever she says exists or doesn't exist under that widow Crane's roof, the word landed somewhere soft, and I have just put my finger on it.

"That's protection," she said carefully, "from what?"

"From nothing, if you accept." He sat down again, folding his hands. "From scrutiny, if you refuse. Miss Crane, I'll be direct, because I respect precision in others. This town is being inventoried. Every house, every occupant, every head counted and recorded within ten days. Most households will present me with truth. Some will present me with something else, and I will find those, because discrepancies are the easiest things in the world to find if you're patient—one extra cup, one worn floorboard, one name that appears on no tax roll. I would prefer not to spend my patience on you. I would prefer to spend it on your master's coastlines, which are apparently the only accurate ones in existence."

"You'd pay me to finish his atlas. For military use."

"For navigation. Ships are grounding, Miss Crane. Sailors drown either way; I merely propose they drown less often on charts drawn by someone competent." He leaned forward slightly. "Consider what I'm offering honestly. Coin. Guild recognition—I can have your examination waived and your mastership certified by Friday. Legal protection for yourself and everyone under your aunt's roof. In exchange, the atlas, completed under army supervision, bearing perhaps a modest credit line. Your master spent his life mapping this coast so people wouldn't be lost on it. I am offering to let that work do exactly what it was made to do. What objection can conscience possibly raise?"

It was, he reflected later, the best argument he had. It was even, in its way, sincere. Voss had never much troubled himself about wars; he troubled himself about accuracy. A truthful chart saved lives regardless of whose flag flew over the harbor, and he genuinely could not see—had never been able to see—where the treason lay in that.

But he watched her face while he said it, and what he saw was not persuasion. It was a woman standing in a burning building deciding which rooms to carry things out of.

"I can't commit to anything today," she said.

"Of course not." He slid the coin case and the draft across the table toward her side. "Keep both, whether you sign or not. Consider them a retainer against your consideration." A flicker crossed her expression—she would not touch the money, he guessed; she might not even take the paper. It didn't matter. What mattered was planted.

"One question," she said, rising. She had not touched either offering. "The officer who wrote in the margin. *Check the bar at Kessel Point.* Do you know what became of him?"

Voss blinked. Of all the exits she might have taken from the conversation, he had not mapped this one. "I don't. The annotation is unsigned."

"He was right about the bar," she said. "My master was right about everything on this coast, including the parts the admiralty declined. Whoever finished his atlas would be finishing the most truthful document in the empire. I imagine whoever wanted it would want it very badly." She inclined her head, precise as a compass needle settling north. "Thank you for the courtesy, Commandant. You'll have my answer soon."

Then she was gone, footsteps measured down the corridor, and Voss stood alone in the lamplight with his open case of gold and his unsigned commission and the peculiar sensation—rare, unwelcome, almost pleasurable—of having been measured by someone he had intended to measure.

* * *

That night he worked late in the cellar. Brandt brought the day's findings at ten: a stationer on Molenstraat had sold paper and vellum in quantity to the Crane household, irregularly, paid cash, roughly every six weeks for three years. A chandler confirmed lamp oil beyond ordinary domestic use. And a harborside informant—paid, unreliable, but occasionally golden—reported that the old ferryman Piet Lansen had been seen, more than once in the past three years, running his boat at odd hours and odd courses, and that on at least two occasions a young woman matching the apprentice's description had been aboard.

Courses, thought Voss. Not destinations. Courses.

He stood at the long table and unrolled the army survey again, the beautiful, confident, lying thing, and looked at it the way he imagined Aldous Crane had looked at it fourteen years ago: as a document containing an argument between the empire and the sea, which the sea was winning.

Somewhere in this town, an unfinished atlas held the correction. Perhaps more than a correction—he was too careful a man to assume that a mapmaker who ran boats at odd hours was only making maps. But that was a problem for later, and Voss was a great believer in solving problems in order.

First the atlas. Then the household arithmetic. Ten days, and he was a patient man, and patience, properly applied, was indistinguishable from a trap closing.

He turned down the lamps and stood a moment in the green half-dark among his maps, thinking about the apprentice's parting words. *Whoever wanted it would want it very badly.* She had said it as a warning to herself, he judged. She should have said it as a warning to him.

Because she was right. He did want it very badly. And Voss had learned, over twenty years of soldiering and surveying, that the things he wanted badly were the things he eventually got—or the things that ruined him trying.

He locked the cellar, climbed the stairs into the smell of requisitioned wine, and began drafting, in his small meticulous hand, the order for a full census of Vellsmark.

Every head. Every name. Every floorboard.

Ten days.
