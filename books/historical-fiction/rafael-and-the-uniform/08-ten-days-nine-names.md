## 8. Ten Days, Nine Names

The commandant's notice came with a wax seal the color of dried blood, and it was still lying on Bess's kitchen table at dawn when Noor took it upstairs to the attic and set it beside the atlas.

*Every head of household shall register all occupants by name, age, occupation, and place of origin. Registration closes in ten days. Failure to comply will be treated as concealment.*

Concealment. The word had teeth. She'd read enough requisition notices in her life—Aldous had made her study the language of governments the way other masters taught scripture—but this one was written by a man who counted things for a living, and she could feel his patience in every line. Voss wasn't threatening them. He was simply informing them of what happened next.

Noor sat down at the worktable, pulled a fresh sheet toward her, and began to do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the grey coats arrived.

Eleven people slept under Aunt Bess's roof. The house's papers said five: Bess herself, Noor, Piet—who had been there so long he'd become furniture in the eyes of any clerk—and two names that were real but half-forgotten, a widow who'd died two winters back and whose room had been quietly reassigned. Six souls, then, that the ledger would have to account for out of thin air or thin graves.

She dipped her pen.

*Margret Sollen, seamstress, departed for her sister in Kaldvik, spring of last year.*

Ilse. Noor wrote the lie in a careful imitation of Bess's hand—not Bess's current hand, which trembled slightly on cold mornings, but the hand from three years ago, steady and round. Aldous had taught her that too, though he'd never known what she'd use it for. *A mapmaker who can't forge a signature is a mapmaker who hasn't studied hands,* he'd said once, meaning river deltas and coastlines, the way water writes its name over and over until you can copy it from memory.

She crossed it out. Too neat. A woman doesn't leave without taking her sewing box, and Ilse's box sat in the corner of the room she shared with the widow's old trunk, full of thread the color of everything.

*Departed for Kaldvik, autumn.* Better. Autumn departures explained themselves. Nobody asked questions about autumn.

Her pen hovered over Tomas. That one was almost easy, which was its own kind of shame. *Tomas Brandt, nephew of the proprietor, at sea since March aboard the merchant vessel* —she needed a ship, something plausible, something small enough not to appear on manifests anyone could check— *the* Gannet. There was a Gannet; there had been a Gannet; whether it still existed was a question she prayed no one would ask. Drowned men didn't need berths on evacuation ships. Lost-at-sea was the kindest lie available to her, and she hated herself a little for how smoothly it came.

Then Anselm.

Anselm was the hard one. He had papers—stolen, forged, bought, Noor had never asked which—but papers could be checked now in ways they couldn't before the army came. Voss's clerks would cross-reference. They'd have regimental rolls. A deserter wasn't an absence you could invent; a deserter was a presence you had to erase completely, and erasure left marks. Noor knew this the way she knew ink weights. Every deletion leaves a ghost in the paper—a margin narrower than it should be, a line weight that stutters where the writer's nerve failed.

She wrote nothing for Anselm yet. Instead she lit the candle stub, held the first draft over the flame, and watched Margret Sollen's invented journey to Kaldvik curl and blacken.

That was when she heard the floorboard on the stairs.

* * *

Ilse stood in the attic doorway with a basket of mending on her hip, watching the burning page with an expression Noor couldn't read—which was unusual, because Ilse's face was normally a public document. Three years of boarding-house dinners had taught Noor every variation of it: the tight smile that meant a soldier had followed her home again, the flat look that meant someone had mentioned Kaldvik, the bright performance of cheerfulness that meant none of the above and she simply wanted everyone to stop asking.

This expression was new. It was quiet. It frightened Noor more than the others.

"You're good at that," Ilse said.

"It has to be done."

"I know it has to be done." Ilse came into the room and set the basket down on the window seat, among the maps-in-progress, as if she belonged there, as if she hadn't spent three years making herself small in this house. "I've been downstairs listening to all of you talk around it for two days. Bess wants to tell the truth except for me. Piet wants to bribe somebody. Tomas wants to hide in a root cellar like a turnip." She looked at the candle. "Nobody asked me."

"I'm sorry. We should have—"

"No." Ilse held up a hand. "That's not—" She stopped, started again, and Noor watched her choose her words with the same care Noor gave a coastline. "I saw the name you burned. I couldn't read much of it upside down. But I saw my own name in your handwriting before that. Margret Sollen. You gave me a whole other person, just like that." She snapped her fingers, and the sound was very loud in the attic. "You gave me a sister in Kaldvik. Do I have a sister in Kaldvik?"

"No."

"Did I ever?"

"No."

"So somewhere in that ledger there's going to be a woman who doesn't exist, who has my face and my hands and a life I never lived, and she'll be the only version of me anyone official remembers." Ilse's voice stayed level. That was the worst part. Noor had expected anger; anger she could answer. This was something else. "And you decided that. Not me. You sat up here alone and decided what my absence looks like."

"The alternative is registering you," Noor said. "Registering you means the rolls. The rolls mean they trace where you came from, and where you came from has people looking for—"

"I know what it means." For the first time her voice cracked, just at the edge, and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "I've known what it means since I was sixteen years old. I don't need you to protect me from the information, Noor. I need you to let me be a person while you do it."

The candle flame leaned in the draft from the stairwell. Downstairs, faintly, Piet was singing to himself in the low way he sang when he was worried, some ferryman's song with more water in it than words. Noor looked down at the ash on the worktable—the ghost of Margret Sollen, already scattering—and felt something shift in her chest that she didn't have a name for.

"Aldous used to say," she began, and stopped, because she suddenly wasn't sure the story ended the way she'd always told it to herself.

"What did he say?"

"That maps exist so no one gets lost." She laughed, but there was no humor in it. "He believed that. Truly. He drew coastlines for forty years because he thought if the world were accurately described, nobody in it could be erased." She gestured at the ash. "And here I am, three years after he died, doing exactly the thing he built his whole life against. Erasing people. Professionally. Neatly."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. Then she sat down across the worktable from Noor, uninvited, and folded her hands.

"Maybe you're reading him wrong," she said.

"He's dead, Ilse. I'm the only one left who read him at all."

"Are you?" Ilse nodded at the atlas, closed and wrapped in oilcloth in the corner. "Because I've seen the way you work on it. You don't work like someone finishing a book. You work like someone keeping a promise. And promises aren't one-directional." Her chin lifted—that stubborn set Noor had learned to recognize, the seamstress's spine. "He promised the world accuracy. Fine. But what did the world promise him? What did you promise *him*, sitting at his table all those years? Because I don't think it was 'never bend the truth.' I think it was 'never let anyone be lost.' And right now those are two different instructions, and you picked one without asking anybody."

Noor opened her mouth. Closed it.

"Write me out of the ledger if you have to," Ilse said. "I understand why. I'll even help—I know how to make a departure look real, believe me, I've rehearsed leaving my whole life. But you'll do it with me in the room, and you'll show me the finished page, and if I tell you Margret Sollen's sister lives in a village instead of a city, then the sister lives in a village." She stood, took up her basket, and paused at the door. "You get to decide who survives, Noor. That's already terrible enough. You don't also get to decide who we are."

The door closed behind her. Noor sat alone in the attic with the ash and the atlas and the commandant's notice, and after a long time she took out a fresh sheet, and instead of writing she sat with her pen dry, thinking about a girl who had rehearsed leaving her whole life, and what it meant that she'd finally found a door worth staying behind.

* * *

By evening she had a system.

It was the kind of system Aldous would have appreciated, which was another wound: a working file of the household arranged not as people but as entries, each one assigned a fate that could survive scrutiny. She laid the drafts out on the worktable and went through them the way she proofed charts, hunting for the errors that betrayed forgery—inconsistencies of hand, of ink age, of logic.

Bess, Piet, Noor: registered true. The truth was the strongest material she had, and she meant to spend it wisely, hoarding lies for the places truth would kill.

Old Widow Harrow, deceased two winters past: restored to the record. A death that had actually happened was worth more than gold. Noor even had the burial date, roughly, and the sexton owed Bess favors.

Tomas Brandt: lost at sea aboard the Gannet, March of this year. She aged his handwriting into Bess's older letters to give the entry provenance—a correspondence, a worry, a grief already mourned on paper. When Tomas saw the draft he'd gone white and thanked her, which somehow made it worse.

Margret Sollen—no, *Ilse*, they'd agreed on that much, Ilse would keep her own first name at least inside these walls—departed for her aunt's village outside Kaldvik in October. Ilse had chosen the village herself. It had a church with bad records, she said, burned in a fire six years ago. Noor hadn't asked how she knew that.

Which left Anselm.

Noor sat with his page long after the others were done. Anselm Brenner, late of the Ninth Regiment, deserter, a man whose existence in this house was itself a capital offense against two different governments. She could not make him absent. Absence invited inquiry, and inquiry would find the worn floorboard outside his door, the second cup in the cupboard, the boots too large for anyone else in the house. She could not make him dead; deserters' deaths got verified by the army, eagerly. She could not make him registered; registration was a noose with his name already on the tag.

There was a third option. She kept circling it the way water circles a stone, refusing to land.

Voss wanted a cartographer. Voss had said so in that pleasant, patient office that smelled of lamp oil and other people's secrets: *an unfinished atlas by a master's hand is a loss to the crown, Fräulein Crane.* He'd offered coin. He'd offered protection. Protection, Noor had understood even then, was a word that cut both ways—it could mean a wall around you, or it could mean you were valuable enough to keep alive, and both meanings put you in a cage with gilded bars.

But a cage with gilded bars had room in it. Sometimes. If you were clever about the arrangement.

If she completed the atlas for him—if she walked into that office and became indispensable—then perhaps indispensability could stretch to cover a household. Perhaps a man who collected maps could be persuaded that a boarding house containing a trained apprentice was an asset worth overlooking. Perhaps Anselm could be folded into the ledger under some arrangement she couldn't yet see, some trade she hadn't yet named.

Or perhaps it was the trap. Perhaps the moment she handed Voss anything, the thoroughness she was counting on would turn on her like a tide.

Noor gathered the finished drafts, weighted their corners, and blew out the candle. Through the dormer window the harbor lay black under the occupation's new lamps, and beyond it, invisible, the blockade ships rode at anchor like punctuation at the end of a sentence she hadn't finished writing.

Downstairs, a door opened and closed quietly—later than any decent hour. Noor froze, listening. Footsteps crossed the hall, hesitated on the stairs, and stopped.

Then, soft as a confession: "Fräulein Crane? Are you awake?"

Anselm's voice. And beneath it, something she'd never heard in it before.

Fear.
