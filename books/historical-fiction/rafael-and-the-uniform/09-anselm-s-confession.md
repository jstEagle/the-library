## 9. Anselm's Confession

Anselm Brandt had learned to sleep in the army the way other men learned to drink—out of necessity, then out of habit, then out of a hunger he couldn't name. He slept shallow, one ear always open, boots within reach of his hand. Three years since he'd walked away from the 14th Rifles, and still, when a door slammed somewhere in Aunt Bess's boarding house, his body came up off the mattress with his heart already counting exits.

So when he woke in the grey hour before dawn and heard footsteps on the attic stairs above him—not the creaky third step but the careful ones that meant Noor—he was already awake.

He lay still anyway. Listening was cheaper than talking.

The house had been strange for days. He'd felt it the way you feel weather coming: Noor burning paper in the stove at midnight, the smell of ink and singed cloth; Tomas rehearsing lies under his breath while mending nets, stammering through them worse than through anything true; Bess counting coins at the kitchen table with her lips moving, doing arithmetic she didn't like. Piet hadn't sung once all week, and Piet sang even at funerals.

The army was coming. Anselm had known it before anyone said it aloud. You spent four years in a uniform, you learned what the sound of boots on harbor stones meant, the way a fisherman knows rain by the gulls going quiet.

He'd told them he was a dockworker who'd fallen out with a foreman. That was the story he'd arrived with three years ago, soaked and half-starved, and Bess had looked at him for exactly as long as it takes to decide whether a man is lying about something dangerous or just about everything else, and then she'd handed him a towel and put him to work hauling coal.

She'd never asked again. He'd never offered. That was the covenant of the house: your past was yours to carry, and everyone pretended not to see the weight of it.

But the weight was shifting now. He could feel it sliding toward all of them.

* * *

He found Piet on the pier that morning, mending a net that didn't need mending. The old man worked the mesh without looking at it, eyes on the water, where a grey transport lay at anchor past the breakwater with her decks crowded.

"More of them," Anselm said.

"Third ship this week." Piet pulled a knot tight. "They're not here to garrison. They're here to close us down."

"You've said so."

"I've said worse things that were true." Piet finally looked up at him. His eyes were pale as sea-glass and missed nothing, which was the trouble with old fishermen; they read men the way they read weather. "Bess is drawing up the ledger today. The census. Voss wants every name."

"I know."

"Does Voss's clerk know yours?"

The question sat between them like a landed fish, still kicking.

Anselm looked out at the transport. Somewhere on those grey decks were men from his own army, maybe men from his own regiment, men who'd eaten from the same field kitchens, marched the same roads. Men who would stand in a line someday and shoot prisoners against a wall because an officer said the word *procedure* and everyone nodded.

"No," he said. "Nobody official knows my name. That was the point."

"Names have a way of surfacing," Piet said. "Like bodies. When the water gets rough enough." He went back to his netting. "I'm not telling you to run, boy. I'm telling you the tide's turning, and you should decide what you're carrying before it does."

* * *

He decided at two in the morning, which was when he made most of his decisions—the honest ones, the expensive ones.

He came downstairs barefoot because he didn't want to wake the whole house with his boots, and found the kitchen lamp already lit. Noor sat at the table with papers spread around her like a card game, and there was ink on her fingers and something wild and tired in her face that he recognized, because he'd worn it himself in another life, on nights before operations that couldn't be spoken of.

They looked at each other across the lamplight. Two liars, off duty.

"Couldn't sleep either," she said.

"I've been sleeping fine," he lied. "It's the mornings I don't trust anymore."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her. She gathered some of the papers away from him—too slowly, he noticed, too deliberately—and he let her, because he wasn't here for her secrets. He was here because of his own.

He sat down across from her and put both hands flat on the table, the way you do when you're trying to hold yourself still.

"Noor. I need to tell you why I came here. All of it. Before anyone asks me questions I have to lie through, I need someone in this house to know the truth."

Her hands stopped moving over the papers.

"Bess took me in without asking," he said. "Tomas doesn't talk much and Ilse flinches at uniforms and nobody's ever pressed me either. I love this house for that. But it's not just mercy, is it? It's also that everybody here has decided their silence is load-bearing. Take one story out and maybe the roof comes down on everyone." He swallowed. "Mine's the worst one. And if Voss's people start pulling threads—and they will, men like that always pull—I'd rather you hear it from me than from a sergeant with a list."

"Then say it," Noor said quietly. "Or don't. It's your choice, Anselm. It's the only thing left that is."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he reached into his shirt and drew out the oilcloth packet he'd carried against his chest for three years, through checkpoints and searches and one very long night in a drainage ditch outside Harrow Fen, and laid it on the table between them.

Noor didn't touch it.

"In the autumn of the second year of the war," Anselm said, "my regiment took a village called Kesselbruck. There were irregulars there—farmers with old guns, mostly. We lost four men taking it, and our captain lost his temper, or found a use for losing it. Twenty-two prisoners. Boys, some of them. One was missing an arm from before the war." He kept his voice level. He'd practiced this, though he'd only ever practiced it alone, saying it to the dark. "They were shot in the mill yard. Not in battle. Not in escape. Standing up, blindfolded, in rows. I was on picket duty a hundred meters away. I heard all of it."

Noor's face had gone very still.

"Afterward, Captain Ehrenberg wrote his report—twenty-two rebels killed attempting escape, standard language, everyone signed. But there was a lieutenant, a man named Corvus, who'd written letters to his brother the whole campaign. Corvus was sick about Kesselbruck. Sick in a way that makes men stupid. He gave me a letter to smuggle to a newspaper contact in the capital—a full account, names, dates, orders given in writing. He couldn't post it himself; officers' mail was read. So he gave it to a private he trusted, and that private was me." Anselm's mouth tasted like ash. "And I sat with that letter in my pocket for six weeks, and I told myself I was waiting for the right moment, and then the regiment moved north and the moment kept not arriving, and then one night I understood I was never going to deliver it, because delivering it meant admitting what I'd done nothing about. So instead I deserted. With the letter. Which means I didn't free it and I didn't destroy it. I just stole it and ran."

"What happened to Corvus?"

"Died at Veldt Crossing the following spring. Shrapnel. His brother never got anything." Anselm turned the oilcloth packet over once with one finger. "Ehrenberg commands a battalion now. Under Voss. Corvus's old company commander is Voss's provost marshal. If the census goes wrong, if any thread gets pulled hard enough, they won't find a dockworker with a grudge. They'll find Private Anselm Brandt, deserter, last seen carrying correspondence that could hang three officers." He met her eyes. "I didn't run here to hide, whatever I told Bess. I ran here because it was the direction away from what I'd failed to do. And now the war's come to sit on your doorstep, and I'm sitting in your kitchen with the proof in my shirt, and I'm asking myself the same question I've been asking for three years: what am I waiting for?"

Noor was silent a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs, Tomas turned over in bed, and the floorboards spoke.

"Aldous used to say," she said finally, "that a map exists so no one gets lost. That was the whole job. Not territory, not ownership. Just—so no one gets lost." She looked at the packet. "You've been carrying a map of something, Anselm. For three years. And you're the only one who can't read where it leads."

"It leads to a firing squad," Anselm said. "Mine, possibly. Or theirs. Depends who holds it."

"That's not what it leads to. That's just where it stops."

* * *

Bess came down at first light, as she always did, and stopped in the kitchen doorway with her shawl half-fastened, reading the room the way she read every room: Noor's face, Anselm's face, the oilcloth packet sitting on the table like a guest no one had invited.

"Well," she said.

Anselm stood up. His legs felt like borrowed legs.

"Mrs. Bess. I owe you a confession and an apology, and probably rent back-pay for the version of me you thought you were housing."

"Sit down, boy, you look like a fencepost." She poured herself tea without offering any, which meant she was frightened. Bess offered tea when she was calm. "How bad?"

He told her. All of it, faster this time, the words worn smooth from three years of rehearsal in the dark. Bess listened the way she did everything—thoroughly, without flinching until the exact moment she flinched, which was at the twenty-two prisoners, not at the deserter part. He watched her understand, in real time, that she'd been feeding and sheltering a witness to a massacre for three years, and that the soldiers massing in her town answered to the officers named in the letter.

When he finished, the kitchen was very quiet.

"And you've kept this on you," Bess said, "the whole time. Against your chest."

"It seemed safer than anywhere else."

"Nothing is safer than anywhere else, that's the whole discovery of being old." She set down her cup harder than she needed to. "Those names. Ehrenberg. The provost marshal. They're here, or coming here, under Voss. And you're telling me you want to stay. In a town the army is closing like a fist. To fight your own private war with a piece of paper."

"Not a piece of paper. Proof. Written orders, Anselm—Corvus quoted them. If that letter reached the right tribunal—"

"There are no right tribunals," Bess said. "There's the army judging the army, and there's the grave. I've buried enough to know the difference."

"Then what do we do?" His voice cracked and he hated it. "Just sail away? Let them close the port and burn the records and pretend Kesselbruck never happened? I stood a hundred meters away, Mrs. Bess. A hundred meters. I heard it. Every morning I hear it. Staying alive isn't the same as staying silent, and I've been doing both, and I can't—" He stopped. Breathed. "You took me in. All of you. People this house will never know how much I—"

"Don't." Bess held up a hand. "Don't you dare turn gratitude into permission. That's how good people get talked into dying stupidly." She looked at Noor, and something passed between the two women that Anselm couldn't follow—some older argument about debts and dead men. Then back to him. "If that letter surfaces, it doesn't surface alone. It surfaces with your name attached, and your name attaches to this address, and this address attaches to ten other people who trusted you with their lives by never asking your business. Do you understand what I'm saying?"

"Yes."

"Do you care?"

"Yes." And he did, God help him, he cared so much it felt like drowning. "But I care about the twenty-two too. They don't get a household. They don't get a berth on the last ship. Nobody's counting heads for them, Bess. Somebody has to be."

* * *

By noon the whole house knew, because Noor had made the decision that the household survived on shared truth or died on convenient silence, and she'd chosen truth. Ilse stood by the stove with her arms wrapped around herself, looking at Anselm like she was recalculating everything she'd ever felt safe about. Tomas sat very straight, hands flat on his knees, and said, in the longest sentence anyone had ever heard from him:

"If—if they take you for questioning, they'll take whoever answers the door. That's how it works. That's how it always works."

"So I should go," Anselm said. "That's the vote, isn't it. Everyone's thinking it."

Nobody said yes. Nobody said no, either, and the silence was worse.

It was Piet, leaning in the doorway smelling of tar and low tide, who broke it.

"The boy's not wrong that he's a fuse," Piet said. "And he's not wrong that some fuses are worth lighting. Both things are true, and the trick is figuring out which one blows up your house." He looked at Anselm. "What is it you actually want to do with that letter? Not what's noble. What's possible."

Anselm had been turning this over since two in the morning, and the answer had come to him ugly and clear, the way real answers do.

"There are resistance contacts in the fishing fleet. Piet, you know it, don't pretend you don't—I've seen the signals on your boat. I want to give them the letter. Names, dates, orders. Let it become ammunition in hands that know how to fire it. And if they'll take it, I'll go with it. Out through the blockade, wherever they're sending things these days. I'll testify. I'll spend the rest of my life being a witness if that's what it costs." He spread his hands. "That's the offer. The letter leaves this house, and so do I, and none of you ever have to be in a room with my name again."

Silence again. But a different silence—Ilse's shoulders had come down half an inch, and Tomas was nodding, small quick nods, and Bess was staring into her cold tea with an expression Anselm slowly recognized as grief.

"You sound like Aldous," Bess said at last. "He used to make offers like that. Everything paid forward, nothing kept for himself. Look where it got him." She stood, abruptly, joints cracking. "We'll talk about routes after dark. Piet, you know who to signal. And Anselm—" she paused in the doorway, not turning around, "—for what it's worth, I'm glad the man I fed for three years turned out to be the one in your story who was ashamed. Ashamed men can be trusted. Content ones can't."

She left. Piet pushed off the doorframe and followed. Ilse touched Anselm's arm on her way past—two fingers, brief, the most she'd touched anyone since he'd known her—and went upstairs.

Which left Noor, still at the table, still with ink on her fingers, looking at the oilcloth packet where it lay between them.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When you hand that letter over—when it stops being yours—you tell me first what you're trading. All of it. Because I've learned something lately about what happens when people trade pieces of other people's lives without showing their work." Her jaw tightened. "And Anselm. Whatever you promised them. Whatever they ask for in return." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and afraid in equal measure. "Make sure you know what it costs before you sign anything. Because in this house, we've started learning that every bargain has a price tag somebody else ends up paying."

Anselm picked up the letter and slid it back against his chest, where it had lived for three years, warm as a second heartbeat.

He had no idea, yet, how right she was.
