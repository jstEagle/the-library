## 10. The Berth List

Piet had read harbors for fifty years the way other men read faces, and the *Vrouw Anke* was telling him everything before her captain said a word.

She sat low in the water at the customs berth, too low for a coastal trader running light. Her hull paint was fresh above the waterline and scabbed with salt below it — a ship that had been somewhere she wasn't built to go. Her crew moved along the deck without talking. Piet had seen crews move like that twice in his life: once before a storm, once after a war.

He came alongside in his ferry skiff, shipped his oars, and knocked on the master's cabin door the way he'd been doing since before the commandant's grey coats arrived — casual, unhurried, an old man paying a social call.

Captain Verhagen opened the door himself. That was answer number two. Masters who still had cargo contracts let their mates answer doors.

"Piet." The captain's face did something complicated. "You'd better come in."

The cabin smelled of coffee gone cold and lamp oil. On the desk lay a paper Piet recognized by its weight even folded — requisition orders, stamped with Voss's office seal. He sat down on the locker because his knees had opinions about standing.

"Say it plain," Piet said.

Verhagen poured coffee anyway, out of habit or kindness, and pushed the cup across. "Last civilian sailing. The navy takes the port after that — full military traffic, civilian berths closed indefinitely. My owners got the notice yesterday."

"Indefinitely." Piet tasted the word like bad water. "That's army talk for never."

"That's army talk for never." Verhagen drank his own coffee standing up, looking out the porthole at the gray water. "I can take passengers. Seven of them. Seven, Piet, not one more — my owners have already sold six berths to families in Havnvik, and the naval office will count heads at the gangway personally. Seven is what I can swear to on my manifest. Eight becomes smuggling, and smuggling gets my ship confiscated and my papers pulled."

"And the price?"

Verhagen looked at him then, and Piet saw the man was ashamed, which meant the number was ugly. "Everything you've been saving. I know about the tin under your floor. Half the harbor knows about the tin under your floor, so don't look at me like that. It's war pricing. It's robbery. I'd apologize, but apologizing doesn't fill my coal bunkers, and if I don't leave with full bunkers I don't leave at all."

Piet turned the cup in his hands. Fifty years of hauling people back and forth across a harbor no wider than a river, and now the arithmetic had come for him too. He thought of Bess's kitchen table, scarred by three generations of elbows. He thought of the tin under the floorboards, which held eleven years of fares and small kindnesses and Ilse's sewing money folded into paper packets marked in her careful hand.

"How long do I have to decide?"

"Decide? You have until I sail. Nine days. But the berths—" Verhagen spread his hands. "Seven berths in a town where everyone suddenly remembers they have cousins down the coast. If you want them, you pay today. Someone else pays tonight."

* * *

Bess set the tin on the kitchen table and didn't open it right away. She just stood there with her hand flat on the lid, the way she stood over sleeping children sometimes, checking the breath of them.

"Count it," Piet said.

"I know how much is in it."

"Count it anyway. Out loud. So we all hear the same number and nobody spends the evening pretending it's bigger than it is."

So she counted it. Eleven years of the boarding house, coin by coin, packet by packet: Piet's ferry takings, Tomas's wages from the chandler's before the chandler's closed, Ilse's sewing money, Noor's map commissions from merchants who paid cash and asked no questions. Bess's voice stayed level through all of it, the voice she used for reading out debts at month's end, and when she finished, the silence afterward was the loudest thing the room had ever held.

"It's enough for seven," she said. "It's exactly enough for seven."

"Then we're four short," Tomas said.

They were all there around the table — Bess, Piet, Noor, Tomas, Ilse, old Marta from the attic room, and Anselm sitting near the door where he always sat, close to exits. The others were upstairs or at work, but everyone knew this conversation would be repeated to them within the hour, because a house like theirs had no walls in it, only agreements.

"Four short," Bess repeated. "So we decide."

"No," Noor said.

Everyone looked at her. She was standing at the window with her arms crossed, and Piet realized she'd been at the window the whole time, watching the street instead of the table, refusing the arithmetic with her whole body.

"We're not deciding anything," she said. "We're not drawing lots, we're not ranking ourselves, we're not—"

"Noor." Bess's voice was gentle and iron underneath. "Sit down."

"No." She turned from the window, and Piet saw her eyes were bright and furious. "You want to sit here and count people like coins, fine, but I won't help. There are nine days. Things change in nine days. Ships get found, berths open up, the blockade—"

"The blockade tightens," Piet said quietly. "I read the harbor this morning. They brought in two more patrol boats. Whatever changes in nine days, child, it won't change toward mercy."

Noor sat down. She did it slowly, like someone surrendering a position, and Piet understood that was exactly what it was.

* * *

The argument went the way arguments go in families — which is to say sideways, and in circles, and through territory nobody wanted to cross.

Marta said she was sixty-eight years old and had buried a husband and would not take a berth from a young person, and if anyone put her name on that list they'd have to carry her aboard tied hand and foot, which would use up a berth anyway, so really the mathematics favored leaving her.

Tomas said Marta was being ridiculous, and Marta told him not to raise his voice at his elders, and Tomas said he wasn't raising his voice, and Ilse said he absolutely was, and for a moment the room was just a room again, ordinary and bickering and safe, and Piet wished it could stay that way forever.

Ilse herself said nothing for a long time. When she finally spoke, it was quiet and terrible. "My name shouldn't count. I'm not on any ledger. Officially I don't exist, which means officially I can't sail either — they'll check papers at the gangway, won't they? They'll count us against something."

The silence that followed was the sound of everyone realizing the same thing at once. Seven berths, and at least two of their names existed nowhere except in forged ink and each other's memories. Papers could be made — Noor's hand could make anything — but papers were exactly the kind of thread Voss pulled at.

Anselm shifted near the door. "I'll stay."

"You'll do no such thing," Bess said.

"Bess." His voice was steady. Piet had learned to read soldiers the way he read harbors, and everything in this man's posture said he had already decided this weeks ago and was only now informing them. "If they catch me with papers, they don't just hang me. They trace me. To this house, to all of you. Every person I pull onto that ship makes the rest of you heavier. I'm dead weight with a price on it. I stay, and I take my chances, and maybe I settle my own accounts while I'm at it."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer it with. Piet watched Noor's hands close into fists on the table and watched her open them again, deliberately, finger by finger, like a woman practicing patience the way other people practice scales.

And then — because someone had to say it, and because Piet was too old and too tired to leave the hard thing unsaid — he said the other part.

"There's one more name we're all dancing around."

"Noor—" Tomas started.

"I didn't say her name."

"You didn't have to." Piet kept his voice level, an old man's voice, weather-report voice. "She finished the atlas. Three years in an attic. She's the one Voss wants, the one Voss will keep wanting, and the one who can read whatever Aldous left in those margins better than any of us. If anyone in this house has work left to do in Vellsmark, it's her. And if anyone in this house can find another way off this coast after we're gone — a fishing boat, a cove, some route in that atlas we haven't thought of — it's her."

"I know," Noor said.

Her voice was calm. That was the worst part. Piet had expected fury and got calm instead, and calm from Noor Crane was more frightening than shouting from anyone else in the world.

"So you're saying I should stay," she said. "None of you will say it outright, so let me say it for you. Noor stays. Noor volunteers. Noor, who has spent three years keeping every single one of you alive and invisible, gets to be the last name on the list, the leftover, the remainder. And you'll all feel noble about it because you're sacrificing her instead of yourselves, which is the easiest sacrifice there is — spending someone else."

"That's not what anyone is doing," Bess said.

"Isn't it?" Noor looked around the table. "Tomas, tell me you weren't thinking it. Marta. Piet — you said it yourself, five minutes ago, dressed up in harbor-reading and practicality. Everyone here has spent this entire evening arranging themselves around the shape of my staying, and calling it arithmetic."

Piet opened his mouth and found nothing in it. Because she was right, and worse than right — she was right in the way that made everyone's love look like cowardice.

Ilse reached across the table and took Noor's hand. "Then change the arithmetic," she said simply. "You're the clever one. Find us eleven berths, or find us a boat, or find us a way to make seven mean eleven. That's your job now. Not deciding who goes — deciding how we all do."

Noor looked down at their joined hands for a long moment.

"Nine days," she said finally. "Give me nine days."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up, Piet found Noor in the hall, standing with her coat already on, a lantern in her hand.

"Where are you going at this hour?"

"To the atlas." She pulled the door open. Cold harbor air came in with the smell of tar and salt. "There are things in it I haven't read properly yet. Margins, symbols, routes — Aldous drew things I never understood, and I think I'm done not understanding them."

Piet nodded slowly. "And if you find what you're looking for?"

She paused on the step, half in the lamplight and half out of it, and for a moment she looked so much like Aldous Crane that Piet's chest hurt — the same stubborn set of the shoulders, the same look of a person staring at a coastline only they could see.

"Then nobody stays behind," she said. "Not one. Not even me."

The door closed behind her, and Piet stood alone in the hall listening to her footsteps fade toward the attic stairs, and above him, faint through the ceiling boards, he heard the scratch of a pen beginning to move.
