## 11. Marginalia Decoded

The attic held three lamps and I lit all of them.

It was wasteful—oil cost money we didn't have, and the light leaking under the eaves could be seen from the street if a man stood in the right place on the cobbles and looked up. But tonight I wanted to see everything. Tonight I was going to read my master's letters the way I should have read them years ago: not as an apprentice reading for instruction, but as a woman reading for her life.

I spread the atlas open across the worktable, weighted at the corners with the brass ink pots, and I laid Aldous's letters beside it in chronological order, oldest first, because that was how he had taught me to solve any problem in draftsmanship. *Start with what the mapmaker knew when he began,* he used to say. *Everything after that is correction.*

There were forty-one letters. I knew this without counting because I had counted them a hundred times over three years, usually at night, usually when the house was asleep and the silence got loud enough that I needed something to hold. They were unremarkable letters. That had always been the trouble with them. A master writing to his absent apprentice ought to have written about technique, about commissions, about the state of his health—and he did write about those things, dutifully, page after page of them, in a hand so steady it never wavered even in the last year when I knew—I knew—his eyesight was failing.

Steady hands in failing eyes. I had thought it was pride.

I smoothed the earliest letter flat and read it again, but this time I wasn't reading words. I was reading placement.

*The herring run early this year. Old Piet says the shoals came in past the second marker buoy, which surprises no one who has studied the eastern shelf.* 

Ordinary. Piet said things like that constantly; half of it was fisherman's superstition dressed up as science. But look where the sentence sat on the page—not centered, not flowing with the paragraph, but tucked against the left margin, alone, two lines below the rest of the text. And look at the word *buoy*. In the atlas, on plate fourteen, the chart of the eastern approaches, there was a marker buoy drawn just off the shelf line, and beside the buoy—

A symbol. One of the symbols. The little hooked cross that Tomas had asked me about, the ones I'd told him were decorative flourishes, the way masters decorate, the way old men doodle.

My mouth went dry.

I pulled plate fourteen closer and found the hooked cross. Then I went back to the letter and counted. The sentence about the buoy was the third marginal line in the letter. On the plate, counting from the compass rose clockwise, the hooked cross was the third mark.

I did not let myself believe it yet. Belief comes too easy at night, in lamplight, when you want an answer badly enough. Aldous himself had warned me: *An eager cartographer sees coastlines in cloud shadows. Check your work twice, then have someone else check it once.*

But there was no one else. Not tonight. So I checked it four times myself.

Letter seven. Marginal line: *Ilse's mother sends regards from the mill road; she says the new wheel turns sweeter than the old.* Third marginal line again, and the phrase *mill road* sitting oddly in a letter written before Ilse had ever come to Vellsmark—a name used like a key left in a lock for someone who hadn't arrived yet. I turned to plate nine, the inland survey, and followed the mill road east with my finger until it reached its junction with the drovers' track. There: a symbol. Two parallel strokes with a dot between them, like a door seen edge-on.

A door. A safehouse marked as a door.

My hands were shaking by the time I reached letter twelve, and I had to stop and press them flat against the table and breathe, because the shape of it was assembling itself in front of me and the shape of it was enormous. Every odd sentence in forty-one letters—the fishing reports, the weather notes, the little domestic fragments about neighbors and repairs and the price of tar—every one of them sat in the margins, numbered by position, and every number corresponded to a symbol on a plate, and every symbol sat on the map beside something real: a cove, a cellar entrance, a stretch of shingle, a well.

He hadn't been writing to me. Or rather—he had been writing to me, but through the writing he had been leaving instructions, and the atlas was the cipher key, and I was the only person alive who had both halves.

*Check your work twice,* I heard him say, dry as chalk dust. *Then sit down and consider what you've found, girl, because maps are never only maps.*

* * *

Tomas brought tea up around midnight without being asked. He set the cup down at the edge of the table, away from the papers—he had learned that much in three years of sharing this attic with me—and stood there a moment, looking at the spread of plates and letters with the expression of a man watching weather change.

"You've gone pale," he said. "Or pink. It's hard to tell with the lamps."

"I've found something."

"Is it bad?"

I almost laughed. "It depends entirely on what happens next."

He nodded slowly, the way he did when he understood less than he wanted to admit but more than he let on. Tomas was like that—his stammer made people underestimate him, and he had stopped correcting them years ago because being underestimated kept a person safe. He of all people knew the value of that.

"The symbols," he said. "The ones I asked about. You said they were decoration."

"They're routes." The word came out of me sounding stranger than I expected, lower, almost reverent. "Safehouses. Landing points. Tide windows." I turned plate eleven toward him, the western shore, and put my finger on the cluster of marks near Gullskar Point. "This one here—two strokes and a dot—is a door. A building you can enter and be hidden. This hooked cross is water passage, a channel deep enough for a small boat at certain states of the tide. And these—" the small circles with tails, scattered along the coast like seeds, "—these are people. Contacts. Each circle is someone Aldous trusted."

Tomas leaned close. His breath fogged faintly in the cold attic air. "How many?"

I had already counted. Twice. "Thirty-one circles. Nine doors. Six channels." I paused. "One cove."

"Cove?"

"Here." Plate sixteen. The southern shore, past the salt marshes where the charts officially showed nothing but mudflats and a wreck hazard. Aldous had drawn the marshes faithfully, correctly, every bank and channel true—but threaded among the true channels was a false one, or rather an unlabeled one, running south behind the barrier spit into a pocket of water no official chart acknowledged. And beside it, drawn small and precise as a signature: a hooked cross inside a circle. Water passage through a door.

"A boat could slip out there," Tomas said slowly. He wasn't from the sea, but three years in Piet's orbit teaches anyone to read water. "Past a blockade, if there ever was one."

"If there ever was one," I echoed.

Neither of us said what we both meant: that there was one now, and tightening daily, and that grey-coated patrols rowed the harbor mouth every night with lanterns hooded.

Tomas straightened up. "Aldous built this," he said. "For fugitives."

"For people who needed to disappear," I said. "Which is the same thing."

He looked at me then, really looked, and whatever he saw in my face made him reach over and squeeze my shoulder once, hard, before he took the empty teacup back downstairs. He didn't say anything else. There wasn't anything else to say yet.

* * *

I worked until the lamps guttered low, and what assembled itself in the blue hour before dawn was a portrait of a man I had lived with for six years and apparently never met.

Master Aldous Crane, cartographer, had spent the last decade of his life mapping two countries at once. The first was the official one—the commissioned coastlines, the harbor surveys, the estate plans, the work that paid for ink and bread and my apprenticeship. The second was drawn only in margins and only in code: a network of thirty-one people, nine shelters, six tidal passages, threaded along eighty miles of coast like stitches holding a wound closed.

The letters told me how it worked, once I learned to read them properly. The marginal sentences weren't random—they were updates. *The herring came in past the second buoy* meant the eastern channel had shifted, that the safe approach had moved, that the hooked cross on plate fourteen needed adjusting. *The new wheel turns sweeter than the old* meant the mill road safehouse had changed hands, changed keepers, was still good. When a contact died, the circle on the map got a small stroke through it—hollowed out, retired, mourned in a language of one line. There were four hollowed circles. Four people, over ten years, who had carried strangers along this coast and paid whatever price carriers sometimes pay.

And the tide windows—God, the tide windows. Aldous had recorded them not as tables but as verse, little rhymed couplets tucked into letters about weather:

*When the moon rides low and the wind sits west,*
*the southern door opens an hour at best.*

I read that couplet six times before I made myself turn to the almanac pages Piet kept nailed up in the ferry shed and confirmed it: a window of roughly one hour, certain nights each month, when the channel behind the southern spit ran deep enough for a loaded boat and shallow enough everywhere else that a patrol vessel drawing real water couldn't follow.

He had timed escape routes by moonlight and rhyme. He had hidden a railroad for the hunted inside the polite correspondence of an aging craftsman. And he had sent the key—the atlas—to his apprentice, and died before he could explain a single word of it.

Why? I sat with that question while the sky outside went from black to iron. Why leave it coded? Why trust me with a puzzle instead of a confession?

Because he couldn't know when I would need it. Because a plain explanation could be tortured or stolen or simply read over the wrong shoulder, but a code split between two documents—one in the attic, one in a bundle of letters—was safe unless both halves fell into the same hands. Because he had been betrayed once already—I remembered Bess's tight mouth whenever I asked about the illness, the year she refused to discuss—and betrayal teaches a man to write in invisible ink.

And because, some quieter voice suggested, he wanted me to find it this way. Wanted me to earn it. An inheritance handed over is a gift; an inheritance decoded is a commission.

I am his heir. Not of paper. Of purpose.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the dormer window, and with it came the sound of the town waking under occupation: boots on the quay, the bugle call for the garrison's muster, the groan of the requisitioned warehouse doors. Ten days since Voss's notice had gone up. Eight days left to register the household. Seven berths on the last ship, for eleven souls. And somewhere in the commandant's offices, a man who collected maps was waiting to learn whether the dead master's atlas could be made useful to uniforms.

I gathered the letters back into their oilcloth wrap, but I kept one out—the last one, dated nineteen days before his death. I had always read it as a farewell. Now I read it again and saw the margin held two lines, the final marginal sentences he ever wrote me:

*The work is finished enough. What remains is only finishing.*
*Mind the southern door, child. It opens wider than you think.*

Finished enough. What remains is only finishing.

He had known. Sitting in this house with his eyes going and his heart failing—or whatever it was that was failing, whatever the truth of his death was—he had known the atlas would pass through hands that might want it for other purposes, and he had left me a last instruction folded inside a goodbye. Mind the southern door. Protect the cove. Whatever else happens, whatever bargain gets made, the route itself must survive.

I wrapped the letter with the others and tied the cord, and my hands were steady now, steadier than they'd been all night. Below me the boarding house was stirring—Bess raking the stove, Piet's cough, the thump of Tomas hauling water. Eleven people. Eight days. One coded coastline that could carry us all out through the mudflats if I could decode the rest of the tide verses in time, and one commander across town whose patience, I suspected, was nearly as short as his ledger.

I pinched out the lamps and stood a moment in the grey light, one hand resting on the atlas cover, feeling under my palm the faint ridges of the boards, the weight of everything Aldous had trusted to paper.

Then Bess called my name up the stairwell—sharp, urgent, the voice she saved for soldiers at the door—and the whole night's revelation compressed itself into a single cold beat behind my ribs.

Someone was knocking below.
