## 12. Inspection Day

Ilse heard the boots before anyone else did. Three years of running had tuned her ears to the particular sound of soldiers on cobblestones—not the loose, drunken stagger of men off duty, but the metronome tread of men with orders. She was pinning a hem in the front room when the rhythm stopped outside Aunt Bess's gate, and her needle froze mid-stitch.

"Noor," she said, too quietly. Then again, louder. "Noor. Boots."

Noor came down the stairs two at a time, pencil still tucked behind her ear, ink on her fingers from the morning's ledger work. She looked out the window without touching the curtain—Ilse had learned that trick from her, never touch the curtain—and went very still.

"Four of them. And the commandant."

Bess appeared from the kitchen wiping flour on her apron as if flour could make a house look innocent. Piet came in from the yard, and behind him Tomas, whose face had already gone the color of candle wax. Anselm was up at the tannery road cutting hides for Piet's cousin, which meant he was safe, or at least not here to be found.

"Everyone breathe," Bess said, and her voice was the voice she used when the roof leaked or the chimney smoked—the voice of a woman who had outlasted two wars by refusing to be surprised by them. "We are what we have always been. A widow, her niece, her nephew home from the sea, an old ferryman who lodges in the loft, and a boy who does odd work."

"And me?" Ilse said.

Noor looked at her, and for half a heartbeat something moved behind her eyes—something quick and terrible, the arithmetic of one bed too many done at speed. "The dumbwaiter," Noor said. "Now. Take your sewing basket. If they open the panel, you're mending for the household and you came down late."

"The shaft goes all the way to the kitchen—"

"It doesn't matter where it goes. It matters that you're not in a bed." Noor caught her wrist, brief and hard. "Ilse. *Now.*"

There was no time to argue and no argument worth having. Ilse took the stairs three at a time, grabbed the sewing basket from under her mattress, and slipped into the back bedroom just as the knocker sounded below—three heavy strikes, unhurried, the knock of a man who had never once needed to hurry.

The dumbwaiter panel was set into the wall beside the linen press, painted the same grey-green as the wainscoting, and it opened on a wooden shaft barely wider than her shoulders. She stepped in, pulled the panel shut until only a finger's width of light remained, and stood on the frame of the little car three floors down, her back against the wall, her breath going shallow and loud in her own ears.

Through the gap she could hear the front door open. Bess's voice, warm and unhurried: "Commandant Voss. What an honor. We don't get many officers calling."

"I regret the informality of the visit." Voss's voice was exactly as Ilse remembered it from the day he'd posted the requisition notices at the harbor—dry, precise, a voice that read documents aloud even when speaking. "A routine verification of the census ledger. I find routine is best conducted unannounced. Routine households have nothing to rearrange."

*And we,* Ilse thought, her heart slamming, *rearranged everything three days ago.*

Boots on the floorboards. Four sets plus his, moving through the hall. She could track them the way she'd once tracked her father through the house in Brennenstadt, before the letter came, before the uniform came for him, before she'd learned that the sound of boots in a hallway was the sound of your life dividing into before and after.

* * *

They started upstairs. Of course they started upstairs. Ilse pressed her forehead against the panel seam and watched slices of hallway light slide past as her eyes adjusted to the dark.

She heard Noor come up after them—hearing Noor was easy; she moved like water, like someone who had spent three years practicing being unheard. "Commandant. The rooms are small, I'm afraid. My aunt takes in lodgers to keep the house."

"So the ledger says." Paper rustled. "One widow, proprietor. One niece, seamstress. One nephew, merchant sailor, currently at sea. One ferryman, retired. One apprentice cartographer—you. Five names, five persons." A pause long enough to fall into. "Yet the tax rolls from three years ago list six rooms let."

"My brother held the house before my aunt," Noor said smoothly. "He kept more lodgers. Times being what they were, we've had fewer applicants."

"Mm," said Voss.

Ilse had heard that particular syllable from tax assessors and harbor police and once, terribly, from a woman in a railway office who held Ilse's forged papers up to the light for eleven seconds. *Mm* was the sound a careful man made when he was deciding whether to believe you, and it was worse than any question because it gave you nothing to answer.

Boots crossed the landing. A door opened—Tomas's room.

"This is my nephew's," Bess said. "At sea since spring, poor lamb. He keeps it tidy for when he returns."

"He keeps it very tidy for a sailor," Voss said.

Ilse closed her eyes. Tomas had made the bed with hospital corners because Noor had drilled him on hospital corners, and now the perfection of it hung in the air like a smell. She heard one of the soldiers run a finger along the windowsill and report, in a bored voice, that there was no dust. Dust was a problem. They had dusted. Of course they'd dusted—they'd spent three days making the house look lived-in by exactly five people, and every surface they'd cleaned testified to the cleaning.

"There's been illness in the house this summer," Noor said. "I kept the rooms aired. Old habit from my master—he said bad air killed more mapmakers than bad weather."

"Crane," Voss said. "The cartographer. You were his apprentice."

"For three years, yes."

"And you finished his atlas."

Another pause. This one had teeth in it. Everyone in Vellsmark knew the army wanted the atlas; everyone knew Voss collected maps the way other men collected medals. Ilse gripped the sewing basket until the wicker bit her palms.

"That's between me and my aunt," Noor said. "It's a private commission. Master Crane's family engaged me to complete it for them."

"His family is in the capital."

"His *sister's* family," Noor said, without a flicker, and Ilse marveled at her even as terror squeezed her throat shut. Noor had invented that sister six months ago for the assessor's visit, and here she was, alive and useful, a whole fictional woman holding up half their lies.

The door closed. Boots moved on—to Piet's loft above the kitchen, then down the back stairs, and Ilse flattened herself as the shaft shivered with footsteps passing within arm's reach of her hiding place. Through the seam she saw a slice of grey coat, a buckle, a hand resting briefly on the dumbwaiter panel itself.

She stopped breathing.

The hand tapped the wood once—a man testing whether a thing was solid—and moved away.

* * *

They gathered in the kitchen. Ilse heard it all through the floor beneath her feet, every word rising up the shaft as if the house itself were repeating it to her.

"Coffee, Commandant? We've real beans, traded off a fishing boat—"

"Thank you, no." A chair scraped; he sat. "Your ledger, Miss Crane, matches your household. That is satisfactory."

"Then we're grateful for your thoroughness," Bess said.

"Do not thank me yet." Papers again. He carried their forged page with him, Ilse realized; he had brought their lie in his own satchel and was comparing it against the house in real time. "Five persons registered. Yet I count certain discrepancies that may be nothing. I will enumerate them, so you may correct my errors."

Silence. Ilse could feel the whole household holding its breath through the floorboards.

"One. In the room described as the nephew's, the bed has been slept in recently. The pillow retains an impression. Sailors at sea do not leave pillow impressions."

"Tomas slept here the night before he shipped out—" Bess began.

"Two." He rode over her without raising his voice. "The floorboard outside the third bedroom is worn in a pattern consistent with regular foot traffic over years. Your ledger says the room has been vacant except for occasional lodgers. Occasional lodgers do not wear floorboards. Three. In your cupboard I observed nine cups. A household of five might reasonably own nine cups—people accumulate crockery. But four of those cups match, in a set of six, and matched sets are bought new, together, by households expecting to grow. Or households larger than they admit." He paused. "Four. There is a second toothbrush cup in the washroom, chipped, and a hair ribbon on the laundry line that is neither your niece's nor yours, Madam—the colors are wrong for both women I have seen."

Ilse's legs were shaking. She locked her knees. The ribbon. Her blue ribbon, her one vanity, drying on the line like a confession.

"These are small things," Voss continued, and his voice did something strange—it softened, almost kindly. "Each one explainable. Together they suggest a household of perhaps six. Or seven. I am aware that in wartime, people shelter relatives. Cousins. Friends' daughters. I am not a monster, Madam Bess. But my orders require accuracy, and my superiors read numbers, not explanations. If there is a sixth person under this roof, she is safer registered than hidden. Registered persons eat. Hidden persons disappear."

"We have told you the truth," Bess said, and her voice cracked just enough to be human, just enough to be true.

"Yes," Voss said. "You have told me a truth. Whether it is *the* truth—" A chair pushed back. "I will return. Not announced, next time either. I would advise no changes be made to the house. Houses remember, Miss Crane. Floorboards especially. It is why I like old buildings; they keep better records than ledgers."

Boots toward the hall. Then they stopped, and Ilse's heart stopped with them.

"One more thing." His voice came from directly below her, in the kitchen doorway. "This dumbwaiter. Where does it run?"

"To the cellar," Noor said. "It hasn't worked in years. The rope's rotten."

"Rotten rope," Voss repeated, in the tone of a man writing something down. "Everything in this town is either broken or at sea or dead. Convenient, the state of Vellsmark." A beat. "Good day, ladies. Ferryman."

Piet grunted. The front door opened, let in a knife of street noise, and closed. The boots receded down the cobbles in their terrible metronome time, four pairs and one, fading toward the harbor.

* * *

Ilse stayed in the shaft until hands knocked on the panel—Noor's knuckles, the soft double tap they'd agreed on months ago for exactly this. Even so, Ilse made her repeat it twice before she let the panel swing open and folded out into the back stairwell on legs that barely belonged to her.

Noor caught her elbow. Up close, Ilse could see the cost written on her: the shine of sweat at her hairline, the pulse jumping in her throat. Perfect performance, and underneath it, terror same as hers.

"You heard," Noor said.

"Every word." Ilse's voice came out ragged. "The ribbon. God, Noor, the *ribbon.*"

"I'll burn it tonight."

"Burn the cups while you're at it. Burn the floorboards." Ilse laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked, the laugh of a person standing too close to an edge. "He counted our cups, Noor. He counted them and he *left.* Why did he leave?"

Down in the kitchen, Bess was sitting very straight at the table with both hands flat on the wood, and Piet was pouring himself aquavit at ten in the morning, and Tomas was in the doorway looking like a boy who'd been told the sea was coming for him on land. Nobody answered her question, because nobody had one.

"He suspects," Bess said finally. "But suspicion isn't proof, and Voss is a man who acts on proof. He wants us frightened. Frightened people make mistakes—or they fix their mistakes before he comes back, and then he comes back and finds a house of exactly five and knows he was right to squeeze."

"Or," Piet said, setting down his glass with a click, "he already knows about Anselm, and he's letting us sit here like fish in a net so he can pull the whole catch up at once."

Noor had gone to the window, watching the empty street. When she turned around, her face had settled into the look Ilse had learned to fear and trust in equal measure—the look she wore when the maps came out, when the lying began, when Noor decided something.

"He said houses remember," Noor said quietly. "So we'll give him a house worth remembering. Five cups out, the ribbon gone, Tomas's room aired cold by tomorrow. And Anselm doesn't sleep here again—not tomorrow night, not ever. Piet, can you move him to the herring shed?"

"Aye."

"And Noor?" Ilse said. "What do we do about *me?* Because next time he brings dogs, or soldiers who search the shaft, or—" Her voice failed and she hated it. "Next time there won't be a dumbwaiter deep enough."

Noor crossed the kitchen and took Ilse's face in her ink-stained hands, the way she did when she was about to tell a truth she'd been carrying alone.

"Then next time," Noor said, "you won't be here at all. None of us will. I've been working on something—on something of Aldous's—and I think I finally understand what he left us. But I need four days and I need everyone to hold."

"Hold onto what?" Tomas asked from the doorway.

Noor looked around the table—at Bess, at Piet, at Ilse—at the whole improbable family the ledger refused to count.

"Onto each other," she said. "And pray the commandant doesn't come back before the tide does."
