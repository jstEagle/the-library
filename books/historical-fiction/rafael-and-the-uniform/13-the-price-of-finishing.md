## 13. The Price of Finishing

The summons came the way weather comes to Vellsmark in autumn: without announcement, without mercy, and with everyone pretending they'd known it was coming all along.

A corporal arrived at the boarding house before the fog had burned off, standing at the door like a nail driven into the morning. He didn't knock so much as announce. "The Commandant requests the cartographer's apprentice. Eleven o'clock. The customs house."

*Requests.* Noor folded her apron over the back of a chair and caught Bess's eye across the kitchen. Her aunt's hands went still on the bread dough—a small, terrible stillness—and then resumed kneading, because that was what Bess did with fear: she worked it into something else.

"You'll wear the grey," Bess said. Not a question.

"I'll wear the grey." The good dress, the one that made her look like what she was supposed to be: an apprentice finishing a dead man's work, nothing more, nothing less. A woman whose ledger was flawless and whose conscience was, officially, empty.

Tomas appeared in the doorway holding her coat like a squire arming a knight for the wrong war. "Noor—"

"Tomas." She took the coat from him. His hands were shaking. Hers weren't, which surprised her; she would have expected them to. But fear, she was learning, came in waves, and hers had crested three days ago in the customs house when Voss had first said *the atlas*, and since then she'd been living below the waterline where everything was quiet and cold.

"Come back," he said, which was Tomas for *I love you* and also for *please*.

"Always do."

Piet was waiting by the door with his cap in his hands, and he looked at her the way he looked at the harbor when the barometer fell. "Wind's turning," he said, meaning nothing, meaning everything. "Be home before it does."

---

The customs house smelled of paper and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where men make decisions about other people. Two soldiers flanked the stairs. Neither looked at her. Both saw everything.

Voss received her in the map room.

She should have been prepared for it—the requisitioned charts pinned wall to wall, the long table under green glass, the smell of ink that was almost her own language—but she wasn't. It was like walking into a church built by someone who worshipped a different god entirely. Every coastline on those walls was a place someone might drown. He had collected them the way other men collected medals.

He stood at the window with his back to her, and for a long moment he let her stand there, unacknowledged, which was itself a message: *I know you're here. I don't need to look.*

"Your master was Aldous Crane," he said finally. Not turning. "Thirty-one years resident of this town. Trained under Vanterpool in the southern school. Commissioned twice by the Admiralty before the war made such commissions inconvenient." Now he turned, and his eyes were the color of the fog off the point—grey, patient, certain of rain. "You were his apprentice for six years. You've spent the last three completing his atlas. I'm told it's nearly done."

Word traveled fast in an occupied town. Word traveled fast anywhere people were frightened, because fear is a currency and everyone spends it.

"It's a coastal survey," Noor said. "Scholarly. Coastlines, soundings, tide tables. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," Voss repeated, tasting the words. He crossed to the table and laid one hand flat on a chart of the northern approaches—the same chart, she realized with a lurch, that hung in Piet's memory behind his eyes every time he brought the ferry in. "Then perhaps you can explain to me, Miss Crane, why my transports keep finding bottom where your master's survey says there's nine fathoms of clear water."

The silence stretched. She let it. Silence was a tool Aldous had taught her too—*never fill a pause with panic, girl; fill it with thought*.

"Sand moves," she said. "It's what sand does. A survey is a photograph, Commandant, not a promise. Three winters of storms will redraw half the shoals along this coast. Any honest chart is already out of date the day it's printed."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite approval. Recognition, maybe—the look of a man who has set a trap and watched the animal step neatly around it.

"So you agree the charts are wrong."

"I agree the sea is honest and the charts are only as good as the year they were drawn."

"And you could correct them."

Here it was. She felt it arrive the way you feel a wave under a boat—before it breaks, before anything shows above the surface. "I could resurvey the approaches, yes. With time. And instruments. And freedom to move along the coast, which I note is currently forbidden to me."

"Which is why we are speaking." Voss pulled out a chair. Sat. Waited until she sat across from him, and hated herself a little for obeying the choreography. He opened a folder—his folder, the thin one she'd seen once before—and slid a single page across the green glass.

Her own ledger. Copied in a fair hand. Eleven names reduced to seven, her careful arithmetic rendered in someone else's script, and beside it, in the margin, notes in Voss's own cramped precision: *bed count — eight. cups — two sets. floorboards worn in a path to the third-floor landing where no registered occupant sleeps.*

He knew. He had known since inspection day. He had stood in her kitchen drinking tea from the second set of cups and said nothing, and every hour since had been him letting her believe she'd passed.

"The boarding house is irregular," he said mildly. "Occupants unregistered. Papers absent. Under standing orders, that is harboring, and harboring carries penalties I won't insult us both by naming." He folded his hands. "However."

*However.* The whole sentence balanced on it.

"However, I am a man who prefers solutions to punishments. Punishments produce corpses and paperwork. Solutions produce working charts." He tapped the ledger copy. "Complete the atlas. Survey the coast under military authority—you'll have papers, a boat, whatever instruments you require. In exchange, my attention to the arithmetic of your household becomes... less rigorous. Inspections become rarer. Eventually, perhaps, unnecessary."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I inspect again. Thursday." He said it without emphasis, which was its own kind of emphasis. "And this time I bring dogs. Dogs are thorough in ways men aren't permitted to be. They don't read ledgers. They simply find what breathes."

Ilse. Anselm. The dumbwaiter shaft that had held Ilse once and could not hold her forever. Noor kept her face still through sheer craftsmanship, the same way she drew a coastline: line steady, hand not allowed to know what the heart was doing.

"You're asking me to draw maps for the army that occupies my town."

"I'm asking you to do accurate work and be paid honestly for it. The uniform is incidental." He rose, went to the wall of charts, and touched one—a transport route, red-lined, with a cluster of small black crosses where ships had found the shoals. Fourteen crosses. "Fourteen vessels aground since spring. Two lost with all hands. Your master mapped this coast better than anyone alive, Miss Crane, and he left the job half-finished, and the sea has been charging interest ever since. I need the account settled." He turned. "You need your people breathing. We are, you'll notice, in a position to help each other."

*A position.* As if it were furniture they shared instead of a trap they occupied from opposite sides.

She should have said yes immediately. That was the play—he expected either refusal or surrender, and both served him. Refusal gave him Thursday and the dogs. Surrender gave him her, harnessed, grateful, cheap. Either way the power stayed where it sat, on his side of the table, in his grey uniform, in his quiet voice.

But Aldous had taught her one more thing about maps, and about men who used them: *always ask what the other hand can't do.*

So Noor said, slowly, "Resurveying the approaches properly takes weeks. Soundings in every season of tide. I'd want the *Griet*—Piet Jansz's boat—and freedom of the harbor, and I'd want it understood that my apprentices"—she let the plural stand, daring him—"work with me."

Voss considered her. The fog-colored eyes did their patient work.

"Weeks," he said.

"Accurate work takes the time accuracy requires. Rushed charts are how fourteen ships found the bottom."

Another flicker at the mouth's corner. He didn't like being quoted against himself, but he couldn't argue with it, because arguing with it meant defending the wrecks.

"Two weeks," he said. "Survey parties go out under escort. You report progress here, every third day." He wrote as he spoke, quick precise strokes, already building the order that would bind them both. "And Miss Crane—the completed atlas comes to me. All of it. Master Crane's original included. When the work is done, the Crown owns its coasts."

There it was: the true price, named at last. Not her labor. The atlas. Aldous's thirty-one years, his life pressed between boards, handed over to the uniforms he'd spent his last years quietly betraying.

"When it's done," Noor said, "you'll have your charts. Fresh ones. Drawn by my hand, guaranteed current."

"The original."

"A working copy. The original stays with the estate while the copy is verified. Standard practice—it protects both parties against error." She heard herself inventing procedure with a straight spine and thought: *this is what he trained me for. Not the drawing. This.*

Voss looked at her for a long moment, and she understood suddenly that he knew he was being managed—that some part of him had known since she walked in—and that he was allowing it anyway, because fourteen wrecks outweighed one apprentice's small disobediences. For now.

"Every third day," he said again. "First survey party tomorrow, dawn. Don't be late, Miss Crane. Accuracy requires punctuality too."

---

She walked home through streets gone strange with khaki and grey, past requisitioned warehouses flying unfamiliar flags, and the wind was turning just as Piet had said, coming round cold off the north with the smell of ice in it though the ice was weeks away yet.

They were all waiting in the kitchen. Of course they were. Bess with flour to her wrists she hadn't bothered to wipe. Ilse with a mending basket she hadn't touched. Piet by the window reading the sky. Tomas pacing a groove into the floorboards that no ledger would ever explain.

"Well?" Bess said.

"He wants the atlas." Noor sat down at the table, in her place, the wood warm under her palms from the stove. "The finished atlas, in his own hands. In exchange, the household stops existing to him. Ilse, Anselm—all of it. He tears up the arithmetic."

Silence. Then Ilse, quietly: "And if you give it to him?"

"Then everything Aldous hid in it—every safehouse, every route, every name he drew into the margins so people like us could breathe—belongs to the men hunting people like us." Noor turned her cup, watching the tea shiver. "Unless."

Tomas stopped pacing. "Unless what?"

Unless she did what cartographers did. Unless she remembered that a map is never the territory, that ink lies beautifully, that the man who draws the coast decides where the deep water goes. Unless she bought time—not days anymore but weeks of official access, a boat, papers, Piet at a tiller under military pass, freedom to stand on any point of this coast and look hard at exactly what she needed to look at.

"Unless I take my time," Noor said. "Accuracy requires it. I told him so myself."

Bess looked at her for a long moment—looked at her the way she looked at dough that had finally begun to rise—and then, wordlessly, put another cup on the table and poured.

From outside came the sound of boots on cobbles, a patrol passing, fading. Piet turned from the window, and his old face was unreadable as open water.

"Two weeks," he said. "That's what you bought us?"

"That's what I bought us."

"Then we'd best learn," Piet said, "what a body can do with two weeks."

And far out past the point, under the grey turning wind, the blockade ship showed its lights early—as if, somewhere in the dark, someone else was counting the days too.
