## 15. The Cove at Low Tide

Tomas had never been good at waiting, which was strange, because most of his life had been waiting—for his stammer to pass, for his father's temper to pass, for the war to pass. Waiting was the one thing he'd mastered and the one thing he hated. So when Piet said they'd need to see the cove with their own eyes before anyone trusted it, Tomas volunteered before his fear could raise its hand.

They left before dawn, taking the cliff path north out of town where the garrison's lanterns couldn't reach. Piet walked like a man much younger than he was, planting each footfall with a ferryman's economy, and Tomas followed in his wake, carrying the coil of rope and feeling every one of his twenty-two years and several more besides.

The fog was thick enough to drink. Somewhere below them the sea worked at the rocks with its patient, indifferent sound.

"You're quiet," Piet said. It wasn't criticism. Piet said everything like it wasn't criticism, which was one of the reasons Tomas would have followed him off a cliff, if asked.

"Thinking."

"Careful. That's how trouble starts."

Tomas almost smiled. "Noor says the symbols mean something. A route. She thinks—" He stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and things that were real could be taken away. "She thinks Master Crane moved people. Out. The way we're trying to go."

Piet grunted. They came over the shoulder of the headland and stopped, both of them, at the same instant, as if the view had put out a hand.

Below, folded into the cliffs like a secret kept for a hundred years, lay the cove.

It was small—smaller than Tomas had imagined, a gray lens of water maybe forty meters across, ringed by black rock except for a single gap on the seaward side where the tide ran through a natural channel no wider than a fishing boat. On the landward side, half-hidden by gorse, stood the ruins of an old smokehouse, its roof fallen in, its stone walls still standing. Aldous Crane had drawn this. Had stood here, or sent someone here, and marked it with a symbol shaped like a hook with three dots, and trusted that someday someone would read it and live.

"It's real," Tomas said. His voice cracked on the word, the way his voice did, but Piet didn't look at him sideways. Nobody in the house ever looked at him sideways anymore. That was the thing about belonging somewhere: your flaws stopped being interesting.

"It's real," Piet agreed. "Question is whether it's usable." He crouched at the cliff edge, studying the water with the squinting attention he gave to weather. "Channel runs east-west. Tide comes through fast—I can hear it from here. We'd need low water slack, maybe forty minutes of calm, maybe less. And the entrance is narrow enough that a patrol boat wouldn't fit through."

"That's good."

"That's good," Piet conceded, "if we're the only ones who know that. Patrols don't need to come inside. They need only sit offshore and watch the gap." He rose, knees cracking. "Come down. Careful on the scree."

* * *

The descent took an hour and cost them both some skin. Inside the cove the air was different—still, salt-sharp, echoing. Piet waded in to his thighs and tested the bottom: gravel, then sand, then a shelf of rock that dropped away clean. He came back up shaking water from his sleeves.

"Deep enough close in. A boat could lie against the north wall, under the overhang. From the sea you'd see nothing." He was quiet a moment, doing arithmetic Tomas could see moving behind his eyes like fish under ice. "One boat. Say six people a run, packed tight, wet, scared. Two runs if everyone holds their nerve. Three if the first run draws attention."

"How many of us?"

"Eleven. Minus whoever sails proper on the ship." Piet looked at him. "You've done sums before, boy. You know what two runs through patrol waters means."

Tomas did. It meant the second run happened after the first run might have been discovered. It meant someone had to bring the boat back. It meant—

"Why are you telling me the bad parts?"

"Because you're going to be in that boat, and I want you rowing, not panicking." Piet said it without cruelty, the way you'd tell a man the stove is hot. "And because I'm old, and if it goes wrong I'll likely be the one who stays behind, and I'd rather you heard it from me than found out by surprise."

"I won't let you stay behind."

Piet smiled, and it transformed his whole seamed face into something younger. "That's what Bess said when she took me in. 'I won't let the world be cruel to you, Piet.' Sweet girl. Wrong, but sweet." He clapped Tomas on the shoulder. "Four days. The blockade tightens in four days—more boats, tighter grid, I saw the signal flags this morning. After that, this gap closes for good. So whatever Noor decides, it happens soon, and it happens once."

They climbed back up as the fog burned off, and from the headland Tomas looked back once at the little lens of water, already imagining it full of his family, already imagining it empty.

* * *

They got back to Vellsmark past noon, slipping in along the tanner's lane to avoid the checkpoint on the harbor road. The grey coats had multiplied; there were now two sentries outside the customs house and a new notice board, freshly painted, listing requisitioned warehouses. One of them was Piet's old boat shed. Piet looked at it for exactly as long as it took to memorize the seal on the door, and then looked away, and said nothing, and Tomas understood that some silences were load-bearing.

The boarding house was too quiet when they reached it.

Tomas felt it before he could name it—the particular hush of a house holding its breath. Bess was in the kitchen, hands flat on the table, staring at nothing. Ilse sat by the window with mending in her lap that she wasn't doing. Noor stood at the foot of the stairs, and her face was the color of paper.

"What is it?" Tomas said. "What's wrong?"

Bess looked up. "Anselm's gone."

"Gone?"

"His room. Empty. Bed made—he made the bed, that's the worst of it, that's the part I can't—" Bess pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "His kit's gone. And the letter, Tomas. The letter he kept in the floorboard. I checked."

The letter. The letter Anselm had finally shown them nine nights ago, the confession he'd carried for two years like a stone in his boot—the order signed by a major, the names of executed prisoners, the proof that officers now serving under Commandant Voss had done murder in uniform. He'd shown them because the household was deciding whether to send him away, and he'd wanted them to know what they were sending away. Or maybe he'd wanted to know himself.

"He wouldn't just leave," Ilse said from the window. Her needle had stopped. "He argued with us for days about staying. He wanted to stay. He said—" She stopped.

"He said he had a war of his own to fight," Tomas finished quietly.

Noor spoke for the first time. "There's something else." She held out a scrap of paper, and her hand was steady in a way Tomas recognized as effortful, the way her voice went steady when she was frightened. "This was under his plate."

Tomas took it. The writing was quick, slanted, pressed hard:

*Don't wait for me. Whatever you decide about the boat, decide fast. If I'm not back by Thursday, count me among the eleven no more.*

"He knew about the boat?" Piet said sharply. "We only decided last night."

"Bess told him," Noor said. "He had a right to know."

"And now he's taken the letter out of this house," Piet said, "and God knows where he's gone with it, and if he's caught—if any soldier turns out his pockets—"

"Then he hangs alone," Bess said, "and not in my parlor." But her voice broke on *alone*, because Anselm hadn't been alone in that house for eighteen months. He'd fixed the roof. He'd taught Tomas to whittle. He'd sat with Bess on Sunday evenings while she read aloud, pretending the reading bored him, staying until the candle guttered.

Nobody said any of that. Saying it would have been like pressing on a wound to check it was deep.

Ilse set down her mending at last. "Maybe he went to do something brave."

"Brave and stupid wear the same coat," Piet said.

"Then he's wearing it for us," Ilse said. "All of us. Did anyone think of that? Every day he stayed, we were all standing next to a hanging offense. Maybe he finally saw it."

Tomas looked at the note again. *Count me among the eleven no more.* Eleven minus one was ten, and ten still didn't fit in seven berths, and the arithmetic that had been unbearable yesterday had somehow become worse, because now one of the numbers was a man who'd whittled him a whistle the first week he arrived, when Tomas's stammer was so bad he couldn't order bread without the baker finishing the sentence for him.

"We should go after him," Tomas said.

"With what?" Piet spread his hands. "Vellsmark has four roads out and soldiers on all of them. You want to walk into the dark asking after a deserter? You want to describe him to a patrol?" He shook his head slowly. "Anselm knows what he's doing. He always has. That's what frightened me about him from the start."

* * *

Evening came down over the harbor like a lid. From the attic window, Tomas watched the lights of the patrol boats strung across the roadstead—one, two, five, seven—and counted them twice to make sure. Seven. There had been five yesterday.

Downstairs, voices rose and fell around the table: Bess and Noor and Piet, arguing in the low urgent register they used now instead of ordinary talk. The ship. The berths. The cove. Four days. Tomas wasn't called down; none of this was decided by him, though all of it would happen to him. That had been the shape of his whole life until Aunt Bess's door opened, and even now, loved as he was, fed and sheltered and called family, there were rooms in this house where he was still a boy waiting to be told.

But tonight he found he didn't mind waiting. Because downstairs they were arguing about how to save him, specifically him, all of them, and the argument itself was a kind of rope thrown across water.

A knock at the front door stopped every voice at once.

Not a hammering—a knock. Civil. Twice. In a town where the army knocked with rifle butts, a civil knock was somehow more frightening.

Tomas heard Bess cross the hall. Heard the bolt. Heard the door open onto cold air, and then a voice he knew, roughened and hurried, saying words he couldn't make out—except one.

The word was *atlas.*

Tomas was halfway down the stairs before he remembered to be afraid. At the foot of them, framed in the doorway with fog swirling past his shoulders and his breath steaming, stood Anselm—soaked to the waist, mud to the knees, a cut over one eyebrow, and behind him in the lane, barely visible in the murk, the outline of someone else, someone waiting who had not been invited in.

"No time," Anselm said. His eyes went past Bess, past Tomas, and found Noor. "I found the resistance. They'll move us out by sea—all of us—but they want payment." He swallowed. "Noor. They want the atlas."

Behind Anselm, in the fog, the stranger shifted, and Tomas saw the dull gleam of a hooded lantern being unshuttered just enough to show a face he didn't know, watching the house the way Piet watched weather.
