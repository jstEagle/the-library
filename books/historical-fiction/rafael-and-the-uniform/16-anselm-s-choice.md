## 16. Anselm's Choice

The rain had stopped by the time Anselm reached the breakwater, but the wind hadn't, and it pushed at his back the whole way down the quay like a hand that wanted him gone. Good. Let it. He'd spent three years letting Vellsmark hold him still; it was fitting that the last walk out of it should be uphill against nothing.

He had told Bess he was going for lamp oil. He'd told Piet he needed to check a line on the *Maren*. He'd told Noor nothing at all, which was the only honest thing he'd said in two days.

The letter sat inside his coat, against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth the way you wrap something that matters more than your life and less than someone else's. He could feel its corners through the wool. He had carried it for three years—through the marsh country, through the winter he'd spent half-starved under Piet's roof, through every morning since when he'd woken and thought: *today I could give it away, and today I don't.*

Not because he feared what would happen after. He'd already survived what happens after. He'd stood in the yard at Kaltern Ford with his hands tied and watched Captain Hessler read the names off a list while eleven prisoners knelt in the mud, and he'd heard the volley, and he'd walked back to barracks and signed the roster confirming all deaths were lawful. That was the arithmetic of the army: paper over graves. The letter in his coat was the other kind of paper—the kind that wouldn't stay shut.

No. He'd kept it because giving it away meant deciding that the dead mattered more than the living who'd taken him in. Ilse's burnt fingertips. Tomas's stammer getting worse every time a grey coat came down the street. Old Piet's cough. Bess's ledger with its careful, generous lies. And Noor, who never asked him a single question about the war except one, early on, standing in the kitchen with her sleeves inked to the wrist: *"Was it yours?"* And he'd said yes, some of it was mine, and she'd nodded once and never brought it up again, because that was the whole of her mercy—she let a man keep his own grave tidy.

Tonight he was going to make that mercy spend itself on his behalf.

The contact's name was Maren Kolve. Piet had given it to him weeks ago without explanation, the way Piet gave everything: grudgingly, as if the words cost him. *If you ever mean to use that letter, this is who takes letters.* A fishwife on the east quay by day. Something else by night. Anselm had watched her for six days before approaching—watched which boats she loaded, which soldiers she ignored, which fishermen spoke to her and got a flat look in return.

She was mending nets under an awning near the salt shed when he came to her, and she didn't look up.

"You're the deserter from the Crane house," she said. "Third house past the chandlery. You take the corner room."

His stomach went cold and stayed cold. "Then you know why I'm here."

"I know what you are. Say the rest."

He crouched so their heads were level, so no passerby could read the shape of the conversation. "I have a letter. Names, dates, written orders. It proves prisoners were executed at Kaltern Ford under orders—officers now serving under Commandant Voss. Two of them are on his staff."

Maren Kolve pulled a knot tight. "And?"

"And I want passage. Eleven people, out by sea, before the blockade closes. In exchange, my testimony goes wherever you want it. The letter goes wherever you want it."

Now she looked up. Her eyes were the grey of old rope. "You want us to spend a boat and four crew on eleven strangers, and in return you'll give us the thing we could simply take from you?"

"You could try," Anselm said. "But you'd get the letter and lose the man who can explain it. Half those orders are in cipher shorthand only staff officers used. I can read every line. Without me, it's scrap."

Something moved behind her face—not warmth, exactly. Appraisal. "Eleven is too many."

"Nine, then. The rest have papers."

"Seven."

"Nine." He held her stare. "I know what your boats carry. I've watched them load at night. You're not going to tell me nine berths breaks you."

For a long moment the only sound was the net-mending, the click of the wooden shuttle, the gulls fighting over something behind the salt shed. Then she set down her work.

"Here is what nine costs," she said. "Not coin. We don't deal in coin for this. The atlas."

Anselm blinked. "What atlas?"

"Don't lie to me, deserter, it wastes both our lives. Aldous Crane mapped the smugglers' coast for twenty years, and everyone on this quay knows his apprentice finished it. Tide windows. Anchorages. Which patrols turn where and when. Do you know what that book is worth to people moving men and guns along this shore? It isn't worth money. It's worth the war." She leaned closer. "We take the household out, all nine, and the atlas comes to us. Not copies. The book."

"He hasn't finished it. She has—it's hers. She inked the last coastline herself."

"Then it's hers to trade," Maren said, and went back to her nets as if he had already left.

* * *

He didn't go straight home. He walked the long way, along the seawall, because he couldn't yet walk into that kitchen and say what he'd said. The tide was going out and the harbor floor showed its ribs—old keels, a drowned dinghy, the black stones that grounded careless boats. He stood there thinking about the word *trade*, and how every trade he'd ever witnessed in uniform had been conducted in things that weren't the trader's to give.

That was the thought that finally made him climb the hill. Because he knew—he had known since the day he arrived—that the atlas wasn't really Noor's either. It was Aldous's, and Aldous was dead, and a dead man's wishes are the one currency nobody can refuse without becoming a thief.

He came in through the kitchen door expecting empty rooms. It was nearly midnight. What he found instead was Noor at the table, alone, a candle burned down to a stub, and in her hands—held the way you hold a bird, carefully enough not to crush it, tightly enough not to lose it—was a sheet of paper he recognized by its crease. Three years of creases. His creases.

She looked up at him, and her face was terrible and calm.

"The oilcloth was leaking wax into your coat pocket," she said. "Bess found it when she brushed the coat for mending. She doesn't open other people's letters. So she brought it to me." A pause. "I've been sitting here deciding whether to read it. I decided I owed it to you not to. And then I decided I owed it to eleven people to know what we're carrying." She set it down on the table between them, squared to the edge, the way she squared everything. "So. Tell me what you traded for it, or tell me what it trades for. Whichever is worse."

Anselm took off his coat. Sat down across from her. The candle flame stood perfectly still between them, as if it also wanted to hear.

"Maren Kolve," he said. "East quay. Nine berths on a resistance boat before the blockade closes. In exchange—" and here his voice did the thing it did when he named the true thing, dropped low and roughened, "—in exchange, the atlas. The real one. For their routes, their war."

Noor didn't move for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, airless sound, nothing like laughter.

"Aldous promised Bess it would never serve uniforms," she said. "And I promised myself it would buy our lives. And you've promised it to a third master I haven't even met." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Do you understand what we've done? Between us we've sold the same book twice."

"I sold it to save you," Anselm said. "All of you. I sold the only thing I had."

"No." She lowered her hands. Her eyes were dry, which somehow was worse than tears. "You sold it to pay your debt. To the dead at Kaltern Ford. And that's not nothing, Anselm—I'm not saying it's nothing—but you spent *our* inheritance to pay it, the way the army spends towns." She turned the letter over slowly with one finger. "This is what they do. This is exactly what they do. They take the thing one person carries and make everyone else pay for it."

The words landed like a blow because they were true, and he let them land. "Then tell me the better bargain," he said. "You're the mapmaker. Chart me a way out of this that doesn't cost anyone anything."

Noor stared at the letter for a long time. Outside, somewhere down the hill, a patrol boot-heel struck cobbles in slow fours, passed, faded. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—not softer. Sharper. The voice she must have used on coastlines that refused to be drawn.

"Neither of us gets to decide alone anymore," she said. "Tomorrow we put it to the house. All of it—the letter, Kolve's price, Voss's offer, everything. If we're going to spend each other, we spend each other in daylight." She slid the letter back across the table to him, but kept two fingers on its corner. "And Anselm—if Kolve's people come asking for the atlas, they'll find out there are two buyers at that door already. Voss wants it finished by week's end. Which means whatever we choose, we choose fast—and whoever we cheat will be holding the other end of the same rope."

She took her fingers away. He picked up the letter, feeling its weight triple.

"One more thing," Noor said quietly, as he rose. "Kolve said the book was worth the war. She's wrong. It's worth eleven people. Make sure she learns that before the tide turns—or none of us sails."

He climbed the stairs to the corner room with the letter against his chest again, and lay down without undressing, and listened to the harbor breathing below. Somewhere out in the dark water, between the blockade line and the cove Aldous had drawn in symbols no one alive could read but one, the boats were waiting. And tomorrow, he understood, the family that had taken him in would sit around one table and discover that every escape route they owned ran straight through the thing they loved most.

Below, faint through the floorboards, he heard Noor begin to write.
