## 18. The Last Berth

Bess Crane had spent forty years running a boarding house, and in that time she had learned one truth above all others: you could feed any argument if you put enough bread on the table. Hunger made people reasonable. Full bellies made them generous. But tonight she had baked two loaves and set out the good butter, the honey, even the last jar of preserved plums, and the table was silent as a held breath.

Seven berths. Eleven people.

The paper with Piet's tally sat in the middle of the table like something dead. Bess had watched him write it that morning at the harbor office, his big ferryman's hand pressing so hard the pencil tore through in three places. Seven confirmed passages on the *Merrow's Daughter*, sailing with the morning tide tomorrow. The captain had taken their entire savings—every coin from the tin behind the flour bin, every crown Piet had salted away under his bunk, the silver candlesticks Bess had carried out of her marriage—and promised nothing more than he'd already given.

"Say it plain," Piet said now. He sat with his arms crossed over his chest like a man bracing against a wave. "Someone stays. That's the arithmetic. I didn't make it. I just carried it home."

"And I say we don't accept arithmetic," Ilse said. Her hands were folded tight in her lap, the way they always were when she was frightened—the seamstress who couldn't stop her fingers from working even when there was no cloth. "Four of us can fit in the fishing boat. We've seen the cove. Tomas and Piet scouted it. It's viable."

"It's viable," Tomas said carefully. He always spoke as if each word might break if he handled it wrong. "Viable isn't safe. One run through patrol water is a gamble. Four people, four runs—" He stopped himself, shook his head. "That's not viable. That's suicide with extra steps."

"So we vote," Piet said. "That's why I called everyone to this table. We vote, and no one gets to be a martyr about it afterward."

Bess looked down the length of her table—at Piet with his weathered certainty, at Ilse with her white knuckles, at Tomas who had arrived on her doorstep seven years ago unable to finish a sentence and had never once lied to her since, at Anselm's empty chair, which no one looked at and everyone saw. Anselm had come back two days ago smelling of rain and secrets, and whatever he'd done out there in the dark, he hadn't told them yet. His chair stayed empty because he'd taken his supper standing by the window, watching the harbor like a man counting ships.

And Noor. Noor sat at the far end, near where Aldous used to sit, and said nothing at all.

* * *

The vote lasted eleven minutes, and it was the most beautiful disaster Bess had ever witnessed.

Piet went first, because Piet always went first; he believed hesitation was for people who hadn't already decided. "I stay," he said. "It's my boat. My cove route. If anyone runs the blockade, I'm the one who knows the tide windows. You can't send a stranger's helmsman." He said it reasonably, firmly, the voice of a man stating the direction of a current.

"You're sixty-one years old," Ilse shot back.

"I've been sixty-one for two years and the boat still listens to me better than it listens to you."

"That's exactly my point—you go on the ship, Piet. You've earned a berth with your back straight and dry socks. The cove run needs someone young."

"I'm twenty-two," Tomas said quietly.

Everyone turned. He flushed but didn't retract it. "I'm twenty-two, I know the route now, I helped chart it. And I'm—" his jaw worked, "—I'm the least of you. I mean that kindly. Piet has the boat. Ilse has her trade anywhere in the world. Noor has the atlas. I have a stammer and a strong back. Put me on the boat."

"You will not," Bess said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she intended.

"Aunt Bess—"

"You'll not talk yourself off my table like you're clearing your plate. Sit there and eat your bread."

He sat. He ate his bread. His eyes shone and he stared very hard at the plums.

Then Ilse stood up, and Bess knew what was coming before the girl opened her mouth, because Ilse had the same look she'd had the night she arrived with a split lip and a stolen bolt of linen: the look of someone spending herself all at once because she didn't believe she'd be allowed to give anything slowly. "I'll take the boat," Ilse said. "I'm not registered anywhere. Not on the ledger, not in the census, not in God's own book. If Voss catches a boat in the cove, whoever's on it should be someone who doesn't exist. Let them catch a ghost. What do I lose?"

"What do you *lose*?" Bess repeated, and her voice cracked on it. "Child, you lost enough before you ever walked through my door."

Ilse's chin came up. "I lose less than anyone else at this table. That's just true. And I'd rather be useful than protected. I've been both, Aunt Bess. Useful is better."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Piet said, gruffly, almost gently, "She's right, though. Damn her."

"She's right and she's wrong," said Anselm, from the window.

They all turned. He hadn't moved. The lamplight caught the side of his face, the scar along his jaw he'd never explained and Bess had never asked about. "If it comes to the boat, I take it. Alone if it must be. A deserter running a blockade is expected—a patrol finds a fishing boat full of civilians, they ask questions. They find me, they stop asking." He finally turned around, and Bess saw how tired he was, tired in the bones, tired the way men get when they've been carrying something heavier than themselves for a long time. "My name costs the least too. It was taken from me a long time ago."

"That's four volunteers for a boat that holds four and needs none of them," Piet muttered.

"Then it's settled," said Tomas, "we all stay," and for one mad second Bess thought he meant it, thought the whole table would dissolve into glorious mutual ruin—and then Noor spoke.

* * *

Noor had been quiet so long that Bess had nearly stopped waiting for her to speak, the way you stop listening for a clock you've lived beside all your life. Now she set down her fork, folded her napkin, laid it beside her plate with the neatness of someone closing a ledger, and the room went still the way it goes still when a tide turns.

"No," she said. Just that. Then, when no one dared interrupt: "We are doing what we always do. Each of us is pricing ourselves for the auction and bidding against our own lives. Piet says his age makes him worth less. Ilse says her invisibility makes her worth less. Tomas says his modesty. Anselm says his disgrace. Do you hear it? Every one of you has argued that you are the cheapest thing on this table. And I am telling you the arithmetic is wrong, because it was built by people who don't want to leave and are ashamed to say so."

Bess felt her heart turn over. Three years she had watched this girl ink coastlines in an attic, quiet as a draft under a door, and she had thought she knew her. She had known maybe half.

"The handover to Voss happens tomorrow," Noor said. "He expects me at the customs house with the atlas. Someone has to carry it in, and someone has to survive his looking at it. That person cannot be Piet, who is known to hate soldiers on principle. It cannot be Ilse or Tomas or Anselm, who are not on any register Voss respects. It has to be me. I'm the cartographer. I'm the one whose name is on the census, the one he's summoned twice, the one he trusts—or believes he trusts—to deliver what he paid for." She paused. "So I stay. I make the handover. And then I walk out of Vellsmark the back way, to the cove, and Piet takes the boat out at low tide with me aboard, and we meet the *Merrow's Daughter* beyond the point, or we don't."

"And if you don't?" Bess asked. Her voice came out small. She hated that.

"Then the seven of you are safe on open water, and I'll have bought it with a day instead of a berth." Noor looked down the table, meeting every eye, and Bess saw that her niece—because that's what she was, whatever the blood said, whatever the ledgers said, she had been Bess's niece since the night Aldous first brought her home and Bess had fed her soup and she'd cried into it without making a sound—her niece had done the thing none of the rest of them could do. She'd stopped arguing her own worth and started arguing theirs.

"Piet takes the boat alone," Noor went on. "One man, one run, the smallest silhouette on the water. That's the plan that gives everyone the best odds, including me. And it's not negotiable, because it's not sentiment—it's the only version where the numbers actually work."

"The tide window is narrow," Piet said slowly. Bess could see him testing it, sailor-fashion, looking for the leak. "Two hours past slack water. If the handover runs long—if Voss keeps you—"

"He won't keep me past noon. He wants the atlas more than he wants anything else in this town, including me. I've read his letters. I know what he's missing." Something flickered across her face then, quick and private, and Bess thought: *there it is*, there's the other thing, the thing she's not saying. But Noor smoothed it away like a wrinkle in fresh paper. "I'll be at the cove by the tide window or I won't be there at all. Those are the terms. Vote on them."

There was nothing to vote on. Bess saw that in every face around the table. Noor had done what Aldous used to do—she'd drawn the map so completely that arguing with it felt like arguing with the coastline itself.

Piet looked at her a long moment. "Your uncle would've drawn it the same way."

"My uncle would've drawn it better," Noor said. "But he taught me the pencil grip."

Tomas laughed—cracked, wet, relieved—and Ilse reached over and squeezed his wrist without looking at him, and Anselm came at last from the window and sat down in his chair and ate, and just like that the thing was decided, the way real decisions happen: not by voting, but by someone finally being willing to hold the pen.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and the house had gone to its separate silences—Tomas whittling in the kitchen doorway, Ilse mending by candlelight, Piet out checking the boat a third time though the tide wouldn't turn for hours—Bess climbed the attic stairs with a lamp.

Noor was at the worktable, of course. The atlas lay open before her, and she was doing something to the margins, small precise strokes, her face lit gold and intent. She didn't look up when Bess came in. She never did, when she was working; Aldous had been the same, and it had driven his sister half mad for thirty years.

"You could have let us vote you onto the boat," Bess said. "You could have let us sacrifice you. It's what we were all trying to do."

"I know." Noor's pencil didn't stop. "That's why I couldn't."

Bess set the lamp on the shelf beside the jars of ink, beside the smell of linseed and salt that had soaked into these rafters for three years. She looked at the bent head, the steady hand, the margins filling up with symbols no one else alive could read.

Aldous had written to her once, near the end, when he knew what was coming for him. *The girl has the gift,* he'd said, *but the gift isn't the drawing. Any fool with a steady hand can draw a coastline. The gift is knowing what a map is for. It's not for the powerful. It's for the lost. Mark me—one day she'll understand that, and on that day she'll stop being my apprentice and become my heir.*

Bess had read that letter a hundred times and filed it under grief. Standing in the attic now, watching Noor work by lamplight on the eve of everything, she took it out and understood it at last.

"You'll come out through the cove," Bess said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite a prayer, but it was both.

"At the tide window," Noor said. "With the whole atlas in my coat."

"Every page?"

Noor looked up then, and smiled, and Bess saw in her face the girl of nineteen who'd arrived with nothing, and the woman of twenty-three who was leaving with less and carrying more. "Every page. Every name."

Down in the harbor, faint through the attic glass, Bess heard the *Merrow's Daughter*'s bell ring the change of watch—tomorrow's departure rehearsed in the dark. Below it, softer, came another sound: boots on cobbles, a patrol passing, unhurried, thorough.

Twenty hours until the ship sailed. Fourteen until the tide window opened.

And somewhere across the sleeping town, Commandant Reiner Voss was awake too. Bess felt certain of it the way she felt weather in her knee. Somewhere in the customs house, under lamplight, a meticulous man was preparing to receive a masterpiece—and somewhere between his hands and Noor's coat, between the atlas and the cove, between the tide and the patrol boats, the whole family's future would be decided in a single afternoon.

Bess blew out the lamp and left her niece to the dark and the ink.

"Hold the pencil grip," she whispered, and went down the stairs to pack.
