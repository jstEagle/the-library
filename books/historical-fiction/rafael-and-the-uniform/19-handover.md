## 19. Handover

The atlas weighed more than it should have. Forty pounds of paper and board and three years of my life, wrapped in oilcloth against the drizzle, and every step from Aunt Bess's door to the customs house felt like carrying a drowning man on my back.

Bess had wanted to come with me. Piet had offered to walk me there "for the exercise," which was a lie so transparent I almost loved him for it. Tomas had stood in the hall holding my coat like a squire arming a knight, stammering that the buttons were polished, that he'd checked them twice.

I went alone. That was the point of everything I'd built: a woman delivering a map to a soldier should look like nothing at all. A transaction. An errand. The kind of small compliance that occupies armies see a thousand times a day and never remember by evening.

The rain had thinned to a grey mist by the time I reached the customs house steps. Two sentries flanked the door, young enough that their uniforms still fit loosely, and one of them looked at the bundle under my arm and then away again with the studied incuriosity of someone who had learned not to wonder about things.

Commandant Voss's office was on the second floor, at the front of the building, where the windows gave him the harbor. Of course they did. A man who collected maps would want to sit above the thing the maps described, watching the real coastline argue with his charts.

His adjutant announced me and left. The door clicked shut, and Voss did not look up.

He was writing. Or pretending to write. He held the pen the way Aldous used to hold his—loosely, between first two fingers, a grip that came from drafting rather than correspondence—and I catalogued that detail and put it away, because details were the only weapons I carried.

"Frau Crane." He finished his line, blotted it, set the pen down parallel to the page's edge. Everything on that desk sat at right angles. Inkwell, blotter, letter knife, a small brass instrument I couldn't identify but recognized as expensive. "You have it."

"You said you'd know if I didn't."

"I said I'd know if it were unfinished." He extended both hands across the desk—not grabbing, offering—and something in the gesture made my stomach drop, because it was respectful. He handled the request for the atlas the way a man handles a relic. "May I?"

I unwrapped the oilcloth and put it in his hands.

And then I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than forging nine names, harder than lying to Ilse's face about burning her history: I let go.

Voss opened the atlas the way no one else ever had. Not to admire it. Not to grieve its maker. He opened it to the binding and examined the stitching, ran his thumb along the spine, checked the boards for warp. Then he turned to the first chart and began, silently, to read.

I had prepared myself for questions. For suspicion, for traps, for him sliding a loupe across the desk and demanding I account for a river bend or a sounding. What I had not prepared for was silence—ten minutes of it, twenty—the only sounds the tick of a clock and the whisper of paper, while I stood on the carpet like an applicant for a position and watched a stranger touch every page of Master Aldous Crane's life's work.

He read coastlines the way other men read letters. I could tell because his eyes moved in surveyors' patterns: anchorages first, then depths, then the contour of the land behind the shore. Twice he paused over a heading I'd written and mouthed it to himself. Once he turned a chart sideways and held it against the light from the window, checking for watermarks, for erasures, for anything beneath the ink.

The doctored duplicate was good. I knew it was good. I had built it the way Aldous taught me to build anything that mattered: true where it could be checked, invented where it couldn't, and consistent everywhere, because inconsistency is what kills a forgery faster than error. Every depth sounding Voss might verify against a lead line matched the original within tolerance. Every bearing agreed with itself. I had even reproduced Aldous's quirks—the way his north arrows leaned a degree off true as though apologizing for pointing at authority, the little tide-tables in the corners written in his cramped backward-slanting hand, which I could imitate better than my own signature.

What I had removed was the margin work. And what I had added—

Well. What I had added was the reason my hands were steady only because I had locked my knees.

Voss reached the middle sections and slowed. Here the atlas grew dense: the approaches to Vellsmark harbor, the shoal fields off the Skerries, the long grey stretch of coast south toward Halden. Charts I had redrawn four times each, once for accuracy and three times for the lie folded inside the accuracy.

He stopped on the chart of the Narrows.

My heart did not speed up. It did the opposite—it went slow and heavy, like a struck bell settling, and I heard my own pulse in my ears as a low tone that would not end. Because I knew what he was looking at. I had known he would stop here. The Narrows chart was where the current tables mattered most, where a wrong figure grounded ships, and where I had done my finest and most dangerous work: currents rendered beautifully, plausibly, and wrong. Not absurdly wrong. Wrong by a ship's length. Wrong by the difference between clearing the spit at slack water and meeting the ebb broadside.

Aldous taught me that a map lies best when it tells the truth about everything except the one thing you need hidden. *Anyone can fake a coastline,* he said once, tapping a chart of some foreign shore with his pipe stem. *The art is faking a fact. Facts are what people check.*

Voss took out a magnifier. Small, silver-cased, the kind surveyors carry. He bent over the Narrows chart and stayed there for a long time.

Then he moved to the margins.

*Don't react.* Bess's voice, from years ago, teaching eleven-year-old me to hold a teacup through Mrs. Aldridge's gossip: *Your face is a door, girl. Learn what it opens onto.* I kept my face on the middle distance, attentive, mildly bored, the expression of a craftswoman waiting for payment.

His finger found the symbol.

Even doctored, even stripped of the network, the atlas carried marginal marks—I couldn't remove them all without making the removal obvious; too-clean margins around Aldous's known habits would have been its own confession. So I had left the harmless ones: depth annotations, a compass variation note, the little crossed-anchor glyph Aldous used to mark a surveyed anchorage. And one mark I had judged safe. A single spiral, drawn twice, in the corner of the Narrows chart.

It wasn't harmless. I knew it wasn't harmless. In the true atlas, spirals meant a tide window—a time when the cove route ran clear. I had chosen this particular spiral deliberately, because in the decoded system it marked a window that fell outside any plausible patrol schedule, a dead window, useless. A lock with no key behind it. I had reasoned that Voss, if he noticed it at all, would notice a symbol he couldn't decode, and that an undecodable symbol reads as decoration to a man who cannot ask the dead what it means.

But reasoning is not certainty, and certainty is not control, and Voss's finger was on the spiral now, and he looked up at me for the first time since I'd entered the room.

"What is this?"

"A tidal observation." My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it later; at the time I wasn't present enough to be proud of anything. "Master Crane recorded slack-water intervals along the whole coast. Where his observations disagreed with the published almanac, he marked the discrepancy so future surveys would re-check it."

"This appears twice."

"The discrepancy recurred. Two independent observations." I let a breath's pause fall, the pause of a professional recalling tedious detail. "Currents in the Narrows run contrary to the almanac model. The almanac assumes the flood begins at the turn of the tide at the outer roads. In fact the inner basin fills late—it's the shape of the channel, the bottleneck holds the ebb back. Any surveyor working these waters would find the same. It's why the almanac has been wrong about this coast for thirty years." I nodded, slightly, toward the chart. "It's why your transports are drawing more water than their pilots expect."

That last sentence was a gamble dressed as a courtesy. Voss's ships had been grounding all month; Piet heard the talk at the harbor and repeated it to Bess over supper with grim satisfaction. If Voss believed the almanac was the culprit, my explanation confirmed what he already suspected. If he didn't—if the groundings had another cause—then I had just volunteered information he'd want tested.

He wrote it down. That was either very good or very bad, and I wouldn't know which until I was far out of his reach.

"The apprentice," Voss said, still looking at the chart, "learned well."

"She learned everything. She finished everything." I kept my hands folded. "As you asked."

"Yes." He closed the atlas gently, which hurt worse than roughness would have. Then he opened it again to the flyleaf, where Aldous's name was written in his own hand, dated the year he began, and beneath it, added three years ago in mine: *completed by his apprentice.* Voss read the two names the way a man reads a will.

"Master Crane died—" he glanced up, "—how?"

"Fever. Winter before last."

"No family?"

"None."

"And you remained in Vellsmark. To finish it."

"There was no one else who could."

Something moved behind his eyes—calculation, or its cousin, appreciation. "Three years alone with a dead man's work. That is a kind of loyalty most officers never earn from living men." He squared the atlas on his desk, aligned it with the edge, and rested his palm flat on the cover as if taking possession of it formally, an oath. "The army thanks you, Frau Crane. This chart work will save lives. Possibly many."

I thought of whose lives, and kept my face a door.

"The payment we discussed."

"Plus the completion fee you named." He drew an envelope from a drawer without counting it—he had counted it before I arrived; I understood that about him—and slid it across. I took it without counting it either, which was its own performance: the craftsman above haggling.

"One further matter." He rose. Up close he was shorter than I'd imagined and more precise, every seam in his uniform doing exactly what its tailor intended. From a cabinet he produced a document folder stamped with the garrison seal and laid it open on the desk beside the atlas. "Travel papers. Civilian transit, port of embarkation, one bearer."

I looked at the papers and did not touch them yet. Touching them too fast would say how much they mattered.

"You're leaving," Voss said. Not a question.

"After delivery, yes. There's nothing to keep me."

"An apprentice of your quality could find work anywhere. Including," he said mildly, "here. Charting for us. Well paid. Protected."

Here it was—the hook under the coin, the second offer that was really the first one wearing different clothes. He had been testing whether I was a resource or merely a vendor. I bowed my head as if weighing it, letting him see the weight, because a woman who considered his offer was a woman who feared him less than a woman who refused instantly.

"It's generous. But the atlas was my master's life. Mine is elsewhere." I met his eyes. "I'm going home."

"Where is home?" A routine question. A census question. The word *census* rang in me like a struck pipe.

"Up the coast. Family."

He studied me for three full seconds. I have been studied by tax assessors and smugglers and Ilse on the night she arrived with a bruised wrist and a stolen needle case, and none of them studied like Voss. He studied the way he read charts: anchorages first, then depths, then the land behind.

"For the apprentice's journey home," he said, and signed the papers, and stamped them, and pushed them across.

I picked them up. My name was on the first line, spelled correctly. The destination line said *Halden,* which was not where I was going, and did not need to be, because a travel paper is a map of permission, and permissions, like maps, can be read generously.

"The sentry at the door will escort you past the checkpoint," Voss said. "In case anyone asks."

"In case anyone asks," I repeated, and heard how it sounded, and hated how grateful it sounded, because that gratitude was also part of the transaction and he knew it and I knew it and we were both content to let it stand.

At the door I paused. I hadn't planned to pause. But I turned, and Voss had already reopened the atlas to the Narrows chart, and his magnifier lay beside his hand, and he was looking at the margin again—at the space where a second spiral might have been, if a man knew to look for a pattern instead of a discrepancy.

"Commandant," I said. "If your pilots want the current tables explained, send for me before I sail."

"They won't need to. Your notes are thorough." He did not look up. "Good journey, Frau Crane."

The corridor was cold after the office. My legs worked by agreement rather than instruction, and the envelope of coin pressed against my ribs like something alive, and the travel papers rustled in my pocket with every step, saying my name, saying my name, saying *home.*

Outside, the mist had lifted. The harbor lay open and grey, masts nodding, and at the civilian quay—the quay Piet had watched for a week, learning the loading rhythm by heart—a steamer stood with smoke already climbing her funnel in a thin straight line. Tomorrow she would take seven people away from Vellsmark legitimately, with papers, in daylight.

Tonight, the rest of us would go the way Aldous had drawn for us, through the cove at low tide, in a fishing boat with blackened lamps.

I walked home through streets that suddenly looked like a map I was leaving, memorizing them anyway. At the corner by the chandlery, Tomas was waiting where he shouldn't have been, hopping foot to foot under the awning, and when he saw me his face did something complicated and young, and I realized he couldn't read my expression any better than Voss could read my margins.

"Well?" he managed.

"He took it," I said. "We sail tomorrow."

Tomas exhaled like a man surfacing. And it was only then—walking the last blocks with his chatter breaking over me like weather—that I let myself feel the triumph, and discovered it felt exactly like drowning: pressure everywhere, breath nowhere, and the terrible knowledge that the surface was still a tide and a patrol boat and one dark run through the Narrows away.

Behind us, in a warm office, Commandant Voss was reading an atlas I had lied into being, and somewhere in those careful pages a single spiral waited, meaningless, a decoy standing guard over the truth.

I only hoped the decoy would keep standing for one more day.
