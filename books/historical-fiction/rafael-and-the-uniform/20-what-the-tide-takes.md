## 20. What the Tide Takes

### PIET

The tide would turn at 4:11. Piet had known that the way he knew his own pulse, had felt it in his knees for two days, and now he stood at the harbor mouth in the blue-black hour before dawn and watched the water begin its long exhale out of the cove channel.

Behind him, the town slept under grey coats. Ahead of him, the last civilian ship out of Vellsmark sat at her berth with her running lights lit like a woman holding a candle at a window.

Seven berths. He'd counted them twice, then a third time with the broker's man standing over him breathing cheap tobacco, because arithmetic done once in fear was arithmetic done wrong.

Seven.

He'd made this walk to the harbor every morning for forty-one years, and today it was a funeral march wearing a festival's clothes. The *Meridian* would take Bess, Tomas, Ilse, Marta, old Grete, young Fenna, and the Kessler boy — seven names on legitimate papers Noor had bled three nights to make true enough. And then she would come back down the cliff path to him, and they would take the other road. The one Aldous had drawn.

Piet spat into the dark water. He didn't pray anymore; the sea had cured him of prayer by never once answering. But he did talk to it, quietly, the way you'd talk to an old dog.

"Not today," he told it. "You'll not have them today."

* * *

### NOOR

Noor stood at the boarding house door with the ledger under her arm and watched the seven of them come down the stairs carrying everything they owned in bags small enough to lie about.

Bess wore her good shawl. Ilse wore the coat with the mended elbow, the mend visible if you looked, which Ilse said was the point now — she was done being invisible, even in the small ways. Tomas carried the atlas case strapped across his chest like a child, though the atlas inside was only paper and ink, the true one already gone ahead in oilcloth under Piet's floorboards. What Tomas carried was bait, or a decoy, or simply weight — Noor hadn't decided which and hadn't told him.

"Say nothing at the gangway unless someone asks," Noor said, for the fourth time. "If they ask, you are going to your cousin in Halden. You've never been happier."

"I've never been sadder," Grete said mildly, and everyone laughed, and Noor loved them so hard it felt like a wound opening.

Marta hugged her first, quick and fierce. Then Fenna, crying without shame. Then the Kessler boy, who shook her hand as though this were a business transaction, which maybe it was.

Then Ilse. She held on longer than she'd ever held on to anyone. "You get on that fishing boat," she whispered. "You don't get to decide for me again whose name gets remembered. So remember yours."

Then Bess. Bess took Noor's face in both hands the way she'd done when Noor arrived at fifteen, orphaned and sharp-tongued, and looked at her for a long moment.

"You were never a boarder," Bess said. "I want that understood. Not by you. By the record." Her eyes went, just briefly, to the ledger under Noor's arm. "Whatever you wrote in there. Correct it later."

And then they were walking toward the harbor through streets still grey with dawn, and Noor stood in the doorway until she couldn't see them anymore, and then she went upstairs and burned the forged drafts after all — all of them, every invented death and plausible absence, feeding them page by page into the stove while the house went quiet around her.

Some erasures, she thought, watching the curl of flame, were a kind of writing.

* * *

### TOMAS

The gangway plank bounced under Tomas's feet and he hated it, hated the give of it, hated the smell of tar and cold iron, but most of all he hated that his legs worked fine while his voice wouldn't come at all.

A soldier checked papers at the head of the plank. Grey coat, young face, bored eyes. He took Bess's documents, read them, looked up at her face.

"Purpose of travel?"

Tomas's throat closed on schedule. It was always like this — the words assembled themselves somewhere behind his sternum and then jammed, six or eight of them pressed together in the doorway of his mouth, none willing to go first.

But Bess didn't wait for him. "Family relocation," she said, in the voice she used when the bread was burning and no one had better comment. "My nephew is simple. Forgive him."

The soldier glanced at Tomas — a flick of the eyes, the kind of look you'd give a barrel or a crate — and stamped the papers.

Simple. Tomas had been called it before, and it had always stung, and standing there on the bouncing plank with the stamp still wet he decided, with a clarity that surprised him, that he would carry the word home to Halden and set it on a shelf like a tool. Because it was a tool. It had gotten seven people past a gun.

He looked back once from the rail. Vellsmark lay along its curve of gray water, roofs and chimneys and the boarding house he couldn't see, and above it all the low hills where Aldous Crane had walked with his sketchbook for thirty years.

He didn't see Anselm anywhere. He tried not to think about what that meant.

* * *

### ANSELM

Anselm walked into the commandant's headquarters at half past nine in the morning, in his old uniform jacket, with his letter folded against his heart, and asked to see whoever handled court-martial inquiries.

He'd rehearsed it a hundred ways and settled on the simplest: walk in, hand over the letter, let them arrest him, and be loud about it. Loud was the whole trick. A deserter surrendering voluntarily, demanding trial, shouting about war crimes — that wasn't a quiet thing you processed in an office. That was a spectacle. Officers would come out of their rooms to see it. Guards would gather. A courier would be sent for. Voss himself might come down to look.

Every minute of it, minutes bought at full price.

Because the tide turned at 4:11 in the morning, yes — but the cove run didn't happen at high noon. Piet's window was tonight, near midnight, and the patrol boat *Kestrel* made her sweep of the southern headland at irregular hours, and Anselm had spent yesterday learning her schedule from a dockhand who owed him. Tonight she'd pass the cove around eleven. If something happened ashore at ten — something loud, something that pulled the *Kestrel* in toward the harbor lights instead of the dark southern water — the fishing boat would have her run clear.

So Anselm gave himself a day. He drank coffee in the market square where soldiers drank coffee. He memorized faces. At nine-thirty he put on the jacket he'd sworn never to wear again, and felt it settle on his shoulders like a debt coming due.

At the headquarters door, a guard stopped him. "State your business."

"Deserter, Eleventh Coastal Regiment," Anselm said, loud enough for the street. "Turning myself in. I have evidence regarding my former officers, and I'll give it to no one below a colonel."

The guard stared. Somewhere behind the wall, a bell began to move.

Anselm smiled. *Loud,* he reminded himself. *Be loud.*

* * *

### PIET

By dusk the fishing boat *Grietje* sat in the cove's inner pool, riding so low with three extra souls' worth of nerves that Piet kept checking the waterline out of habit.

Noor was aboard. That had nearly killed him, the argument on the cliff path — she'd wanted to help load, he'd wanted her off the beach before the light went, and they'd stood toe to toe like two rocks the sea couldn't move until finally he'd said, *Aldous left you the map, girl, not the oars,* and she'd gone white and then gone aboard.

With her came the two others: Lotta from the chandlery, whose name had never been in any ledger anywhere, and old Hendrik the netmender, who was ninety if he was a day and had refused passage on the *Meridian* with the observation that ships sank and coves didn't.

Now they waited. The light died. The stars came out hard and cold, and Piet watched the headland silhouette for the running light of the *Kestrel*, and Noor sat beside him in the stern with Aldous's chart open on her knees, reading it by feel more than sight.

"She'll come early," Noor said.

"They're never early."

"A man like Voss is never late either. He runs schedules because schedules are how you own people." She traced the margin symbol — the little hooked mark that meant *safe water, keep to starboard of the mid-channel rock.* "If she comes early and we're still here—"

"We won't be."

"And if we're caught—"

"Hendrik," Piet called forward, to cut her off, "how's the water?"

"Cold and full of fish," Hendrik said. "Same as sixty years ago."

Piet almost laughed. And then, across the black shoulder of the southern headland, a light moved. White, steady, sweeping — and far too soon, and pointing not out to sea but *in*, curving toward the town, toward the harbor, toward whatever was burning bright enough over there to pull a warship off her station like a moth to a lamp.

"What is that?" Lotta whispered.

Piet stood up so fast the boat rocked. Fires — two of them, orange bloom against the harbor quarter, and beneath the wind he could just hear it, thin across the water: bells. Alarm bells, many, all at once.

"That," he said slowly, "is Anselm."

Noor's hand found his arm and gripped it hard. "That's Anselm *spending himself*," she said. "Go. Go now, Piet, before they remember the south water exists."

Piet put the tiller over.

* * *

### NOOR

They ran the channel with the engine throttled to a whisper, which was madness and also the only way — noise carried over water at night farther than light did, and the patrol boat's guns carried farther than anything. Noor knelt amidships with the chart, calling the marks in a voice pitched barely above breath.

"Starboard of the mid-channel rock. Hard to starboard after the second marker. There's four feet under the keel here and no more — trust the chart, not your eyes."

Hendrik, at the bow, laughed softly into the dark. "Trust a dead man's ink. Fine thing."

"It's the only thing I've ever trusted," Noor said, and meant it, and heard it land among them like something dropped — because the dead man's ink had gotten them this far, and the dead man's friend had just set two fires in a garrison town to buy them minutes, and minutes were the only currency tonight that mattered.

The headland fell away behind them. The harbor fires shrank to a smudge of orange. The channel narrowed, the cliffs closing in black on either side, and the water went smooth as poured lead, and for three hundred yards they were inside the earth itself, invisible, unfindable, held in Aldous's lines the way a name is held in a book.

Then the open sea. Cold wind. Stars.

Noor stood up and looked back at Vellsmark one last time — at the fires, at the smoke, at the town where she had finished a dead man's atlas and lost a living friend in the same week — and she did the thing she'd promised herself she wouldn't do.

She counted heads. Four. It should have been five.

* * *

### ANSELM

They took him in the square outside the headquarters, eventually, after the fires were out and the bells had stopped and the colonel's adjutant had screamed at three different people about protocol.

It had worked better than he'd planned. Two fires — the coal store and, God forgive him, the requisitioned warehouse with the army's own paperwork inside, which had gone up like the wrath of heaven and sent clerks running into the night clutching armfuls of burning manifests. The *Kestrel* had come about for the harbor within four minutes of the first alarm. Anselm had watched her turn from the shadow of the fish market and had allowed himself exactly one breath of triumph before he'd walked out into the torchlight and raised his hands.

Now they had him against a wall, arms wrenched back, a sergeant reading his confession aloud in a flat incredulous voice — *desertion, arson, sabotage, claims regarding the Eleventh's conduct in the field* — and Anselm listened to his own crimes being catalogued and felt, absurdly, light.

The letter against his heart was soaked with sweat now, illegible probably, but it didn't matter. He'd copied it. Three copies. One was already on the *Meridian* in Bess's sewing basket, addressed to a newspaper office in Halden. One had gone to the resistance contact two nights ago. This one they could have. Let them read it. Let them burn it. Paper was the cheapest thing in the world to lose if you'd planted it well.

A staff car ground to a halt at the edge of the square. A tall figure stepped out, unhurried, gloved, precise even at midnight. Commandant Reiner Voss crossed the cobblestones and stopped in front of Anselm and studied him the way, Noor had once told Piet, he studied maps — looking for the error.

"Eleventh Coastal," Voss said. "You deserted from a regiment currently serving under my command."

"Yes, sir."

"And you chose tonight, of all nights, to return."

Anselm held his eyes. Behind the commandant, down at the harbor, the *Kestrel* sat uselessly at her berth, her crew on firefighting duty, her sweep of the southern water forgotten. Somewhere out past the headland, a fishing boat named *Grietje* was crossing open sea with four people aboard who had fed him, sheltered him, argued with him, and never once asked him to be anything other than what he was.

"The tide was right," Anselm said.

Voss considered that for a long moment. Whatever he saw in Anselm's face, he didn't like it, and he didn't understand it, and those two things together made him turn away and snap an order to the guards.

They marched him toward the cells. Anselm went without resisting, counting his footsteps, and somewhere around the twentieth step he started, very quietly, to laugh — because the arithmetic was finished, and it had come out right, and no one had ever taught him that a man could be captured and free in the same minute.

* * *

### PIET

Dawn found the *Grietje* forty miles south, hull wet with spray, running before a kind wind toward the free coast. Lotta slept in the wheelhouse. Hendrik mended a net he would never use, because some hands needed work the way hearts needed hope.

Piet steered. Beside him Noor sat with the true atlas open on her knees, wrapped against the spray, and she had not spoken in an hour.

"He knew," Piet said at last. "Before any of us. He did the sums and he signed the bill."

"I know what he did."

"Do you know what he *bought*?" Piet gestured with his chin at the horizon, the clean water, the growing line of the free coast. "Four lives, minus one berth on a ship we didn't have. Best price anyone ever paid in this family, and he paid it smiling."

Noor's jaw tightened. "I'm going to write him down," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she steadied herself the way she always did, with craft, with purpose. "In the atlas. His real name. His real regiment. What he did tonight and why. Every word true, Piet — the way he deserved to have his name written, once, in a book that tells no lies."

Piet reached over and put his rough hand flat on the page she held, covering the coastline, feeling the paper warm from her grip.

"Then write him big, girl," he said. "Men like him are the only landmarks worth charting."

Ahead, the sun came up over the free coast, and the water turned from iron to gold, and Noor opened the ink case she had carried through fire and flood and blockade, and uncapped the pen, and began.
