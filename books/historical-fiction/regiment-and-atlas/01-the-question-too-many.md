## 1. The Question Too Many

The fog came off the Vellmark river before dawn and did not lift until well past noon, and on the morning Elias Voss began his rounds in the mapmakers' quarter, it lay over Cartwright Lane like a sheet drawn up to a sleeping face. He had walked that lane perhaps two hundred times in his nine years as a census taker, and it never looked quite the same twice. The houses leaned together at odd angles, their upper stories jutting so far out that neighbors could shake hands across the gutter, or spit, depending on the season of their tempers. Signboards creaked. Somewhere behind shuttered windows a kettle whistled and was silenced. The whole street smelled of ink and damp plaster and the faint mineral tang of the paper mills downstream.

Elias carried his ledger under his arm like a breviary. It was bound in oxblood leather gone soft at the corners, its pages ruled by his own hand in columns fine as stitching — name, age, trade, years resident, kin, notes. He trusted this book more than he trusted most people, though he would not have said so aloud, because he understood how such a confession would sound. But there it was: a record did not lie to flatter you, did not soften itself for your comfort, did not change its story when the questioning grew long. A record simply *was*. In a city where the war had ended nine years ago according to the proclamations and not at all according to the empty chairs at every table, Elias Voss had come to believe that the only honest thing left was an accurate count.

*Every person counted once; no person counted twice.* That was the oath they had made him swear at the Registry, hand on the brass stamp, and he had taken it seriously in the way he took most things seriously, which is to say entirely, and without any talent whatsoever for letting go.

He began at the corner house, where old Mistress Aldery dyed wool in colors the sun disapproved of, and worked his way down the lane in the order the addresses demanded, because order was the whole architecture of his faith. The dyer gave him her numbers promptly and her opinions freely — the price of mordant, the insolence of apprentices, the way the commission's new tax assessors looked at her vats as if dye were a form of treason. Elias wrote what she said and some of what she meant, in the narrow Notes column where such things belonged.

Two doors down, the frame-maker Herr Brannick answered in his shirtsleeves despite the cold, arms crossed against his chest, and answered each question precisely and none of them warmly. His household: himself, his wife, one son apprenticed to the glassworks. Elias knew this already. He checked it anyway. Trust, but verify — the Registry's second commandment.

The fog thinned by degrees. Light came down the lane the way water finds a channel, pale and uncertain, catching the wet cobblestones and turning them briefly to hammered tin. A cat regarded Elias from atop a rain barrel with the particular disdain cats reserve for men carrying books. He made his third entry, then his fourth, then his fifth, and it was somewhere in the fifth entry that the small wrongness first brushed against him — not yet a thought, only a texture, like a thread pulled slightly loose in a seam you are not looking at.

The mapmakers' quarter held eleven households subject to enumeration. Nine of them matched the tax rolls exactly, to the person, to the year of residence. This pleased Elias the way a well-set typeface pleases a printer. The tenth was the widow Quennel, who had taken in her sister since spring, an addition the rolls would catch at Michaelmas, and he noted it with satisfaction: the record would be corrected; fairness was a machine that worked, if slowly.

And then there was the eleventh.

The Sterling house stood at the lane's far end, where Cartwright Lane bent toward the millrace, and it was taller than its neighbors and graver, its frontage hung with ivy that had been permitted, year after year, to climb one course higher, as if the house were slowly being archived under green. The sign above the door read **STERLING & MARSH — CARTOGRAPHERS**, though Marsh had been dead six years and the paint had begun to surrender the letters one by one. Elias had known Master Aldous Marsh distantly, the way a census taker knows everyone — by his figures. Three persons in the household: master, apprentice, housekeeper. Then two, after the arrest. Then, per the rolls, still two.

He knocked.

She opened the door herself, which surprised him; he had expected the housekeeper, if there still was one. Yara Sterling was perhaps five-and-twenty, with ink stains on her fingers she had not bothered to hide and a smudge of the same along one jawbone, as though she'd pushed her hair back from her face mid-work. Her eyes were the gray of unsized paper, and they went over Elias with a quick, professional attention — measuring him, he would think later, the way a cartographer measures a coastline, noting where it bends.

"Census," he said, and showed the stamped card. "Elias Voss, Registry Office. A routine revision of households in your quarter."

"Routine." She stepped back to admit him, and something in the word turned over once, like a stone in a streambed, and settled again. "We've nothing changed since spring."

"Changes are what I look for."

Inside, the house smelled of glue size and cold tea and, beneath both, the dry vanilla breath of old paper — a smell Elias loved without admitting he loved it, because it was the smell of things set down and kept. The front room was a workshop surrendered to winter: a long table under a scarred linen cover, a wall rack of rolled maps labeled in a cramped hand, shutters half-closed against the damp. On the table, beneath the cover's edge, he glimpsed the corner of a sheet ruled in fine lines, and the glimpse snagged in him the way a splinter snags, though he could not have said why. She moved, casually, and the cover shifted, and the sheet was gone from view.

"Your household," Elias said, opening the ledger. "Miss Yara Sterling, cartographer, twenty-six years resident—"

"Twenty-one. I came at five."

He corrected it. The nib scratched. "Occupation?"

"Apprentice. My master is dead."

"So the rolls indicate. And your housekeeper? Marta Ilves, per the last enumeration."

"Gone home. To her people. Across the border." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice — too quick to name, present long enough to notice. "Last autumn."

"I see." He wrote it. *Ilves, M., departed, destination across-border.* And then, because his pen was honest and his habit was honesty, he added in the Notes column: *unverified.*

It was when he began totaling that the loose thread finally pulled taut.

The Sterling house was assessed for duties on the basis of four hearths. Four hearths meant the assessors expected four persons, or had expected them once, and assessments were amended slowly, grudgingly, the way all official arithmetic resented subtraction. The tax rolls listed three names: Marsh, deceased; Sterling; Ilves. With Ilves gone, that left two living persons in a house taxed for four.

"Forgive me," Elias said, and heard in his own voice the mildness he used for delicate questions, the tone of a man asking about a leak rather than a flood. "The assessment on this property assumes four occupants. Even allowing for your late master and the departure of Mrs. Ilves, that accounts for—" He stopped, recalculated, though he had already done it twice in his head while walking down the lane, and gotten the same answer both times, and a third time just now, hoping the arithmetic might have the decency to change. "That accounts for two fewer than the hearths suggest."

The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, a cart ground past through the thinning fog, and the house shivered faintly around them, the way old houses do, as if listening.

"Rooms stand empty," Yara Sterling said. "Empty rooms are still assessed. You know how the commission loves its hearths."

"I do know." He smiled, to put her at ease, and saw that the smile did not put her at ease, and that she noticed his noticing, and that neither of them acknowledged any of it. "Only — a house of this size, kept warm in four rooms, with a workshop of this scale. An apprentice alone could hardly manage the trade's output. Most cartographers keep a journeyman at least. Sometimes two."

"Trade's quiet. Has been, since—" She gestured vaguely eastward, toward the border no one named and everyone measured distances from. "Peace hasn't been good for maps. Nobody wants new ones of places they already lost."

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, a very good answer — wry, plausible, delivered with the flatness of someone who has said it before to other questioners. Elias wrote it down. And the writing of it was the problem, because his pen recorded not only her words but the small interval before them, the half-second in which her eyes had gone, involuntarily, to the floorboards near the eastern wall. Not to the covered table. To the *floor.*

He finished the column. Two persons. He drew his closing line beneath it — straight, ruled, unambiguous — and found his hand hesitated over the figure 2 the way a man hesitates over a signature he does not fully trust.

"One more matter," he said. "Purely administrative. The rolls carry an older entry for this address. Peacetime registers, pre-war. They list a fourth name — Dain Corvath, lodger, soldier on convalescent leave. The entry was never formally closed. Do you recall him?"

The silence lasted three seconds. He counted them, because counting was what he did instead of breathing when the world went strange.

"I was a child," she said. "Soldiers passed through constantly, that summer. I don't remember lodgers."

"No," Elias said mildly. "I suppose you wouldn't."

He gathered his ledger, bowed the small precise bow the Registry taught, and let himself out into a lane that had emerged fully from the fog while he was indoors, blinking, washed in hard clean light that showed every crack in the cobbles. The cat had gone. The kettle smell had gone. Everything ordinary had resumed its ordinary shape, and Elias walked back toward the Registry with his book under his arm and the number 2 sitting in his mind like a pebble in a boot.

Four hearths. Two souls. One name unclosed in the ledgers of a war that had officially ended and unofficially refused to.

A discrepancy so small that any reasonable man would set it down as clerical drift — a stale assessment, an unamended roll, the usual sediment that accumulates wherever officials meet time.

Elias Voss was not entirely a reasonable man. He was, by his own quiet accounting, a man who believed that records were the last fair thing left standing in Vellmark, and that a census taker who ignored a discrepancy was a man who had chosen, in however small a way, to lie. That evening he sat late at the Registry among the other lamps, and he pulled the pre-war registers, and he ran his finger down to the Sterling house, and there it was again in another hand, another decade:

*Dain Corvath. Lodger. Discharged to quarters, convalescent.*

And beside it, in ink darker than the rest, a later annotation he had never seen in any register before, stamped with a seal he did not recognize — a seal bearing the arms of the Armistice Commission itself:

*Entry superseded. Do not pursue.*

Elias read it twice. Around him the Registry murmured with pens and coughs and the settling of paper, the great honest machinery of the count going about its work, and he sat perfectly still, holding the page, feeling the first cold edge of the question that would not, from that night forward, ever quite leave him alone:

Superseded by whose order?

And pursued by whom, if he did?
