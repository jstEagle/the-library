## 2. What the Master Died For

Ten years before, the night the soldiers came for Master Aldous Renwick, it had been raining in Vellmark — not the soft autumn rain that made the gutters sing, but a hard, driving rain that came off the border hills sideways and turned Cartwright Lane into a brown river. Yara remembered that because she had been mopping water from under the door when the knocking began, and the mop was still in her hands when they took him.

Three knocks. Then three more. Then the door itself shuddering on its hinges.

She had looked at her master across the lamplit workshop. He had been bent over the great table where the atlas lived by day, disguised among lesser maps like a wolf among sheep, and he had not seemed surprised. That was the thing she would carry longest, through all the years after: he had not seemed surprised. He had set down his pen with the care of a man finishing a sentence rather than interrupting one, squared the nib against the rest, and said, quietly, as though remarking on the weather:

"The floorboards, girl. Under the drying rack. And whatever they ask you, you are an apprentice who grinds pigment and sweeps floors. You know nothing. Grief makes people stupid; use yours."

Then he went to open the door himself, so they could not say later that she had let them in.

* * *

There were six of them, in the grey coats of the Armistice Commission's enforcement arm, though everyone in the quarter called them what they were: ink-men. Men who erased. Their officer carried papers in an oilskin wallet and read the warrant aloud while rain blew in past his shoulders and spotted the floor Yara had just cleaned.

Aldous Renwick, it said. Cartographer. On suspicion of producing cartographic materials contrary to the Armistice Accords of the ninth year, articles concerning the delineation of borders pending final ratification.

"Pending ratification," Aldous repeated, mild as milk. "Nine years pending. A slow document."

"You'll come with us, sir."

"I expect I will." He pulled his coat from the peg — the good coat, the one with the worn cuffs he refused to let Yara mend because mending, he said, was admitting time had won — and paused at the threshold to look back at her. Only at her. The ink-men might as well have been furniture.

"The Veridian plates need re-inking," he told her. "Third shelf. Don't let them dry out."

It was nonsense. There were no Veridian plates on the third shelf; there never had been. It meant: stay calm, keep working, be ordinary. It meant: I am trusting you with everything I have ever made.

Yara nodded and said, "Yes, Master," in a voice that did not shake until the door closed behind him.

* * *

They did not find the atlas that night, though they spent four hours looking, and she gave them the run of the place because there was nothing else to do and because refusing would have been its own confession. Two of them turned over the flat files where finished surveys slept in their linen wrappers. One went through the pigment cabinet, holding up jars of lapis and vermilion to the light as if truth might be ground into powder and kept in glass. The officer sat in Aldous's chair and asked questions in a bored voice.

How long had she been apprenticed. Seven years. Did the master correspond with anyone beyond the city. Merchants, mostly, and the guild in Corvenna. Had he ever spoken against the Commission. Her master spoke about rivers, she said. Rivers did not take sides.

The officer smiled at that, thin and unpleasant, and wrote something down.

At some point past midnight one of them crossed the room toward the drying rack — and Yara's heart stopped, actually stopped, a fist closing inside her chest — but he only leaned his rifle against the wall beside it and went to warm his hands at the stove. All the while the atlas lay beneath the boards not two paces from him, wrapped in oilcloth, sleeping in the dark with its impossible borders folded up inside it like wings. She stood in the middle of her own workshop and made conversation about pigment prices and thought: *I am standing on my master's life's work and I must not look down.*

They left near dawn. The officer's last words were that the master would be held for questioning, nothing more, that she should keep the shop open, that order was a kindness. Then the grey coats were gone down Cartwright Lane into the rain, and Yara locked the door, and slid down against it with both hands pressed over her mouth so the neighbors would not hear whatever sound came out of her.

He did not come back. Not that day, not the next. On the third day a clerk arrived with a receipt for "confiscated materials" — a list so vague it could have meant anything or nothing — and a notice that Aldous Renwick had been transferred to a facility whose name was blacked out with a stamp. On the eleventh day, the notice of death by fever arrived, unsigned, undated except by the stamp, with instructions for disposal of effects.

No body. No grave. Eleven days, and a word like *fever*, which in those years covered a multitude.

She was nineteen years old. She went down into the cellar, knelt on the cold stone, and lifted the floorboards her master had shown her once, years before, saying only, *remember this spot, girl, in case I'm ever indisposed.* She drew out the oilcloth parcel and carried it upstairs to the light, and unwrapped it, and saw what he had died for.

* * *

The atlas was not beautiful, which was the first shock. She had expected beauty. What she found instead was something more terrible: clarity. Forty-one sheets, each one a region along the whole contested length of the frontier, drawn in Aldous's fine, unornamented hand. And on every sheet, in red — the color reserved for corrections, the color of a schoolmaster's pen — the true lines.

Not the armistice line, the famous Line of the Ninth Year, the elegant compromise that had ended the shooting without ending anything else. The actual lines. Where the villages really lay. Where the rivers really ran, including the two tributaries the official maps had simply deleted, along with every village that depended on them. Survey dates in the corners going back twelve years — before the war, during the war, after — each one witnessed, each one signed, each one a small stone of evidence fitted against the next.

And in the margins, in Aldous's tiny ciphered hand, the names. Villages. Populations. Dates of depopulation. One entry, circled twice, that she read four times before she understood it: a village called Serevan Hollow, recorded as surveyed in the seventh year of the war, population entered as *nil* — and beneath it, in fresher ink, *evacuated under commission order 114-C, see annex*. An order no public record contained. A village that had existed, and then, on paper, had never existed at all.

She understood, sitting there in the grey morning light with the atlas open across her knees, why the ink-men had come. This was not a map. This was an accusation forty-one pages long. If it reached the armistice commission — the real commission, the one that still met, still delayed, still signed nothing after nine years of peace-that-was-not-peace — it would prove that the border had been drawn to punish, that the war's end had been strangled in committee, that men in grey coats had unmade villages with stamps and stamps alone.

Or it would get whoever carried it killed, exactly as it had gotten Aldous killed.

She rewrapped it. She put it back beneath the floorboards. She swept the cellar, lit the stove, opened the shutters, and ground pigment, because grief makes people stupid and hers was going to have to be very clever indeed.

* * *

Dain Corvath came to her in the spring, or what passed for spring in Vellmark, when the thaw turned the lanes to mud and the river ran high and brown. He knocked the way soldiers knock — two raps, brisk, apologetic — and when she opened the door he was leaning against the frame as though the frame were holding him up rather than the reverse. A soldier, yes: she could see that even before she saw the uniform under the civilian coat, the faded grey-green of the Ninth Frontier Regiment, mud-stained, one sleeve dark with old blood that had soaked and dried and soaked again. Younger than the wound made him look. Twenty-three, perhaps twenty-four.

"Master Renwick's apprentice," he said. Not a question. His voice was roughened, as if he'd been talking to himself for a long way. "I served under him. Before. He surveyed our sector." A breath. "I heard what happened. I walked here to hear it properly, from someone who knew him."

She should have sent him away. Every instinct the last eleven days had taught her said send him away. But he was bleeding again, she could see it now, fresh blood seeping through the old crust at his forearm, and he swayed slightly on the doorstep like a man who had used up his last mile somewhere on the border road, and she thought of her master saying *keep the shop open*, meaning *keep faith*, and stepped aside to let him in.

His name, he said, was Dain Corvath. He had been a guide, once — born in the hill country the maps argued over, a boy who knew the paths before he knew his letters — until the regiment made him a scout and the war made him a survivor. He had met Aldous Renwick in the fourth year of the fighting, guiding a survey party through country no one else would cross, and he had spent three weeks watching the old man measure a world that was trying to kill them, patiently, precisely, as if accuracy were a form of prayer.

"He told me something, near the end of it," Dain said, while she cleaned his arm and he stared at the ceiling and breathed through the sting. "He said most people think maps are about land. They're not. They're about whether people count. A map is a list of everyone the drawer decided matters." His jaw tightened. "Our village isn't on the army maps anymore. Serevan Hollow. They struck it. After the evacuation. As if striking the name strikes out the people."

Yara's hands went still over the bandage.

"I came to ask him," Dain went on, "if it was true. What he'd found. Whether anyone was keeping a true account. And now they tell me he's dead, and—" He looked at her then, directly, and his eyes were the grey of the border hills in winter, and there was nothing in them of a man asking for a favor. There was a man asking for a verdict. "Was it true? What he was making?"

She had promised herself, kneeling on the cellar stones, that no living soul would ever learn what lay beneath her floorboards. She had rehearsed the denial, the blank face, the apprentice who knows nothing. And now here was this half-dead boy asking her to spend the truth like coin, and every part of her that loved her master rose up and said: *this is what it was for.*

"There is an atlas," she said.

She watched the words land in him. Watched him close his eyes.

"It has your village in it," she said. "Every village. The true borders. The survey dates. The orders they've buried. My master spent nine years proving that the war didn't end — that it was *kept* from ending — and the proof is under my feet right now, and the men who killed him are still looking for it."

"They'll sign the armistice eventually," Dain said slowly. "The commission. Everyone says this year, maybe next."

"And it will be signed over false maps, and the lie will become permanent, and no one will ever be able to say otherwise." She heard her own voice harden into something she barely recognized — her master's voice, coming out of her mouth. "Unless the true maps arrive first. Unless they're *part* of the signing. Entered into record. Witnessed."

Dain sat forward, wincing at his arm, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere down the lane a cart groaned through the mud, an ordinary sound in an ordinary city, all of it built on a floorboard and a lie.

"When?" he asked. "When do we deliver it?"

"Not yet." She said it gently, because he flinched. "You don't understand what it is yet. Right now it's a dead man's paper and a living woman's death warrant. Delivered wrong, it vanishes into the same black stamp that ate him. It has to go to the commission at the right moment, in front of witnesses, too public to bury. It has to be *unrefusable*." She took a breath. "When there is peace to deliver it to. Real peace, or the moment before it's signed. When the whole world is watching the table and the ink. That's when. I swear it. On my master's name — I swear it, Dain Corvath. Your village goes back on the map. All of them do."

He studied her for a long time. Then he nodded once, soldier-fashion, a bargain sealed.

"I'll hold you to it," he said. "However long."

He stayed three days, sleeping in the loft above the workshop, eating what she could spare, telling her in the evenings about the hill country and the paths and the village that no longer officially existed — names she wrote down later, secretly, in the margins of a practice chart, adding them back to the world one letter at a time. Then he went, before dawn, back toward the border and the regiment and the war that would not admit it was over.

She stood in the doorway watching the mud take his footprints, and she felt the promise settle onto her like a coat she would wear for years.

Ten years. She kept the atlas beneath the floorboards. She kept the shop open. She grew older and more careful, and learned to forge a hand as well as she could draw one, and answered questions from clerks and officers with the smooth blankness her master had taught her, and every night before sleep she laid her palm flat against the boards and felt the truth underneath, patient as seed.

Until the morning a census taker named Elias Voss climbed the steps of number seven Cartwright Lane with a ledger under his arm, and knocked — two raps, brisk, almost apologetic — and asked her one question too many.
