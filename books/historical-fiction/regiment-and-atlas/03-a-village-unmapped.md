## 3. A Village Unmapped

The rain had stopped by morning, but the mapmakers' quarter still smelled of it — wet slate and river mud and the iron tang of gutters running brown down Cartwright Lane. Elias Voss walked the quarter with his satchel dry under his oilcloth coat, his boots choosing the higher stones out of habit, the way a man's feet learn a city even when his mind is elsewhere.

He had spent three evenings with the tax rolls since his visit to number nine, and the discrepancy had not dissolved. It never did. That was the nature of discrepancies: they were patient where men were not. One name on the rolls, absent from the household. *Dain Corvath.* The ink in the register was old, the entry dated six years back, when the census had last been taken in full. A lodger, then. Or family. The relationship column held only a dash, which was itself unusual — Elias had never met a clerk who liked leaving columns empty, and so an empty column meant someone had wanted it empty, or someone had been careless, and carelessness in that quarter seemed unlikely given everything else he'd seen there.

He climbed the steps to number nine and knocked twice, as before.

Yara Sterling opened the door already dressed for work, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her forearms smudged with ochre and a blue-black pigment he didn't recognize. She looked at him the way she had looked at him the first time: without fear, without welcome. An appraisal.

"Mr. Voss."

"Mistress Sterling. I've some follow-up questions. It should take no longer than yesterday."

"Nothing ever takes no longer than yesterday." But she stepped back and let him in.

* * *

The workshop smelled of linseed and cut paper and something older beneath it — dust in the grain of the floorboards themselves, decades of it. Maps hung from every wall like trophies or like saints' icons; Elias could not decide which. He set his ledger on the cleared end of her worktable, and she watched him do it, and he understood that she was noting exactly how close he stood to what she did not want seen.

"You keep the room warm," he said, opening the ledger. "For the inks?"

"For my hands." She flexed them, pigment cracking at the knuckles. "Cold hands make trembling lines, and trembling lines make bad maps, and bad maps get people lost. Ask me your questions, Mr. Voss. You came all this way through the mud."

He consulted his notes, though he did not need to. "Your household. The tax roll of six years ago lists two adults resident at this address. Yourself, and one Dain Corvath. Yesterday you told me you live alone."

"I do live alone."

"Then when did Mr. Corvath leave?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped compass — small, precise, pointing somewhere she did not want to go. Yara did not flinch. She picked up a rag and began cleaning her hands, working the rag between each finger as though the answer might be hiding there.

"Six years is a long census ago," she said. "Men move. Men die. Men go to war."

"The war ended nine years ago."

"The war," she said mildly, "never officially ended. I'd have thought a census man would know that. You write it on every form."

Elias wrote nothing. He looked at her over the top of the ledger, and she met his look with the flat patience of a woman who had answered officials before and expected to answer them again.

"When did he leave?" he asked again.

"Why does it matter?"

"Because the record matters." He heard how it sounded even as he said it — the catechism of his trade, worn smooth by repetition — and pressed on anyway. "A household pays tax on its members. If a man was listed and has gone, the record should say when, and why, and where to. Not because anyone suspects anything. Because a record with a hole in it isn't a record. It's a rumor wearing a record's clothes."

Something moved behind her eyes at that. Not anger. Recognition, almost — as if he had quoted someone she once knew.

"My master used to say something similar," she said. "'A map with a blank space invites conquest.'" She tossed the rag onto the table. "Dain Corvath left in the spring after the last full census. He was conscripted. The army took him, and the army keeps its own records. Write to them."

"I did."

The word hung there. Yara went very still, the stillness of a person who has practiced stillness until it became her native language.

"And?" she said.

"There is no conscription record for a Dain Corvath." Elias turned the ledger toward her, though the page held only his own neat notes. "There is no service record. There is no death notice, no discharge, no prisoner listing. There is—" he paused, and made himself finish it— "a decree of designation. Enemy combatant, retroactive to the date of the armistice talks. Under that decree, all records pertaining to him were transferred to the commission and sealed."

"You seem very well informed for a census taker."

"It took four days and two bribed clerks to be informed this well. That's rather the opposite of sealed."

She laughed — one short note, genuine and quickly folded away. Then she sat down across from him, and Elias saw the change come over her, the way a change comes over weather: not sudden, but total. Her carefulness settled around her like a coat being buttoned.

"Mr. Voss. What is it you actually want?"

"The decree," he said. "I want to see the decree that declared him an enemy combatant. Every designation I have ever processed cites its legal instrument. This one doesn't. When I asked the commission archive for the instrument, I was shown an empty drawer and told the document was 'in review.' It has been in review for nine years."

"Then perhaps it's a very thorough review."

"Or perhaps," Elias said quietly, "there is no decree. Perhaps there is a name someone needed to make disappear, and a stamp that exists only in the way God exists — everyone swears by it, no one has seen it."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged in the wet wind. Yara studied him for a long moment, and Elias had the uncomfortable sense of being measured against something — weighed on scales whose markings only she could read.

"You're either an honest man," she said at last, "or the best actor the commission ever sent into this quarter. I genuinely cannot tell which."

"That," said Elias, "is the most honest thing anyone in this city has said to me in nine years of honest work."

* * *

She gave him tea, because refusing would have been its own admission, and while it steeped she talked — about pigments, about the survey of the eastern fens her master had completed before his death, about the price of vellum — an unbroken, pleasant, entirely evasive current of talk that Elias recognized as expertly built as any levee. Twice he tried to breach it. Twice the current simply carried him past the breach and onward to the drying qualities of gum arabic.

It was on his way to the door that he saw it.

A map lay half-finished on the side bench — not one of the hanging surveys but a working sheet, a coastal chart, and along its lower margin ran a line of handwriting too fine and regular to be annotation. Elias bent toward it without meaning to, and Yara's hand came down over the margin like a bird settling, casual, absolute.

"Smudge risk," she said. "Wet hands."

"Of course." He straightened. "One last question, Mistress Sterling, and then I'll trouble you no more today."

"You've said that before."

"Dain Corvath. In the old peacetime registers — before the war, I mean — he appears in the parish rolls of a village called Havel's Ford. Population two hundred and eleven. It's the same village that appears in the commission's annexed territories with population zero. No village. No dead. No record of anyone from it, except him." Elias closed his ledger. "Either the village existed and the commission erased it, or it never existed and someone invented a man from nowhere and then declared him an enemy. Both of those are lies, Mistress Sterling. They're just lies pointed in opposite directions."

Yara said nothing for a long time. Outside, the gutters ticked their way toward empty. When she finally spoke, her voice was quiet and perfectly level, and that levelness frightened him more than shouting would have.

"Some questions don't have answers, Mr. Voss. Some questions only have consequences. You should ask yourself which kind you're carrying."

"I carry records," he said. "They're heavier than they look."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and turned back. "For what it's worth — I'm not the commission. Whatever you think I am."

"I know exactly what you are," Yara Sterling said. "You're a man who believes the paper tells the truth. And someday soon, Mr. Voss, you're going to find paper that doesn't — and you'll have to choose which lie you serve. I hope, for your sake, you choose slowly."

He walked down Cartwright Lane through the smell of wet stone, her words walking beside him the whole way. Behind him, in the warm workshop on number nine, he knew she was already moving furniture.

And three streets away, at the commission annex, a clerk he had bribed for four days of truth sent up a message that a census official had been asking about Havel's Ford — and the message, written on commission paper under a commissioner's seal, traveled faster than any census Elias Voss had ever taken.
