## 5. Dain at the Door

The knock came at dusk, when the light through the workshop windows had gone the color of old pewter, and Yara Sterling was grinding walnut ink for the marginalia she had no right to be making.

Three knocks. Not the brisk double-rap of a commission agent — those men knocked the way they did everything, as if the door owed them money. This was slower. Heavier. The knock of someone who had been walking a long time and no longer believed doors opened for anyone.

She stood very still in the middle of the room, brush suspended, and let the silence answer for her. Outside, Vellmark was settling into its evening noises: cart wheels on wet cobbles, a fishwife calling her last prices, somewhere the bell of the customs house marking the hour with its cracked, apologetic tone. Nine years of that bell. Nine years of learning which sounds meant danger and which merely meant the city going about its business.

The knock came again. Then a voice, low and ruined, speaking through the wood as if the wood were an old friend:

"Yara. It's raining, and I'm tired, and I would very much like to stop being careful."

She knew the voice before she knew the name. That was the terrible thing about it — her body recognized him first, some animal ledger opening in her chest, ten years of entries reconciled in a single breath. She crossed the room and lifted the bar, and there he stood in the doorway, dripping.

Dain Corvath had been twenty-four years old the last time she saw him, lying in her master's cellar with a musket ball in his shoulder and the smell of gunpowder still in his hair. The man on her step now was thinner than memory allowed, weathered to the brown of tanned leather, his hair gone grey at the temples though he could not have seen forty. A soldier's coat, officer's cut, but stripped of insignia — the buttons torn off, leaving pale ghost-threads where rank had lived. His boots were wrong. Too big, mismatched soles. Boots taken from a dead man or bought from one who wished he were.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. It came out flat, not cruel. Just true.

"A lot of people are supposed to be things." He looked past her into the warm lamplight of the workshop, and something in his face flickered — grief or hunger, hard to say which. "May I come in, or do we conduct this in the street? Because I've spent three days conducting things in the street, and my legs have opinions."

* * *

She fed him before she questioned him. It was what her master would have done — Master Aldous Renner, who used to say that a starving man's testimony was worth exactly what you fed him for it, so feed him well and honestly. She set out bread and the last of the smoked fish, pickled onions, a pot of barley soup she stretched with water from the kettle. Dain ate the way soldiers eat: fast, completely, without pleasure, as though eating were a duty being discharged under fire.

Yara sat across from him with her hands folded and watched him eat and tried not to count the ways this could end.

"The decree," she said, when the bowl was empty. "The one that names you an enemy combatant. I've never seen it. Elias Voss couldn't find it. Half the clerks in the customs house swear it doesn't exist."

"Because it doesn't." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "There was a list, after the ceasefire. Names forwarded to the commission by officers who needed their own records cleaned. Men who'd seen things. Men who'd kept documents." He smiled, and it was an ugly thing, all teeth and no warmth. "I kept documents, Yara."

"Your dispatches."

"My dispatches." He leaned back, and the chair creaked under him like it was complaining too. "Order 114. You remember me telling you, that night in the cellar? The order that moved the Third Column east two weeks *before* the border villages burned. Two weeks. Someone gave the order knowing. Knowing, and then the commission sat on the inquiry for nine years because half of them signed the logistics requisitions themselves."

"I remember." She remembered everything. She remembered pressing his good hand between both of hers while her master climbed the stairs to bar the door against the patrol, promising, *when there is peace to deliver them to.* She had been nineteen and certain that peace was a place, like Vellmark, like the coast, somewhere with a fixed position on a map you could reach by traveling long enough.

"And now?" she said. "Now you want them delivered. Now. Three weeks — maybe less — before the armistice session. With commission agents crawling the quarter asking questions about atlases."

"Now," Dain said, "or never. That's the arithmetic of it, Yara. The armistice gets signed with the old maps annexed — the maps everyone agreed to lie with — and Order 114 goes into the record as a supply error, a footnote, a tragedy nobody ordered. And every man who moved that column gets to die respectable." He spread his hands. "Or your atlas goes in first. Truth entered before treaty. They can't redact what's already spoken aloud in session."

"They can do worse than redact."

"Yes," he said quietly. "They can. I know."

* * *

She made him a bed in the cellar — the same cellar, though the bloodstains had long since been scrubbed from the flagstones, or perhaps they had simply sunk too deep to find. She brought down blankets that smelled of camphor and the lamp, and when she turned to go up the stairs he caught her wrist. Gently. A question more than a grip.

"You hesitated at the door," he said. "Ten years, and you hesitated."

"Ten years is a long time to keep a promise to a man declared dead."

"I'm not dead."

"No," she said. "You're worse. You're a story other people are telling. The commission tells you as a traitor. Whoever sent those men with commissioner's seals tells you as a target." She pulled her wrist free, not unkindly. "And tonight you walked into my house and told yourself as a patriot. So forgive me if I need a night to work out which version is standing in my kitchen eating my fish."

He laughed — a short, startled bark, the first human sound he'd made since arriving. "You've gotten sharp."

"I've gotten alone. It does the same thing." At the top of the stairs she paused. "There's a census man, Dain. Elias Voss. He's already asked about you once. He'll ask again. If he finds you here—"

"He won't."

"That's not a promise anyone can make."

"It's the only kind worth making," he said, settling back against the wall, and closed his eyes like a man accustomed to sleeping wherever sleep found him. "Go on. Lock the door. Dream of maps."

* * *

But she didn't sleep. She sat at her desk with the atlas open beneath the lamp, her master's true borders glowing faint gold under the walnut ink, and she listened to the small sounds of the house — the tick of the stove iron cooling, rain starting again against the glass, and below, through the floorboards, the uneven rhythm of a man breathing in her cellar.

Patriot or pawn. She turned it over and over like a coin she couldn't stop flipping.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said to him: the men with the commissioner's seals had come asking about the atlas *before* Dain arrived. Which meant either they were hunting him and expected him to run to her — in which case every step he'd taken toward Vellmark had been tracked, permitted, funneled, and her house was already a trap with its jaws politely open —

— or they weren't hunting him at all, and the two arrivals, his and theirs, were coincidence. And Yara Sterling had stopped believing in coincidence the year her master was dragged out of this same house at midnight for the crime of drawing rivers where the state insisted there weren't any.

A third possibility sat in the corner of the thought like a spider she refused to look at directly: that Dain himself didn't know which he was. That a man can be aimed without ever feeling the hand on his shoulder. That they had let him go — let him walk three hundred miles through checkpoint country on forged papers she didn't dare ask about — precisely so that he would lead them to whatever he came to collect.

She looked down at the atlas. Her master's hand, steady even in secret. *Truth entered into record,* he used to say, *is the only fortress that doesn't need garrisoning.* But fortresses get besieged, and garrisons starve, and she was one woman with a lamp and a floorboard.

Below her feet, the breathing changed. Dain was awake. She knew it the way she knew a misprinted contour line — instantly, in the bones.

* * *

In the morning there was frost on the inside of the window, and a knock at the door that was nothing like Dain's knock. Brisk. Double-rapped. Entitled.

Yara set down her tea untasted. Through the frosted pane she could see a shape on her step, grey-coated, and behind it, further up Cartwright Lane, a second shape standing beside a commission motorcar with its engine running — idling in the cold morning like a patient animal.

She glanced once toward the cellar hatch, visible through the open kitchen door, its edge just proud of the floorboards.

Then she smoothed her apron, arranged her face into the mild blankness of a mapmaker's apprentice with nothing to declare, and went to answer the door.

The man on her step held up a document sealed in green wax, and smiled with perfect official courtesy, and said:

"Mistress Sterling. We're told you've recently had a visitor."
