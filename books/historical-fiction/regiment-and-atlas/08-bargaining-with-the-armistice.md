## 8. Bargaining with the Armistice

The clerk's name was Anselm Reiter, and he smelled of lamp oil and fear.

Yara had watched him for three days before she approached him — a small man in a gray coat who took his supper alone at the Tallow & Compass, always at the corner table by the window, always with the commission ledger open beside his plate as though proximity to it could protect him. He had ink stains on his right cuff in four different shades, which meant he worked late and corrected often. He had a wedding band worn thin as wire. And twice, when he thought no one was looking, he had looked at her.

That was how she knew he wanted to talk.

She chose the third evening, when rain came down the windows of the tavern in long silver threads and the lamplight turned everything the color of old parchment. She set down across from him without asking, and he did not object, which was itself an answer.

"You're the mapmaker's girl," he said. His voice was quiet, pitched under the noise of the room, the way men learned to speak in Vellmark after the war — everything important said at half-volume.

"I'm the mapmaker."

"Your master is dead."

"My master's work isn't." She kept her hands still on the table. Stillness was a language too; her master had taught her that before he taught her lettering. "And you've been looking for it for eleven days, Herr Reiter. Your agents searched Cartwright Lane on Tuesday. They took Marta Ilves instead of me because they don't know what they're searching for. I do."

Reiter's hand moved toward the ledger, then stopped. In the lamplight Yara saw the tremor in it, fine as a hairline crack in glazing.

"Not here," he whispered.

"No," she agreed. "Somewhere you choose. Somewhere with one door."

He chose the record annex on Saltern Street — of course he did; clerks trusted only rooms built to hold paper — and he chose midnight, when even the gulls slept. Yara arrived with the atlas wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat, its weight against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and found him waiting among the shelved decades with a single candle and two chairs that had not been set out for anyone in years.

* * *

The negotiation lasted until the candle guttered.

"The armistice session convenes in nine days," Reiter said. He sat with his back to the shelves, as though the accumulated truth of ten thousand documents could shield him from the document in front of him. "The commissioners arrive from both capitals. There will be signatures. There will be toasts. There will be nothing in those signatures about Kestrel's Hollow, or the survey dates, or the eleven months the fighting continued after both armies knew the border had shifted."

"And whose fault is that?"

"Mine," he said simply, and the word fell between them like a stone into deep water. "Not mine alone. But mine. I certified the transcripts, Frau Sterling. I entered the amended dates into the rolls myself. I told myself it was temporary — that peace first, truth after. Peace first. Truth can wait. That is what we all told ourselves, and the waiting became a profession."

Yara unwrapped the atlas slowly, letting the oilcloth fall open the way her master had taught her to open a wound — all at once, so the pain came whole and could be survived. The leather cover caught the candlelight, scarred where a boot had pressed it into floorboards nine years ago.

"One copy exists," she said. "You're looking at it. My master died carrying it, and I have spent nine years finishing what he couldn't deliver. Here are my terms. First: Dain Corvath receives full amnesty — written, sealed, dated before the armistice session, so that he walks into that chamber a free man. Second: Marta Ilves goes home. Tonight if possible; tomorrow at the latest. Third: the atlas enters the record entire, every page, every marginal note, appended to the treaty as an annex in my master's name."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange you get the only thing that can make your signature mean anything."

Reiter looked at the atlas for a long time. When he finally reached out and touched the cover, his fingers were gentle, almost reverent — the touch of a man who had spent his life among documents and had never held one that mattered.

"I believed you'd burn it," he said.

"Some nights I wanted to."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because ash convinces no one." She drew the atlas toward herself again, and something flickered across his face — loss, or relief, she could not tell. "I need your answer, Herr Reiter. Not the commission's. Yours."

He gave it in the way of clerks: he pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and began to write. Amnesty for Dain Corvath, enemy designation revoked, decree of the ninth year annulled as improperly issued — he wrote the phrase *improperly issued* twice, pressing hard, as though the nib could carve the words into the world rather than merely onto paper. Then the order for Marta Ilves' release, signed with the authority he clearly possessed and had never dared to use.

"There," he said, blowing on the ink. "Done. Both instruments will be executed within two days."

Yara felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the raid — not trust, nothing so generous, but the sense of a door actually opening rather than merely being described to her. "Then tomorrow you'll see the atlas."

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

But his eyes went back to the open pages, to the true borders drawn in her master's patient hand, and Yara saw something move behind them that she would spend the night failing to name.

* * *

She named it the next evening, when she returned with the atlas and found the copying desk prepared.

It stood in the center of the annex like an altar: lamps arranged in a careful crescent, blotting papers squared, pens laid out in ranks, and beside them — this was the detail that stopped her breath — a stack of fresh folio sheets, ruled and headed in commission format, each one labeled in Reiter's precise hand: *Annex A, Revised.*

"Revised," Yara said.

Reiter did not look up from arranging his pens. "A fair term. You'll want the pages legible, consistent with treaty formatting—"

"Herr Reiter." She set the atlas on the desk but kept her hand flat upon it, claiming it the way one claims ground. "Show me a revised page."

He hesitated. It was a small hesitation, less than a breath, but she had spent nine years reading silences the way other people read print, and she read this one perfectly. Slowly, he drew a folio from the stack and laid it beside the atlas, open to the page her master had titled *Surveys Contradicted: The Eleventh Month.*

On the left, the original — her master's cramped, furious notation, the dates that proved both armies had known the war was over while the shelling continued. On the right, Reiter's revision: the same facts, softened, the damning dates rendered vague — *discrepancies in the record of the period* — the deliberate prolongation dissolved into bureaucratic weather. No accusation anywhere. No one to blame. A truth filed down until nothing sharp remained.

"This isn't transcription," Yara said. Her voice came out very quiet, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "This is erasure with better handwriting."

"It's survival." For the first time he raised his eyes to hers, and she saw that he had not slept. "Frau Sterling — Yara. Listen to me. If that chamber reads these pages as written, the commissioners from both capitals will bury the treaty to bury the blame. There will be no armistice. There will be a ninth year of war, a tenth. Do you understand? Your truth will kill thousands before it saves one."

"My truth already cost—" She stopped. Marta Ilves sat in a cell because of it. Dain was starving in a flooded cellar because of it. Her master had died face-down on a road because of it. "Everything it has cost has been paid by people who never got to decide whether it should be spoken. That ends now."

"Then you doom the treaty."

"Then I let the commissioners decide whether they want peace or comfort. That's their work, Herr Reiter. Yours was to keep the record honest, and you just showed me how well you've done it."

He flinched as though she had struck him. And here was the thing Yara could not turn away from, the thing that made her hands shake as she gathered the atlas back into its oilcloth: he was not a villain. She had imagined her enemy for nine years — commissioners with cold faces, soldiers with torches — and none of them were this: a tired man with thin wedding band and four shades of ink on his cuffs, who had freed a prisoner and pardoned an outlaw yesterday and meant to soften history today, sincerely believing it mercy.

Power, she thought, does not corrupt the cruel first. It corrupts the kind, because the kind can always find a reason.

"Keep your instruments," she said. "Free Corvath. Free Ilves. I won't take back what you've signed. But the atlas comes to that chamber whole, or it doesn't come at all — and if it doesn't come, there is nothing on your revised pages worth signing."

"They'll strike it down. They'll strike *you* down."

"Perhaps." She wrapped the last fold. "But my master taught me that a map is a promise made in ink. You don't get to redraw it because the country changed its mind."

She was at the door when he spoke again, and his voice had gone strange — hollowed out, the voice of a man hearing his own reflection break.

"The session convenes in eight days. The commissioners arrive in seven. And Yara — the chairman's advance party landed this morning. They are already asking about you. By name."

The rain had stopped outside. Through the annex window, Vellmark lay gray and shining under a clearing sky, and somewhere in that brightening city, men with commissioner's seals were saying her name aloud.

Eight days. She pulled her collar up and stepped into the light, counting backward, and wondering how many of those days belonged to her still.
