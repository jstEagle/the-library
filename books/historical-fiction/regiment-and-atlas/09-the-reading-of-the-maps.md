## 9. The Reading of the Maps

The armistice hall had been a granary once, and it still smelled faintly of grain and mouse, as though the building itself remembered a time when what was stored there fed people instead of feeding on them. Yara stood at the edge of the public gallery with the atlas bound against her chest beneath her coat, its corners pressing into her ribs like knuckles. Around her, clerks arranged documents in tidy stacks; commissioners murmured behind a long table draped in green cloth that had been borrowed from some funeral. The morning light came through high windows in pale slats and lay across the floor like drawn blades.

Elias sat two rows below her among the record-keepers, his census ledger open on his knee, his pen uncapped. He did not look up at her. They had agreed on this — no glances, no signals, nothing a watching man could read. But she could see the tension in his shoulders, the careful stillness of his hands, and she knew he was reciting to himself the thing they had rehearsed in her kitchen until dawn: *the record is only fair if someone is willing to stand behind every line of it.*

At the commissioners' table, Commissioner Aldous Vane — the same seal that had appeared on the forged survey orders, the same hand that had signed the decree making Dain Corvath a ghost — adjusted his spectacles and called the session to order. His voice was dry as old vellum.

"The commission convenes to ratify the terms of cessation. The provisional boundary lines have been prepared. Are there objections from the floor?"

Yara's heart beat so loudly she was certain the whole hall could hear it. She thought of her master, Marta Sterling, being led out of the mapmakers' quarter at midnight, her wrists bound, her voice level even then: *They will draw the lines where it profits them, child. Someone must remember where they truly run.* She thought of the neighbor, Brenna Hale, sitting in a commission cell because she had been standing on Cartwright Lane when the agents needed someone to take. She thought of Dain, hidden since the raid in the flooded cellars beneath the tannery, coughing in the dark, waiting for her signal or for soldiers, whichever came first.

She walked down the gallery steps.

The sound of her boots on the stone was very loud. A clerk looked up. Then another. By the time she reached the rope-line separating the public floor from the commissioners' table, half the hall had gone quiet, and Vane was peering at her over his spectacles with the mild annoyance of a man disturbed by weather.

"This session is not open to petitioners."

"I'm not petitioning." Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'm presenting evidence." She drew the atlas from beneath her coat and laid it on the green cloth. The leather cover, scarred and water-stained, looked small under the great seals and banners. "The Sterling Atlas. Compiled over nineteen years by my master, Marta Sterling, Royal Surveyor, who died in custody before she could deliver it. It contains the true borders. Not the provisional ones. The surveyed ones."

A murmur ran through the hall like wind through wheat. Vane's face did not change, but his fingers went still on his papers, and Yara understood suddenly that he had been afraid of exactly this — not of lies exposed, but of truth entered into record where it could no longer be unwritten.

"There are no unsanctioned maps," he said. "All surveys were commissioned through this office."

"Then you won't mind verifying the dates." She opened the atlas to the first plate — the eastern marches, inked in her master's fine, unyielding hand — and turned it toward the table. "Every border here was surveyed between the third and ninth years of the war. Every one. Compare those dates to your own commission records, Commissioner, and tell me how your office certified boundaries that could not have been surveyed until years after the fighting moved on. Tell me how the line here—" her finger touched the village of Ashenmere, its little cluster of rooftops rendered with heartbreaking care, each house individually drawn— "came to be drawn through a settlement that your own census shows held four hundred and eleven souls. Four hundred and eleven, Commissioner. And then it held none."

The silence was total now. Somewhere above, a pigeon shifted on a beam and dislodged a small rain of dust into the light.

"You are describing matters beyond your competence," Vane said quietly. "And beyond your legal standing."

"Yes," Yara said. "I am describing them anyway."

* * *

Elias had rehearsed this moment so many times that when it arrived it felt less like courage than like stepping off a ledge he had already fallen from. He rose from his seat among the record-keepers, and the movement drew every eye, because record-keepers did not rise; record-keepers were furniture that wrote.

"Commissioner." He lifted his ledger. "With respect to the panel, I hold the office of census taker, second district, Vellmark. My entries are sworn instruments of record. I wish to enter testimony into the proceedings."

Vane turned to him slowly, and Elias saw something pass across the commissioner's face that he had seen only once before — on the day he had first found the discrepancy in the tax rolls, on the face of his own supervisor when Elias asked one question too many. It was the look of a man measuring whether you were dangerous enough to destroy.

"Testimony regarding?"

"Village of Ashenmere." Elias opened his ledger to the page he had copied three times, once for the record, once for safety, once because he could not stop reading it. "Suppressed from the rolls by order of this commission in the seventh year of the war. Population at last full count: four hundred and eleven. Deaths recorded in parish registers and witness statements gathered by myself and by Yara Sterling: two hundred and six, killed when the boundary was deliberately redrawn to place the village inside the contested zone, thereby justifying continued hostilities in a sector whose resources—" his voice caught, and he mastered it— "whose resources were under commission administration for the duration. The war did not continue there, Commissioner. The war was *administered* there."

"Strike that," Vane said. "It will not be minuted."

"Minute it or don't," Elias said. "There are forty copies. I wrote them myself. I am meticulous; it is my one virtue." He heard, absurdly, a few quiet sounds from the gallery that might have been laughter or might have been weeping. "You taught us that the record is the state's memory, sir. You cannot blind a memory and call it peace."

He set the ledger down on the table, beside the atlas, and uncapped his pen, and began — slowly, clearly, aloud — to read the names. All two hundred and six. The hall sat in a silence so complete he could hear the scratch of his own pen and the tick of the clock over the door. He read farmers' names and midwives' names, children's names, the name of the priest who had stayed to bury them all. When he reached the hundredth name his voice cracked, and Yara stepped forward without thinking and stood beside him, her hand flat on the atlas, and he kept reading.

* * *

They were still reading — Elias had passed one hundred and sixty names, and the light through the high windows had gone from white to gold — when the doors at the back of the hall opened.

Dain Corvath stood in the doorway, and Yara's heart stopped and started again, because he was supposed to be in the cellars, supposed to wait, and instead he was here, gaunt and gray-faced, wearing a soldier's coat brushed clean of mud, walking down the aisle with his hands visible and empty at his sides. Two guards started forward; Vane raised one finger and stopped them.

"Dain Corvath," Dain said, and his voice carried, ruined and clear. "Enemy combatant, by decree. I've come to surrender." He stopped at the rope-line, an arm's length from Yara, and looked at her, and what passed between them was nine years long. Then he turned to the table. "You can't sign a peace while I'm still a reason for war. So here I am. Try me. Try me in the open, with the record read, with the names said. Or admit there never was a decree worth the paper it wasn't written on."

"Corvath—" Vane began.

"He's right." The voice came from the end of the table: the conflicted clerk, Merrick, gray with exhaustion and shame, rising to his feet with a folder clutched to his chest. "Commissioner, I can't do this anymore. The survey dates. The redaction drafts in your own office — I carried them. There is no lawful decree. There never was." He laid the folder down beside the atlas. "It's all here. Strike whatever you want. The copies exist."

For a long moment Vane sat motionless, and Yara watched him understand, with the terrible clarity of a drowning man, that the room had changed around him — that forty copies existed, that two hundred and six names had been spoken aloud into a hall full of ears, that the granary was full of witnesses and the doors were open. That the record, which he had spent nine years bending, had finally bent back.

"The commission," he said at last, and his voice was ash, "will recess to consider the evidence presented."

"No," said a woman's voice from the gallery — old, cracked, unmistakable. Yara turned and saw old Widow Sarelle, who had lost three sons to the administered war, gripping the rail. "No more recesses. Nine years of recesses. Read the terms *now*. With the atlas annexed. With the names entered. In front of all of us, or explain to us why not."

And the gallery, which had been silent for hours, began — softly at first, then all together — to say the names back. Elias's list, learned by heart or read from smuggled copies, rolling through the granary like a tide coming in, four hundred and eleven souls made audible at last.

Vane closed his eyes. When he opened them, he reached for a fresh sheet of paper.

But at the rope-line, the guards were already moving toward Dain, and Yara saw the shackles come out, saw the amnesty she had bargained for dissolve in the space between *consider* and *decide*, and Dain met her eyes one final time and smiled — the same crooked smile from a flooded trench nine years ago — as if to say: *this part was always mine to pay.*

"Whatever happens next," he said quietly, "the borders are true now. That was your promise. Keep the rest of your life for yourself."

The guards took his arms, and the first signature touched the paper, and Yara stood with her hand pressed flat against the atlas as though she could hold the whole world steady by its spine — knowing the ink was barely dry, knowing Vane's allies still filled three ministries, knowing that everything they had won could be unmade in a single night of reprisal.

The pen scratched on. Outside, the bells of Vellmark began to ring — but whether for peace or merely for paperwork, no one yet knew.
