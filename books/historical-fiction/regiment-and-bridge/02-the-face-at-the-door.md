## 2. The Face at the Door

There is a particular skill to answering a door slowly, and I had mastered it years before the rain came down that November night like a judgment. You do not fling open a door when your aunt's correspondence sits in a locked drawer and her name appears on more watch lists than some Cabinet ministers. You pause. You listen. You count to five and let whoever is standing outside believe they have caught you mid-supper, mildly annoyed, entirely unremarkable.

I counted to five. Then, because the knocking had come again—smaller the second time, which was itself an oddity—I opened it.

The girl could not have been more than six. She stood on our step with rain running off the brim of a bonnet far too big for her, gripping a carpet bag nearly her own size as though it might bolt. Behind her, the street was empty in that way streets are empty only when someone has recently left them in a hurry. A hansom's wet tracks still shone under the gas lamp, already fading.

"Are you the forgetting house?" she asked.

Every instinct I possessed—which had been honed on four years of reading other people's secrets by candlelight—went very quiet.

"I beg your pardon?"

She held up a note. Her hand was shaking, though whether from cold or from having been coached into terror, I couldn't say. "The man said give this and don't tell my name till you read."

I took the note. The paper was good stock, expensive, the handwriting hurried but educated:

*This is Thomas Wren's daughter. There are men paid to find her. Burn this. Forget this face. If you keep her, keep nothing else.*

I am not a man who frightens easily, but I will confess the rain suddenly felt colder. Thomas Wren. Hanged in March outside Kirkby Mill prison for organizing weavers who had struck against fourteen-hour days—the first hanging for sedition in a generation, done quickly and publicly so that everyone might take note. Everyone had taken note. Edith had taken it hardest; she'd worn black armband for a month and called it mourning, though she claimed it was indigestion.

And now his daughter stood on our step looking—

I looked at her properly then, and the bottom dropped out of the evening. She had his face. Not resemblance—*his*. The same square jaw too heavy for a child's frame, the same dark brows set low over grey eyes, the same stubborn line of mouth that had stared out from every newspaper engraving while the rope did its work. She was Wren's face given six more years, and any constable in London who'd seen those engravings—and they all had, they were pinned in stations like saints' cards—would know her on sight.

"You'd better come in," I said, and broke about six of my own rules in one sentence.

* * *

Her name was Ida, she told me over bread and dripping, because the man on the train had said names were coats and I should wear a new one if anyone asked. She ate with the terrible efficiency of a child who had learned hunger was not always answered. She kept her carpet bag between her feet like an anchor.

Edith came down at half past ten, having been roused from her manuscripts, and stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded, taking inventory. My aunt has never once in my life asked how I feel about anything; she asks what I intend to do about it.

"Well," she said. "Wren's girl."

"You knew he had a daughter?"

"He had a wife who died of consumption and a brother in Manchester who drank. I knew he'd want her somewhere the law wasn't." She crossed the room, crouched to the child's level—a thing Edith Ravensworth does perhaps twice a decade—and studied that face with the grim attention she usually reserved for hostile legislation. "They'll hang photographs of her in every station by Friday if they don't have them already. Wren made martyrs of himself and trouble for everyone who loved him. Sit up straight, child. No—don't cry. Crying is allowed; snivelling is not. There's bread."

It was, I reflected, the most maternal speech my aunt had delivered in living memory, and roughly forty percent of it had been criticism.

The plan, such as it was, formed itself around the obvious fact: she could not stay. Our house had been watched since summer—we assumed; Orsen assumes, it is one of his functions—and a child with the most recognizable face in England could not be hidden in a household whose mistress was already suspected of seditious sympathies. She would need papers, a new coat, a destination. All of which took days.

We had hours.

* * *

The knock on the front door came at two in the morning, and this time I did not count to five.

Two constables, lamplight behind them, rain still falling with the patience of something that intends to continue indefinitely. The taller one had a notebook already open.

"Evening, sir. Sorry to trouble you. We're making inquiries after a missing child."

"Is she yours?" the shorter one asked, peering past me.

"My niece," I said, and heard myself lie with the smoothness that comes of practice, and hated how good at it I'd become. "Asleep these three hours. Is something wrong?"

"Father was hanged in spring. Thomas Wren. The mother's people were to have her, but there's a question of the arrangements." He wrote something. "Might we step in? Just a moment."

Gentlemen of the law do not say *might we* unless they intend to regardless. I stepped aside and let them drip on the hall tiles while I calculated—coldly, horribly, the way I calculate everything—whether the kitchen door was latched, whether Ida would stay silent, whether Edith had thought to remove herself upstairs.

They looked at the umbrella stand. They looked at me. The tall one consulted his book and asked had I seen a hansom cab, a woman in a green coat, a man carrying a child toward the river.

"No," I said, to all of it, and gave them my blandest face, the one I wear for customs men and landladies. "Quiet street. We hear everything and see nothing."

The shorter one smiled without warmth. "That's a useful quality in a house."

"It's the principal amenity."

They went. I watched from the window until their lanterns turned the corner, and then I stood there longer than I needed to, watching the rain stitch the empty street, feeling something crack along a seam I hadn't known was load-bearing.

Here is what I must explain about myself: I burn things. That is my function, my craft, my comfort. Letters arrive, I weigh them like luggage, and most go into the stove before Edith ever sees them. Informants' warnings, coded appeals, love letters from married men—it all goes, and I have slept soundly for years believing that destruction is a form of protection. Nothing kept is nothing found. This has been my creed, and I have repeated it to myself the way other men repeat scripture.

But that night, after the constables left, I went back to the kitchen where Ida slept curled in a chair with her bag clutched to her chest even in sleep, and I found in the pocket of her soaked coat a small card. A photograph. A woman—young, tired-eyed—with a lock of hair pasted beside her and writing on the reverse in a man's hand: *For my Ida, so she knows my face when I cannot show it.* Thomas Wren's hand. Thomas Wren's face, young, unroped, alive in silver nitrate.

My creed says burn it. A photograph is evidence; evidence kept is a noose shared. Four years of discipline sat on my shoulder reciting the arithmetic of survival.

I put it in my breast pocket instead.

I don't fully know why. I tell myself it was strategy—that a photograph of the mother might someday prove Ida's identity, secure her passage, save her life. There is truth in that. But there is also this: the girl had been ordered forgotten by everyone who loved her, handed over with instructions to erase her, and she was six years old, and someone in this world ought to keep one thing of hers on purpose.

So I broke my rule. I kept something. And keeping, I discovered, feels nothing like safety—it feels like holding a coal and refusing to drop it, knowing that eventually you will be burned and unable to regret it.

Ida stirred in her sleep and murmured something. I bent close. She was saying, over and over, half-dreaming: *Don't tell my face.*

Upstairs, Edith's lamp was still burning. And beneath my window, at half past three in the morning, I heard a hansom slow to a walk in the wet street outside our house—and did not speed up again.
