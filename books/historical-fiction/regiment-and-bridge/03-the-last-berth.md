## 3. The Last Berth

The informant was Mrs. Quill, and I confess the discovery did not surprise me so much as it disappointed me. There is a particular melancholy in learning that the woman who watered your aunt's ferns and complained about the price of coal had also been selling the contents of the wastepaper basket to a Detective-Sergeant Hobbes of Scotland Yard for eleven pounds a month.

Eleven pounds. Edith's secrets, wholesale, at less than what she paid me quarterly for boots.

I found her out on a Tuesday, in the manner one finds out everything—by accident, through diligence. A letter had come addressed in a hand I knew to be false, the loops too careful, the ink too fresh for its claimed age. It asked after "the Manchester correspondence" in terms no friend of my aunt's would use, because no friend of my aunt's called anything *the Manchester correspondence*; they called it *the bundle* or *the papers* or, more often, nothing at all, having agreed years ago that some subjects are discussed only by firelight and never by post. Mrs. Quill collected the letter from the hall table at half past four and delivered it, unopened but memorized, to a man reading a newspaper outside the tobacconist's on the corner. I watched her do it from the upstairs window while pretending to trim a lamp, and I thought: eleven pounds a month. The price of a soul in this city has dropped scandalously since the war.

Edith took the news standing up, as she took everything, with her chin set at the angle that made grown men apologize for opinions they hadn't yet finished forming.

"Quill stays," she said. "We feed her nothing true and plenty of nothing."

"And when they come?" I asked. Because they would come. The sedition bill was crawling through Parliament like something with too many legs and an appetite, and every paper carried rumors of lists—names gathered, addresses marked, arrests scheduled with the tidy efficiency of a railway timetable. My aunt's name was not merely on such a list. I suspected her name had been used to draw the line under it.

"They will come," Edith said, "when they come," which is the whole of her philosophy compressed into a sentence, and then she went back to her writing, because martyrs are made in kitchens as much as anywhere, and someone had to draft the circular that would make forty mill girls in Bolton dangerous by Friday.

It was Ida who changed the arithmetic.

You must understand what the child had become in our house over those weeks. She had stopped flinching at doors. She had learned to sit still through police whistles without looking up from her slate. She had developed, God help us, Edith's own habit of listening to conversations through walls with her whole body, head tilted like a bird's. Thomas Wren's daughter, wearing his face like an inheritance nobody wanted, growing braver by the day in the exact proportion that the world grew more dangerous—which is to say that the two of them were racing, and I could not tell you who was winning.

On Thursday night the knock came—not the polite knock, not the knock of tradesmen or friends, but the flat double rap of men who announce themselves by touching wood. Through the window I saw the lanterns. Four of them. And behind the lanterns, holding a paper he did not need to read, stood a man from the Home Office with the satisfied look of a clerk whose list has finally been countersigned.

They did not take her that night. The warrant required witnesses, a magistrate's presence, ceremony—the law likes its murders dressed for church. But the man at the door read the name aloud anyway, for the pleasure of it, I think: *"Edith Ravensworth, charged under the provisions of the Sedition Act, upon warrant."* And Edith stood in her own doorway in her shawl and said, "Thursday, then. How efficient," and closed the door, and I heard her laugh once, low, before she stopped herself.

That was Wednesday. We had until Monday.

* * *

Ida's interlude — the child under the stairs:

There is a cupboard under the stairs where Orsen says I must go when the knocking comes, and there is a loose board, and behind the loose board is a tin box, and inside the tin box is a photograph of my father.

I am not supposed to know about the photograph. But I know everything in this house, the way I know everything, because knowing is the only thing they let you keep when they take your coat and your name and your father off the gallows at Wandsworth. I have looked at him four times. He looks angry in the photograph, but Aunt Edith says he wasn't angry, he was *certain*, and that angry men hang and certain men are remembered, and I have decided to be certain instead.

Orsen burns letters. I watch him sometimes through the banisters, feeding the stove, and I used to think burning was losing. Now I think burning is deciding. He decides what the world gets to know, and he stands very straight when he does it, like a soldier, except soldiers burn things for kings and Orsen burns things for people who can't write their names.

Tonight the house is quiet in a wrong way, like a held breath. Orsen sat with Aunt Edith a long time in the study and neither of them talked loud enough for me, which means it's important, which means tomorrow somebody cries or somebody leaves. When Orsen came out he looked at me a long time across the hall, and his face did the thing it does where he is counting, and I understood—I am six, not stupid—that I was in the sum.

I put the photograph back behind the board. I practiced being certain. I am getting better at it.

* * *

The shipping clerk's name was Ambrose Pell, and he owed my aunt a debt he never explained and we never asked about, which is how debts between decent people ought to work. He came to the house on Saturday disguised badly as a coal merchant, and he laid it all out on the kitchen table like a man laying out cards he already knew were losing cards.

"One berth," he said. "Midnight steamer, the *Corinthian*, out of the Surrey Commercial Docks, Canada-bound. One berth, one name, one life. After Monday the port authorities get the arrest lists, and there'll be no berth, no steamer, no nothing for anyone in this house. This is the last door, Mr. Ravensworth, and it closes at midnight."

One berth. I looked at it sitting there on the table, that single invisible ticket, and I felt the full weight of the arithmetic descend on me like weather.

Because here is what the sum contained: one child whose father had already been hanged and whose face was worth more alive than any document; one woman who was not a woman but a movement wearing a shawl, whose arrest would be a blow, yes, but whose escape would be a triumph printed in every radical paper from here to Winnipeg; one nephew who could pass unseen through any door in London because he had spent thirty-one years making himself furniture. Three lives. One door.

"Orsen takes it," Edith said, before I had even finished thinking. She said it the way she said everything, as though the matter had been settled by committee centuries ago and she was merely announcing the minutes. "You take the berth, you take the archive, you go to Canada, and you carry on."

"I don't carry on," I said. "I'm not—you don't carry *on*, Aunt Edith. People carry on. I screen letters."

"You are the only person alive who knows which letters mattered," she said, and for one moment her voice cracked along a fault line I hadn't known was there, and I understood that she was afraid—not of the prison, never of the prison, but of the archive, of the forty names, of the Bolton girls, of everything she had built being fed into some Home Office stove by men who burned without deciding. "They cannot have the papers, Orsen. Whatever else happens, whatever you choose—they cannot have the papers."

And there it was. Not *save yourself*. Not *you are young, you have years*. She asked me for the papers, because asking for herself was a language she'd lost somewhere around 1889 and never bothered to relearn.

"I can sew the archive into a coat in three hours," I said quietly. "A child's coat. No constable in England searches a six-year-old going to her aunt in Toronto."

Edith looked at me. And I watched her understand, and I watched her refuse to understand, and I watched the refusal lose, all within the space of a breath.

"No," she said.

"The ship won't wait for the argument."

"*No,* Orsen."

"She has her father's face," I said. "You told me yourself, the first night—forget this face, and I couldn't, and neither will history, if she lives long enough to show it. You can be arrested and tried and written about for fifty years. She can only be *hidden*, and hiding expires. Everything about her expires unless it crosses the water tonight."

The clock in the hall struck nine. Somewhere above us Ida turned in her sleep, dreaming whatever certain children dream.

Edith put both hands flat on the kitchen table, the way she did before addressing a crowd, and for a moment I saw her not as my aunt but as the thing her enemies feared—a general with no army left, ordering her last soldier to abandon the field because the flag mattered more than the man carrying it.

"If you do this," she said, "they will take you. They will transport you, Orsen. Seven years, fourteen, the rest of your life in some place with a name like a cough. For a child who won't remember your face."

"That," I said, "sounds familiar."

She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to a blessing I would ever get, and I took it.

At half past ten I woke Ida, and I dressed her, and I brought out the small grey coat, and threaded the needle.

What she said to me at the dock, and what I burned rather than kept—and why, for the first time in my life, I regretted the burning—is another matter entirely.
