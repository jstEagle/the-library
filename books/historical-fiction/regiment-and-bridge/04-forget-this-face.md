## 4. Forget This Face

The arithmetic was simple, which is what made it obscene.

One berth. Two lives worth saving by any honest count—three, if you counted the archive, and I did, because Edith had taught me to count paper among the living. A shipping clerk named Pell with a weak heart and a weaker loyalty to the Crown had pressed a single ticket into my palm at the dock office that afternoon and said, *midnight tide, don't be late, and don't be seen*, in the tone of a man rehearsing his own alibi.

Edith ordered me to take it. She used the voice she reserved for landlords and undersecretaries, the one that made disobedience feel like bad manners rather than moral courage.

"You are young," she said, standing at the window with her back to me, watching for constables the way other women watched for weather. "You are nobody. You can cross an ocean and become someone else before the ink dries on their warrant. I am Edith Ravensworth, Orsen. My name is the movement's coat of arms. They will hang it or jail it either way, but they will not let it quietly emigrate."

"And Ida?"

"I did not say leave the child."

"No," I said. "You said take the berth. Singular. Which is how you always begin, isn't it—the singular thing—and then you wait for me to do the arithmetic aloud so you needn't hear yourself say the rest."

She turned then. For one unguarded half-second she looked her age, which was a thing I had never once seen her do in nine years of employment, and I understood that this was the greatest gift she knew how to give anyone: the sight of her frightened.

"The archive goes in the child's coat," she said quietly. "Pell owes me more than a ticket; he owes me a blind eye at the gangway. The pages are small enough. Her coat has a double lining—I'll sew tonight. No customs officer in the Empire pats down a six-year-old girl crying for her mother."

"She hasn't cried for her mother once."

"No," Edith said, and something crossed her face like a shadow of a bird. "She cries for her father. Which is worse, and braver, and exactly why it must be her."

I want it recorded—I want *something* recorded, since no court will ever hear it—that I argued. That I stood in that cold drawing room and argued until my voice cracked like a schoolboy's, and that Edith Ravensworth listened to every word with the courtesy she extended to worthy opponents, and then won anyway, because she had been winning arguments against men since before I could read.

What she could not win was the warrant. We heard the knock-up man calling the hours past two in the morning, and we both knew the plainclothes men would come with the light. So we worked instead of sleeping. She sewed; I folded. Forty-one pages of names, routes, codes, debts owed and favors banked—the whole nervous system of a movement that would otherwise die with its head. I held each page to the candle flame first, just long enough to warm the wax, because cold wax cracks, and cracked seals mean questions. My aunt's hands shook threading the needle. Mine did not, folding. We were, I think, each pretending the other couldn't see.

Ida slept on the kitchen chair with her chin on her fists. She woke once and watched us without asking, which told me everything about what six years under Thomas Wren's roof had taught her about adults whispering over papers.

At half past four I knelt to button her into the coat. It hung heavy on her small frame, forty-one pages of sedition riding against a child's ribs, and she looked up at me with Thomas Wren's grey eyes and asked the question I had spent three days dreading.

"Am I going where Papa went?"

"No." My voice came out wrong and I corrected it. "Your papa went where they sent him. You're going where he wanted everyone to go. There's a difference, and when you're older you'll understand it, and when you understand it you'll be angry, and your being angry is the whole point of you."

"That's a strange thing to tell a child."

"It's the only true thing I have. Take it."

At the door she did the thing I will carry to whatever prison holds me: she reached up, took my face in both hands, the way a woman does in paintings, entirely too solemn for six years old, and said, "Forget this face."

"Not likely," I said. "It's a memorable one. Wretched luck for you."

* * *

They came at seven, as promised, four of them, and Inspector Halloway at the front wearing the satisfied expression of a man who has already dictated his memoirs. He had a warrant for Edith Ravensworth. He read it twice, enjoying it.

I met them on the step in Edith's grey coat, her hat, her very posture—I had been studying her for nine years from behind a steering wheel; imitation was the least of my skills. I gave him her name in her clipped vowels. I gave him her defiance, pre-packaged for the newspapers: *I regret nothing and I will say so at trial.*

Halloway squinted. Informers, I reflected, are rarely paid for close observation; they are paid for confirmation, and he had been told whose house this was, and here stood its mistress, tall and grey and insufferable. The wig helped. The mourning veil helped more. Edith had dressed me herself, adjusting the veil with two fierce tugs, and the last thing she said as a free woman was, "If you weep under there, Orsen, I shall never forgive you."

I didn't weep under there. I saved it for the cell, alone, where even grief must learn discretion.

The rest went quickly and badly, which is to say normally. The archive was ash by noon—I fed it page by page into the kitchen range while a constable watched me with boredom, assuming the tall lady was burning correspondence out of spite. Let him assume. Spite is such an innocent motive. By evening the papers were smoke over Southwark and I was in a cell in Clerkenwell, answering to Edith's name, charged under statutes I had only just memorized the penalties for.

At trial I said nothing. Edith would have said everything; that was precisely why I couldn't. Silence, I reasoned, would be attributed to dignity. Dignity they could print. Fourteen years' transportation they could also print, and did, beneath a photograph of me in the veil that fooled no reader who had ever actually met her—but fooled the law, which had never bothered.

I lost the name Ravensworth, the rooms in Southwark, the country itself. On the transport ship, bound south while Ida sailed north, I stood at the rail and performed the last act of my old trade: I inventoried what I carried. One body, one memory, one face I had been ordered to forget and had instead committed to permanent retention. Everything else had gone into the fire or onto the ship. It was, I noted wryly to myself, the smallest luggage I had ever packed, and the heaviest.

* * *

Fourteen years is a long time to keep a secret that everyone believes you don't have.

Tasmania taught me patience the way drowning teaches swimming. I worked, kept my head down, discovered that a man who can read and cipher is valuable anywhere, even here—especially here, among men the Empire had decided to stop counting. I taught letters in the evenings to convicts who wept over their own names written in their own hands. It occurred to me more than once that Edith would have called this the movement, continued by other means. It occurred to me more often than that that I missed her with an ache that had stopped being dramatic and settled into something domestic, like a draught under a door you simply learn to live around.

She died in my third year away. A warder's newspaper mentioned it—one column, no photograph, the Empire tidying up after itself. I read it eleven times and then I burned it, because some habits are load-bearing.

And then, in the winter of 1913, fourteen years almost to the month since a child buttoned into a too-heavy coat walked up a gangway, a letter arrived at the depot addressed in a hand I did not know but should have—it was Edith's hand, learned faithfully by someone with excellent eyesight and better memory.

The postmark read Manitoba.

Inside was a single sheet. No greeting, because none was needed. A list of names—mill workers, organizers, widows, all of them alive, organized, and *funded*, the network rebuilt on the forty-one pages sewn into a child's lining, every debt collected and favor called in across an ocean and fourteen winters. And at the bottom, above a signature that was not a signature but a small drawn bird, in the style of a woman I had known for nine years and obeyed forever:

*You were told to forget a face. I have made certain the Empire cannot. There is a seat opening on a ship out of Hobart in the spring. The passenger list says one berth. Come argue the arithmetic with me—you always did it aloud, and I find I have missed hearing it.*

I sat with that letter a long while, letting the warders' bells ring the hours past uncounted.

Then I began, as instructed, to argue. Aloud.
