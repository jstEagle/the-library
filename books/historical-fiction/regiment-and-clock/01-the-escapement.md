## 1. The Escapement

The path up to the observatory was a spine of packed earth and old gravel that switched back through the pines, and Lena Whitlock climbed it the way she did everything — with a toolbox in one hand and her father's habits in the other. *Count your tools before you leave. Count them again before you return.* She had counted them at dawn by lamplight: eleven pieces, including the jeweler's loupe she wore on a cord around her neck and the long-nosed pliers her father had ground down himself from a pair of sewing scissors. Eleven. The number sat in her mind like a stone in a shoe.

Fog still clung to the valley below, filling it to the brim so that the town of Aldenholt appeared drowned — rooftops surfacing here and there, the church spire like a mast going under. Somewhere beneath all that white water, three thousand people were waking to bells that had rung four minutes late on Monday, six on Tuesday, and this morning, if the pattern held, would ring whenever they pleased.

It was not supposed to be possible. That was the whole point of the master clock.

She crested the hill and the observatory rose out of the mist's upper edge, its dome pale as a skull against the dark sky remnants of night. Below the dome, in the round stone heart of the building, lived the clock: a machine the height of two men, built forty years ago by a firm in the capital whose name was engraved in brass above the pendulum and which everyone had forgotten anyway. From that one mechanism, iron rods ran out through the walls to relay dials across the valley — the railway station, the mill office, the registrar's tower, every church that could not afford an escapement of its own. When the master spoke, the valley repeated it. When the master stumbled, the trains waited politely for nothing, and the factory whistles argued with the noon bells, and the schoolmaster kept his own watch and glared at it.

Old Horst met her at the door without greeting. He had been keeper of the clock since before Lena was born, a stooped man with a beard gone the color of wood ash and hands that trembled now unless he pressed them flat against something. He pressed them flat against the doorframe.

"You came," he said.

"Your letter said the third hand stops at the quarter."

"My letter said worse than that." He stood aside to let her pass. "But letters travel downhill slowly, and I wanted you to climb with your mind still open."

Lena had restored eleven clocks in her nineteen years, counting mantel clocks and the great regulator at the bank and the carillon at St. Ulrich's that had been struck by lightning twice and forgiven neither time. Worn pivots, broken teeth, mainsprings set like coiled snakes, weights run down because someone forgot to wind — these were the ordinary sins of machines, and she knew their faces. Her father had taught her that every fault tells you how it happened. A worn pivot says *years*. A snapped spring says *greed or ignorance*. Dirt says *neglect*.

What she saw when she entered the clock room said none of those things.

The room smelled of oil and cold stone and, faintly, of camphor, as though someone had tried to keep moths out of a place where no moth could live. Morning light came down through the slit windows in gray bars. The clock stood against the far wall, its case open, and even before she crossed the room she felt the wrongness the way one feels a room where a quarrel has just ended.

"Show me," she said, setting the toolbox down.

Horst showed her.

The escapement — the little kingdom of pallets and teeth that doles out the driving weight tick by measured tick, turning raw falling force into time — was the first thing. Its pallets had been filed. Not worn; *filed*, with a fine cut that left bright metal gleaming where forty years of polish should have been dull amber. Someone had taken steel to steel and reshaped the angle of engagement by perhaps half a degree, enough to make each impulse slightly late, slightly uneven, slightly wrong. It was beautiful work. It was the work of someone who understood exactly what they were ruining.

Then the pendulum. Its bob was a heavy brass cylinder, adjustable by a nut beneath, and the nut had been turned a quarter revolution and then — this was the detail that stopped Lena's breath — turned back again, but not quite, leaving the rod's effective length altered by a fraction no casual eye would catch. And the suspension spring, the thin blade of steel from which the whole pendulum swung, bore a scratch near its top clamp. Not damage. A *mark*. As if someone had measured how far they dared go and recorded it.

"Who has keys to this room?" she asked.

"Me," Horst said. "And the registrar keeps a duplicate in his safe, for emergencies. And there is a third key." He looked at her with eyes that were too steady for a man his age. "Which my predecessor lost twenty years ago and never told anyone about."

Lena crouched before the escapement and put the loupe to her eye. Through the lens, the world became honest. She traced the filed pallets, and her stomach tightened with something that was not quite fear and not quite admiration. Half a degree. Whoever had done this hadn't wanted to break the clock — breaking it would summon repairmen and questions within a week. They had wanted to *bend* it. To make time itself unreliable in a way no one could prove, so that gradually the whole valley would stop trusting its own hours, and the master clock would become an old man's opinion instead of a law.

"Someone wanted the town to be late," she murmured.

"Someone wanted the town to doubt," Horst corrected. "Late can be corrected. Doubt spreads."

She straightened and walked the mechanism top to bottom, hands moving behind her, touching without looking, the way her father had drilled into her until it wasn't a skill anymore but a sense. The great barrel. The maintaining power that keeps the clock running during winding — intact. The chime train, silent until the hour — she checked the cams on the chime barrel, the pinned drum that told the hammers what order to speak in, and found them untouched. Whatever the saboteur had wanted, they had not wanted to change the clock's voice. Only its patience.

Only its honesty.

"Lena." Horst had lowered himself onto the keeper's stool by the wall, and his trembling hands were pressed flat on his knees. "Before you begin. Ask yourself who profits when a town cannot agree on the hour."

"The railways lose money. The mills lose—"

"I mean truly ask." His voice was quiet and rough, like a file drawn once across soft wood. "Not who loses coins. Who loses something else."

She didn't understand him yet. She noted that too, filing it away beside the mark on the suspension spring — a measurement of how far she dared go in her own thinking.

* * *

She worked through the morning, and the fog burned off the valley below until Aldenholt lay exposed and ordinary, smoke rising straight from breakfast chimneys, the eight o'clock train drawing in four minutes behind its printed hour while passengers on the platform checked pocket watches and shrugged at one another with the shrug of people who had begun, already, to accept that time here was negotiable.

Lena dismantled the escapement piece by piece, laying each part on a cloth in strict order, and made her drawings. Her father's rule: *never touch steel until you've drawn what you intend it to become.* The pallets she could re-cut true; she'd need her stones and a full day. The pendulum she could rebalance in minutes. The scratch on the spring she photographed in her memory the way her father had taught her to photograph everything — *evidence outlives intention* — and wondered, not for the first time since climbing the hill, why the thought of evidence made her palms sweat.

Because of the ledger. Because her father had spent the last years of his life making marks on paper the way this saboteur had made marks on steel — small, precise, deniable alterations designed to bend a system without appearing to break it. Forged names. Careful entries in the registrar's great book, names that existed nowhere else in the world except in the mouths of the people they sheltered. She had grown up watching him practice a signature until it matched, watching him age ink with tea and sunlight, watching him weigh each alteration to the fraction of a degree past suspicion. She had held the lamp. She had never once asked him to stop.

She pushed the thought down where she kept it, beneath the daily floor of herself, and returned to the work.

By noon she had the escapement stripped and was cleaning the pivot holes with pegwood and spirits when she found it.

Inside the count wheel housing — the rotating wheel that governs the striking sequence, a place almost never opened because almost nothing ever went wrong there — wedged behind the locking detent, lay a sliver of paper, folded to the size of a thumbnail. She drew it out with the pliers, expecting a scrap of packing material, a fragment of an old repairman's note.

It was a train timetable.

A corner of one, torn along a printed line, dated fourteen months ago, showing departures from a station she did not recognize in a language that was not quite German and not quite anything else. In the margin, in pencil, in a hand small and controlled, someone had written a single word:

*Wait.*

Lena sat back on her heels. The clock loomed over her, its weights hanging motionless in their channels, its whole arrested anatomy open to the light. Outside, the valley glittered in the sun, innocent, punctual enough, believing in its own hours.

Someone had hidden a timetable inside the heart of the thing that governed all time in Aldenholt. Someone had slowed the town's pulse by degrees, patient as frost, and left behind a message no one was meant to find — or perhaps a message meant for whoever finally came to fix what they had broken. *Wait.* Wait for what? Trains that had departed fourteen months ago? A war that everyone said was coming and everyone said couldn't?

Her father's voice, from years ago, from the lamplit workbench: *Every fault tells you how it happened, Lena. And every deliberate fault tells you why.*

She folded the scrap carefully and put it in her breast pocket, next to her heart, where documents that frightened her seemed to belong.

Behind her, Horst stirred on his stool. "Found something," he said. It was not a question.

"How long," Lena asked, "has the clock been slowing?"

He considered the ceiling, the beams, some point far above both. "Since spring," he said. "Perhaps since winter. Perhaps—" and here his hands trembled against his knees, and he pressed them flat, "—perhaps longer than I am willing to say aloud."

"And the person who did it?"

Horst looked at her then, and in his ash-colored face there was an expression she would spend many nights afterward trying to name: grief wearing the mask of relief, or relief wearing the mask of grief.

"Climb down tomorrow," he said. "Go to the registrar. Copy your parts records. And while you're standing at his counter, look at the great ledger — the book of names. Look at it closely, girl. Then tell me what a forged name and a filed pallet have in common."

The clock ticked in the silence between them, slow, uneven, lying.

Lena picked up her pliers, and her hands were steady, and her heart was not.
