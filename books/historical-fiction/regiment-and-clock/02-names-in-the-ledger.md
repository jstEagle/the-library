## 2. Names in the Ledger

The registrar's office smelled of dust and sealing wax, a smell that had not changed since Lena was nine years old and her father first brought her here to file their papers — *Whitlock, Elias; Whitlock, Elena* — as though the two of them had grown up out of the valley soil like any other family. She had believed it then. Children believe what documents tell them.

Now she stood at the counter with her notebook open and her gloves still on, because the office was cold in the way of rooms where no one had ever hurried.

"Parts records," she said to the clerk behind the desk. "For the observatory clock. I'll need everything going back — oh, thirty years, if it goes back that far."

The clerk was a narrow man named Pfeil who wrote with his tongue pressed against his lower lip, as if the effort of penmanship were also an effort of thought. He looked up at her over half-moon spectacles.

"The master clock," he said. "It's stopped, then? Half the town's been asking."

"It's stopped." She kept her voice even. "I've been engaged to restore it."

"*Engaged*." He said the word the way a man says *infected*. "Bold of the council. That mechanism's older than this building. My father said no one but the keeper ever understood its temper."

"No one but the keeper understood it because no one else was allowed near it," Lena said. "That's not temper. That's neglect."

Pfeil's mouth twitched — whether toward offense or amusement she couldn't tell — and he disappeared into the back room where the town's paper memory lived. While he was gone, Lena let her eyes travel the front office: the high windows with their panes of wavy glass, the pigeonholes labeled by street, the great ledger itself, chained open on its lectern like a Bible.

She had known it would be there. Everyone knew the ledger was there; it was the town's most boring relic, consulted for taxes and marriages and little else. But knowing a thing is in a room is different from seeing it, and seeing it was different from what happened to her chest when she looked at it now — a slow tightening, as though a hand had reached through all those years and closed around her lungs.

Her father had stood at that lectern. She could see him there suddenly, so clearly it made her blink: his coat brushed, his hat held politely under his arm, his handwriting small and upright and utterly unremarkable, which was the whole art of it. He had taught her penmanship on Sundays. "A hand that draws attention," he'd told her once, tapping her wrist, "is a hand that gets remembered. Bore them, Lene. Bore them absolutely."

She'd thought he meant it as a lesson about letters. She had been slow, all those years, about what he'd actually been teaching her.

"Here we are." Pfeil returned with two bound folders and dropped them on the counter with a thump that raised dust. "Observatory expenditures. Parts requisitions. You'll find the clock entries under 'Public Works — Horological.' Mind you copy, don't remove. And sign the visitors' book."

Lena signed. Her own name came out steady, which surprised her.

The folders were fat with decades of invoices — brass stock, wire, pivots, a suspicious quantity of lamp oil — and she bent over them gratefully, because work was a place to hide. She copied figures. She noted dates. Somewhere around 1889 the purchases went strange: springs ordered twice, a new pendulum rod when the old one showed no wear, weights replaced whole instead of recast. Someone had been feeding the clock parts it didn't need, or someone had been selling the surplus. She marked the pages with thin pencil ticks and let the pattern assemble itself in the back of her mind, where patterns liked to wait.

But her eyes kept drifting up. Over the top of the folder. To the lectern.

The ledger lay open, as ledgers do when clerks have no reason to close them, to the current year's page. Names in columns. *Born. Married. Died. Removed.* The whole town reduced to ink, each life a line item. She found herself reading down the column of recent entries without meaning to — the Kessler twins born in March, old Frau Albrecht died in April, the Brenner family *removed, destination unrecorded*, which was how the valley always wrote it when people left without saying where they were going.

"Lena Whitlock."

She looked up. Pfeil had come around the counter and was standing beside the lectern, adjusting his spectacles, wearing the expression of a man about to be helpful.

"You'll want the historical volumes too, I expect, given the age of your mechanism. We keep the superseded registers back there, but the main ledger holds everything since the fire in '71." He turned a page backward with one finger, reverent, showing her the depth of the record. "Everything since before you were born. Everything since before your father was born, come to that."

"Before my father was born," Lena repeated.

"Mm." Pfeil licked his thumb and turned another page, and another, walking backward through time while Lena stood frozen with her pencil hovering over her notebook, a drop of graphite about to fall. The pages whispered past — 1904, 1896, 1888 — and she understood, with the particular horror of watching a door open that you had spent your whole life assuming was locked, exactly what he intended to show her.

"Pfeil," she said, "I really only need the parts—"

"Everyone likes to see it," he said happily. "Their own line. People stand right where you're standing and ask me to find the day they were written in. It's the closest thing we have to scripture, my grandfather used to say. Every soul in the valley, accounted."

He stopped turning. His finger came down on the page.

"Here. 1879. The Whitlocks. Your grandfather's entry — no, great-uncle, isn't it, the elder Elias — and here..." His finger slid. "Elias Whitlock, entered as resident, occupation clockmaker, origin — hmm."

The office was very quiet. The stove ticked. Outside, a cart rattled over cobbles and faded away.

Lena did not move. Later, she would take pride in that: that she did not move, did not breathe fast, did not look at the page with anything more than a clerk's mild curiosity. But inside, everything had gone white and ringing, like a struck bell heard from underwater.

Because she could see the entries, and she could read them, and reading them she could hear her father's voice saying *bore them, Lene* — and the entries were good. They were very good. Two families' worth of names written in her father's careful upright hand, dated years apart so no pattern would show, origins listed in the flat administrative shorthand the valley used for everyone, occupations plausible, witnesses cited by name — witnesses who were themselves invented, or dead, or bought. Her father had built false men and women out of ink and set them living in this book, and the book had believed him, and the town had believed the book, and for thirty-four years the lie had sat here under official seal, breathing quietly among the true names like a sleeper among sleepers.

And now she was standing next to it, and the clerk was pointing at it, and her own name — her real, ordinary, innocent name — was four lines below the forgery, entered in the same hand, by the same man, on the same afternoon.

"Origin," Pfeil said again, frowning slightly. He peered closer, tilting the page to catch the window light. "That's odd. The ink's different here. See? The rest of the column's iron gall, gone brown like everything else from that decade, but these three lines—" he touched the margin above her father's entries, "—still black. Someone re-inked this section. Or..."

He trailed off. He looked at the page. Then, slowly, he looked at Lena.

The moment stretched. In it Lena had time to consider several things at once: that Pfeil was not clever but he was thorough, that thoroughness was worse, that her father's forgeries were beautiful and beauty was evidence, that the Vosses' names — *if anyone ever checked* — lived in these same pages in the same beautiful hand, that the drop of graphite finally fell from her pencil onto the floor between them with a click she was certain the whole building heard.

"My father restored the register after the damp got into the storeroom," she said. "'98, wasn't it? I remember him complaining about it for weeks. He redid whatever the mold had eaten. He was proud of his hand; he'd never let a clerk touch it."

It was a guess built on nothing, delivered with a shrug. Pfeil stared at her. Then his face opened with relief, because people want explanations, they will take almost any explanation offered kindly enough.

"'98, yes! There was water damage, I'd forgotten entirely. Well." He chuckled, straightening his spectacles. "Your father was a careful man. The council should have paid him better."

"He'd have agreed," Lena said dryly, and something in her chest unlocked enough to let her breathe again. "May I see the volume from '79? Since you've got it open. For the parts provenance — the clock was overhauled that same year, I want to compare the dates."

"Of course, of course." He turned the ledger toward her, helpful now, eager even, and stepped away to fetch the second folder he'd forgotten.

Lena looked down at the page.

Up close, the forgeries were flawless — except for the one thing Pfeil had seen and misunderstood. Her father *had* re-inked those lines, but not in 1898. He had done it recently. Within the last two years. She knew his habits; she had inherited his eyes. A man does not refresh old lies unless he expects someone to come looking for them.

Her father had known the war was coming before anyone else in the valley did. He had known what registries become when armies arrive — not scripture, but inventory. He had strengthened his forged names the way a householder reinforces a door.

And he had never told her. He had died in the winter, coughing, mending clocks, saying nothing, leaving her a workshop full of hidden refugees and a ledger full of beautiful ghosts, trusting her to be as careful as he had been.

Pfeil returned with the folder. Lena took it, thanked him, copied what she needed with hands that were almost steady, and walked out into the bright cold street feeling the entire weight of the town's paper memory pressing against her shoulder blades.

*Every soul in the valley, accounted.*

She had gone three steps down the registrar's steps when she saw them: grey uniforms, six of them, dismounting in the square outside the church. Soldiers. Not garrison men, not customs riders — field-grey, with the stiff new cut of them, and an officer in the middle consulting a folded map of the valley as though the town were already a diagram.

One of the soldiers carried an empty wooden crate, stenciled on the side in white paint:

**RECORDS — AUDIT**

Lena stood on the steps with her notebook against her chest and watched them cross the square toward the registrar's office, toward the lectern, toward the chained-open book where her father's careful ghosts waited to be counted.

* * *

They reached the door before she decided anything, which was how decisions worked now, she suspected — the world moving faster than caution. The officer in charge went in first. Through the frosted glass of the registrar's door she watched shapes move, heard Pfeil's voice rise in protest and fall in obedience, and then heard the sound she would hear in her sleep for weeks afterward: the heavy scrape of the ledger being lifted off its lectern, all those years of names sliding across wood, all those lives picked up in two hands.

When the officer came back out, the book was wrapped in sacking and cradled in his arms like an infant. He paused on the steps, scanning the street — and for one absurd instant his eyes passed over Lena, a young woman with a notebook and dusty gloves, nobody, nothing — and moved on.

Then he gave an order, and the soldiers turned, not toward the barracks, not toward the station.

Toward the hill. Toward the observatory.

Lena stood very still on the registrar's steps, holding thirty years of parts invoices against her heart, and understood that whatever was coming had already arrived — that it had perhaps arrived years ago, in her father's careful black ink, waiting for exactly this day — and that the clock on the hill, stopped and silent and full of sabotage, was now the most dangerous object in the valley.

She turned and walked home the long way, through the lanes, rehearsing what she would say to Marta Voss.

There was, she realized, no longer any version of tomorrow in which the ledger's ghosts stayed buried. The only question left was who reached them first — the army, or her father's daughter.
