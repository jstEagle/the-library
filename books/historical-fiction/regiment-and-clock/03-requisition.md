## 3. Requisition

The drums began before the church bells finished the hour, and Lena Whitlock knew at once that something was wrong with the world's arithmetic.

She was in her workshop when the sound came up the hill — not the bright, ceremonial drumming of a festival but a flat, businesslike rattle, like a thousand small hammers deciding something. She set down her tweezers over the Voss children's breakfast plates, still warm from the stove behind the partition wall, and went to the window.

They came up the valley road in a column of grey, two hundred men at least, their boots striking the cobblestones in unison so that the whole town seemed to have acquired a single pulse. Behind them rolled a field kitchen, a wire cart, and an automobile of the kind Lena had only seen in engravings — black as a beetle, gleaming, absurd against the crooked timbered houses of the market square.

"Frau Whitlock." It was Frau Brenner from the chandlery, breathless on the stairs, not waiting to knock. "Soldiers. In the square. They're nailing papers to the fountain."

Lena's first thought, absurd and immediate, was for the clock. *If they've come about the clock—* But no one in this town connected her to the observatory yet except old Horst, and Horst told no one anything anymore.

Behind the partition, little Anneli Voss was singing to herself, a nonsense song about a goose. Lena crossed the room in three strides and put her finger to her lips through the curtain gap. The singing stopped. A pair of dark eyes appeared in the gap and then vanished, obedient, frightened, gone.

"I'd best see it myself," she said to Frau Brenner, and reached for her coat.

* * *

The square had never held so many people and so little noise at once.

The officers were different from their men — taller in the way that good feeding makes people tall, their grey uniforms cut close and correct, silver collar-pips catching the morning light. One of them stood on the fountain's rim directing two soldiers with a hammer and a sheaf of printed orders. Another — younger, fair-haired, with the tired eyes of someone who had already read every document he would ever sign — stood apart with a ledger under his arm, and Lena's heart stopped before her mind caught up and told her it was only a merchant's daybook, some shopkeeper's accounts requisitioned along with everything else.

Not the registrar's ledger. Not yet.

But then a sergeant came out of the registrar's office carrying exactly that — the great book with its cracked green leather spine, its brass corners, its weight of eleven hundred names — and laid it on a trestle table before the young officer as reverently as if he were laying down a wounded man.

Lena stood at the edge of the crowd, a clockmaker's daughter among bakers' wives and carters, and watched the past being carried into the open air.

Her father had been dead four years. The forged entries were nine years old. She had been fourteen when he sat her down at this very square's edge, on this very cobblestone, and said: *A name is only ink until someone believes it. So we will make very good ink.* And he had — beautiful ink, entries in the registrar's own hand mimicked so perfectly that even the registrar had believed them, because the registrar wanted to believe that his town had always been whole. *Voss* became *Vossel*, established 1881, carpenter, Lutheran. Three other families became three other harmless lineages, grafted onto the town's history like scions onto hardy rootstock.

And now the whole tree was on a trestle table in the sun, and men with clipboards were opening it.

"—by order of the District Command," the officer on the fountain was reading, his voice trained to carry without effort, "all civil records are placed under military audit for the purpose of mobilization verification. All households will present themselves for confirmation of residence and identity within seven days. The observatory is hereby requisitioned as a signal station effective immediately."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat. Old Herr Kessler, who had buried two sons in the last war nobody talked about, spat carefully into the gutter. Frau Brenner clutched Lena's sleeve.

"Seven days," she whispered. "What does *verification* mean?"

"It means they check the book against your face," said Herr Kessler grimly. "It means if you're in the book, you exist, and if you aren't—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

On the trestle table, the ledger lay open, and a staff clerk turned its pages with a licked thumb. From where she stood, twenty feet away, Lena could not read a word. She did not need to. She could see the pages turning — page forty-one, forty-two — and somewhere in the middle of that green book her father's careful hand was waiting, patient as a trap, for the day someone looked closely.

The fair-haired officer closed the ledger himself. His hands did it gently, Lena noticed. Almost apologetically.

Then his gaze lifted and swept the crowd, and for one lurching moment it seemed to pass over her face and stop. It didn't stop. But Lena walked home faster than dignity allowed, and did not look back at the square where the ordinary world, tilting slowly all morning, finally went over on its side.

* * *

Lieutenant Brandt had signed forty-one requisition orders in six hours, and each signature felt smaller than the last.

He had joined the signals corps because it was the honest branch — wires and timetables, facts that either carried or didn't. His father had been a railway inspector for thirty years and had taught him that a schedule was a moral document: every train that left on time was a small promise kept to several hundred strangers. Brandt believed that. He had built his life on the belief that order was a form of kindness.

Now he stood in the registrar's overheated office while the town clerk — a damp little man named Pfeil whose hands would not stop moving — explained, for the third time, why the ledger could not possibly be removed from the premises.

"The seals, Herr Leutnant. The municipal seals. If the book leaves this room, I am legally—"

"Herr Pfeil." Brandt kept his voice level. He had learned in the first week of commissioning that anger achieved nothing except making everyone stupider. "I am not confiscating the ledger. I am borrowing it. There is a difference, and the difference is that you will get it back, intact, with a receipt." He slid the requisition slip across the desk. "District Command requires verified rolls of every resident between the pass road and the river inside seven days. Troop trains begin moving on the eighth. If the rolls are wrong, trains leave with gaps in them, and gaps in trains become gaps in regiments, which becomes dead men at the front whose names we couldn't match to bodies. That is what the seals are for, Herr Pfeil. Not for keeping books warm."

Pfeil stared at the slip. His hands finally went still. "You speak as though the war has already begun."

Brandt looked out the window, at the square where his sergeants were posting orders to a crowd that read them in silence, the way people read weather. "Hasn't it?"

The clerk surrendered the ledger with both hands, like giving up a child to a wet nurse. Brandt took it to the trestle table in the square, opened it to the first page, and began.

It was, he had to admit, a beautiful piece of record-keeping. Decades of entries in a consistent professional hand, cross-referenced by household, occupation, parish. This was what order looked like when it was done with love instead of merely duty. Somewhere in these pages was every soul in the valley, and by Friday he was supposed to know that every soul matched its entry.

He was three pages in when Sergeant Mahler leaned over his shoulder. "Signal section reports the observatory cleared, Herr Leutnant. The old keeper made no trouble. Just asked whether we intended to keep the clock wound."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That we'd requisition it too, if it came to that."

Brandt frowned. "The clock stays wound. Whatever else happens, that mechanism runs. Half the valley's timetables hang off it — the railway, the mills, the church bells. If we let it die, we'll spend the whole mobilization arguing about what time it is." He wrote the instruction out properly, because an order spoken was an order forgotten. "Post a detail there. Two men, no interference with the works. And find out who in town repairs clocks. If the master's failing like the keeper claims, I want it seen to by someone who knows it."

Mahler noted it down. "There's a watchmaker's daughter up the hill road. Whitlock. The trade passed to her when the father died, by all accounts."

"Send word, then. Politely." Brandt returned to the ledger.

Page after page. Miller, baker, cooper, teacher. Names like stones in a wall, each one load-bearing. He worked through the morning with the sun climbing and the crowd thinning and the drums of the relief column sounding far away, toward the pass, and it was not until the light shifted and his tea had gone cold twice that he noticed it.

A smell. Faint, under the ink and dust. Tobacco — a particular pipe tobacco, sweetish, that he hadn't smelled since he was nine years old sitting on his father's knee in a signal box outside Breslau.

His father had smoked that blend for thirty years. His father had died in March.

Brandt sat very still, telling himself it was the paper, the age of the paper, some preservative. Then he turned the next page and found the source: marginalia, decades of it, tiny annotations in a precise hand recording barometric readings beside each date. The registrar had been a weather-keeper. And the handwriting of those annotations—

It wasn't identical to his father's. Nothing was ever identical. But it was the same *kind* of hand, the same disciplined slant, the same habit of crowding numbers against the rule lines. For a moment the office dissolved and he was a boy again watching his father's pen move, and grief arrived so suddenly and completely that he had to set the pen down and press his knuckles to his mouth.

"Herr Leutnant?" Mahler, at the door. "Message from the observatory detail. The keeper — Horst — he's asking again about the clock. Says if it isn't repaired by month's end, the whole valley's time goes soft. He says—" Mahler hesitated. "He says it matters more now than it ever did. Wouldn't say why."

Brandt looked at the ledger, at all those faithful names, at the annotation beside yesterday's date recording a falling glass.

"Tell the watchmaker's daughter she's hired," he said. "Whatever her fee is, the army pays it."

* * *

The summons came up the hill at dusk, delivered by a corporal too young to shave, who handed Lena a folded paper stamped with an eagle and waited, embarrassed, while she read it.

*The undersigned requests the services of Fraulein Whitlock, clockmaker, at the observatory station, regarding repair of the master regulator. Remuneration per district schedule. Reporting at earliest convenience. — Brandt, Lt., Signals Detachment.*

She read it twice. Once as a craftswoman — *master regulator, repair, earliest convenience* — and once as a daughter of forgers, counting the days. Seven days for verification. A clock to rebuild in less. And somewhere below in the town, in a cellar behind the partition wall, Marta Voss was frying potatoes that smelled like safety, and Anneli was singing her goose song again, softly, believing the walls would hold.

Lena folded the order and put it in her apron pocket, where it lay against her body like a second heartbeat.

Up the hill, then. Toward the clock, and the soldiers, and whatever the world was becoming.

She banked the fire, told the Vosses to keep the lamp low, and stepped out into a night that smelled of woodsmoke and gun oil — and above the rooftops, faint but unmistakable, she heard the observatory's dying clock strike the hour: eight strokes, the last one late, dragging, almost pleading.

Someone, she thought, should teach it to say something truer than that.
