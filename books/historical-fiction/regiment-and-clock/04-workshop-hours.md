## 4. Workshop Hours

The lamplight in the workshop made a small country of its own.

Lena had learned this years ago, working beside her father: that a bench lamp threw a circle of amber no wider than an outstretched arm, and everything inside it became the whole world — the brass wheel turning under her file, the shavings curling like pale hair, the oil catching light in a bead of gold. Everything outside the circle was another country entirely. Darker. Colder. Full of things she could not afford to look at yet.

She bent closer over the escapement anchor, letting the lamplight narrow her world to the size of a pallet jewel.

Three taps came from behind the partition wall.

Not knocks. Taps — the rhythm they had agreed on, *da-da-da... da*, the way her father used to signal her down from the loft when strangers were at the door. She set down her file, wiped her hands on the leather apron, and crossed the room without making the third floorboard sing.

"Klara?" she said softly.

A seam of candlelight showed along the edge of the partition — the old fitting for a storeroom door her father had never gotten around to hanging properly, thank God for his laziness. Klara Voss's eye appeared there, dark-ringed, watchful.

"The little one won't sleep," she whispered. "He keeps asking if the ticking is someone coming."

Lena's chest tightened. "Tell him it's the house's heart," she said. "Tell him houses have hearts, and as long as you can hear one, the house is alive, and anything alive can keep you."

Klara considered this with the gravity of a woman who had crossed two borders in winter and did not spend belief lightly. Then she nodded once and withdrew, and the seam of light went dark.

Lena stood a moment in the middle of her shop, listening. The regulator clock on the wall ticked its steady eight beats to the swing. Underneath it — faint, almost imagined — the sound of a boy being told about the heart of a house.

She went back to her lamp.

* * *

The escapement had defeated three men before her, or so the observatory's records suggested, and Lena was beginning to understand why. It wasn't the wear. Wear was honest; wear told you a story of decades and asked only patience. What she'd found when she opened the movement on the second evening was something else — the pallet stones ground at a slight, deliberate angle, the counterweight bored with an extra hole that shifted its balance by grams, grams enough to make the clock lose four seconds an hour until the error compounded into minutes and the minutes into lies. Someone had wanted time in this town to run slow, and had wanted it to look like age.

She had not yet said aloud what she suspected. Saying it would make it a fact, and facts had a way of requiring action.

Instead she cut new pallets. It was slow work, jeweler's work, and she was grateful for the slowness. Her father had taught her that hands were a mercy — that when the mind ran like a frightened horse, you gave it a road of small precise tasks and let it tire itself out. Fit the stone. Check the angle. Stone the surface to a mirror. Fit, check, stone.

By midnight the first pallet sat true in its seat, and she allowed herself to look up, and the world beyond the lamplight came back all at once.

The workshop at night was full of shapes that were not furniture. A trunk that breathed. A coat hung on a nail that shifted its weight from one foot to the other. Behind the partition, four people lived in the space where her father had kept timber stock, sleeping on sawdust mattresses, eating what she carried in from the market in a basket sized for one.

The Vosses. Though that was not their name — that was the name her father had written into the registrar's ledger eleven years ago, in a hand he had practiced for weeks until it looked like a clerk's hand, tired and upright and unremarkable. *Voss, Anton. Voss, Klara. Voss, Josef.* Entries with dates and witnesses and the registrar's own ink drying over them, official as scripture. He had done it because the real names would have meant the border police and the trains going east, and because a man who repairs clocks believes, stubbornly, that broken things can be mended.

And now the ledger with those entries in it sat in the town hall under a grey uniform's arm, requisitioned, sealed, audited — every forged line of her father's careful pen waiting to be read by eyes trained to read nothing else.

Lena picked up her file again. Fit, check, stone. Her hands knew what to do even when she didn't.

* * *

Near two in the morning the partition opened, and Anton Voss came out — a big man gone soft with hiding, moving like a man walking on river ice. He stood at the edge of her lamplight, holding himself back from it, as if the light were a country he needed papers to enter.

"You should sleep, Fräulein Whitlock," he rumbled.

"After this pair." She tilted the anchor toward him so the lamplight slid across the fresh-cut stone. "See? That face will kiss the escape wheel forty thousand times a day. If I leave one burr, the clock stumbles. And this clock—" she stopped herself.

"This clock is the town's heartbeat," Anton finished quietly. "Klara says the whole valley listens to it. The trains. The factories." His voice dropped further. "The soldiers too, now."

"Yes." She turned back to the work, because looking at him made the fear climb her throat. "They want it running true. They want every departure timed to it."

"And you are the one who makes it true."

"I'm the one who makes it *run*," she said. "True is a bigger word than people think."

Anton was quiet for a while. In the silence she heard the boy coughing behind the wall — the small dry cough that Klara dosed with honey and boiled pine needles, the cough that had been getting better, then worse, then better, tracking the weather of their fear.

"My father wrote your name in that ledger," Lena said abruptly. It came out before she could stop it, and once started, the rest followed. "All your names. He forged them. Eleven years ago. I found the practice sheets once, in a drawer, and he told me it was calligraphy practice. I believed him. Children believe their fathers the way clocks believe their springs — completely, until the spring breaks."

Anton lowered himself onto the apprentice stool across the bench, uninvited, and she was glad of it. "You're afraid the ledger will hang us," he said simply. "Yes. We know what we cost you. We've known since the soldiers marched in."

"You don't cost me anything. My father's lie costs me. Or my truth does. I haven't decided which yet." She laughed, a thin unhappy sound, and pressed her thumb against the corner of her eye. "Forgive me. It's late, and the work makes me honest."

"Work does that," Anton agreed. He leaned forward, studying the great skeleton of the movement laid open on her bench — the going train, the strike train, the cams stacked like a city of brass. "My father was a millwright. In the old country. When I was a boy I thought machines were magic that had decided to be useful." He touched nothing, but his finger traced the air above the escape wheel. "Then I learned machines are just promises. Every gear is a promise to the next gear. Break one promise and the whole machine becomes a liar."

Lena looked at him. "Who taught you that?"

"A man who broke his promises to save us," Anton said, "and never regretted it." He rose, stiffly, and moved back toward the partition, pausing at its shadow-line. "Sleep after your pair, Fräulein. The boy asked tonight whether you were the angel of the house. Klara told him angels don't swear under their breath at brass. He was very disappointed."

The ghost of a smile reached her mouth and stayed there after the partition closed.

* * *

She slept three hours on the cot in the back and woke with her father's voice already talking in her head, the way it did some mornings, patient and dry as paper.

*A forgery isn't a lie, Lene. It's a translation. You take the truth and write it in a language the fools can read without asking questions.*

She had been nine. She had nodded solemnly and understood nothing, and understood everything, and the understanding had gone down into her bones like oil into a bearing, silent and permanent.

Now she sat at the bench in the grey hour before dawn, and the thought arrived the way thoughts do when the body is too tired to refuse them: *the ledger is a mechanism too.* Names as teeth. Dates as pivots. The registrar's seal as the mainspring that drove the whole thing. And somewhere in that mechanism, her father had cut teeth of his own — false ones, fitted so well that the train had run smooth for eleven years. But an army auditor was a different kind of hand on the winding crank. Auditors checked depth of engagement. They counted the teeth.

Fit, check, stone. She worked. The lamp burned low. Outside, the valley lay under frost, and somewhere down in it the church bells hung dumb in their towers, forbidden to speak except on the master clock's authority, and the master clock lay gutted on her bench like a patient on a table, trusting her with its open chest.

It was nearly six when she heard them — boots on the cobbles below, several pairs, unhurried, the walk of men who had been given a street and intended to use it. Grey coats in the blue morning. One of them stopped before her shuttered window, and she saw through the slats the flash of a peaked cap, young-faced beneath it, a man consulting a folded paper and then the numbers painted on the doorframes, checking one address against another the way she checked a pallet against its stone.

The knock, when it came, was correct. Three measured raps.

Behind the partition wall, in the dark, a child stopped breathing.

Lena stood very still in her lamplight, which had become, without her noticing, the only lit thing on the whole dark hillside street — a beacon, a confession, a target. She looked at the escapement half-finished on her bench, at the door three steps away, at the partition.

Then she untied her apron, hung it on its peg, and went to answer.
