## 5. The Officer's Watch

The watch had been his father's, and it had stopped at 4:17 on a morning Brandt could not remember and could not forget.

He turned it over in his palm as he walked down from the square, past the shuttered bakery, past the church whose bell had rung eleven minutes late for three days running — the whole town drifting out of true like a boat slipping its mooring. The requisition orders were posted now on the notice board in three copies and two languages. By evening every window on the market street had a grey reflection in it: soldiers measuring, counting, writing things in ledgers.

His own ledger sat heavy under his arm. He was beginning to hate the weight of it.

The clockmaker's workshop stood at the end of Tanner's Lane, a narrow building squeezed between a cooper's yard and the blank wall of a stable, its upper windows lit already against the early dark. A wooden sign — *Whitlock & Son, Clocks Repaired* — hung by one chain, the *Son* half-obliterated by weather. He had read somewhere in the town file that the son was dead, or gone; that only the daughter remained.

He knocked. The sound inside went quiet — not merely paused but *stopped*, the way a room stops when someone is listening to decide whether you exist. Then footsteps, brisk, and the door opened on lamplight and the smell of oil and brass.

"Lieutenant." Lena Whitlock looked at him the way she had at the observatory: levelly, without fear, which unsettled him more than fear would have. "You've come about the clock."

"I've come about a watch." He held it up. "It keeps stopping. I'm told you're the only person in the valley who might know why."

Something moved behind her eyes — calculation, he thought, though about what he couldn't say. She stepped back from the doorway. "Bring it to the bench. Mind the shavings."

* * *

The workshop was smaller than it looked from outside, and warmer, crowded with the patient machinery of small hours: lathes, drawers of tiny drawers, a wall of clocks all disagreeing politely about the time. A lamp hung low over a workbench scarred by a century of blades. And along the back wall, oddly, a partition — new-looking timber, floor to ceiling, closing off what must have been the storeroom. A curtain where a door should be.

Brandt noted it the way he noted everything now, automatically, the war having taught him that rooms have backs to them. Then he put the noting away. He was off duty. He had told himself so twice on the walk down.

"Your father's work?" he asked, nodding at the sign outside.

"My father's everything." She took the watch from him without ceremony, held it to her ear, and frowned. "When did it stop?"

"It doesn't stop. It stops *and starts*. It'll run perfectly for a day, then lose an hour overnight, then catch itself up by noon. It's maddening. It's—" He stopped, because the word that arrived was *haunted*, and he was not a man who said such things.

But she nodded as if he'd said it. "They usually are," she said. "May I?"

She worked with the lamp pulled close, and Brandt found himself watching her hands rather than the watch — quick, sure, the nails cut short, a burn-scar across one knuckle shaped like a comma. There was a comfort in watching anyone work at anything well. He'd forgotten how much he missed that. In the army, everyone was busy and nothing got finished.

"The balance staff," she murmured. "Someone's been at this. Amateur repair — a heavy hand." She glanced up. "Who tried to fix it before me?"

"My father. Before he died." The words came out flatter than he intended. "Eleven years ago. He wasn't a watchmaker. He was a railway clerk. He believed a man ought to be able to mend what he owned."

"And did he believe that about himself? That he could be mended?" She said it lightly, not looking up, the way people say dangerous things when they want them mistaken for small talk.

"He believed it about other people," Brandt said. "Mostly. He was a difficult man. Principled in ways that cost other people the price."

"Mm." She opened the case back. Inside, engraved into the dust-fogged steel, a monogram: *A.B.* His grandfather's initials. Three generations of Brandts had carried this watch toward some appointment or other, and here it was in the hands of a stranger in a valley they intended to make punctual by force.

"You resent him," she said. Not a question.

"I—"

"You do. You talk about him like a man describing weather he was caught in." She set her loupe to her eye. "I used to think resentment was shameful. Now I think it's just unfinished business wearing a uniform."

Behind the partition, very faintly, something creaked. A settling of timber, Brandt told himself. Old buildings spoke at night. He kept his eyes on the bench.

"My father wanted me to be a clergyman," he said, surprising himself. "I wanted to be an engineer. Bridges. Things that hold things up honestly. Instead—" he gestured vaguely at his own grey sleeve, at the ledger on the corner of her bench, at the whole grey apparatus of his life. "Instead I count names."

"That's not nothing," Lena said quietly. "Names."

"You'd be surprised how much it is nothing. You write them down, you verify them against faces, you stamp them, and none of it holds anything up. A bridge either stands or falls and everyone can see which. A list just—" He stopped again. He was saying too much. He was saying too much to a civilian in a requisitioned town while his men slept in the schoolhouse, and there was a creak behind that partition again, longer this time, and beneath it — he would swear later, though he never did — a breath drawn small and held.

Lena's hands had gone still on the watch. For a moment the workshop held only the tick of forty disagreeing clocks.

"Your father," she said at last, "the clerk. Did he love railways because of the timetable, or despite it?"

"What a strange question."

"It's the only question worth asking a man who counts names." She looked up, and the loupe made one eye enormous and unguarded. "Because timetables are promises. That's what my father taught me. Every departure printed on a board is a promise that the world will still be legible tomorrow. Some men love that promise. Some men spend their whole lives learning it's a lie told in good handwriting."

"And which was your father?"

She smiled — briefly, crookedly, and it transformed her face the way lamplight transforms a room. "Both. That's why he was difficult too." She bent back to the watch. "I'll need an hour. There's tea if you drink it. There's nothing else, so don't ask."

* * *

They drank tea standing up, which he understood was the custom of workshops everywhere, and talked, or half-talked, in the sideways manner of two people circling a thing neither would name. She asked about the observatory — whether the soldiers respected the instruments, whether old Horst was being fed properly, whether the requisition meant the dome would be drilled for a flagpole (it did not, he assured her, though he wrote down that she'd asked). He asked about the master clock, and she gave him a craftsman's answer, precise and unrevealing, like a locked drawer presented handle-first.

"They say the whole valley runs on it," he offered. "Every bell, every factory whistle. I didn't understand that, before. In the city, time comes from everywhere and nowhere. Here it comes from one hill."

"One mechanism." She turned her cup slowly. "Which is why whoever broke it understood this place better than your general staff ever will. Break the hill and the valley wanders."

"We're going to fix it," Brandt said. "That's the order. When it runs true, everything synchronizes to it. Trains, garrisons, dispatches." He heard the sentence arrive in the room and wished he could recall it, because her cup had stopped turning, and the warmth between them — thin, unlikely, real — thinned with it.

"When," she repeated.

"Within the week, if you keep your schedule."

"My schedule." Something flickered across her face, fast, like a bird crossing a window. "And after it's synchronized, Lieutenant? What does the valley get for its punctuality?"

"Order," he said, and hated the word in his mouth. "Safety. The war passes through instead of staying."

"Does it ever just pass through?"

He had no answer that wasn't a lie, so he drank his tea. Behind them, one of the wall clocks began to strike nine, and then another, offset, and then a third, and for half a minute the little shop rang with overlapping voices — bronze arguing with bronze — until they settled, one by one, into silence.

"Listen to that," Lena said softly. "Forty clocks and no two of them agree. My father used to say a town isn't a place, it's an agreement about time. Break the agreement and see what people turn out to be."

"And what do they turn out to be?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she was deciding, he watched most of it happen and missed the conclusion.

"Themselves," she said. "Only that."

* * *

He left near ten, taking the watch with him, running true — she had fitted a new staff, polished the pivots, done in an hour what his father had fumbled at through a decade of Sundays. At the door he paused.

"Miss Whitlock. The partition wall in your storeroom. New timber, is it? Water damage?"

Her face did not move at all. That was the remarkable thing; he had interrogated enough men to know every variety of flinch, and she produced none. Only stillness, and behind the stillness something almost like respect.

"Damp gets in off the stable roof," she said. "I'll thank you not to report my carpentry to the audit."

"Miss Whitlock, I—" He stopped. Everything he could say next was either a betrayal of his oath or a lie to her, and he found he was unwilling to do either, so he stood in the doorway holding his father's watch like a man holding a live coal. "Thank you for the repair," he said finally. "I'll recommend you for the observatory work. You're clearly the best hand in the valley."

"Clearly," she agreed, and closed the door — gently, not slammed, gently, which somehow felt worse.

He walked back up through the dark with the watch ticking against his ribs, steady now, honest now, and thought about bridges, and lists, and the difference between the two. A bridge holds weight. A list only records it. Somewhere behind a partition wall in a clockmaker's shop, he was nearly certain, someone had been breathing very quietly — a family, perhaps, or a memory, or his own conscience learning to speak.

In the morning, he knew, the verification orders would reach his desk. Names against faces, house by house, starting with Tanner's Lane.

He wound the watch, set it by the observatory clock glowing on the hill, and told himself he would sleep.

He did not.
