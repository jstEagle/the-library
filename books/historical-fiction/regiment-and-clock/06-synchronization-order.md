## 6. Synchronization Order

The rain came in off the valley like a rumor of worse things to come, and Lena heard the boots on the cobbles before she saw them — two pairs, unhurried, stopping beneath her awning.

She was soldering a tooth back onto the great wheel's spare pinion, and her first instinct was the one her father had drilled into her: when soldiers come, be boring. She set down the iron with deliberate slowness, wiped her hands on her apron, and opened the door as if she had been expecting nothing at all.

Lieutenant Brandt stood under the dripping sign of WHITLOCK & SON, CLOCKS AND WATCHES REPAIRED, his cap darkened to slate by the wet. Behind him a private held an oilcloth folder against his chest as though it might dissolve.

"Fräulein Whitlock." Brandt's voice carried that careful flatness she'd begun to recognize — a man reading from a page he hadn't written. "May we come in? It concerns the observatory clock."

"The workshop is small."

"Small is fine."

She stepped aside and hated how loudly the floorboards spoke beneath military boots. Behind the partition wall, three feet of pine and a bolted door separated her from Marta Voss and the children, who had been told, always, to be silent as pendulums when boots sounded. Lena had taught them the word *pendulum* herself, made it a game. Now the word swung through her skull with its own terrible rhythm.

Brandt took off his cap but did not sit. He looked around the workshop the way she'd seen him look at things before — not searching, exactly. Cataloguing. The bench, the lamp, the half-disassembled striking train laid out on felt. His gaze rested a half-second too long on the second teacup drying beside the basin.

"Progress?" he asked.

"On schedule." A lie so smooth it frightened her. "The escapement is nearly rebuilt. Another week, perhaps less."

"Then you'll want to know what your week is worth." He nodded to the private, who opened the oilcloth folder and handed over a single sheet, stamped twice in violet ink. "Read it, please. Aloud, if you would. I'm instructed that the town should hear it from someone they've met, rather than from a wall."

Lena read.

*By order of the District Command: upon restoration of the Observatory Master Clock, said instrument shall constitute the sole authorized time-standard for all railway departures, troop movements, and industrial shifts within the valley. All public clocks shall be synchronized to it. Verification of civil registers shall commence seven days prior to the first scheduled departure. Households whose entries cannot be verified will be remanded for processing before trains roll.*

*Processing.* The word sat there in its official ink like a coin worn smooth by use — a word designed so no one would ever have to say what it meant.

She looked up. "This says every clock in town answers to the master."

"It does."

"And anyone whose name doesn't match their face—"

"—will be dealt with before the first departure." Brandt's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "That is what it says."

The rain filled the silence. On the partition shelf, hidden behind a tin of polishing rouge, the Vosses' little clock ticked — the one Lena had silenced with a cork in its case each night, forgetting this morning, forgetting because Marta had smiled at breakfast and the world had briefly seemed a place where clocks could just be clocks. Its ticking now sounded enormous. She watched Brandt's eyes flick toward it, hold, and return to her face with nothing in them at all, which was somehow worse than curiosity.

"When does verification begin?" she asked.

"Six days after the clock runs true." He took the sheet back, squared its edges. "Which means, Fräulein Whitlock, that the speed of your hands has become a matter of some consequence. To a great many people."

"To the army," she said.

"To the army," he agreed. And then, quieter, in a register that belonged to no order or regulation: "To everyone waiting for those trains. There are eleven thousand names in that ledger, Miss Whitlock. Most of them are only people who want to be somewhere else than here when the shooting starts. I'd prefer the machinery between them and their departure work flawlessly. That is why I came myself, in the rain, instead of sending a corporal."

Something in her wanted to hate him and found the hatred kept slipping its grip. He was the lock on the cage, and he was polite about it, and politeness was harder to fight than cruelty.

"You said 'most of them,'" she said carefully. "Of the names."

"I said most." He put his cap back on. Rain ran off the brim. "A ledger is paper, Miss Whitlock. Paper is patient. It records what people wrote, not what people are. I have found in my short career that these are frequently different things." He paused at the door. "Your father was the registrar's assistant once, wasn't he? Before he died?"

Her heart performed a maneuver no escapement could regulate. "He did clerical work. Years ago."

"So the record shows." Brandt looked at her for a moment longer than the conversation required, and whatever moved behind his eyes, he filed it away unspoken. "Finish your clock, Fräulein. The whole valley is standing in your doorway, whether it knows it or not."

Then the boots were gone down the wet street, and Lena stood alone with the violet stamp burning in her memory and the little clock still ticking behind the partition like a confession.

* * *

Marta emerged before Lena even reached the wall — she must have been pressed against it the whole time, listening through the wood the way refugees learn to listen, with their whole skeletons.

"We heard enough," Marta said. Her German carried the flat vowels of the eastern provinces; her hands carried flour from the bread she'd insisted on baking in Lena's oven, because idleness, she said, was the one country that had refused them entry everywhere. "Verification. Names against faces. They will go door to door."

"They'll go door to door," Lena confirmed. She sat down heavily on the workstool, and for once let her hands be empty, which felt obscene. "Six days after the clock runs. Which means every day I work faster brings that day closer. Every day I slow down—" She stopped.

Marta finished it for her. "Every day you slow down, they wonder why the clockmaker is stalling."

The trap had teeth on both sides. That was the elegance of it — she could see now that it was elegant, the way a well-made movement was elegant, every part bearing on the next. Finish the clock, and the timetable locked into place with her family inside it. Delay the clock, and suspicion arrived ahead of the soldiers. There was no gear ratio that escaped the mechanism. Someone had designed this, and whoever they were, they understood time better than kindness.

Karl appeared in the loft hatch above the partition, twelve years old and all elbows, with his sister Ruth peering past his shoulder. They'd been up there among the stored cases, breathing dust and silence for two hours.

"Is it true they take people whose papers fail?" Karl asked. No fear in it — he'd burned through fear months ago, on the road. Just the flat question of a boy doing arithmetic.

"It's true," Lena said.

"Then we leave before then." He shrugged, as if stating that water was wet. "Over the pass, before snow. Papa said the pass closes mid-month."

"Your papa also said the pass was watched from the toll house," Marta said sharply, and then softened, reaching up to touch the boy's ankle through the hatch. "It isn't arithmetic yet, Liebchen. Let it become arithmetic first."

But it was already arithmetic, Lena thought, looking at the bench, at the delicate steel parts laid out like surgery. It was already numbers: six days of verification minus five days of remaining work left one day of margin, and margin was a luxury measured in lives.

* * *

She worked until the lamp burned low, and the work itself did what work always did for her — narrowed the world to the width of a pivot. She fitted the new escape wheel, tested the locking faces under magnification, adjusted the pallets until the tick-tock of the test movement fell into that perfect dead-even cadence that Horst called *an honest heartbeat*. Honest. Everything honest except the hands holding it.

Near midnight she gave up on sleep and walked up through the wet streets to the observatory, letting herself in with Horst's key, because the old keeper had taken to sleeping in the chair by the stove downstairs and had told her weeks ago, *come and go, girl, the building knows you now.*

She climbed to the clock room and stood before the open case, the gutted movement hanging like a ribcage of brass, and she asked it the question she couldn't ask anyone else.

*What do you want from me?*

Because that was the thing about a clock — it didn't care about orders stamped in violet. It cared about truth. Escapement, impulse, lock, release. You could lie to a registrar; you could forge a line in a ledger; but you could not lie to a going train. Either the wheels turned or they jammed. Either the chime rang the hours or it rang something else entirely.

Something else entirely.

The thought arrived the way thoughts do in clock rooms at midnight — fully formed, cold, and undeniable. She thought of the chime barrels she'd cleaned, the pins set into them note by note, the Westminster pattern her father used to whistle while shaving. Pins could be moved. Patterns could be changed. A bell sequence was, after all, just another kind of writing — and she was, God forgive her, her father's daughter, raised on the principle that any text could be edited by someone brave enough and steady-handed enough.

She wasn't sure yet what message a bell could carry, or to whom. But the shape of the idea settled into her chest and began, quietly, to keep its own time.

Behind her, on the stairs, a floorboard creaked.

"Late for keeping watch over me, Miss Whitlock," said old Horst's voice, ragged and amused, "or early for keeping watch over something else?"

She turned. The old man stood in the doorway wrapped in a blanket, lantern in hand, his eyes bright with the fever that was eating him alive and with something else besides — something that sharpened when he saw where she was looking.

Horst followed her gaze to the chime barrel. And smiled.

"Ah," he said softly. "So you've finally asked the right question. Come and sit by the fire, girl. There's a voice in this machine you don't know yet — and I think you're ready to hear it."
