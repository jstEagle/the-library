## 7. Sabotage Reconsidered

The clue came on a Thursday, in the shape of a filing mark too clean for rust.

Lena had spent three days convincing herself the escapement's damage was age — that some long-ago apprentice with a heavy hand had scored the pallet faces, that the missing tooth on the third wheel was simple metal fatigue. It was easier to believe in wear than in will. Wear forgave everyone.

But this morning, cleaning the crutch with spirits of ammonia, she found the scratch. A single line, dead straight, running across the brass like a surveyor's mark. Rust did not draw straight lines. Fatigue did not draw straight lines. Only a file held by a steady hand drew straight lines, and only someone who understood the mechanism would have chosen that exact spot — where one groove would steal just enough impulse from each swing to slow the whole train by four seconds an hour. Four seconds. Not enough for anyone to notice day to day. Enough to let the town drift gently out of true, week over week, month over month, until every clock it trusted was quietly lying.

She sat back on her stool and looked at the great movement looming above her, all its wheels stilled, and felt the floor of her certainty tilt.

Someone had wanted time here to stop. Or nearly stop. Someone who knew clocks well enough to wound them without killing them.

There were perhaps six people in the valley with that knowledge. Two were dead. One was herself.

* * *

She climbed the narrow stair to the keeper's quarters at dusk, carrying the scratch question in her chest like a coal she didn't dare set down.

Horst's room occupied the observatory's east shoulder, beneath the dome's curve, and it smelled of camphor and cold tea and something else underneath — the sweetish, waxy smell of a body eating itself. He lay in his bed against the far wall, propped high on pillows, and he watched her come in with eyes that had gone the pale grey of old pewter but had lost none of their arithmetic.

"I wondered," he said, "when you'd find the line."

Her breath went out of her. "You."

"Me." He said it without apology, the way another man might admit to owning a coat. "Sit down, girl. My legs are finished but my voice still works, and I've been rehearsing this conversation for two years."

Lena did not sit. She stood in the doorway with her fists closed, feeling the strangest mixture of things: fury, and relief, and something dangerously close to kinship. "Do you know what you did? The trains run late now. The factories fine their workers over minutes they never lost. The church bells have been lying to half the valley since spring—"

"The church bells," Horst said, "have been telling the truth slowly." He coughed then, a deep wet sound, and waited it out with the patience of a man long accustomed to his own ruin. When he could speak again he gestured to the chair beside the bed, and this time Lena took it, because whatever anger she carried, she would not spend it on a dying man standing up.

"Why?" she asked.

He turned his head toward the window, where the last light was going amber on the hills. Beyond them, faintly, came the sound of the garrison drilling in the square — boots, a shouted count, the crack of a drum. They both listened to it as if it were weather.

"In 1870 I was your age," he said. "I kept this clock through the last war, and I watched what happened when the timetables ran true. Trains of boys going east on schedule, every hour, precise to the minute, because precision is what we do and we thought it was innocent. Precision is never innocent, Lena. It is always precision *for*. Somebody winds the wheel; somebody else is ground by it." His hand emerged from the blanket — thin as a bird's foot, but steady — and pointed at the ceiling, toward the great mechanism overhead. "So when the orders began coming down from the capital this spring, the requisitions, the mustering rolls, I asked myself what one old man with a file could do. And the answer was: four seconds an hour. Slow everything down. Delay the arrival of it all by as much as I dared without breaking anything that couldn't be mended."

"They sent for me because of you."

"They sent for you because of me, yes. And I let them, because I needed someone to mend it eventually — after. Whatever *after* turns out to be." His pewter eyes came back to hers. "I'm sorry for that. I chose you the way one chooses a successor. It isn't fair. Nothing about inheritance is fair."

Lena looked down at her hands. They smelled of brass polish and lamp oil. "My father used to say the opposite," she heard herself say. "He said a clockmaker's job is to be honest to the minute even when the minute is terrible. That the whole world rests on people agreeing about time."

"And your father," Horst said softly, "kept more than one set of books, if I know anything about men who come to valleys under new names."

The words landed in her like a dropped wrench. She looked up sharply, but his face held no menace, only the mild attention of a man who has spent fifty years watching a town through a telescope and missed nothing.

"How—"

"The Vosses," he said. "Or whatever their name was before your father wrote them into the ledger as Vosses, twenty years ago, with his lovely careful hand. I saw him do it. I've seen everything from this hill, child. That's what observatories are for." He coughed again, shorter this time, and waved off her half-rising alarm. "I'm not threatening anyone. I'm telling you why I'm giving you what I'm about to give you. Because you understand already — better than I ever did — that a name can be a mechanism too. That a ledger has an escapement. And that sometimes the honest thing and the merciful thing are different machines entirely, and a craftsman has to choose which one she's building."

* * *

He made her wait three days while he gathered strength, sleeping most of each afternoon, and on the third evening he had her light both lamps and bring the box.

It was a plain pine case, iron-cornered, older than she was, and inside, nested in wool gone grey with age, lay a set of cams — chime cams, she saw at once, a dozen of them, cut from hard bronze, each shaped along its rim with lobes and valleys as intricate as coastline. Beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, lay small files, gravers, and a pin gauge worn shiny from use.

"The strike train is the clock's second voice," Horst said. He made her hold the box on her knees while he talked, as though the weight of it mattered, as though he were handing her something heavier than bronze. "Everyone thinks the going train is the clock — the hands, the hours, the keeping of time. But the going train can't speak. All it does is count. The chime train *says* something. Twelve strokes at midnight means one thing; eight strokes at noon means another. The whole valley reads its sentences without knowing they're reading."

He lifted one cam from the wool and turned it in the lamplight, and the lobes threw moving shadows across his face.

"Now. The hammer lifts when the cam lobe passes the lifting piece — so, if you recut the lobes, you change what the bells say. Cut them shallow and close together, you get a stutter, a hurry. Deep and spaced, you get calm. And if you know the pattern — really know it, the way a musician knows a tune — you can put a sentence into the bells that only certain listeners will hear as anything other than music." He set the cam back down with exaggerated care, as if returning a sleeping thing. "I cut these during the last war and never used them. I told myself I lacked the nerve. Now I lack the time, which is worse."

Lena stared at the box. Her pulse had gone strange. "A sentence. What kind of sentence?"

"The kind that says *go*," Horst said simply. "The kind that says *tonight, the north road, before the counting stops* — to anyone who's been taught to listen." His gaze fixed on her, and there was nothing vague left in it, nothing of the drifting invalid. "You'll need to teach your listeners first, of course. A code is useless without two ends. But that part, I think, is already done — isn't it? You have people under your roof whose names exist only because your father wrote them there. People who'd listen very carefully indeed if the bells began saying something unusual."

The room seemed to contract around the truth of it. Lena set the box on the bed and pressed her palms flat to the pine lid, feeling the grain under her skin, grounding herself the way her father had taught her to ground a loose movement: firmly, and without sentiment.

"If the army synchronizes to this clock," she said, "every timetable in the valley locks to it. Departure schedules. Verification sweeps. Everything measured from the moment the master strikes."

"Yes."

"Then whoever controls the chime controls—" She stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things demanded action.

"Whoever controls the second voice," Horst finished for her, "controls what the town hears at the moment it matters most. I slowed the hands, girl, because slowing was all I could imagine. You're younger than I was. Imagine better."

Outside, the drill yard had fallen quiet. In the silence the master clock's tick came down through the floorboards, patient as a heartbeat, counting toward whatever midnight was coming.

Horst slid back against his pillows, exhausted now, his face the color of candle tallow, but his hand found the box and pushed it an inch toward her — a small gesture, deliberate as a signature.

"Take them," he said. "And take the files. And when you cut, cut clean. A sloppy cam tells lies to everyone. A good one tells the truth only to the people it loves."

* * *

She worked past midnight in the clock room, not on the escapement but on learning — laying the cams out on felt, studying each lobe with her loupe, tracing the profiles onto paper until her own hand could reproduce them from memory. The going train hung open beside her, repaired now, honest again, waiting only for final assembly. Four seconds an hour returned to the valley. The war would arrive on schedule after all, unless—

Unless.

Near two in the morning she heard the stair creak, and froze, and then relaxed by degrees as the footsteps resolved themselves: slow, uneven, the tread of a man climbing by will rather than strength. Horst appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt and dressing gown, absurdly formal, as though visiting a colleague.

"You're still here," he said.

"I live here now. Practically."

"Mm." He lowered himself onto the workbench stool with a grimace and sat a while, listening to the tick, watching her papers of traced profiles flutter slightly in the draft from the dome. Then: "They're posting the synchronization order tomorrow. Did you know? Brandt's courier came up at dusk. Once the order's signed, verification teams start at the ledger within the week. Names against faces, house by house." He glanced at her sidelong, pewter eyes unreadable. "I mention it only because a craftsman should always know her deadline."

Lena's hands had gone still on the felt. Through the wall she could feel the clock ticking, steady, indifferent, counting down toward a midnight that would belong to the army unless she made it belong to something else first.

"Then I'd better learn faster," she said.

Horst nodded once, satisfied, and began the slow climb back to his bed. At the door he paused, one hand on the frame.

"One more thing, girl. When the night comes — and it will come soon — don't decide alone. Decide with the truth in the room, whatever it costs. I decided alone, forty years ago and two years ago both, and see what it bought me." He tapped the doorframe with a knuckle, a small dry knock. "Alone is how clocks break."

Then he was gone, and Lena sat alone among her traced lobes and her borrowed files, and reached for a graver.
