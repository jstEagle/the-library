## 8. Verification Day

Brandt woke before the bugle, as he always did, and lay listening to the town breathe beneath his window. It breathed differently now than it had a month ago. A town with soldiers in it breathes in short, careful sips — doors opening and closing too softly, footsteps timed to avoid the patrol rhythms everyone has already memorized. He knew the sound the way a doctor knows a fever.

The verification lists came down from the adjutant's office at first light: forty-one names for the eastern quarter, thirty-six for the river streets, each entry to be checked against a living face before noon. Brandt took the hillside route himself. He told himself it was because the hillside households were few and far between and could be covered quickly by one officer on foot; he told himself it was efficiency. He was getting practiced at telling himself things.

His pocket watch sat in his waistcoat where it always sat, repaired now, keeping perfect time. Lena Whitlock had returned it to him eleven days ago with the crystal polished and the hands set to the observatory signal, and he had wound it every morning since with a small ceremonial care that embarrassed him if he thought about it too long. Eleven days since he'd stood in her workshop among the brass shavings and the smell of oil, talking about fathers, and heard — or almost heard — nothing at all behind the partition wall except the ordinary sounds of a working room.

He had thought about it every day since. That was the trouble.

* * *

The hillside went quietly. An old woman whose ledger name was older than she was. A widower who answered questions through a door he never fully opened. Two brothers whose surname appeared twice in the register with different birth years, which the clerk had annotated in red ink with the single word *query*, and whom Brandt recorded as verified because they were plainly who they said they were and plainly terrified of being asked again. He wrote their names in his neat officer's hand and felt the pen heavier than pens should be.

By mid-morning he stood at the top of the lane below the observatory road, where the workshop sat tucked against the slope like something the hill had grown rather than built. Smoke rose thin from its chimney. Through the open door he could see lamplight even in daylight — Lena worked by lamp whatever the hour, as though the sun were an unreliable colleague.

She was at the bench when he knocked, sleeves rolled, a jeweler's loupe pushed up into her hair. She looked up, and he watched the whole sequence cross her face in less than a second: recognition, warmth, then something clamping down over both like a brake shoe closing on a wheel.

"Lieutenant Brandt." She wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried, perfectly ordinary. "You've come about the clock?"

"I've come about the ledger." He held up the folder. "Verification. Every household on my list."

"Of course." She stepped back to let him in. "Though I can save you some walking. I'm not in your book."

"You're not?"

"The Whitlocks registered in the valley only after my father bought the property. Before that—" she shrugged, "—we were nowhere official. My father was particular about paperwork. He'd have liked your audit, actually. He believed a name should match a person exactly once, and then be left alone forever."

Brandt opened the folder anyway and ran his finger down the column of hillside entries, mostly to have somewhere to put his eyes. The workshop was warm. The forge banked low. On the bench, disassembled escapement parts lay ranked on felt with the discipline of a surgical tray.

"It runs?" he said. "The master clock. You've got it running?"

"Running. Not yet right." She came to stand beside him at the bench, close enough that he could smell machine oil and, beneath it, woodsmoke. "There's a difference. Running is easy. Right is the hard part. Right is what your timetables will hang on."

"And when will it be right?"

"Soon." She said it steadily, but he had learned in these weeks to hear the seams in her steadiness — the way a clockmaker hears the tick under a chime. "Before your synchronization order needs it. You have my word."

He nodded and closed the folder. And then, because the inspection required it and because he wanted the moment to last longer than it should, he said, "I'm required to view the premises. Cellar, outbuildings, any rooms where persons might be lodged unregistered."

Something moved in the room. Not visibly — nothing fell, nothing shifted — but the air itself seemed to tighten the way it does before a bell is struck.

"The cellar," Lena said. "It's full of scrap and stock timber. There's no lodging there."

"Nevertheless."

He followed her to the back of the workshop, past the partition wall he remembered from his earlier visits, past a workbench stacked with finished repairs awaiting collection. She lifted the cellar hatch and lit a candle stub on the way down, and the light below showed him what she'd promised: crates, offcuts of oak and beech, a dead potbelly stove, coils of wire. Ordinary clutter, honestly dusty.

He stood at the top of the stairs while she descended, holding the candle high.

And he heard it.

Breathing. More than one person's breathing, deliberately slowed, deliberately shallow, coming from behind the stack of stock timber against the far wall — no, from beyond it, from the gap he could see now between the timber and the stone, a darkness deeper than the cellar's own dark. A child's breathing, quickest of the three, and someone — a mother's hand, he imagined — pressing quiet into it.

Lena had stopped moving. The candle flame stood perfectly still in her grip, as still as if she had stopped her own heart to keep it from flickering.

Brandt looked at the timber. He looked at the darkness beyond it. He counted, without meaning to, the seconds between breaths, because counting was what he did, because numbers had never yet lied to him the way men did.

Three breathers. Perhaps four. The Voss family — he had seen the name in the seized registrar's ledger the week it came through his office, an old entry in a firm, careful hand, the handwriting of a man who understood exactly how much weight a written name could bear. Voss, Voss, Voss, and a daughter born the year the border moved. Verified, the clerk's red ink had noted, *by prior record*. Prior record. A father's forgery, standing guard over strangers for fifteen years.

Above him, faintly, the workshop's regulator clock ticked on, indifferent.

"Anything down there?" Lena asked. Her voice was level. Her knuckles were white around the candle.

He made himself take the time an honest inspector would take. He walked the length of the cellar floor, boots loud on the boards, loud enough to cover any small sound the dark might make. He looked at the stove. He looked at the wire. He crouched, once, by the timber stack, close enough that if he exhaled too deeply the breath would touch whoever waited behind it, and he said, in the flat tone of a man reading a form aloud:

"Clear."

Then he climbed the stairs, and Lena closed the hatch, and neither of them spoke until they were back at the bench among the brass and the lamplight.

"You didn't look," she said quietly. It wasn't gratitude. It was a question aimed like a blade, testing whether he would flinch.

"I looked," Brandt said. "At everything that was there."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He straightened the folder in his hands. "Miss Whitlock. When the timetable locks — three days from now, when the master clock becomes the army's reference — every unverified person in this valley goes on a detention list. Not a deportation list. Yet. But lists grow. I've watched them grow." He met her eyes. "Whatever you're doing with that clock, do it before the trains are scheduled to it. After that, time stops belonging to this town."

For a moment she looked at him the way she looked at a movement she couldn't yet diagnose — total attention, no mercy in it.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

Because you repaired my father's watch without asking what it meant to me. Because you talked about fathers as though they were clocks — mechanisms we inherit, running ahead of us or behind us our whole lives, keeping their time inside our time. Because I stood in a cellar this morning and chose, in the space of one breath, which side of the ledger I am on.

"Because I inspected the premises," he said, "and found them clear."

He clicked his heels — the gesture felt absurd, and he let it be absurd, a soldier's costume over a man's decision — and left.

* * *

The afternoon verifications went on. House by house, name by face, the red-inked queries resolved into signatures and frightened nods. In the square, the requisitioned church bells hung silent, waiting to be re-tuned to the master clock's authority, and children played around the hoardings of mobilization notices as though paper were just weather.

At the adjutant's office Brandt filed his reports. Forty-one verified, thirty-four verified, two deceased, one moved to the coast before the war. His handwriting stayed neat all the way down the page.

At the bottom of the hillside sheet, where the form asked for remarks, he wrote: *Premises of L. Whitlock, clockmaker, inspected. No irregularity.*

The pen hovered a half-second longer than it needed to. Then he blotted the line, signed, and closed the book.

That evening he stood at his window with his father's watch open in his palm, watching the second hand move the way his father had taught him to watch it — not measuring the minute, but trusting it. Somewhere across the dark valley, in a workshop lit by lamplight, a woman was building something out of gears and lies and loyalty, and tomorrow night it would be finished, and the day after that the trains would run on its word alone.

He found that he was not hoping she failed.

He found that he was counting the hours until midnight, like a man waiting for a bell — and dreading, with the other half of his heart, what it would ring.
