## 9. The Second Voice

The last wheel went in at half past nine.

Lena felt it seat itself with a click she heard through her fingers more than her ears — the great third wheel of the striking train dropping onto its pivot, meshing with the pinion below it, and suddenly the whole movement was alive again under her hands, a sleeping animal whose heart had remembered its rhythm. Oil gleamed along the arbors. The pendulum hung plumb and patient. She had worked eleven days on this clock, and now, in the lamplight of the observatory's dome room, it looked less like a machine than like something resurrected.

She did not let herself feel the triumph. Not yet. Triumph was for people whose work was finished.

Her tools were laid out on a folded cloth beside the movement, and among them, wrapped in chamois, lay the graver she had taken from Horst's box three nights ago, after they had buried him in the morning frost behind the observatory wall. *The clock has two voices,* he had told her, his lungs rattling like a loose mainspring. *The first voice tells the town what time it is. The second voice — the second voice is yours, if you're brave enough to cut it.*

The Vosses would be listening at midnight.

She spread the chime cams on the bench: four iron discs, each notched with the profile that lifted and dropped the hammers — twelve strokes, one stroke, three strokes, the quarter-hours falling in their ancient order. Horst had taught her to read them the way she read ledger pages, by touch and shadow. And Horst had taught her something else, on his last lucid afternoon, when he'd pulled from beneath his cot a battered notebook of cam profiles going back forty years: that the sequence could say things. That a man who knew the wheels could make the bells spell.

The Vosses had lived above a clockmaker's shop in their old country. Marta Voss had told Lena this once, quietly, while kneading bread in the back of the workshop: *My father repaired the cathedral clocks before we ran. I learned the strike patterns as a child. Twelve bells means noon; nine means night prayer.* A family that listened to bells the way other families listened to weather.

So Lena had spent two evenings composing. Not music — the cams couldn't do melody — but arithmetic. The Voss children knew the valley road east; what they needed was timing, and timing was the one language a clock spoke natively.

Her plan, cut into iron:

At midnight, instead of the ordinary twelve strokes followed by the quarters, the master clock would ring thirteen. One extra stroke — impossible, unmistakable, an error no clock ever made, which meant it could only be a message. Then, at each quarter-hour through the small hours, a pattern: at one o'clock, the hour struck twice and then once more after a pause — *two-one*, the signal for the eastern track, the old shepherd's count Marta had described. At two, three strikes then a single — *the pass is open*. Each quarter-hour repeating, hammering the instruction into the dark, until dawn made the message moot either way.

It bought them nothing but certainty and a head start over the timetable that would lock at six in the morning, when Brandt's clerks would seal the synchronization and every soldier in the valley would begin moving on army time. Six hours of darkness. A family of five, a cart, a mountain pass.

Lena fitted the first recut cam to its arbor and turned it slowly, watching the hammer lift lever ride the new profile. *Click. Click-click.* Thirteen strokes' worth of iron where twelve had been.

The dome room was cold enough that her breath showed. Below her, through the floor, she could feel the pendulum's faint pulse in the boards, steady as a second heartbeat. She thought of her father, who had forged names into a ledger so that people could become someone else; and of Horst, who had bent a clock's soul so that a war might arrive late; and understood, with a clarity that frightened her more than any officer's uniform, that she was their daughter and their heir, and that tonight she would sign her own name in iron.

She picked up the graver.

* * *

The second cam fought her. Iron does not forgive, and her hands were not as steady as they had been at nine o'clock — fear had entered them somewhere around the third notch, settling into her wrists like damp. She had to stop twice and walk the length of the dome room, arms crossed against the cold, counting the pendulum's beats until they counted her.

At twenty minutes past eleven she was cutting the last notch of the final cam — the two-one pattern, the eastern track — when she heard boots on the observatory stairs.

She knew the tread. She had known it since the afternoon Lieutenant Brandt had brought his broken pocket watch to her workshop and sat across from her talking about fathers, his gloved hands careful on her bench as though the tools were holy. He climbed slowly, the way a man climbs when he is giving himself every chance to turn around.

Lena set down the graver. She considered hiding the cams. It took her perhaps a full second to decide not to — a second measured, some part of her noted absurdly, by the very escapement ticking behind her.

He came up into the lamplight with snow melting on his shoulders and his cap in his hand, and stopped when he saw the bench. Saw the original cams laid out like extracted teeth. Saw the recut disc still gripped in the vise, its fresh notches bright as wounds.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Above them the dome ticked. Somewhere far below, the town slept inside its last unsynchronized night.

"The verification teams finished at dusk," Brandt said at last. His voice was level, official, and underneath it she heard the other thing, the thing that had been growing between them since the watch repair — the loneliness of a man who did his duty carefully and hated parts of it. "The trains lock to this clock at six."

"I know."

"Then you know what those are." He nodded at the cams.

"They're instructions," Lena said. Her mouth was dry, but her voice came out steadier than she had expected. "Marta Voss's father repaired cathedral clocks. She knows bell-counts the way soldiers know bugle calls. At midnight this clock will strike thirteen. At one, two-and-one — the eastern line. At two, three-and-one — the pass." She swallowed. "By six, when your timetable locks, they'll be over the saddle and out of the valley. Out of your ledger. Off your lists."

Brandt set his cap down on the bench, next to the graver, with the deliberation of a man putting down a weight he had carried too far. "You're telling me," he said slowly, "that you're using the master clock of this valley — the clock my orders require me to synchronize — to smuggle five fugitives out from under a mobilization."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me standing here, holding the tool, with the evidence on the bench, because—"

"Because I'm done lying in workshops and cellars." It came out harder than she intended, and she let it stay hard. "You stood in my shop with your men checking names against faces while a child breathed through the floorboards under your boots. You heard her. Don't tell me you didn't."

Something moved in his face — not guilt exactly; recognition, which is worse and better. "I didn't report it," he said quietly. "That's already a crime. Everything since is just arithmetic."

"My father forged our names into the registrar's ledger when I was six years old," Lena said. "Ours, and the Vosses', and a dozen others'. People who ran here with nothing. He gave them paper lives because paper was all anyone would give them. When your army seized that ledger, it seized the only country those people have ever owned." She picked up the graver. Held it out between them, handle first, the way one offers — or surrenders — a weapon. "There. Arrest me if that's your duty. But the last cam needs ten minutes of cutting, and after that I'll go wherever the empire sends me."

Brandt looked at the graver for a long time.

Then he took off his gloves, finger by finger, and laid them beside his cap.

* * *

They worked without speaking for the first minutes, shoulder to shoulder at the bench, and Lena discovered that a soldier's hands and a clockmaker's hands understand each other. He held the cam true while she cut, turning it at her nod, and his grip never wavered even when her own did. The graver sang through the iron. Bright curls of metal fell away and lay on the cloth like filings of some precious ore.

"You'll lose everything," she said once, not looking up. "Your commission. Maybe more."

"I signed an oath to protect this valley," Brandt said. "Not to hunt the people in it. I've been rereading my orders for three weeks hoping they'd change." He turned the cam a degree. "They don't. So I'm choosing which words in them to hear."

The last notch opened under the blade at seven minutes to midnight. Lena blew the swarf clear, fitted the cam to its arbor, and slid the retaining pin halfway home. Her hands were shaking now — properly shaking, the terror arriving late, the way it always did, like a creditor after the feast.

"It won't hold," she whispered. "If the pin seats wrong, the whole strike train jams and the clock rings nothing at all, and they'll know, they'll come up here before the hour's out—"

"Then we seat it right." Brandt's voice was very calm. "Show me where."

She guided his fingers to the pin, hers over his, both of them feeling for the alignment — the tiny kiss of machined steel finding its machined groove, invisible, decisive. The pendulum swung. The minute hand crawled toward the twelve with the indifference of all clocks toward human affairs.

"Now," she said.

Together they pressed the pin home.

It seated with a click so small that only two people listening with their entire souls could have heard it — and then the movement gathered itself, the warning wheel lifted, the fly spun, and overhead in the belfry the great hammer drew back for the first stroke of midnight in eleven days.

One. Two. Three. The strokes rolled out over the frozen valley, over the sleeping rooftops, over the rail yard where the locomotives breathed steam into the dark. Seven. Eight. Nine. Lena found she was gripping Brandt's arm. Eleven. Twelve—

A silence, long enough to break a heart.

Thirteen.

The thirteenth stroke rang out over the town like a door thrown open, an impossibility broadcast to every ear awake within ten miles, and Lena laughed — one cracked, terrified, disbelieving note — because it had worked, the iron had spoken, the second voice was loose in the world.

Far away, thin on the wind, a dog began to bark. And then another. And then, from the direction of the workshop, faint but unmistakable: the creak of a cart axle, and the quick scuff of feet on frost, going east.

Brandt stood at the dome window watching the darkness, his face lit silver by the moon, saying nothing. When he finally turned, he picked up his cap and put it on, and became, visibly, deliberately, an officer again.

"At six o'clock," he said, "a courier will bring me the synchronization confirmation from this clock. I will sign it. My report will state that the restoration was completed without irregularity." He met her eyes. "And you, Fräulein Whitlock, will tell me the rest of it. All of it. The ledger. Your father. Every name. Tonight, while we wait — because there is one thing worse than a lie written in a book, and that's the truth left unwritten between people who have cut iron together."

Lena looked at her hands, still trembling, still smelling of oil and iron.

"All of it," she said. "But not here. They'll have heard the thirteenth stroke at headquarters too."
