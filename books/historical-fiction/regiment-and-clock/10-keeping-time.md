## 10. Keeping Time

The trains left at dawn, as the timetable demanded, and the valley heard them go.

Lena stood at the observatory window with her palms flat against the cold stone sill and watched the smoke rise from the station in long grey banners, one after another, drifting east over the roofs of a town that had learned in a single week to measure its life by army hours. The master clock ticked behind her, true now to the second, its escapement singing the small silver song she had spent three months teaching it. Somewhere on the high road beyond the ridge, four people were moving through the dark on legs that hadn't stopped shaking since midnight — and the clock's chimes were still ringing their last warning in her ears, though the bells themselves had long gone quiet.

They would be over the pass by now. Marta Voss had carried the little ones; Stefan had carried the tools. Lena had recut the cams so that the twelve strokes of midnight broke apart into a pattern no registrar would ever read but every soul hiding in her cellar had memorized in a single candlelit hour: *three-and-two, pause, seven* — the old shepherd's signal her father had taught her as a girl, meaning *go north, go now, take nothing you cannot run with.*

She had set the last pin herself. And Brandt had held the lantern.

* * *

He came to the workshop an hour after the trains departed, when the light was still the color of dishwater and the town smelled of coal smoke and wet stone. She heard his boots on the stair — she knew them now, the measured tread of a man who had decided never again to hurry anywhere he didn't wish to arrive — and she opened the door before he could knock.

The partition wall stood bare behind her. The straw was gone, the blankets folded and taken, the cellar swept. Only the smell remained, faintly: bread and fear, baked into the wood over weeks.

"Sit down, Lieutenant," she said. "There's something I have to tell you."

Brandt did not sit. He stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands, looking older than his years and younger than his rank, and said quietly, "I know what you're going to say."

"You don't."

"The ledger," he said. "Page forty-one. Six names in your father's hand — the same hand, I realized too late, as the entries for the Whitlock family itself." He turned the cap over once, twice. "I audit documents for a living, Miss Whitlock. I am not good at my job, or I would not be standing here. But I am not blind either."

Her heart should have hammered. Instead it went strangely still, like a pendulum at rest between swings.

"Then you know what I did," she said. "My father forged our names into that book twenty years ago so the registrar would see Germans where there were none, and I let those pages stand because six living people were breathing in my cellar, and I would do it again. I altered your clock's voice to warn them. I stole a night from your timetable. And I'm telling you this because—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway. "Because you trusted me. Because keeping it from you makes me a liar twice over, and I've had enough of my father's silences to last two lifetimes. Whatever you file, file it against *me*. Not the Vosses. They're over the pass by now, and if you send soldiers after them—"

"They won't find them." Brandt set the cap down on the workbench, next to the scattered pin-vices and the oilstone worn hollow by three generations of Whitlock hands. "The report I filed this morning states that verification of the workshop premises found no irregularities. It states that the chime irregularity reported by several residents on the night of the twelfth was a mechanical fault during final adjustment, corrected the following day. It states—" and here his mouth twisted, not quite a smile, "—that the apprentice clockmaker is recommended for the permanent post of observatory keeper, the position having been vacated by the death of Herr Horst, of natural causes."

Lena stared at him. "You'd falsify all of it?"

"I would omit what I cannot unknow," Brandt said. "That is different, and I intend to keep telling myself it is different for the rest of my career, so kindly do not argue with me while I still believe it." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw the cost of it sitting on him like a coat cut too heavy for the season. "I took an oath to a ledger, Miss Whitlock. You took one to people. I find, this morning, that I respect the second oath more. That is not loyalty. That is arithmetic. Six lives against a page of ink."

"And yours?"

"My duty is to see that the timetables hold and the men reach their trains and the war, God help us all, proceeds according to schedule." His voice dropped. "What happens over a mountain pass in fog is weather. Not scheduling."

* * *

Horst was buried on the Thursday, in the small churchyard below the observatory path, with four mourners and one bell that rang true for the first time in a year. Lena had cleaned and oiled the church bell's yoke herself the night before, working by lamplight with Horst's own tools, and when the note came out round and unbroken across the graves, she thought she felt the old man's hand somewhere near hers on the rope — impatient, exacting, faintly amused.

*You slow the clock,* she thought at the headstone. *I'll keep it honest. Between us we might get time approximately right.*

The army garrison thinned through the autumn as the regiments moved east, and the town contracted around its absence like a fist relaxing finger by finger. The factories resumed their own schedules, negotiated rather than decreed. The church bells drifted back into their ancient, imperfect agreement with the sun. But every evening at six, and every noon, the valley paused by habit now and listened up the hill — because the master clock ran true, truer than it had ever run, and a whole town had learned in one hard season that time was not a thing you owned but a thing you kept, together, or lost together.

Lena kept it.

She moved into the keeper's quarters above the instrument room in November, carrying two boxes and her father's forgery kit, which she fed to the stove page by page until the fire had eaten every pen, every nib, every bottle of iron-gall ink he had ever used to make lies look official. The last thing to burn was a blank sheet with the registrar's watermark. She watched it curl and go black and thought: *the truth costs. Pay it once, honestly, and it stops compounding.*

Brandt wrote to her, at first monthly, then less. His letters were short and dry and full of timetables, and between the lines they were something else entirely. *The 7:40 continues to depart on time,* he wrote in December. *I have begun to distrust any train that does.* And once, without preamble: *I passed a family on the road near Hohenfels. A woman, a man, two children. The boy waved at my horse. I did not stop. Some things are better verified by not looking.*

She wrote back about the clock. About the frost heave that shifted the tower's foundation half a millimeter each winter and how she compensated with a wedge of cork under the northeast leg, a trick Horst had never written down, which meant she was the last person alive who knew it, which meant someday she would have to teach someone, or it would die with her like so much else.

In spring, a new apprentice came up the hill — a girl of fourteen, sharp-eyed, blunt-fingered, sent by the parish because she could not sit still in school. Lena put the escapement tools in her hands and said, "First lesson: a clock doesn't want your strength. It wants your attention."

And the girl asked, "Who taught you?"

"A dying man who wanted to stop time," Lena said, "and a father who wanted to erase it. Between them I learned that you can't do either. You can only keep it — honestly, or otherwise. We're going to start with honestly."

Below them the valley turned toward evening, lamps coming on street by street, the trains running on schedule, the bells preparing their six o'clock agreement. Above them the master clock ticked on, its second voice silent in its cams, waiting — as all faithful things wait — to speak only when speaking was worth the cost.
