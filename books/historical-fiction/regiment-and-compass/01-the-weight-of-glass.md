## 1. The Weight of Glass

There is a particular way a man learns to hold a lighthouse lens, and it is not the way the manuals tell you. The manuals say *support from beneath*, say *clean with chamois only*, say *never breathe directly upon the glass*. What they do not say is that you hold it the way you would hold anything that has outlived its maker — carefully, and slightly in awe, and with the private suspicion that it knows more than you do.

The Fresnel at Gullwrack Point weighs better than two tons if you count the clockwork, which I do not, because counting it makes my back ache in sympathy. I have polished her facets every October morning for eleven years now, and she has never once thanked me, and I have never once expected her to. That is the whole of our arrangement. I keep her bright; she keeps ships off the teeth of the headland. We are both too old for sentiment.

"Tam's mended the third net," Wren said from the doorway of the lamp room, not quite coming in. She never quite comes in before I've finished. She stands in that doorway like a bird deciding whether the window is glass or sky, which, given her name, seems fitting enough to be suspicious of.

"Third net or the same net three times?"

"The same one three times," she admitted. "He's getting better at the knots, though."

"He's getting better at hiding the bad knots where the light doesn't reach." I set down the chamois and flexed my fingers, which had gone stiff around the cloth. "Send him up. If he's going to lie to me about rope work, he can do it to my face."

She went, and I heard her boots on the iron stairs — she takes them two at a time when she thinks I'm listening, which I always am — and then Tam came clattering up after her with the net over his shoulder like a fisherman's shroud, fourteen years old and all elbows and apology.

"It was the third net," he said.

"It was the third net," I agreed, "mended by a boy who learned his sheet bend from a gull." I took the net and turned it in my hands until I found the flaw: a knot pulled tight but never seated, the sort of thing that holds until the first real weight hits it, and then doesn't. I didn't fix it in front of him. That's the trick of teaching — you let them find their own errors or they learn only to avoid your eyes, and avoiding your eyes is a habit a boy carries into worse rooms than this one.

"I'll redo it," Tam said.

"You will." I handed it back. "And Tam — the light doesn't reach everywhere, but I do."

He grinned at that, quick as a match strike, and took the stairs down two at a time because he thought I wasn't watching. I was. I always am. It is the one skill grief has left me fully intact: the watching.

* * *

Maren used to say I mothered them badly and constantly, which she meant as a compliment, though it took me years to hear it that way. *You fuss like a woman with one pot on the stove,* she'd say, *and everything in the house goes into the pot.* And I'd tell her that everything in the house fit in one pot because she kept the house small on purpose, and she'd laugh, and that was that — eleven words exchanged, a marriage conducted entirely in shorthand, the long sentences saved up for later, for someday, for the winters when the storms would come and there'd be nothing to do but talk.

There were four of those winters. There were not five.

I don't think of it that way. I want that understood, if anyone ever reads this — and no one will, because I keep no journal; journals are how men get caught saying what they mean. I don't think of it as *four winters*. I think of it as: the lens needs polishing, the nets need mending, the stores need inventory, Wren needs new bootlaces, Tam needs a haircut he'll refuse, the fog bell needs greasing, the gallery rail has rust at the north corner, the storm shutters need their hinges seen to before the equinox gales. I think of it as a list. Lists are honest. A list never asks you why you're still standing in your dead wife's kitchen at four in the morning holding a kettle that has already boiled twice.

The kettle was fine this morning. Everything was fine. I made porridge with the salt butter because Wren says the sweet butter tastes like surrender, and I set three bowls, which is the correct number, and I did not set a fourth, which is also correct, and has been for three years now, and I have stopped noticing the correctness of it, mostly.

"Eat," I said, because they were both reading — Wren had salvaged a month-old paper off a supply boat, hungry for word of the war the way other girls are hungry for sugar, and Tam was drawing a lighthouse in the margin of his arithmetic book that looked suspiciously like it was firing on something.

"They're saying the talks might actually finish it," Wren said, without looking up. "Armistice. Before Christmas, maybe."

"Mm."

"You could pretend to care, Bram."

"I care enormously. Eat your porridge."

"She means you could look up from the salt cellar," Tam said, "and act like a person who lives in the world."

"I live in a lighthouse. The world comes to me by boat, usually complaining about the weather." I sat down with my own bowl. "When the war ends, the supply schedule changes, the coal rationing ends, and Vossgaard's boats stop running dark through the channel because there'll be nothing left to smuggle past anyone. That is my interest in your armistice. Now eat."

Wren looked at me over the top of the paper with her sister's exact gray eyes, which is a cruelty the world arranged without consulting me, and said, "You've got an opinion about everything except the things that matter."

"I've got opinions about everything that matters. I just don't waste them on things that might happen. Things that happen are enough to be going on with."

But she was right, and I knew she was right, and the worst part of raising children is that eventually they start being right at you, deliberately, with aim. The truth was I did care about the armistice. I cared the way a man cares about a storm forecast: not because he wants the storm, but because he wants to know what shape the days will take. Three years I have been keeping this house in the space Maren left behind it, the way water keeps the shape of a withdrawn hand — and I had begun to suspect, in the secret ledger where I keep such suspicions, that peace would change the shape of the days too. Peace makes demands. Grief, at least, asks nothing but attendance.

* * *

Afternoon, and the wind came round to the southeast with a wet smell in it, the smell the sea gets when it's thinking about winter. I sent Tam up with the oil can for the clockwork — supervised, since the incident with the escapement in spring, which we do not discuss, per the treaty of April — and I went down to mend the gallery rail myself, because there are jobs I give away and jobs I keep, and the ones that involve looking south along the coast are jobs I keep.

From the gallery you can see the whole sweep of Gullwrack: the point, the shingle cove below it, the workshop squatting above the tideline with its slate roof going green, and beyond that the gray line of the channel where the water runs deep and cold between us and the mainland. On a clear day you can see the ferry smoke. Today was not clear. Today the sea was the color of unpolished pewter and moving like something with an opinion.

Maren grew up in that workshop. Her father repaired signal lamps for the coastal service — the little harbor beacons, the fog-signal relays, the whole nervous system of the shoreline — and she learned soldering before she learned handwriting, and she taught Wren the soldering and Tam the handwriting, or possibly the reverse; the two of them were always trading skills like pirates trading flags. After she died I kept the shop open because closing it would have been a kind of sentence, and I am not the judge here. The lamp room is mine. The workshop is hers, and I mind it the way you mind a grave: swept, tended, untouched.

Mostly untouched. I go in to oil the hinges and check the roof and take inventory of tools that haven't moved since the funeral, and I stand in the middle of it for a minute or two among the smell of flux and machine oil that is so exactly her that some mornings it takes me at the knees like a wave, and then I leave, and lock it, and put the key on the hook, and that is the whole ceremony.

Today I stood in it a little longer than usual, because the wind had a sound in it I didn't like — a low note under the higher ones, the sound of weather being assembled somewhere out of sight — and because the light in the shop was doing something strange with the dust, making the air visible, and I caught myself thinking, absurdly, *she'd know what that wind means.* Not *I should check the barometer.* *She'd know.*

Eleven years a keeper. Three years a widower. And still my first instinct in every uncertainty is to ask a dead woman.

I locked the shop. I put the key on the hook. I wrote *grease fog bell* on the slate in the kitchen, under *bootlaces for W* and above *haircut (T) (futile)*, because the list is honest, and I am honest, and between the two of us we manage.

* * *

Evening came early, the way it does once October sets its mind to it. I lit the lamp — the great lens taking the flame and breaking it into her nineteen white spokes, sweeping, sweeping, patient as anything holy — and I came down and found Wren at the table with the old paper again, reading the shipping notices, and Tam asleep over his arithmetic with ink on his cheek, and I did not wake him. I banked the fire instead, quietly, the way you do when the house is full of something you don't want to disturb.

"Storm coming," Wren said, without looking up. "The gulls are all sitting facing inland. Da always said—"

She stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, over the table, over the sleeping boy, over the three bowls washed and drying on the rack. *Da always said.* She hadn't spoken of either of the dead in months — none of us had; we had an unwritten law about it, enforced by nobody, obeyed by everybody, and I had told myself for three years that the silence was kindness, was healing, was the family resting.

Standing there with the poker in my hand, I understood for the first time that the silence might simply be a wall, and that walls keep things out, and that walls keep things in.

"Your da said the gulls know before the barometer does," I finished for her, gently. "And he was right, which annoyed the meteorological office considerably."

Something in her shoulders came down half an inch. "Yes," she said. "That."

Outside, the wind found its lower register, and the lens swept overhead, and far out in the dark the sea began to gather itself for whatever it intended to bring us.
