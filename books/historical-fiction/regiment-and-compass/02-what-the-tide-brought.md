## 2. What the Tide Brought

The gale had spent three days trying to take Gullwrack Point apart, and on the fourth morning it gave up, sulked out to sea, and left the coast looking like a pocket turned inside out.

Wren Montrose stood at the top of the cliff path with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and surveyed the wreckage the sea had offered up like an apology it didn't mean. Kelp in black ropes. A crate staved open, spilling cork floats like eggs from a broken nest. Half of Mr. Pellow's fence from two miles down the strand, which meant Mr. Pellow would be along by noon to claim it and complain about it in the same breath. And driftwood — always driftwood, pale as bones, as though the tide were a magpie that collected only one kind of shiny thing.

She had come out to gather what was useful. That was the story she told herself, anyway. The truth was that after three days shut in the cottage with Tam's restless drumming of fingers and Bram's careful, terrible silence, she'd have walked into the sea herself just to hear a different sound.

"You'll want boots," Bram had said when she'd announced she was going down to the rocks.

"I have boots."

"Those are shoes that have seen disappointment." He'd said it without looking up from the lamp he was cleaning, his hands moving over the brass with the tenderness other men reserved for their children or their rifles. "There's a difference."

And so Wren wore the boots, laced to the knee, and went down alone, because Tam was mending net in the workshop and someone had to let him believe he was being useful instead of kept. At fourteen he could smell pity the way sharks smelled blood, and the whole household had learned to move around him gently, as if grief were a floorboard that might give way.

The wind had died but not its memory. It came off the water still in gusts, tugging at her scarf, carrying the smell of salt and drowned things. Wren worked the tideline methodically, the way Maren had taught her —

She stopped.

That happened sometimes. Six years now since the fever took her sister, six years since Wren had been fifteen and furious at God and the sea and the doctor who'd arrived too late, and still the habit of thinking *Maren would know* ambushed her in ordinary moments. Maren would know how to mend this net. Maren would know whether the herring would run late this year. Maren, who at nineteen had been more mother than sister, more captain than either, who'd married a lightkeeper and dragged her little brother and sister to the edge of the world and made a family out of scraps and stubbornness — Maren would have known exactly what to make of this morning's leavings.

Wren bent and pulled a length of good rope from under a tangle of weed. Then another. She filled her sack by degrees, working south along the rocks toward the point where the cliffs folded inward and the sea left its strangest offerings, because the current bent there and dropped things the way a pickpocket dropped what wasn't worth the risk of keeping.

It was there, wedged in the crevice between two granite slabs, that she saw it.

At first she took it for a piece of wreck — a bit of sailcloth, maybe, or oilskin torn from a fisherman's foul-weather gear. But it was too regular for that. Too deliberate. A packet, wrapped in oiled canvas and bound with tarred twine, seated deep in the crack of the rock as if it had been placed rather than deposited. As if the sea had carried it a hundred miles specifically to slot it here, in this seam, like a letter posted to a very particular address.

Wren knelt. The rock was cold through her trousers; she barely noticed. She worked the packet free with fingers gone suddenly clumsy, and it was heavier than it looked, denser, and when she turned it over her heart did something complicated and painful in her chest.

There was writing on the wrapping. Ink, faded but legible, in a hand she knew better than her own.

*M. Montrose.*

Maren's hand. The confident forward slant of it, the way she crossed her T's like she was cutting them free of something. Wren knew that hand from shopping lists and ledger margins and letters sent home from the mainland school, from the recipe card for barley broth that still lived in the kitchen drawer even though nobody could follow it right, because Maren had written *a handful* where any sensible person would write measurements.

But Maren had been dead for six years. Buried up on the headland under a stone Bram had cut himself, in ground that froze too hard in winter for digging and softened only long enough each spring for the gulls to argue over.

And yet.

Wren sat back on her heels in the shingle and held the packet at arm's length, the way you'd hold a snake or a miracle. The tide sucked and gurgled among the rocks below. Somewhere overhead a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

Her first thought, the shameful one, the one she'd never admit to a living soul: *she's alive.* Some part of Wren — the part that was still fifteen, still standing in the doorway of the sickroom, still refusing — lit up like a struck match. People survived fevers they weren't supposed to. People were lost at sea and walked out of the surf years later. There were stories. There were always stories, and every one of them had been some grieving fool's last comfort before sense returned.

Her second thought arrived slower and colder: the date.

Because there was a date, written under the name in the same sure hand, and Wren read it twice, then a third time, tracing each numeral with her eyes as though they might rearrange themselves under scrutiny.

Three years ago. Three years past the fever, past the grave, past the morning Bram had stood in the kitchen with his hat in his hands and said *we'll manage,* in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant and finding it politely worded.

Dated three years after her death. In Maren's handwriting.

Wren's match-flare of hope guttered and went out, and what replaced it was worse than grief. Grief she knew the shape of. This was something with edges.

*Someone is playing a game.* That was the sensible explanation. Someone had learned Maren's hand — forged it, or traced it, or simply written in a similar slant — and sealed a letter and set it adrift, and the sea had delivered it to the exact crevice of rock where the Montrose family's own tide-line ran. Coincidence. Coincidence happened. Fishermen found messages in bottles all the time, most of them jokes, some of them hoaxes, one famous one from a ship lost forty years past.

But whoever heard of a hoax wrapped in tarred twine and weighted to sit precisely in a rock seam? Whoever heard of a hoax that got the handwriting *right*?

Wren became aware, distantly, that she was shaking. Not from cold. From something closer to hunger — that awful, animal want to know, warring with the animal certainty that knowing would cost her.

She should take it to Bram. That was the obvious thing. Bram, who fixed everything, who had held them together with wire and routine and sheer bloody competence for six years. Bram, who never spoke Maren's name aloud, not once, not in all that time, as though saying it might wear the word out. What would he do? He'd go still — that particular stillness of his, the lamp-room stillness — and he'd take the packet with steady hands, and he'd say something practical about ink and water and paper, and inside he'd fold up like a lens hood closing, and they'd lose him for a week behind his work. Or worse — worse was possible — he wouldn't fold up at all. He'd look at the handwriting and feel nothing but suspicion, because six years of silence can calcify into something harder than grief.

No. Not yet. Wren tucked the packet inside her coat, against her ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

She finished gathering rope. That was the discipline Maren had taught her without ever teaching it: finish the task in front of you before you run at the one behind. Her hands knew the work even while her mind churned. Rope, floats, half a crate lid sound enough to burn. By the time she climbed the cliff path her sack was full and her thoughts were anything but.

* * *

She didn't open it in the cottage. She couldn't have said why, exactly, except that some letters wanted privacy the way some wounds wanted air, and she suspected this was both.

Instead she waited for the afternoon, when Bram took himself up to the lamp room for the weekly stripping of the clockwork — four hours, always, rain or shine, his cathedral — and Tam sprawled in the workshop over the net-mending he pretended to resent. Wren pulled her own coat on, told Tam she was checking the crab pots, received his grunt of magnificent indifference, and walked out past the goat shed to the old boat hull overturned against the fence, where she could crouch out of the wind with the Atlantic at her back and no windows facing her.

Her knife slid under the tarred twine. It resisted, then gave.

Inside the oilskin: a second wrapping, waxed linen, and inside that, dry as the day it was closed, a letter. One sheet, folded in thirds. Paper of good stock — mainland stock, stationer's stock, nothing like the cheap blue-lined stuff Maren had always bought because it was a penny less. Which meant either the forger was wealthy or —

Or.

Wren unfolded it with hands that refused to be steady, and read.

*If you are reading this, then the sea has done what I asked it to, and my faith in the tide has finally been justified to my family, who always said I trusted it too much.*

Wren made a sound. Not quite a laugh, not quite anything else. Because that was Maren — that was her exactly, wry even at the edge of a grave, addressing the reader across impossible distance as if across a supper table.

*By now I will be dead. I am sorry for the bluntness of it, but there is no gentle way to post a letter three years into the future, and I have never been gentle where efficiency was concerned. If the arrangement I have made fails, then this letter will wash ashore near our rocks, and one of you — I pray it is Wren, who was always the one who checked the tideline properly — will find it.*

*I have put my trust in a man whose name I will not write here, because names are dangerous things and paper keeps them too well.*

The wind picked up. Wren hunched over the page, shielding it with her body like a candle flame.

*I write this because I have discovered something on this coast that no one wants a part of, least of all me, and least of all my family. Ships come into our waters that have no business there. Signals pass that are not ours to send. And men in clean boots ask questions in ports that should have no answers to give. I have tried to stand between this thing and the people I love, and I have learned that standing between is a position one cannot hold forever.*

*So I have done what I can. I have made arrangements. I have left instructions. And I am trusting the sea, and the timing, and whichever of you finds this, to finish what I could not.*

*Do not go to the authorities. Do not go to Bram. I love him beyond the reach of words, which is why I must warn you: whatever happens, whatever evidence appears, however certain it looks — he does not know. He must not carry this. Keep it from him until the moment comes when keeping it would destroy more than telling it would save. You will know that moment. You will hate that I have left the knowing to you. I am sorry. I have always been sorry, ahead of schedule.*

*Burn this after reading. Trust the workshop. And watch for the man with clean boots.*

*Your sister,
M. Montrose*

Wren read it again. And a third time. The gulls wheeled. The tide kept its appointments.

*He does not know.* Of course he didn't. Bram, who lied so rarely and so badly that his one attempt at a fib about the state of the bread lasted eleven seconds. Whatever shadow Maren had tangled with, she'd kept it off him deliberately, the way you kept weather off a lamp — and she'd left the keeping of the secret to Wren. To *Wren*, who at fifteen hadn't been able to keep the secret of the broken crockery for four hours.

And *watch for the man with clean boots.* Written three years dead, and already Wren's skin prickled with the memory of every stranger who'd come up the cliff path asking after shipments and signals. None recently. None yet. But the sentence sat in her chest like a swallowed hook.

She looked at the letter a long time. Burn it, Maren said. And Wren understood why — paper kept names too well, paper could hang a family, paper in the wrong hands was a noose. Every sensible instinct said fire.

But the letter also said *I have left instructions*, and *trust the workshop*, and those were threads, and Wren had spent six years learning that you did not cut threads your sister tied. Not when she'd tied them dying, or near it, for you.

So instead of burning it, Wren folded the letter small, and did not put it in the stove when she came inside, and did not tell Bram at supper when he glanced at her quietness and asked if she'd caught a chill, and did not tell Tam when he kicked her under the table out of pure brotherly spite and she kicked him back harder, out of pure sisterly tradition.

She slept with it under her mattress, dry as bone, close as guilt.

And on the third morning after, when she came out to fetch peat and saw a figure coming up the cliff path from the landing stage — tall, unhurried, uniformed, his boots gleaming like polished glass in the weak sun — Wren stood very still in the doorway and felt her sister's warning settle over her like a hand on her shoulder.

The man with clean boots had come.
