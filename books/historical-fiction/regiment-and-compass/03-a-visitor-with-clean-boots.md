## 3. A Visitor with Clean Boots

The army arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like a personal insult. Storms come when they please, and wrecks come when the sea decides, but bureaucracy — bureaucracy keeps office hours. Captain Ellery Voss stepped out of his boat at half past ten in the morning, dry as an ledger page, wearing boots that had never once been wet.

I want that understood from the start. I have lived at Gullwrack Point for eleven years, and in that time I have seen fishermen land here with their knuckles split and their boots full of their own blood, seen revenue men climb the rocks swearing and soaked to their teeth, seen a bishop carried ashore unconscious after underestimating the swell by a considerable margin. Every soul who comes to Gullwrack comes wet or comes broken. This man came neither way. He picked his footing down my ladder as though he'd rehearsed it, and he stood on my landing stage looking around with the mild interest of a man pricing a house.

"Keeper Montrose." He offered a hand. His grip was dry too. Everything about him was dry. "Captain Voss, Coastal Signals Inquiry. I'm inspecting the light stations between here and Harrow Neck."

"Inspecting what about them?"

"Their condition. Their efficiency." He smiled. It was a good smile, well made, and it did no work whatsoever. "Their reliability."

I said, "The light's reliable," and turned to lead him up, because there is no sense standing on a landing stage arguing with a man whose boots are still clean. The rocks would do some of my talking for me.

* * *

Let me be honest about something, since honesty is about to become scarce in this account: I am not a liar. I have never been one. A keeper who lies is worse than a keeper who drinks; the lamp doesn't care how you feel, but the logbook does, and the logbook is the only conversation you'll ever have with the people who come after you. In eleven years I have written eleven years of true things. Fog, wind, ships passed, ships failed to pass. The truth is a discipline like any other, and I kept it the way I keep the glass — polished daily whether it needs it or not.

So when Voss sat down at my kitchen table, accepted tea he didn't drink, and asked me his first question, some animal part of me already knew where the morning was going. It knew before I did. Animals always know first.

"When did you last repair signal equipment, Mr. Montrose?" he said.

"Equipment of mine? Constantly. The clockwork alone —"

"Not your equipment." He set a photograph on the table between us, face up, gentle as a dealt card. "Army equipment. Helio shutters. Signal lamps. Navy pattern, mostly, requisitioned through the depot at Corvenna and marked for coastal batteries." The photograph showed a crate, stencilled, lid pried open. I could see the corner of a lens inside, brass fittings, the particular grey of military paint. "Crates like this have been arriving along this coast for eighteen months. Some go where they're meant to. Some don't."

"And you think they come here."

"I think," said Voss, "that this is the only workshop within forty miles capable of the work. I think the nearest battery is twelve miles south and has its own fitters. And I think, Keeper Montrose, that you are exactly the kind of man who could re-silver a mirror and true a shutter mechanism without leaving so much as a fingerprint of doubt on it."

It should have been flattering. It wasn't. Being seen clearly by the wrong man feels very much like being caught.

"My workshop repairs fishing gear," I said. "Nets. Winches. Once a church bell from St. Anselm's, which I regretted immediately."

"Mm." He looked at the photograph a moment longer, then turned it face-down, which I understood to be a courtesy and also a threat. "There are deserters on this coast, Mr. Montrose. Boys who walked away from the batteries, from the transports. Some of them are being moved north — fed, hidden, ferried. Somebody's organizing it. Somebody with skill, access, and a reason to be trusted."

"You've been talking to the wrong gulls."

"Probably," he agreed pleasantly. "I talk to a great many gulls. Most of them lie." He took out a notebook, small, black, and wrote nothing in it, which was somehow worse than writing. "Tell me about your shipments. What leaves Gullwrack, and when?"

"Fish," I said. "In season. Wool, occasionally. Wren's jam, which nobody buys twice."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. "And what arrives?"

"Bags of flour. Paraffin. Salt. The post." My voice was steady. I remember noticing that, the way you notice your own heartbeat in a quiet room — with surprise, and then with a kind of horror. Because underneath the steady voice, the animal part of me was doing arithmetic. *The workshop. The second bench, the one Tam built last spring. The crates we told ourselves were salvage. The night in October when I found brass filings under the door and decided — decided, deliberately, with my eyes open — not to ask.*

You can lie with your mouth while your memory betrays you. That's the trap of it. Voss didn't need me to confess anything. He only needed me to keep talking until the shape of what I wasn't saying became visible, the way a ship becomes visible against fog light before the fog lifts.

"Your late wife," he said.

And that — that I hadn't prepared for, because I had spent three years making sure no one asked.

"What about her."

"She was a Merren. From the fishing family at Low Tide Row. Sister to the two young people who live with you now." He consulted nothing. He knew all of it already; the notebook was theatre. "She drowned three years ago. There were questions at the time about the boat she was aboard. Cargo manifests that didn't reconcile."

"There were questions about everything in those years," I said. "Half the coast was running something past someone. That's not a plot, Captain, that's a war economy."

"No," Voss said softly, "a war economy is when everyone cheats a little. This is different. This is organized. And organized things have organizers, and organizers have reasons, and reasons, Mr. Montrose, are what I collect." He stood, buttoned his coat, and looked around my kitchen — at the mended nets hanging by the stove, at Tam's half-carved boat on the sill, at Wren's charts pinned over the sink with her own corrections inked in red. He looked at all of it the way a customs man looks at a hold. "Thank you for the tea."

"You didn't drink it."

"No." At the door he paused. Clean boots on my clean floor, and not one grain of Gullwrack sand clinging to them, which even now strikes me as the most unnatural thing about the man. "For what it's worth, Keeper, I believe you. I believe you're exactly what you appear to be — a careful man keeping a careful light." He put his cap on. "That's why you're worth asking twice."

Then he was gone, down the ladder, onto his boat, out past the point, and I stood in my doorway listening to his engine fade and understanding that I had just done something I had never once done in fifty-one years.

I had lied. Not loudly, not completely — but I had held back the crates, and the filings, and the October night, and every silence is a stone in the wall of a lie. The logbook in my head had a smudge on it now. It would never come off.

* * *

Wren found me an hour later, still at the table, her letter — my wife's letter, the impossible oilskin packet the tide had given us — burning a hole through the floorboard beneath my feet where I'd hidden it. She read my face the way she reads weather.

"He'll be back," she said.

"Yes."

"And we're not ready."

"No."

She sat down across from me and put both hands flat on the table, and for a moment she looked so much like Maren that I had to look at the teapot instead. "Bram," she said, "what did you tell him?"

"The truth," I said. Then, because the discipline had already cracked and there was no sense pretending otherwise: "Most of it. Which is the same as none of it."

Wren nodded slowly, and I saw something settle behind her eyes — a decision being made in a room I wasn't invited into. "Then we'd better get ready," she said, "before he asks twice."

She went out toward the workshop, and I sat a while longer with the cold tea, listening to the sea work at the rocks below, thinking that I had spent my whole life learning to guard a light against the dark, and had only just discovered the dark could wear clean boots and carry a notebook, and that everything I actually loved was standing on the shore in plain view of it.
