## 4. The Workshop Ledger

I want to say I found the ledger by accident, because that's the version I'd tell at supper, if I ever told it at supper. But the truth is I went looking. Fourteen-year-olds are not built for not-knowing. We're like gulls that way — we see something glinting under the surface and we dive on it whether it's a fish or a boot.

It started with the boots.

Not real boots. Boot-prints. Or rather the absence of them. Tuesday morning after the gale, Bram had me sweeping the workshop floor — his idea of teaching me humility, since the week before he'd caught me telling Wren that keeping a lighthouse was mostly sitting down. "Mostly sitting down," he'd said, handing me the broom, "and the rest is sweeping up after people who think it's mostly sitting down."

So I swept. And while I swept I noticed something that a person who wasn't raised by Bram Montrose would never have noticed: the floorboards near the back bench were clean in a way they shouldn't have been. The rest of the floor wore its usual coat of sawdust and salt and copper filings, but there was a rectangle of bare wood, about four feet by three, just behind the lathe. Somebody had been kneeling there. Working there. And somebody had swept up after themselves before leaving — badly, in one corner, where a curl of brass shavings had escaped the broom.

Nobody in our house sweeps. That's not an insult; it's arithmetic. Bram sweeps when he's worried, which is often enough, but he sweeps the whole floor, every board, like a man doing penance. Wren doesn't sweep, she reorganizes, which is worse. And me — well. The broom and I have an understanding: it stays leaning in the corner looking decorative.

Also, the shavings weren't ours. Ours are soft yellow, from the brass rod Bram keeps for mending lamp fittings. These were hard and greenish, naval bronze, the kind used in signal shutters and heliograph mounts. I knew because I'd spent a whole rainy afternoon last month asking him why we didn't use it for anything, and he'd said, "Because it's army metal, Tam, and we're not the army," in a voice that closed the subject like a door.

* * *

The workshop sits behind the keeper's cottage, a long low shed my sister Maren — Bram's wife, though I still can't say her name out loud without my chest doing something complicated — had helped build the summer I was ten. She'd wanted a place where the family could mend things together instead of apart, which tells you everything about Maren: she believed broken things could be fixed as long as you held all the pieces at once.

We mend signal equipment there. Officially. The Coast Signal Service sends crates down twice a season — shutter assemblies, wick mechanisms, the glass prisms nobody else within fifty miles trusts themselves to handle — and Bram repairs them, and stamps the work ticket, and a supply launch collects them. It's honest work. It's our work. It's the only work I've ever watched Bram do with his sleeves rolled and something almost like peace on his face.

Which is why the green shavings sat in my stomach like swallowed sea-glass all through Tuesday's supper. I chewed my fish. I answered Wren's questions about school with monosyllables she was too distracted to notice were monosyllables. And I watched Bram, really watched him, for the first time in maybe two years.

Here is what you should understand about Bram Montrose: he is not a warm man. He is a precise man. He irons his shirts on a board balanced across two chairs even though no one on Gullwrack Point will ever see them. He winds the cottage clocks every Sunday at six. He signs his name on work tickets with a stamp — a little brass stamp he keeps in the drawer of the workshop desk — because his handwriting, he says, looks like a spider drowned in ink, and signal paperwork deserves better.

He came into the world already forty years old, Wren says. She means it fondly. Mostly.

And Tuesday night, over supper, the precise man did something imprecise. He reached for the salt and knocked the pepper instead, and instead of righting it immediately — he always rights things immediately — he looked at the spilled pepper for a long moment like he couldn't remember what it was or how it got there.

That night I lay awake listening to the wind worry the eaves and rehearsed every innocent explanation. Army surplus shavings, blown in through a cracked pane. A repair job for a passing trawler he hadn't mentioned. A man is allowed to sweep a corner of his own floor.

A man is allowed. But a man who has nothing to hide does not go quiet when the investigator's launch comes round, either. And Captain Voss — clean boots, clean gloves, questions shaped like ordinary conversation but weighted like sinkers — had been here twice in eight days.

So Wednesday, when Bram rowed out to check the moorings of the light itself, I let myself into the workshop with the key from the hook, and I did the thing I'd promised myself I wouldn't do.

I opened the desk drawer.

* * *

The stamp was there. That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't know it yet — just a small cold weight of brass in my palm, the letters MONTROSE & FAMILY, SIGNAL WORKS worn smooth from years of pressing. I put it back and pulled out the ledger beneath it.

Our ledger. Green cloth cover, spine cracked, Maren's handwriting fading on the flyleaf where she'd written *Property of everybody, so mind your manners*. Every job we'd done since before the war logged inside: dates, crate numbers, parts, hours. Bram's entries were neat as typeset. Mine, from the last year, sprawled and misspelled. Wren's were beautiful and slightly smug.

I turned to November. And there it was, in Bram's neat-as-typeset hand:

*Nov 3 — Signal shutter assembly, CS-114. Received damaged. Repaired, tested, returned.*

Except we had received no crate CS-114. I know because I log the crates now, part of my training, and I would have remembered the number, and also because the receiving shelf held exactly four crates, numbered 118 through 121, waiting for collection.

I turned pages backward. October: *Heliograph mirror mount, repaired, returned.* September: *Shutter mechanism, CS-97.* August: *Lamp regulator assembly.* Six jobs since midsummer, each stamped with the brass stamp, each signed off, each — as far as our actual shelves and our actual memory were concerned — never having existed.

Six ghosts, wearing our name.

My hands had gone strange and far away. I stood in the cold workshop breathing through my mouth the way you do when the smell of something is too much, except it wasn't a smell, it was a thought, and the thought wouldn't stop growing.

Then I knelt where the floorboards were clean, and I looked under the bench, and I found the rest.

There was a crate there. Not one of ours — no Coast Signal markings, just stenciled initials, R.M., and a shipping label from a chandler in Harwick, forty miles south. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: a signal shutter assembly, half disassembled, its mechanism stripped and cleaned with a precision that made my chest ache, because I recognized the hands that had done it. There was only one person alive who adjusted a pivot screw the way this one had been adjusted — backing it off an eighth-turn past snug so the shutter fell silent instead of clacking. Bram taught me that trick. Said the army built their gear loud because loud men designed it, and quiet work was worth more than anyone paid for.

Beside the crate, a coil of wire. Not our wire. And tucked under the bench lip, a folded paper, which I should not have read, and read anyway.

A list. Names, some crossed out. Dates. And beside four of the names, in pencil, in handwriting I did not know: *passed north — safe.* Passed north. North of Gullwrack Point there is nothing but coast and coves and, if you believed the gossip Wren pretended not to repeat, men who'd walked away from the war being moved from hiding place to hiding place like contraband.

Deserters. The word arrived in my head with all its teeth out. Men who ran. Men supplied by someone with access to signal equipment — because a deserter network that can't signal its boats is a network of drowned men — and repaired by someone with our tools, our stamp, our ledger, our name.

I sat down on the floor among the shavings and tried to make the sums come out.

Bram had done the repairs. That much was certain; the work was his fingerprint more surely than any stamp. Bram had hidden the crate. Bram had written the ghost entries to make the shipments look official, look *ours*, look legitimate. Which meant Bram Montrose — the man who ironed shirts nobody saw, who wound clocks, who had raised me since I was nine with a fairness so exact it sometimes felt like a kind of distance — was a smuggler. Was maybe a traitor, depending on which side of the word you stood, and I genuinely could not find the side I stood on.

Unless.

Unless someone else had used the workshop. Someone with a key. There were three keys: Bram's, mine, and the spare on the hook, and the hook was empty Wednesday morning, and I told myself I'd hung it back properly, and then I checked, and I had.

Someone else. Who? The chandler's label said Harwick. Voss had asked about Harwick. Voss had asked about shipments and about men and about whether we ever took outside commissions, smiling his clean smile, and Bram had lied — I'd heard it, I just hadn't known until now that it was a lie — saying *we keep to ourselves, Captain.*

Or. Another unless, one I didn't want, one that sat in my ribs like a fishhook: unless Bram was being framed. Unless someone with access — someone trusted, someone family-shaped — was using our name as a wrapper, and Bram knew, and that was why he'd gone quiet and spilled the pepper and looked at nothing.

But that made no sense either, because the entries were in Bram's hand, and I would know his hand anywhere. He wrote my allowance in that hand. He wrote *well done* in that hand, twice a year, sparingly, like rations.

I put everything back. Crate under the bench, paper folded, ledger squared, stamp in the drawer, key on the hook. I swept the escaped shavings into the corner where they'd been, because some part of my brain insisted on preserving the scene like evidence, and another part understood I was now concealing a crime, and both parts were too tired to argue.

* * *

I made it as far as the gate before Wren found me. She has a radar for my face; fourteen years of practice, minus the ones before she took me in, but she says she made up the difference with interest.

"You look," she said, "like the tide brought you something you wish it hadn't."

"It didn't bring me anything."

"Mm." She leaned on the gate beside me, watching the water do its gray arithmetic. "Voss's launch came round again this morning. While you were sulking in your room. He and Bram talked in the kitchen for twenty minutes, and afterward Bram washed a cup that was already clean."

The cold went all the way through me then. Because that was it, wasn't it. The pepper. The clean cup. A man scrubbing at something that wouldn't come off.

"Wren," I said, and my voice came out younger than I wanted it to, "do you think Bram would lie to us?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she'd tell me I was being ridiculous. Instead she looked at the horizon, and her face did something old and careful, and she said, "I think everyone lies, Tam. The question is always what for."

Behind us, in the workshop, the spare key hung on its hook, and the crate sat under the bench, and somewhere between the harbor and the hills, men were moving north on signals our name had kept alive — and I had until supper to decide whether the man I loved most in the world was a criminal, a victim, or a stranger I'd been setting a place for all along.
