## 5. Salt and Silence

The letter had a way of waiting for me. I'd leave it folded in the oilskin under the loose board beneath my bed, and every morning it seemed to have grown heavier overnight, the way a stone does when you're the one expected to carry it.

I waited three days before I opened it again. Not from courage. From arithmetic. Three days was how long it took Tam to wear himself out mending crab pots and Bram to run through his rounds twice as often as the lamp required, which is what he did whenever his mind was chewing on something he refused to name. Three days meant nobody would look at me too closely while I sat in my room doing nothing that looked like anything.

The second reading was worse than the first. The first time, I'd read it the way you'd read any letter — top to bottom, hungry, believing. This time I read it like a map, because that's what Maren had left me. Not a letter. A map with words on it.

*They watch this house,* she'd written, in her small, slanted hand — the same hand that had labeled all her jam jars and corrected my schoolwork in the margins. *So I will not write plainly. If you are reading this, then you already suspect what I only learned in the dark: that the boats that come to Gullwrack do not come for fish.*

I sat on my bed with my back against the cold wall and let that sentence sit in me.

I had been eleven when Maren married Bram, twelve when she died. Old enough to remember her laughing in the workshop, sleeves rolled, soldering iron in hand, teaching Tam to hold wire steady. Young enough that most of what I remembered of her last year was her being tired. I'd filed that tiredness under grief — she'd lost our parents in the influenza, after all; she'd taken on two children who weren't hers by blood; of course she was tired.

It had never once occurred to me that she might have been afraid.

*You will want to believe I was foolish,* the letter went on. *Don't. I knew what I was doing when I let them think they'd recruited me. A woman with a dead husband's name and a brother-in-law's signature stamp is invisible in a way no man in a uniform ever manages to be. They needed someone who could repair their instruments without questions. I made myself useful so I could make myself dangerous.*

My hands were shaking. I pressed them flat against the oilskin, against her handwriting, as if I could pin the words still long enough to understand them.

She had gone into it deliberately. Our Maren — who cried at the death of gulls, who once walked four miles in the rain to return a shilling a fisherman had overpaid her — had walked into a smuggling ring supplying deserters, wearing our family like a disguise, carrying our name into rooms where names got people drowned.

And she had done it for us. That was the part I couldn't get around. Every line of it pointed home: *I found out what they wanted before they understood I'd never give it. The harbor beacon at Gullwrack has been sabotaged — a fault built into the timing mechanism, subtle enough that only its maker would find it. When the next great storm comes, boats running dark will break on the point, and the blame will fall on the keeper who failed to keep it.*

*On Bram,* she wrote. *They mean to hang your family with the wreck of other people's sons.*

*

I didn't sleep that night. I lay listening to the house settle and the sea work at the rocks below, and somewhere past midnight I heard Bram climb the tower stairs on his rounds, though the lamp needed nothing at that hour. His footsteps were slow. Counting things, maybe. Keepers count. It's how they pray.

In the morning I watched him across the breakfast table and tried to see what Maren had seen: a man being fitted for a crime he hadn't committed, cheerful about it, oblivious. He passed Tam the butter. He asked whether the storm glass was falling. He looked tired in the ordinary way, the grief way, and I thought: he doesn't know. Three years he's lived inside the shape of her death without knowing she'd spent that last year fighting for him.

*I have hidden the rest where only family would think to look,* the letter said near its end. *Where we kept everything worth keeping. Where I taught you both that a thing is only lost if no one loves it enough to go looking. The workshop remembers. So do the walls.*

The workshop.

Our workshop — the one room in the house that still smelled like her, turpentine and hot flux and the particular chalky smell of lens polish. The room where Bram now spent his evenings repairing signal equipment for the army, signing off each piece with the stamp that bore his own name. The stamp that, according to Maren, had already signed its way into something rotten.

I told myself I'd go that afternoon. I told myself that right up until Captain Voss's boat appeared in the cove, clean and grey as a knife blade, and I watched Bram walk down to meet it with his shoulders set like a man walking into weather.

*

He came back an hour later looking like a man who'd swallowed a stone and decided to pretend it was dinner. He said Voss had inspected the signal station and found everything in order. He said it twice, which is once more than Bram ever says anything.

Tam believed him. Tam believes everything, bless him; it's his best quality and the one that will someday drown him. I smiled and agreed and washed the dishes, and all the while the letter burned against my ribs where I'd taken to keeping it, folded inside my bodice, like a coal I couldn't put down and couldn't put out.

That night, when the house had gone quiet and the lamp turned itself above us, patient as a heartbeat, I took a candle and went out across the yard to the workshop.

The door stuck the way it always sticks, and I shouldered it the way Maren taught me — low, with your hip, not your pride. Inside, the dark smelled exactly like her. That nearly finished me. I stood in the doorway with my candle shaking until the wax dripped onto my knuckles and the sting gave me something else to feel.

*The workshop remembers. So do the walls.*

Walls. Which wall? She'd have chosen the one she used. I thought about her at the bench, always at the north bench, facing the window, facing the sea — because of course she did, Maren faced whatever she was working on, even when what she was working on was staying alive.

I went to the north wall and held the candle close and looked for what wasn't there. Not a seam, not a hatch, nothing so honest. What I found was a shelf bracket, cast iron, holding a row of Bram's spare burners. Ordinary. Except the bracket was mounted a full hand's width higher than its twin on the south wall, and there was no reason in the world for a difference that small unless someone had moved it, or mounted it high to reach something behind.

I lifted the burners down. I worked my fingers behind the bracket, expecting splinters and disappointment.

My knuckles knocked against paper.

There was a cavity behind the plaster, shallow and dry, and inside it a bundle wrapped in oilcloth — the same oilcloth as my letter, tied with the same tarred twine. I pulled it out and it was heavier than the first, thicker, and through the cloth I could feel the edges of many pages.

I didn't open it.

I want that written down somewhere, since no one else knows to write it: I stood in my dead sister's workshop with her whole confession in my hands and I did not open it, because through the oilcloth I could see the topmost page was stamped with an army crest, and beside the bundle lay a folded plan of the harbor beacon's timing mechanism, annotated in her hand, and I understood suddenly that this was not a letter anymore. It was evidence. Evidence of what she'd done, and what had been done to us, and possibly — my mind supplied helpfully, in Captain Voss's clipped voice — evidence of exactly the kind of conspiracy he'd come asking about.

If I opened it, I would know everything. And if I knew everything, I would either have to tell Bram and Tam — and watch the truth crack them open the way it had cracked me — or carry it alone, which was what Maren had done, and look what that had cost her.

I rewrapped the bundle. I put it back behind the bracket. I hung the burners back in their row, straightened them until they looked untouched, and stood there a long moment with my palm flat against the plaster, over the place where her words slept in the dark.

"I'm not her," I whispered to the wall. "I'm not brave in the right direction."

The workshop said nothing. Workshops are good at keeping secrets; it's houses that gossip.

*

For six days I carried it. Six days of passing Bram the bread and watching Tam sketch beacon designs in the margins of his accounts book and saying nothing, nothing, nothing, while the secret ate holes in me the way salt eats through rope — slowly, invisibly, until one day the load bears weight and the next day it doesn't.

Six days of listening, too. Because the letter hadn't just given me guilt; it had given me ears. I noticed that the supply boat from the mainland came on Thursdays now instead of Tuesdays, and stayed longer than supplies require. I noticed that the new signal equipment Bram repaired came with serial numbers filed off the mounting brackets — I saw them myself, helped carry a crate, felt sick the whole way to the workshop. I noticed that when I mentioned Captain Voss at supper, Tam's fork stopped halfway to his mouth for exactly one second.

One second. But I was watching now. Maren had taught me that, posthumously, from behind a wall.

On the sixth night, the wireless in the signal station — the army's wireless, that Voss's men had installed — carried a fragment through the rain to anyone standing in the yard. Peace talks stalled at Versailles, the operator's voice crackling, armistice terms disputed, no settlement before spring. No peace coming. Which meant, I thought, standing in the wet dark with my hood up, that the storm season wasn't ending. Which meant the great storm Maren had promised was still out there, gathering.

Which meant whatever she'd planned, whatever instructions waited in that oilcloth bundle, was on a clock.

I went inside. Bram was at the table with the ledger, doing sums by lamplight, and for a moment I almost told him — the words were actually in my mouth, *Bram, there's something you need to know about Maren* — and then I saw his face in the lamplight, that careful, guarded tiredness, and I thought: not like this. Not as a blow. Whatever I gave him, I'd have to give whole, with proof, with a plan, or it wouldn't save him, it would only break him earlier than the army managed it.

"Everything all right, Wren?" he asked, not looking up.

"Fine," I said, and lied to him for the first time, easily, terribly. "Just checking the weather."

The storm glass was falling. I could see it from the table. I don't know if he could.

That night I lay awake and made my decision the way you make any decision at seventeen: alone, frightened, and certain anyway. Tomorrow, or the day after, or the day after that — soon — I would take Tam aside, because Tam was the one who loved machines and would understand the sabotage, and together we would open the wall. Not Bram. Not yet. Bram first had to be ready to hear that the woman he'd mourned had been braver and more deceived than he'd ever imagined, and I couldn't hand him that truth until I'd found what Maren had left us to do about it.

Below my window, the sea kept up its work on the rocks. Somewhere out in that black water, the storm was deciding when to come.

And in the workshop, behind a shelf bracket, my sister was waiting to tell me how we'd survive her.
