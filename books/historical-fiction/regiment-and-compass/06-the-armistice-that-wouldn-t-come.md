## 6. The Armistice That Wouldn't Come

The news came by pigeon, which struck me as absurd even at the time. Three years of war carried across the world on the backs of men and ships and telegraph wire, and the final word about whether it was ending arrived in a tin can strapped to a bird's leg.

The message was for Captain Voss, not for me. But Voss had taken to reading his correspondence standing at my kitchen table, uninvited, the way a man reads letters when he wants you to watch him read them. He held the slip of paper a long moment, and something in his jaw shifted — the smallest recalibration of a man who had been promised an ending and been told *wait*.

"Well," he said, setting it down flat, as if I might snatch it. "Paris has stalled."

"Has it."

"The Americans want this, the French want that, and everyone wants to go home for Christmas." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. A bitter man you could understand. This one sounded like a clerk reporting a delay in the post. "Which means my commission is extended, Mr. Montrose. Indefinitely. And so is my interest in your cove."

I kept polishing the lens cloth over the table, though there was nothing on the table to polish. It's a trick of the trade. Keep your hands busy and your face stupid, and men talk past you instead of to you. I'd learned it from Maren, actually, though I'd have chewed off my own arm before saying so out loud. We didn't say her name out loud. That was the arrangement none of us had ever agreed to.

"Stalled talks," Voss went on, "mean nervous smugglers. Nervous smugglers move their goods before the ports fill up with peacetime inspectors. If I were running contraband through Gullwrack Cove, I'd be moving it this week." He buttoned his coat. His boots were still immaculate. Three days of coastal weather and the man's boots looked like they'd been licked clean by something. "There'll be a raid before Friday, I should think. Soldiers from the garrison at Penlow. Perhaps a dozen."

"A raid on what?"

"On whoever's in the cove when they arrive." He smiled, thin and courteous as frost on a window. "You understand I don't choose the timing. The storm does. There's a big one building off the shelf, according to your own barometer — I checked it, I hope you don't mind. Bad weather covers everything: landings, departures, mistakes. And warrants." He tapped his breast pocket, where I supposed the paper slept. "Sleep well, keeper."

He let himself out. In my house. Let himself out, like a man leaving his own kitchen after checking whether his wife had put the kettle on.

I stood at the window and watched him walk down the cliff path with that unhurried stride, and I thought about what a man does when the law arrives with its paperwork and the sea arrives with its storm, and both of them are coming for the same small family at the same hour.

Then I went to find Tam, because there was a thing between us shaped like a ledger, and it wasn't going to fit under the floorboards forever.

* * *

He was in the workshop, of course. The boy lived in that room the way barnacles live on pilings — attached, immovable, half-blind to anything beyond it. Lamplight through fogged glass, solder smoke, his sleeves rolled up showing forearms that had gone from bird-bones to rope in eighteen months of hauling my gear around. When did that happen? Children do it while you're looking elsewhere. They grow in the gaps of your attention.

On the bench before him lay the ledger. My ledger. The one I'd found three nights ago with entries in my own hand that I had never written.

"Tam."

He didn't startle. That was new too; the old Tam would've jumped out of his skin. "Bram." He turned the page toward me without being asked, which meant he'd been waiting for this conversation, dreading it, rehearsing it. "Page forty-one."

"I know what page forty-one says."

"Then say it out loud," he said, quiet, "because I've read it eleven times and it still doesn't make sense. *Received, four signal lamps, condition poor. Repaired, returned.* Dated the ninth of October. Your stamp. Your handwriting. Except you were at the light every night of October, because you're always at the light every night of everything, so either you've learned to be in two places—" His voice cracked, and I hated how young it made him sound. "Or someone's using your name. And I need to know which, because if it's you, then everything—"

"It isn't me."

"Then who?" And there it was, the whole question, naked and furious. He stood up and the stool went over behind him and neither of us looked at it. "Who walks into our workshop, Bram? Who knows where you keep the stamp? Who can get in past your locks? Because I've been through every possibility all week and there's exactly one answer left and it makes no sense, so you tell me. You tell me right now."

And God forgive me, my first instinct — my true, deep, dishonorable instinct — was to lie again. To build another wall of competence and keep the boy safe behind it. That's all I'd done since the telegram came, three years back. *Your sister's ship is overdue.* Overdue. As if she'd missed a train. I'd built a house out of not-talking and moved them all into it and called it protection.

But Wren had that letter now. I'd seen her reading it by lamplight, seen her face afterward, and whatever was in it had already cracked the foundation. The walls were coming down regardless. The only choice left was whether we were standing inside or underneath when they fell.

"Sit down," I said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Fine. Stand." I pulled the ledger toward me and put my hand flat on the page, on my own forged signature, and felt the strange comfort of touching evidence instead of memory. "Maren knew about the ring."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the first edge of the storm dragged a branch along the roof.

"Before she died," I said. "Before the war took her, before any of it — she knew. Fishermen moving more than fish out of the cove, gear going missing, boats running dark. She got curious. Maren was never curious about anything halfway. She got close enough to see the shape of it, and then she saw the shape of what it would do to us if it ever needed a workshop and a lighthouse and a man with a reputation for honesty." My voice was doing something embarrassing. I let it. "So she started leaving things where I'd find them. Notes in the netting. A list of boat names tucked in Tam's arithmetic book, if you remember wondering why I suddenly taught you to read tide tables a year early."

Tam had gone very still. "She was watching them."

"She was protecting you. Both of you. And she was careful, but careful people still drown, and I have spent three years not knowing whether her drowning was weather or work." I heard myself say it and was surprised — I'd never once let myself frame it as a question. It had always been *the sea*. The sea took her. The sea is innocent. The sea lets you sleep at night. "That stamp. Someone in the ring has it, or had it copied, or she gave it to someone inside to use. I don't know which yet. What I know is that they've been using this room, my name, and your hands' worth of skill to service lamps for boats that carry deserters out and contraband in. And now an army captain with clean boots is coming back Friday with soldiers and a warrant, and everything in this room is evidence."

Tam sat down slowly, onto the fallen stool, like a marionette with two strings cut. For a long moment he just looked at the ledger.

"She never told me any of it," he said finally. "She told you."

"She told no one. That was the trouble with Maren." And then, because the wall was already down and there seemed no point rebuilding it halfway, I heard myself continue: "She used to sing while she worked, did you know that? Terribly. Off-key with total confidence. She'd sing hymns to sea shanty tunes and shanties to hymn tunes and get the words wrong on purpose to see if Wren would correct her. Wren always did. Maren counted on it." My throat closed around the next part and I pushed through anyway, because I'd decided, apparently, to spend everything tonight. "She was afraid of moths. Absolutely terrified, a grown woman, and she'd never admit it, so she'd send you out of the room to fetch something whenever one got in, Tam, and you never once questioned it, and I never once told you. I let you believe she needed matches."

"Why?" His voice was very small. "Why did we never — why did we just *stop*, Bram? All of us, at once, like we agreed?"

"Because grief is heavy," I said, "and three people can carry it easier if nobody looks at it directly. That's not agreement. That's cowardice wearing work clothes. I'm the worst of it. I buried her in work and called it keeping the light." I laughed, or something near it. "A lighthouse keeper who couldn't bear to look at the dark."

Tam wiped his face with his sleeve, solder-smudged and shaking, and then he did the thing that undid me entirely: he reached across the bench and put his hand on top of mine, on the forged signature, and said, "She'd have hated how quiet this house got."

"She'd have sung until we fixed it."

"Yes." He nodded hard, twice, and then the boy was back — spine straightening, eyes going to the ledger, to the tools, to the half-built lamp frame under the canvas in the corner. "Friday. Voss said Friday?"

"Before Friday. With the storm."

"Then we've got maybe two days and a room full of evidence." He was already pulling the canvas off the frame, already calculating, the way Maren used to calculate, fast and slightly reckless. "Unless the evidence becomes something else first."

I should have asked what he meant. Any sensible man would have asked what he meant.

Instead I noticed, through the workshop window, Wren crossing the yard toward us through the rising wind with the oilskin packet clutched against her chest and an expression on her face I hadn't seen since the day the telegram came — and I understood, with a keeper's certainty, that whatever she was carrying in that oilskin was about to change the size of everything.
