## 7. Instructions in the Wall

Wren woke me before dawn by standing at the foot of my cot and saying my name exactly once, in the voice she'd learned from our sister — the one that meant *get up or be gotten up*. I have never been able to tell those two voices apart. That used to feel like a comfort. Now it felt like a summons.

"It's time," she said.

"Time for what?"

She held up the letter. Our dead sister's handwriting on it, dated three years after her death, which is the sort of thing that either ruins a person's faith in calendars or restores it entirely. "Time to open the wall."

* * *

The workshop smelled of cold iron and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a room that had been shut up with a secret too long. Bram was already there. He stood in the middle of the floor with his arms crossed, looking like a lighthouse that had been asked to explain itself.

"You told him?" I whispered to Wren.

"He deserves to know what we're doing."

"I deserve to know what you're doing," Bram corrected, without heat. He'd spent the night reading the pages Wren finally surrendered to him — the confession, the parts of it she'd decoded alone in Salt-and-Silence secrecy, hoarding our sister's words like a miser because sharing them felt like spending her twice. I understood. When Maren died, Bram didn't cry at the funeral; he re-tarred the gallery railing so thoroughly you could see your face in it. Grief, in this house, wears an apron.

But he'd read it now. All of it. And whatever it had done to him, he'd done it privately, the way he did everything, and come out the other side with his boots laced.

"She wrote all of it," he said quietly. Not a question. A man confirming the weight of a load before lifting it.

"All of it," Wren said. "The ring. The deserter network. The men who leaned on her — the harbormaster's clerk, the one who kept the ledgers for them. She got close enough to learn how they moved gear up the coast. And when she realized they were going to use us—" Her voice snagged. Wren has our sister's habit of finishing sentences even when her throat closes. "—she started planning around it. Around her own death, if it came to that."

"And it came to that," I said, because someone had to say the plain thing out loud, and in this family that job apparently falls to the fourteen-year-old.

Bram looked at me then, and I saw something shift behind his eyes — not softness exactly, but the recognition of softness, the way a keeper notes weather. "Aye," he said. "It came to that." He turned to the north wall. The wall where the shelving stood, where the tool racks hung, where nothing had ever seemed out of place because Maren had built the shelving herself and everything in it had its home. "Show me."

Wren took down the third shelf bracket. Behind it, scored faintly into the plaster in pencil — Maren's pencil, the flat carpenter's pencil she kept behind her ear like other women kept flowers — was a small compass rose. North point marked with an X.

"She drew it where only someone looking for it would find it," Wren said. "And she hid the instructions in the cavity because she knew the workshop would be searched. She says so. 'They will search your rooms, Bram, because searching rooms is free and warrants are expensive.'"

I laughed before I could stop myself. It came out wrong, high and startled, and nobody blamed me for it, which somehow made it worse.

"The X," Bram said. He pressed the plaster with his thumb, feeling for give, working along the seam like a man taking a pulse. Then he went to the bench, came back with the cold chisel, and set the edge against the line. He paused. His hand wasn't steady. I had never seen Bram Montrose's hand unsteady over anything except Maren's grave, and I pretended not to see it now, out of respect, the way you look away from a man praying.

"Do it," Wren said gently.

He did it.

The plaster gave with a sound like a sigh, and behind it was a space the width of two hands, lined with oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth: a tin. A biscuit tin, the kind from the grocer in Kirkwall, dented at one corner. On the lid, in that same blunt carpenter's pencil, our sister had written four words.

*For the three of you.*

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Bram lifted the lid as if it might go off.

Inside were papers. Dozens of them. Pages in Maren's hand — diagrams, lists, a folded chart of the cove with depths penciled in margins, letters sealed with wax, a small brass key wrapped in thread. And on top, a single sheet headed with a line I would carry under my ribs for the rest of my life:

*If you're reading this, you found each other. Good. Now find my beacon.*

* * *

We read it aloud, taking turns, because that's how Maren would have wanted it and because no one of us could have carried the whole of it alone. Wren read first.

*"They sabotaged the harbor beacon in October. I watched them do it — the clerk and his cousins, after the supply run on the Mary Ellen. They cracked the lens housing and salted the works so it would fail in the first hard storm, and when it fails, the rescue boats out of Stromness won't be able to find the channel. They mean for men to drown on the rocks and blame the keepers' neglect. Your neglect. Ours."*

Bram's jaw worked. "That's why Voss wants the workshop. Evidence of tampering. They've arranged it so the evidence lands on us either way — guilty of aiding deserters, or guilty of a dark harbor."

"They win both ways," Wren said. "Unless—"

"*Unless,*" I read next, because the page had come around to me and my hands were shaking and I wanted something to do with them besides shake. "*Fix it. You have everything you need. I bought the replacement prism glass through the Kirkwall agent two years ago — receipt enclosed, in my name, dated and witnessed. It's yours by right. Finish the beacon before the next gale. Let them come and find a light burning where they planned darkness. Work is the only testimony they can't forge.*"

There was more. Pages of it. Which bolts she'd loosened rather than removed, so the housing could be opened fast. Where the prism crate was cached — not in the workshop, clever girl, but under the peat stack behind the byre, dry as a sermon. The brass key fit the signal locker at the harbor station, where she'd left the mounting collars on loan from a fisherman who owed her and knew better than to ask.

And at the bottom of the tin, one page apart from the rest, written slower, the pencil pressed deep enough to emboss:

*"Bram. I know what you'll do. You'll do all of it and say nothing and stand straighter for carrying it. Don't. Tell them things. Ugly ones, funny ones, all of them. A family that only shares work shares half a life. I married a lighthouse and loved it dearly, but I also loved the man inside it, and I want him back for them. For you. Let them hold some of the weight. That's an instruction, not a wish. — M."*

Bram read it twice. Then he sat down slowly on the tool bench, which creaked, and put his face in his hands, and for a long moment the only sound in the workshop was the sea against the point below, patient as ever, keeping its own counsel.

When he looked up his eyes were red and his voice was level. "Well," he said. "You heard her. Instructions, not wishes." He wiped his face once, briskly, like mending a net. "Tam. Peat stack. Bring the crate in without letting the whole parish see you do it. Wren — the chart and the key. We'll want the tide tables and the state of the channel before we touch glass." He stood, and something in the room changed with him, some current reversing. "We've a beacon to build and perhaps four days to build it in, if the barometer means what it's saying. And when Voss comes with his warrant, he's going to walk into a finished light and a family standing in front of it."

"And the evidence?" I asked. "The ledger with your stamp on it? The gear they ran through here?"

Bram almost smiled. It was a strange sight, like sun on a north-facing cliff — rare, and it made everything around it look different. "Maren thought of that too," he said, nodding at the tin. "Keep reading. There's a letter in there addressed to Captain Voss himself. Sealed. Dated October. She posted a copy to the Admiralty the week before she died — registered, from the Kirkwall office. Which means somewhere in an Admiralty drawer, there is already a record of everything the ring did, filed under her name, waiting for someone honest to ask for it."

Wren stared at him. "She mailed it *before*—"

"Aye." He picked up the tin lid and turned it over in his big careful hands. "She lit the lamp before she left the room. All we have to do is keep it burning until the relief boat comes."

* * *

We worked through the morning and into the gray afternoon. I hauled the prism crate from under the peat — heavier than sin, packed in straw and oilcloth, the glass inside wrapped like a sleeping child — and together the three of us cleared the bench, inventoried tools, laid out the collars and bolts Maren's letter told us to expect. It was strange, following instructions from a dead woman and finding every one of them correct, like being steered by a star you can't see anymore.

Somewhere in the middle of it, without anyone deciding it, we stopped moving like three separate people and started moving like a crew. Wren called measurements and I fetched; Bram's hands and mine fit the housing clamps in the old rhythm he'd taught me at eight years old, when my whole job was holding things and asking why. Nobody said anything about Maren. Everybody was thinking about Maren. It was the first time thinking about her hadn't hurt like a splinter — because now she wasn't a wound we tiptoed around. She was the plan. She was the foreman. She was, in her stubborn posthumous way, standing right there with the pencil behind her ear telling us to mind the torque on the third bolt.

By dusk the new prism sat ready on the bench, catching the last light and throwing it across the rafters in small trembling rainbows. Four days' work ahead of us, maybe less. A storm building out past the point like a debt coming due.

And that was when we heard it — wheels on the gravel road above the workshop, slow and deliberate, the way a man drives when he wants to be heard coming. Through the fogged window we watched a government motorcar nose down toward the house, lamps lit against the failing light, and behind it, on the road, a second vehicle with canvas over its bed and soldiers' silhouettes sitting stiff as cargo.

Bram set down his wrench.

"Well," he said, wiping his hands on his rag, calm as Sunday. "They've come to seal the workshop."

He looked at the prism glowing on the bench, at the wall cavity gaping open behind the shelf, at the two of us — and I swear to God he smiled properly this time.

"Then we'd best not be in it."
