## 8. Storm Warning

The barometer began its slow suicide on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning Captain Ellery Voss was standing in our kitchen with his boots still clean.

I want you to appreciate the physics of that. Gullwrack Point had been under siege from the sea for two days straight — spray coming over the cliff wall in sheets, the path down to the cove turned to a river running the wrong direction — and yet here was this man, dry as a ledger, as though the storm had been briefed on his rank and told to keep its distance. He'd rowed out with the last calm tide and simply *arrived*, like weather of a different kind. The kind that doesn't care what it ruins so long as it arrives on schedule.

He had warrants with him. I could see the corners of them through the oilcloth of his satchel, squared off and official, the way death notices are squared off and official.

"Miss Montrose," he said. "Or is it Brightwater? Your sister's records use both."

The question was a scalpel dressed up as small talk. I gave it the smile I save for men who think politeness is a form of interrogation they've already won. "Whichever one makes your paperwork easier, Captain. We wouldn't want you straining yourself."

Bram stood by the stove with his back to all of us, doing something unnecessary to a kettle that did not need doing. Tam sat very still at the table, which for Tam was the equivalent of screaming. Three years of practice had taught me to read this family the way sailors read cloud banks: Bram's silence meant a decision had already been made, and Tam's stillness meant he was terrified of having made one himself.

"Storm's going to be bad," Voss said, glancing at the window, where the glass shivered like something alive. "Barometer's dropped forty points since Falmouth. They're saying it may be the worst in twenty years."

"Fascinating," I said. "You came all this way to discuss meteorology."

"I came," he said, and set the satchel on the table between us, "to seal your workshop."

* *

They did it while the wind screamed against the shutters like something demanding to be let in. Two soldiers Voss had brought with him — boys, really, younger than my sister had been when she died — pasted paper seals across the workshop door and stamped them, and then Voss locked it with an army padlock heavy enough to sink a body, and stood back to admire his work the way small men admire walls.

Half our beacon parts were inside.

That was the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing, standing there in the rain with the salt stinging my face. The lens housing. The governor assembly. The clockwork rotation mechanism Bram had been rebuilding since October, piece by piece, in the only building on the point warm enough and dry enough for fine work. All of it behind an army lock now, with the greatest storm of a generation bearing down on us like a fist.

"You understand why," Voss said. It wasn't quite an apology. Nothing about the man ever was; even his cruelty came pre-laundered. "Signal equipment, unregistered repairs, your brother-in-law's stamp on requisition documents that never passed through any requisition office. If half of what's in that ledger is true—"

"If half of what's in that ledger is true," I said, "then someone used our family, our tools, and our dead sister's memory to run supplies to deserters. And you're not interested in who that someone is, because the someone who wrote your ledger entries has better handwriting than a dockworker and better access than a smuggler."

Something moved behind his eyes. Small. Quick. A man checking a pocket he thought he'd emptied.

"You have evidence to support that accusation?"

"I have letters," I said.

And there it was. The thing I'd been carrying alone for weeks, the secret that had sat in my chest like swallowed glass — Maren's letters, her handwriting dated three years after her funeral, her confession folded into the wall of our own workshop like a seed planted before winter. *She infiltrated the ring,* the letter said. *To protect you. She left instructions. Finish the harbor beacon.* I had told no one. Not Bram, not Tam. I had carried it the way she must have carried the truth of what she was doing — alone, because love is sometimes just another word for the weight you agree to hold without complaint.

But now the workshop was sealed, and the storm was coming, and boats would die on that harbor mouth if the beacon stayed dark, and I discovered that some weights are not meant to be carried alone after all. Some are meant to be spent.

"Letters," Voss repeated.

"From my sister. Written before she died. She knew what was happening — she *found out* what was happening — and she left proof. Names, dates, the chain of hands that equipment passed through." I held his gaze. "Proof that we weren't the smugglers, Captain. Proof that we were the target. Someone needed a family too grief-struck and too isolated to notice what was being done in their name."

* *

We negotiated in the kitchen, because Voss refused the parlor — "too many exits," he said, as if my grandmother's doilies constituted a tactical threat — and I learned something about bargaining with army men: they respect nothing so much as a woman who quotes their own regulations back at them.

"You'll surrender the letters," Voss said. "For verification."

"No."

"That is not a negotiation, Miss Brightwater."

"It isn't? Then let me explain what I thought we were doing." I laid it out flat, the way Maren taught me to gut a fish — quick, clean, no sentiment. "You get copies of every page. You get them tonight. In exchange, the workshop comes unsealed before the storm hits, because half a beacon is inside that building and the other half is in our cellar, and when the rescue stations start pulling drowned men out of that water tomorrow, every one of them will owe their life to whether that light was turning. You can arrest us afterward. You can burn the file or frame us or write whatever report keeps your career warm. But you will not let people die so your paperwork stays tidy."

"The seals cannot be broken without—" 

"Your authority," I finished. "Yes. So authorize it. That's rather the point of you, isn't it?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Rain hammered the roof. Somewhere below us, the sea was gathering itself for the night's work.

"You realize," he said quietly, "that if these letters say what you claim — if your sister was what you claim — then everything I've been sent here to do changes shape. And the people who sent me will not thank either of us for it."

"Then we'll be unpopular together," I said. "It's nearly Christmas. Consider it my gift."

Almost — *almost* — the corner of his mouth moved. It might have been a smile. It might have been a tic. With Ellery Voss the distinction hardly mattered.

"One condition," he said. "I read the originals first. Tonight, before the seals come off. If there's anything in those pages that implicates this family beyond what you've told me, the workshop stays sealed and the warrants go into effect at dawn."

"And if they don't?"

"Then God help me, Miss Brightwater, I'll stand in your workshop myself and hand you the parts."

Tam, who had been silent through all of it, spoke for the first time. "You should know," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's instead of a man's, "she was brilliant. Our sister. Whatever's in those letters — whatever she did — she did it for us. She always did everything for us."

Voss regarded him for a moment with something that was almost gentleness, worn badly, like a coat cut for a bigger man.

"I know what that's worth," he said. "My brother didn't come home from Passchendaele. Somebody kept his letters for me. I've read them four hundred times."

None of us said anything to that. There wasn't anything to say. The war had made a whole country of people carrying other people's handwriting around like organs transplanted where hearts used to be.

"Tonight, then," Voss said, and picked up his satchel, warrants and all. "Before the barometer finishes dying."

* *

He read them at our own table, by our own lamp, while outside the wind rose through registers I had never heard it reach — a sound less like weather than like the sea deciding, finally, to come ashore and collect everything. Bram sat across from him with his arms folded, watching each page turn the way a man watches a surgeon's hands. Tam paced. I sat by the window and watched the dark, and thought about my sister walking these same floors with a secret in her chest, three years dead and still working, still steering us, still — God help me — *protecting* us, from beyond the edge of the world.

Page by page, I watched the fight go out of Ellery Voss. Not all at once. In pieces. The set of his shoulders first. Then his jaw. Then, somewhere around the third letter — the one where Maren named the quartermaster in Falmouth and described, with her terrible clear precision, exactly how our signature stamp had been taken and returned — he stopped reading like an investigator and started reading like a mourner.

When he finished, he sat with his hands flat on the pages for a long time.

"She knew they'd come for you," he said at last.

"She knew everything," I said. "It was her most annoying quality."

He stood. He took the army padlock key from his pocket, and he set it on the table between the lamp and the letters, and pushed it across the wood until it touched Bram's folded hands.

"The seals come down tonight," Voss said. "All of them. Before the storm makes landfall." He paused at the door, rain already reaching for him, and did not quite look back. "There are boats out there tomorrow night, aren't there. Rescue boats. From the station at Porthmear."

"Yes," I said.

"They'll need the light."

"Yes."

He nodded once, and stepped out into the roaring dark, and I heard his boots — finally, blessedly — splash in the flooded yard like an ordinary man's.

Bram turned the key over in his fingers. Tam was already pulling his coat from its peg. Through the window, the first true gusts of the storm came off the sea and struck the house like a broadside, and far out in the black, where the harbor mouth waited with its teeth open, the unfinished beacon stood naked on its stone.

"Get the lanterns," Bram said. "Both of them. And Tam — bring the crowbar. We're breaking into our own house."

We went out together into the teeth of it, four of us now, counting the dead.
