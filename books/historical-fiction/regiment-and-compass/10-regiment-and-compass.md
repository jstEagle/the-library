## 10. Regiment and Compass

The morning after the armistice, Captain Ellery Voss drank tea in our kitchen and I learned that the most dangerous man on the coast of Cornwall took his with too much milk.

He sat where Bram usually sat, at the corner by the window, and he held the cup the way he'd held everything since he arrived — like evidence he was still deciding what to do with. The storm had passed. The workshop door hung off its hinges because we'd broken into our own property with a crowbar and a prayer, and Voss had watched us do it, warrant in hand, and done nothing. That nothing was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

"You understand," he said to Bram, "that this file doesn't close itself."

"I understand," Bram said, "that you've read the letters."

Voss had. All night, apparently, sitting alone in the signal station with Wren's sister's handwriting spread out before him like a confession — which it was, though not the kind he'd come for. Maren Montrose. My sister. Dead three years and still the only person in that room who knew exactly what she was doing.

"The name on the smuggling manifests," Voss said slowly, "the signature stamp, the access to your workshop — it was arranged *for* you. By her. She inserted herself into the ring deliberately, documented everything, and left instructions for a family she knew would be blamed if she failed." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "She didn't fail. She died before she could finish it, but she didn't fail."

"She finished it," Wren said from the doorway.

I looked at her. She looked like someone who hadn't slept, which was accurate, and like someone carrying less than she had been, which was also accurate. She'd told us about the letter the night of the gale, standing in floodwater up to her knees, shouting over the wind — *Maren wrote to me, Maren wrote to me three years after she died* — and I'd decided then that grief makes people say mad things, and then we'd opened the wall, and there it all was.

"Then the file closes," Voss said. "Her name is cleared. Posthumously." He said the word carefully, the way you handle something sharp. "There will be paperwork. There is always paperwork. But no charges will be brought against this household."

Bram nodded once, the way he acknowledged weather reports.

"And the beacon," Voss added, "performed adequately last night."

From Voss, that was a parade with brass bands. Three boats had come through the cove in the worst storm of the year — deserters, smugglers, men the army wanted, men whose names were in Maren's letters — and they'd found the harbor light burning because a keeper, two children, and an army captain's willful blindness had carried a lens through waist-deep water and mounted it by hand. Two of those boats had been carrying wounded men. One of them wouldn't have made the rocks without us. I try not to be smug about it. I am, internally, extremely smug about it.

"Thank you," Bram said.

"Don't thank me," said Voss, setting down his cup. "You broke a government seal."

"Yes," said Bram, and did not apologize, and Voss almost smiled. Almost. On his face it looked like a crack in a seawall.

* * *

We buried the letters on a Thursday, because Maren had been born on a Thursday and Wren said she'd have found that funny — dying in autumn and being honored on her own weekday, like a subscription renewed.

The grave sits on the headland above Gullwrack, where the grass grows sideways and the wind files down every stone ever raised there. We'd marked it three years ago with whatever we could carry: a slab of slate, Bram's chisel-work, just her name and two dates. Now Wren knelt and dug a small hollow at the base of it, and laid the oilskin packet inside, and then the workshop letters, and then the final page — the one Maren had written last, the one addressed to all of us together, which none of us had managed to read aloud yet without someone's voice going wrong.

"We could keep them," I said. It came out before I could stop it. "Copy them first. Keep them somewhere dry."

Wren looked up at me. My sister — my other sister, the living one, though for three years neither of us would have used the word — wiped her nose on her sleeve with tremendous dignity.

"They were never meant to be kept," she said. "They were meant to be delivered. This is delivery."

"It feels like giving her up again."

"It does," Wren agreed. "But we already gave her up once, and look how badly that went. This time we do it properly. Together. With witnesses."

Our witness was a seagull, initially, but then Bram crouched down beside us, and then — uninvited, unexplained, boots ruined in the wet grass — so did Captain Ellery Voss, who stood at the edge of the hole like a man attending the funeral of a stranger he'd grown to respect entirely against his will.

"She was better at this work than I am," Voss said quietly. It was the first time he'd spoken her name aloud. "Infiltrating a network takes nerve. Doing it while writing letters to your family takes—" He stopped. "It takes something I don't have a form for."

"There's no form for most of it," Bram said. "You get used to filing things under weather."

And that — I swear on the lamp itself — that got a real laugh out of the man. Short, startled, like a sneeze he'd been holding in for a month. Then he straightened, cleared his throat, and became a captain again, and Wren pushed the earth back over the letters with her bare hands, and Bram said a few words that weren't scripture and weren't eulogy, just a keeper's words: *she showed us where the rocks were.* And I put my hand flat on the slate, cold as the sea, and thought: *goodbye, Maren. You absolute menace. You saved us twice — once when you lived, and once after.*

* * *

That evening we relit the lamp.

Not the harbor beacon — that was Voss's problem now, or the Admiralty's, and there was talk of making it official, permanent, a proper aid to navigation with our names in some ledger nobody would read. No. The great lamp at the top of Gullwrack Point, the one Bram had tended every night for eleven years and I had helped tend for six of them, the one that had gone dark for exactly one hour during the storm because the whole family had been hauling glass through a flooded workshop instead.

Climbing the tower stairs felt different that night. Everything physical was the same — the iron rail worn smooth, the smell of brass polish and lamp oil, the 117 steps I'd counted as a boy and never admitted to counting. But I climbed them knowing things now. About my brother-in-law, who had lied to an army investigator with a straight face to protect children who weren't his blood. About my sister, who had decoded a dead woman's warnings alone rather than frighten us, and nearly drowned under the weight of it. About Maren, who had loved us in the most complicated way available to a person: by disappearing into danger and leaving maps behind.

Bram opened the clockwork case. Wren trimmed the wick. I pumped up the pressure and watched the gauge the way I'd been taught, and nobody spoke, because lighting the lamp was liturgy and you don't chat during liturgy.

"Ready?" Bram asked, and his voice cracked slightly on it, and none of us mentioned it, because that's how we love each other in this house — by pretending not to notice.

The flame caught. The lens turned. The beam swung out over the dark water, steady as a heartbeat, and below us the coast lay quiet for the first time in years — no ships running dark, no signals flashing lies, no war waiting at the edge of the horizon. Just water, and the light lying across it like a hand.

"For the first night of peace," Wren said.

"For Maren," I said.

"For all of us," said Bram, and wound the clockwork, and the mechanism took up its patient ticking, the sound I've slept beside my whole life.

We stayed up in the gallery longer than we needed to. There was tea in thermoses and a blanket that all three of us fit under if nobody minded elbows, and at some point Bram put his arms around both of us, stiffly, the way he does everything tender, like a man handling glass he's afraid of dropping.

"You know," he said, to the horizon more than to us, "I took you in because I promised her. Duty. A promise." He paused. "That's not why anymore."

"No?" said Wren.

"No." He said it simply, the way he said *yes* to storms and *no* to investigators. "I'd choose you now. Every day, out loud, no promise required. That's the difference between keeping a light and lighting one."

I didn't say anything. I'm not built for saying things in moments like that; I'm built for counting steps and pumping gauges and noticing, later, what mattered. What I noticed later was this: that the three of us stayed until the small hours watching the beam turn, and nobody wanted to be the first to go to bed, and nobody had to be, and that this — this exact arrangement of ordinary people choosing each other above a cold headland — was the whole point of everything. Of Maren's letters. Of the wall she hid them in. Of the lens we carried through the flood.

Regiment and compass. That's what she'd called her plan, in the last page, in her quick slanted hand: *a regiment keeps you alive, but a compass tells you who you are.* She'd meant the army and the light. She'd also, I think, meant us — the family that drills together through gales, and points, always, toward each other.

The lamp turned. The sea breathed. Somewhere below, the tide came in gentle as a cat, and for the first time in my life at Gullwrack Point, I wasn't watching the water for what it might bring.

Whatever came next, we'd light the way ourselves.
