## 3. Requisition

They came into the square at first light, forty men in gray coats, and the sound of them — boots on cobbles, the jingle of harness, a sergeant's voice flat as a slate — carried down Harbor Lane and through the shutters of the print shop like water through a cracked pane.

Oscar stood in the doorway with his arms folded, watching, and told himself it was a procession. Towns had processions. Saints' days, harvest fairs, the funeral of old Bishop Corrigan when he was nine. But processions ended. This one stopped. The soldiers stacked their muskets against the fountain in the center of the square with the ease of men who intended to stay, and began unrolling bedrolls on the church steps, and a corporal chalked numbers on the doors of houses as if the town were already an inventory.

Vellamare had never been inventoried before.

By eight o'clock the market had opened anyway, because markets open, but the sellers kept their voices low and the buyers moved in pairs, whispering. Oscar walked the length of it to deliver an invoice to Grell's chandlery and heard the same sentence everywhere, pitched differently: *What do they want?*

Nobody knew yet. That was the worst of it. Not the knowing — the not-knowing, which let every man fill the silence with his own particular fear.

The answer came at noon, wearing a good coat.

* * *

Captain Aldous Reyne arrived at the print shop at half past twelve, precisely, and knocked twice on the doorframe before entering, though the door was open. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the unhurried manner of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because other men raised theirs for him.

"Master Nakamura." He read the name from a card in his gloved hand, though there could be no doubt. "I'm told you keep your father's shop."

"My father is dead."

"Yes. My condolences, delayed by distance." He stepped inside without being asked, and his eyes went over the shop the way a man reads a room — the cases of type, the press under its canvas, the hanging proofs drying like laundry. "A printing house. How useful a town this is. A harbor, a granary, and now I learn a press."

"We do invoices," Oscar said. "Notices. Weddings."

"And the civic ledger."

There it was. Oscar felt it settle onto his chest like a hand. "It's kept here, yes."

"I'll require it." Reyne removed his glove finger by finger. "The harbor is requisitioned for the provisioning of the fleet — you'll have seen the ships off the point. The granary likewise. And all civil records are to be surrendered for compilation. Conscription lists. Assessments. Certain... clarifications of loyalty." He said the word *loyalty* the way another man might say *weather*. "The ledger of names is the heart of it. Every soul in Vellamare, entered in one book. You can imagine how much labor that saves an officer."

"The ledger belongs to the town council."

"The town council has deferred," Reyne said, "which is a polite way of saying they've agreed. They agreed very quickly, actually." He smiled, and it was a fine smile, warm at the edges, and Oscar understood that everything frightening about this man lived in the warmth. "I'm told you're the acting keeper now. Your father's hand is in that book for thirty years, and yours beside it. A family of clerks. Honest work."

"What will you do with it?"

"Copy from it. Extract from it. Names travel; books stay." He set his gloves on the counter with a small precise tap. "You'll bring it to me at the customs house. Three days. It ships on the morning tide after that — there's a boat waiting on exactly such cargo, so I'd ask you not to make me chase it. Chasing things is the one part of this profession I dislike."

Three days. Oscar heard the number land somewhere below thought, in the body, where dread keeps its accounts.

"If a page is missing—"

"Then I'll know the shape of what's missing," Reyne said mildly, "the way you know the shape of a pulled tooth. Bring me the whole book, Master Nakamura. Men have died over less than a page." He replaced his gloves, nodded once — courteous to the last degree — and paused at the door. "Your father. Did he ever speak to you about the value of records?"

"No."

"A shame. Records are the only thing an empire truly fears. Everything else, we can burn or pardon." The bell above the door gave its small honest ring behind him, absurdly domestic, and he was gone up the lane toward the customs house, his boots keeping perfect time.

* * *

From the window of the customs house, Captain Aldous Reyne watched the harbor take the afternoon light and permitted himself two minutes of satisfaction, which was more than he usually allowed.

Vellamare would be simple. They were always simpler than they believed themselves to be. A town was a machine for producing order, and order produced lists, and lists produced power, and the men who imagined themselves safe — the printers, the clerks, the quiet inheritors of small offices — always handed over the lists eventually, because handing over was the whole of their training. Give them three days and a courteous face and they would bring you their neighbors bound in calfskin, and thank you for taking the weight.

He had done it twice before. In Marrow Bay the ledger had come in on the second day, wrapped in oilcloth by a schoolmaster who apologized for the smell of fish. The schoolmaster's name was in no list Reyne ever compiled, which was itself a kind of mercy, though the schoolmaster hadn't seen it that way afterward.

Reyne poured a measure of wine and did not drink it. Somewhere below, his sergeants were billeting men in the square, and somewhere beyond the point the requisition ship rode at anchor, patient as arithmetic. He disliked nothing about this work except the waiting, and the waiting was nearly over.

Still. The printer's son had asked *what will you do with it* — not *when*, not *where*, but *what*. That was either stupidity or conscience, and Reyne had rarely met stupidity that phrased itself so carefully.

He made a small mark in his own notebook. One name. He would remember to look at it again.

* * *

Oscar did not go back into the shop. He went down.

The cellar workshop ran beneath the whole footprint of the building, dug by his grandfather, cool even in August, lit now by two lamps that made pools of yellow in the dark. The type cases lined the near wall in their long ranks, each drawer a city of small metal letters, and above them the stone shelf where his father had dried proofs for thirty years.

He stood there a long time, listening to the muffled sounds of the square overhead — laughter, a boot heel, the corporal still chalking doors.

Then he lifted the ledger from its place beneath the counter upstairs and carried it down like something injured, both hands, close to the chest.

He had grown up inside this book. He knew its weight before he could read it — four pounds, give or take, the paper gone soft at the corners where thumbs had turned it. His father's entries marched through it decade by decade, upright and unhurried: births, deaths, debts, disputes settled, boats licensed, names changed. Thirty years of Vellamare in one man's handwriting, and then six years of Oscar's own hand growing steadier along the margin of the older pages.

And Reyne wanted it. Wanted it copied, extracted, compiled. Names travel; books stay.

Oscar laid the ledger on the stone shelf and did not open it. Opening it would mean deciding. Instead he knelt and began pulling the shallow drawers of the type case nearest the wall — the ones nobody touched, the broken sorts, the worn letters kept out of habit — and stacked them on the floor, until the case stood empty to its depth, a wooden box the exact length of the ledger and half again too wide.

He lined it with the felt they used to cushion the platen. He wrapped the ledger in oilcloth — the same oilcloth, he noticed with a strange detachment, that Reyne's imaginary schoolmaster might have used — and lowered it into the case, and slid the drawers of dead type back in on top of it, letter by letter, a burial in reverse.

Forty pounds of useless metal over four pounds of irreplaceable paper. Anyone lifting the case would feel only weight.

When it was done he sat back on his heels in the lamplight and listened to his own heartbeat slow.

The book was hidden. Which meant, he understood now, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that the next three days existed for one purpose: to build the thing he would surrender instead. A ledger for Reyne. A twin. Every page, every rule line, every entry in a hand that must pass for his own — because Reyne would be looking at six years of Oscar Nakamura's writing, and officers who compile lists notice hands.

He fetched paper from the ream kept for broadsheets, the closest match to the ledger stock, and cut it to size with the guillotine, and ruled the first page in pale pencil guides. Then he sat down before a fresh sheet with a pen and wrote his own name, ten times, twenty times, loosening the wrist his father had trained to be steady, studying the letters as if they belonged to a stranger whose habits he must learn perfectly.

*Oscar Nakamura, keeper of the civic ledger.* Six years of entries to reproduce. Births. Debts. Deaths. Boats. All of it true, all of it false, depending on which book held it.

Above him, boots crossed the floor of the shop — a soldier, wandering, curious, dragging a fingertip along the counter. Oscar killed one lamp and sat motionless among his cases of type, pen wet, heart loud, until the boots left.

Then he bent to the page and began to copy the town.

Outside, on the morning tide three days hence, a ship waited for a book. It would get one.
