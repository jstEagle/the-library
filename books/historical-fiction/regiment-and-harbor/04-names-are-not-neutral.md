## 4. Names Are Not Neutral

Oscar had always believed a ledger was a dull thing, and he had loved it for that. Dullness was safety. A ledger did not argue. It sat in its column and its rule and said: this much flour, this many days, this name, that debt. His father used to say the book was the town's second harbor — everything came to rest there eventually.

He no longer believed it was dull. He had learned otherwise in one afternoon, at a table lit by two lamps, with the original ledger open before him and forty blank sheets of the good rag paper laid out beside it like shrouds.

Three days. Reyne had given him three days, which meant less than three, because a man who wanted to seem unhurried never was. Oscar had done his arithmetic at midnight: four hundred and eleven pages, roughly nine thousand entries, and a hand — his father's hand — that he would have to wear like a coat cut for another man's shoulders. He could not copy the whole book; no forger alive could reproduce nine thousand entries in seventy hours without error, and error was death now, not embarrassment. So he would do what printers do. He would make a new edition. Abridged. Plausible. A ledger that looked lived-in, inked by a careful clerk over years, recording exactly what a captain would expect a town like Vellamare to record.

Which meant he had to know what the true ledger recorded. All of it. Tonight.

* * *

He began with the debts, because debts were the spine of the thing.

Page after page of them, in his father's upright script: Kessler's rope walk, three years behind on its grain account. The Ostervik wharfage fees, forgiven entire the winter Harald drowned — no, not forgiven, Oscar saw, amended. The word *waived* written small in the margin in pencil first, then inked as though it had always been so, backdated with a date from November. His father had erased nothing. Erasure left scars a careful eye could read. Instead he had rewritten history forward, made the mercy look like policy.

There were dozens of them. The Aldous miller's son's apprenticeship fee, quietly folded into a fictitious credit for "cartage, sundry." Widow Lund's coal debt struck through and re-entered as paid "by subscription of neighbors" — a subscription that had never happened, that his father had simply invented so the woman would not carry shame in the same book that carried her debt. Oscar sat very still, reading his own childhood in reverse. He remembered the winters his father came home smelling of lamp oil and said little. He had thought it was tiredness. Some of it had been tiredness. Some of it had been this — a man up late committing small treasons against arithmetic so that his neighbors would not be ground down by a number.

And other pages. He had known they existed, the way you know a house has a locked room. Debts entered twice under different names to disguise who actually owed what — shielding a debtor whose creditor was dangerous. Interest quietly compounded downward for some men and upward, minutely, for others, so that over ten years the difference became a fortune shifted from one family to another without anyone signing anything. His father had moved money the way water moves silt: invisibly, patiently, with enormous consequence.

Oscar pressed his palms flat against the table until the tremor went out of them.

His whole life he had believed the ledger recorded the town. Standing here at two in the morning with the lamps burning low, he understood at last that his father had *made* the town — nudged it, decade by decade, entry by entry, toward whatever shape a decent man believed a town should have. The book wasn't a mirror. It was a hand on the tiller.

And tomorrow the army wanted the tiller.

* * *

The birth was worse.

He found it because he was hunting it — Maren Ostervik had mentioned once, years ago, drinking his father's terrible coffee, that her daughter had been born in March, and the ledger said May. He had thought it a widow's sentimentality. It was not.

The entry read: *Ilse Ostervik, born 14 May, daughter of Harald Ostervik, harbormaster, and Maren, née Sørensen.* Neat. Correct-looking. But beneath it, faintly visible when the lamplight raked across the page at an angle, was the ghost of earlier ink, scraped and re-ruled. And in the margin, in pencil so light it was nearly breath: *born 9 March. Father unknown. H.O. claims.*

Harald Ostervik had claimed another man's child as his own, and Oscar's father had built the claim into official memory — had scratched out the truth and ruled a clean line over it, so that Ilse Ostervik grew up legitimate in the eyes of church, crown, and tax roll. Somewhere in Vellamare or beyond it was a man — was, or had been — who might have signed that margin note himself, or been denied the chance, or never known. Oscar would never learn which. That was the nature of the book: every kindness it performed was also a theft from someone, somewhere, of a truth they might have wanted back.

He thought of Ilse, sixteen now, working the fish tables with her mother's straight back. She was a fiction. She was also entirely real, and beloved, and hers. Which of those things was the ledger allowed to say?

*Neither,* he thought. *Both. That's why it's a weapon.*

* * *

Tomas Ferro he found near dawn, and finding him felt like touching a wire that was still live.

*Ferro, Tomas, corporal, garrison detachment — died of fever, 3 October, seven years past.* Entered in his father's hand, dated, witnessed, final. Except Oscar knew — had known since the man came down his cellar stairs two nights ago smelling of river mud and fear — that Corporal Ferro had not died of fever. He had deserted, walked north through the salt marshes, and vanished into the long anonymity of the coast. And Oscar's father, who owed Ferro nothing except the memory of the worst night of his own life — the night Ferro had stood at their door in his red-and-white sash and escorted Kenji Nakamura away to prison for printing what the Crown called sedition — that man had picked up his pen and killed Ferro on paper. Gave him a fever, gave him a date, gave him a clean death in a clean bed, so that somewhere north of the marshes a hunted man could stop being hunted.

Why? Oscar turned the question over and over while the sky outside went from black to slate. Gratitude? His father had none to give that man. Pity? Perhaps. Or perhaps it was simpler and colder than either: Kenji Nakamura had spent his life believing that names were not neutral, that a book could kill or shelter, and he had refused to let the ledger be only a weapon even when the weapon pointed at him. He had put the man who ruined him inside the walls of the book. That was not forgiveness. Oscar couldn't decide if it was anything a person could survive feeling. It was craft. It was the deepest kind of printer's logic — you set the type that makes the sentence true, and then you live inside the sentence.

And now Ferro slept twenty feet below him, under the floorboards, breathing slow in the dark among the type cases, while Oscar sat above him holding the proof of his own resurrection in his hands.

* * *

The light was fully gray when Oscar finally closed the true ledger and looked at what he'd built so far: eleven sheets of forged entries, drying in rows. Good work. Convincing work. And suddenly, unbearably, insufficient — because he had planned to forge a *record*, and now he understood he had to forge a *judgment*. Whatever book he handed Captain Reyne would become the army's truth about Vellamare. If he copied the debts faithfully, men would hang for numbers his father had already quietly absolved. If he copied the births faithfully, Ilse Ostervik's existence would become a question an officer could ask. Every line was a decision about who his neighbors would be when the soldiers finished reading.

His father had edited the town gently, over thirty years, in pencil first and ink second. Oscar had three days to edit it wholesale, in ink only, under occupation.

He dipped his pen, and stopped, because there was a sound below — soft, deliberate, the scrape of a floorboard in the cellar that no rat made. Then footsteps on the cellar stair, and a knock from inside, three taps, the signal they'd agreed on.

Oscar slid the true ledger beneath the type cases and opened the forgery on top of it, and went to unbar the door.

Maren Ostervik stood in the gray light with a covered basket on her arm and her husband's old boat cloak still buttoned at her throat, and she looked past Oscar's shoulder at the drying sheets with the expression of a woman doing sums she didn't like the total of.

"You've been forging all night," she said. It was not a question.

"I've been copying," Oscar said.

"No." She stepped inside, set down the basket, and pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "Copying keeps the words. You've been changing them. I can see it from the door." She looked up at him, and there was something in her face that was not accusation — something hungrier. "Your father changed them too. Don't look surprised. Harald figured it out years ago and never told a soul, and I've kept the habit of keeping secrets ever since."

She held out one bare hand, palm up.

"Show me the real book," Maren said, "and then move aside, Mr. Nakamura. Your father's hand is good, but I can write a clerk's script that would fool a bishop, and we have less than three days — and only two pairs of eyes are going to catch what one pair misses."
