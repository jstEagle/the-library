## 5. The Widow's Hand

Oscar had been forging his father for three hours when the bell above the shop door rang.

He didn't hear it. He was down in the cellar with the lamp turned low, the true ledger open beside the false one, a steel pen in his hand and his father's hand living somewhere in his fingers like a second pulse. *Elias Brandt, debt of four crowns, forgiven at harvest, by order of the late clerk.* The words came out too tall. His father wrote small, mean, economical — letters that leaned forward as if hurrying to be done with themselves. Oscar's leaned back, apologetic. A forger's first sin: he couldn't stop being sorry.

The bell again. Then footsteps on the floor above, unhurried, crossing toward the stair.

He blew out the lamp on instinct, which was stupid, because the stair door was already opening and the light from the shop fell down the steps in a long gray blade, and standing in it was Maren Ostervik.

She looked at him. She looked at the two ledgers lying open on the workbench, one old, one new, both unmistakably the same book. She looked at the type cases stacked against the wall where he'd hidden the original three days ago, before Captain Reyne's courtesy arrived in the square like weather.

"Well," she said.

Oscar's mouth went dry. "Widow Ostervik."

"You're forging your father."

"I'm finishing his entries." It sounded thin even to him.

She came down the stairs without asking. That was her way; the whole town knew it. Maren Ostervik had run the harbor office for six years while her husband drank himself courteous, and since his death she had continued to walk into rooms as if they owed her rent. She was fifty-one and built like the pilings she'd grown up among — salt-cured, load-bearing, indifferent to storms.

She picked up the forged ledger. Turned pages. Her face gave nothing away, which frightened him more than anger would have.

"Page forty," she said finally. "Greta Solmund's birth. Your father entered it under the name Solmund. It should be Voss. Everyone in this town knows it and no one ever said so, and now you're copying the lie along with everything else."

"He recorded what he recorded. If I change things, the alterations will show—"

"If you copy lies," Maren said, setting the book down with a click like a lock turning, "then the ledger is just a weapon with better manners. Is that what you want to hand them? A list of names where every mercy is invisible and every debt stands?"

"They'll compare hands. Reyne has clerks. If the forgery fails—"

"Your forgery would fail." She said it flatly, not cruelly, the way you'd tell a man his hull was breached. "You write a fair imitation of grief. But grief isn't the same as the man."

Oscar sat down on the stool because his legs had opinions about standing. Three days of cutting type by day and copying by night, sleeping in the chair, eating bread over the workbench — it had all been holding together by craft alone, and craft was thinner than he'd believed.

"Then I'm finished," he said. "They sail on the morning tide in two days. There's no time to learn my father's hand."

"No," Maren agreed. "There isn't." She pulled off her gloves, finger by finger, and laid them on the bench beside the ledgers like instruments set out for surgery. "But there's time for me."

* * *

Maren Ostervik had learned to write before she'd learned to swim, which in Vellamare marked her as peculiar from the start.

Her father had been a fish-merchant who kept accounts in his head and resented paper, so when Halvard Ostervik — then a young harbormaster's clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a habit of quoting tide tables like scripture — offered to teach her letters in exchange for her father's custom, she had said yes the way you say yes to a door opening. Halvard taught her a copperplate clerk's hand first, the official hand, the one used in harbor registries across three counties. Then, because she was quick and because he liked showing off, he taught her variations. Seven different clerks. Seven different hands. He called it his gallery.

"Harbors are where forgeries happen," he told her once, amused, drunk, honest in the way drink made him rather than cruel. "Every port in the world runs on papers somebody wrote twice. I never wanted you naive, Mare. I wanted you useful."

She had been useful for thirty years. She had logged cargo, entered births and deaths in the parish rolls when the priest was ill, written bills of lading in five hands, and once — during the grain crisis, though she had never told a soul — altered a tariff schedule to keep three fishing families from losing their boats. Halvard had known. Halvard had approved. It was the only year of their marriage they'd truly been partners, and he'd died two winters later with his secrets intact and his gallery of hands living on in hers.

Now she stood in a printer's cellar smelling of ink and lamplight, looking at a young man who thought invisibility was a trade.

"Show me the original," she said.

Oscar slid the type cases aside and lifted out the true ledger — his father's ledger, thirty years of Vellamare in a binding gone soft at the corners. Maren took it with both hands. Not reverence exactly. Recognition. She had watched Anders Nakamura write in it across the counter of the harbor office a thousand times, complaining about the damp, sharpening his pen with a little knife he kept on a string.

She opened to the first page. The handwriting was exactly as she remembered: small, forward-leaning, economical, the crossbars slightly heavy, the descenders clipped short as a sailor's toast.

Then she turned to page forty and read Greta Solmund's entry, and read it again, and understood.

"He corrected himself," she said slowly. "Here — see the ink? This is a later hand over an earlier entry. He went back and changed the name. Voss to Solmund. Or—" she tilted the page to the lamp, "—no. He wrote Solmund first and Voss beneath, struck through. Someone paid him to hide a paternity, or someone begged him to. And he obeyed, but he kept the truth underneath where anyone patient could find it."

Oscar stared. "I've read that page twenty times."

"You've read it as a printer. I'm reading it as a clerk." She closed the ledger gently. "Your father hid mercies inside his errors, boy. That's why the book matters. And that's why your copy is worthless — you're reproducing the surface and losing the graves."

The word landed hard. Graves. Oscar thought of his father in the prison at Karsund, dying of a fever the army hadn't bothered to treat, and how the last letter home had been in this same forward-leaning hand, steady to the end, as if even dying were an entry to be kept neat.

"What do you propose?" he asked.

"I propose you do what you're good at. Cut type, print the forms, manage the ink, keep watch, and stay out of the writing." Maren rolled up her sleeves. "I'll do the entries. All of them. In his hand."

"You can't possibly—"

"Greta Solmund," Maren said, taking up the steel pen, dipping it, and writing eight words on scrap paper in a script so close to Anders Nakamura's that Oscar felt the cellar tilt. "There. Now fetch me the true ledger's last dated entry and don't interrupt me again tonight."

* * *

They worked until the third hour past midnight.

It went badly at first, which neither of them said aloud. Oscar could not stop hovering; Maren could not stop correcting him; and between the hovering and the correcting they ruined four sheets of good rag paper. The trouble wasn't the hand — her imitation grew more uncanny by the hour, as if Anders Nakamura had been resurrected at the workbench and was annoyed about it. The trouble was judgment. Every entry required a decision. Forgive the debt or let it stand? Correct the birth or preserve the lie? Restore Tomas Ferro's name to the roll of the living or leave him dead on paper where dead was safer?

"Leave Ferro," Oscar said, when they reached that line. "If the army finds a deserter listed alive—"

"If the army finds a town where the records are conveniently wrong, they'll dig. And Ferro is either in this town or he isn't. The ledger doesn't make him real." Maren's pen paused. "Is he?"

The question hung there. Oscar realized she already suspected — that she'd suspected since the night soldiers started counting boats, that widows who ran harbors counted everything twice.

"There was a man," he said carefully. "Asking after work. Down at the chandlery."

"Mm." She wrote something small in the margin of her practice sheet, and he couldn't tell if it was a note or a verdict. "And does this man asking after work know what happened at Brenholt?"

Oscar's blood went cold. "How do you—"

"A cousin's letter. Before the army sealed the roads." She dipped the pen again. "A list became a roster. Eleven men shot against the granary wall. So." She looked up at him across the lamp, and her eyes were the gray of water over sand — nothing hidden, nothing performed. "Whatever you're keeping in that cellar besides books, Nakamura, understand that I'm in it now. You handed me the pen. There's no unhanding."

He should have lied. He was, he reflected, spectacularly bad at the parts of conspiracy that involved lying. "The chandlery man comes tomorrow," he said. "After dark."

"Then we have today." She turned back to the ledger, and her voice softened by one degree, which from Maren Ostervik was practically a confession. "My Halvard escorted men to prison too, early on. Revenue duty. He carried the warrants. He never forgave himself, and he never spoke of it, and he taught me seven hands so that someday someone could unspeak it." She drew a clean stroke through a debt of two crowns owed by the Wexler family, forgiving it in Anders Nakamura's hand, in Anders Nakamura's style, with the truth preserved faintly beneath. "Consider this unspeaking."

Oscar found, to his surprise, that his throat had closed. He busied himself with the ink, grinding it, testing it against the batch he'd mixed to match thirty years of oxidation — iron-gall darkening toward brown at the edges, exactly as his father's had. Craft. Craft was something he could hold.

By dawn they had completed eleven pages. The forged ledger lay open between them like a shared crime, which is what it was, and Maren stretched her writing hand until the knuckles cracked, and looked at Oscar with something that had not been in her face when she came down the stairs.

"Tomorrow night," she said, "your deserter, your decisions, and my pen. And Nakamura—" she pulled her gloves back on, finger by finger, "—the requisition ship anchored this morning. I counted its guns from the point. Nineteen. It moved up the channel while we were working."

Oscar went still. "Reyne said three days."

"Reyne says many things courteously." Maren climbed two steps, then paused, silhouetted against the gray blade of light from the shop above. "And this morning a naval cutter landed an officer at the harbor office who asked to see the parish rolls. My rolls. I sent him away with a bill of lading and a smile." Her voice dropped, and the smile was gone. "We are running out of days faster than we are running out of names. Tell your chandlery man not to come after dark."

Oscar's stomach dropped. "Why not?"

"Because," said Maren Ostervik, "he needs to come now."
