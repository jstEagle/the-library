## 6. A Deserter in the Cellar

The knock came at half past two in the morning, three soft raps against the cellar's outer door, the one that opened onto the coal chute alley. Oscar knew the rhythm of it before he knew what it meant — too soft for soldiers, too deliberate for drunks, and nobody else in Vellamare had reason to knock on a print shop in the dark.

He went down with a lamp in one hand and his father's composing stick in the other, which was not a weapon, but felt like one. Through the door he said: "Who's there."

"A dead man," said a voice, low and roughened. "Let me be dead somewhere warm."

Oscar stood very still. He knew the voice. Not from the harbor, not from the market — from eleven years ago, from a rain-slicked street outside this same door, when two soldiers of the Crown had walked his father out between them and one of them had said, not unkindly, *I'm sorry for the weather* — as if the weather were the thing to apologize for.

Tomas Ferro.

The ledger said Ferro died four winters past. Fever, the entry read, in the garrison at Karst. His father's hand, neat and small: *Ferro, Tomas — deceased.* Oscar had set those letters himself. He remembered because it had felt strange to print the death of a man whose face he could still see.

Now the face was pressed to the gap in the door, gray in the lamplight, thinner than memory kept it, with a week of beard and a wound wrapped in filthy linen above the left ear.

"You're supposed to be dead," Oscar said.

"I am." A pause. "It's the best work I ever did. Open the door, Nakamura."

Oscar did not open it. He stood there while the lamp flame leaned in some draft from under the street door, and he thought about all the reasons to turn around and climb back up to bed: the requisition ship riding at anchor beyond the point, Captain Reyne's deadline ticking down like water into a hold, the forged ledger spread across his bench upstairs with its careful false entries, Maren Ostervik's hand waiting to improve his own. And now this. A deserter. A hunted man. The one soldier who had been there.

"They'll hang you if they find you," Oscar said.

"Then I'll have that in common with half the town by spring." Ferro shifted, and Oscar heard the wet gravel sound of exhaustion in it. "Two minutes. That's all I'm asking. Two minutes and then you can send me back into the rain."

Oscar opened the door.

---

Upstairs, in the workshop, with the shutters latched and a blanket around Ferro's shoulders, Oscar lit the second lamp because the man looked like something that would dissolve in shadow. Ferro drank the tea Oscar gave him in three swallows and held the cup out for more without asking, the way soldiers do, the way men do who have stopped believing anyone will offer twice.

"I didn't know where else," Ferro said. "Everyone I knew in this town thinks I'm dead, and everyone who doesn't is a soldier."

"That's not an answer to why here."

"It's the only answer I have." Ferro set the cup down. His eyes went around the workshop — the cases of type, the press under its canvas, the ledger shelf — and Oscar watched him take inventory the way soldiers take inventory, doors first, windows second, weight-bearing walls third. "You're Nakamura's son."

"He's dead too."

"I know. I was there when they took him." Ferro said it flatly, no apology in it, and somehow the absence of apology was worse. "I've owed this town a debt since that night. Since your father especially."

"You escorted him to prison."

"I did."

"And you want forgiveness."

"I want nothing." Ferro's voice stayed level, but something moved behind it, deep down, like a fish turning under ice. "Forgiveness isn't mine to ask. I came because of the ledger."

"The army has my ledger."

"No." Ferro leaned forward. "The army has your ledger *coming*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Listen to me, printer. Two years ago I was stationed in Halloway. You won't have heard of it — nobody hears of places until after. The Crown requisitioned their civic records, same courtesy, same polite officer, same three days' grace. And the ledger of Halloway became a list. And the list became a roster. And the roster became—" He stopped, and started again, quieter. "Eleven men stood against a wall behind the granary. They were chosen out of a book. Debts unpaid, names marked disloyal, a man whose brother had written a letter. The book decided. The book always decides, once it leaves the hands of the people who wrote it."

The lamps burned. Somewhere above, a patrol boot scraped across cobbles, unhurried, a sound like a file drawn slowly across iron.

"My father's book," Oscar said. "Every name in Vellamare. Every debt. Every birth."

"Every mercy too, maybe. If your father was the kind who made them." Ferro looked at the ledger shelf. "Is that the original?"

Oscar didn't answer.

Ferro almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn down to the stub. "You already know what I'm going to tell you. Good. Saves us both."

---

They worked out the rest by lamplight, and it was arithmetic, plain and cold, the kind Oscar could do in his sleep because numbers were the one language that never lied to him.

One ledger surrendered on the tide: every true name, every debt forgiven quietly in the margin, every entry his father had bent or blurred to keep a neighbor whole — delivered straight into Reyne's hands, sorted by men whose trade was sorting people.

One ledger hidden or gone: eleven men against a wall, except the wall would be Vellamare's, and the names would be Ostervik, Brandt, the Kessler girls, old Pieter who owed forty crowns to a man already dead—

"How many names would they take?" Oscar asked.

"Halloway took eleven from six hundred households." Ferro turned the cup slowly on the bench. "Vellamare is smaller. Call it eight. Call it twelve. The arithmetic doesn't care which."

Twelve. Oscar sat with the number. Twelve neighbors, chosen by ink, and the ink would be his — his hand, his craft, his father's trade turned inside out and used as a weapon pointed inward. All his life he had believed the opposite. All his life he had believed that small letters were safe letters, that a man behind a case of type was a man behind a wall.

"There's something else," Ferro said. "The raid tomorrow. Or the day after. Reyne's men are going through the shops looking for pamphlets — seditious printing, that's the word they'll use. When they come here—"

"This shop prints invoices and funeral notices."

"When they come here," Ferro repeated, patient as winter, "they will look in your cellar. And I will be in it."

Oscar looked at him for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Rain had started again somewhere far off, soft against the shutters.

"So I need you gone," Oscar said. "Or I need you useful."

"They're usually the same thing," Ferro said. "In my experience."

---

Maren Ostervik came at dawn, as she always did now, letting herself in through the alley door with bread under her arm and her husband's old oilskin still smelling of tar. She stopped halfway down the stairs when she saw Ferro sitting on the crate by the press.

She looked at him the way one looks at weather that has changed the season.

"Tomas Ferro," she said. "Dead four years, and standing in a print shop."

"Reports of my death were accurate at the time."

"Mm." She set the bread down on the bench, next to the forged ledger, and began unwrapping it with the composure of a woman deciding something. "My husband signed the papers that declared you dead. Did you know that? He laughed about it afterward. Said it was the only kind lie the harbor ever told."

"He was a good man," Ferro said.

"He was a good liar," Maren corrected. "Which is better." She turned to Oscar, and her eyes were sharp and calm at once, the way they were when she corrected a forged date. "How long has he been here?"

"Since half past two."

"And how long before Reyne's men find him?"

Oscar opened his mouth. Ferro answered for him. "A day. Maybe less, if they bring dogs."

"Then we have a day," Maren said, "to make a dead man useful." She pulled a stool to the bench, picked up the pen, and looked at the two of them over it. "Because I've been thinking about the forgery, Oscar. About what Reyne will check. And there are pages in that book — pages your father wrote in anger or in fear — that we cannot afford to let the army read closely. Pages we should have altered already, and haven't, because altering them means knowing exactly which lies will survive inspection."

She laid her hand flat on the ledger.

"And he knows the army's clerks," she said, nodding at Ferro. "Their hands. Their habits. What they look for when they smell a lie. So either he's a liability sleeping in our cellar — or he's the third hand we needed and didn't know it."

Rain on the shutters. Boots in the square, more of them now, counting themselves in the gray light.

Ferro reached out and drew the ledger toward himself, gently, like a man approaching a fire.

"Show me the page," he said, "with my death in it."
