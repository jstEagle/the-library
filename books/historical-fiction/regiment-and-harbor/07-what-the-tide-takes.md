## 7. What the Tide Takes

The requisition ship came in on the morning tide like a debt called in.

Maren Ostervik watched it from the widow's walk of the harbormaster's house, a cup of chicory going cold in her hand. *Vigilant*, the name said on her stern, in letters painted by someone who had never seen the sea do anything but obey. She was a corvette, black-hulled, her gun ports closed for the courtesy of it, and she took up so much of the harbor that the fishing fleet had to moor three deep against the north wall just to make room.

Erik would have hated her. Erik had hated every navy hull that ever crowded his boats, not out of principle but out of arithmetic — a warship at anchor meant nets unfished, crews conscripted, prices at market set by men with muskets. He used to stand on this same walk with his pipe and say, *Maren, the sea feeds everyone until someone decides to own it.*

She drank the chicory anyway. Cold was cold. It went down the same.

Below her, the town was already rearranging itself around the ship's presence. Soldiers on the quay in their gray coats, checking lines of crates. The customs house flying two flags now, royal above civic, as if the smaller one were being slowly smothered. And there — she picked him out without trying — Oscar Nakamura crossing the square toward his shop with a satchel held close to his body, walking the way a man walks when he is carrying something that weighs more than paper.

Maren set down the cup and went downstairs to count her fish barrels.

It was what she did now, since Erik drowned eight months ago off the shallows with two other men and a hold full of herring. Count things. Barrels, nets, coins, days. A widow's walk was for watching; a widow's hands were for lists. Erik had taught her that too, though he hadn't meant to: the winter he'd been sick and she'd run the harbor books herself, entering tonnage and tolls in a hand so like his that the council never noticed the difference. He'd found out after, laughed until he coughed, and then gone quiet and shown her how he made his sevens, how he crossed his fives, how a clerk's hand was a fingerprint and hers needed to be his if anyone ever looked twice.

*Why would anyone look twice?* she'd asked.

*Because someday,* he said, *someone will want to know who owes what. And the person holding the book decides what they find.*

She thought of that every day now.

* * *

Reyne came to the harbor office at midday, unannounced, which was his habit and therefore no longer a surprise, only a weather pattern.

Captain Aldous Reyne occupied the doorway the way well-made furniture occupies a room: correctly, and without apology. His uniform was immaculate. His boots left no salt on her floor because he wiped them, deliberately, where she could see him do it. Everything about the man was consideration turned inside out — you could feel the threat through the politeness the way you felt cold through a window.

"Mrs. Ostervik." He inclined his head. "I'm conducting an inventory of harbor craft."

"Then you'll want my registry." She pulled it from the drawer before he asked. "Every hull, its draft, its owner, its condition. My husband kept it current. I've kept it current."

"I'm sure." He took it, leafed through, and paused — she watched his finger stop on a page the way a hawk's eye stops on a hedgerow. "The *Solveig*. Your husband's boat. Registered to M. Ostervik."

"To me," she said. "Since the drowning. The transfer is entered. You can check the date against the parish record."

"I don't doubt it." He closed the registry and did not give it back. "I'll return this. Mrs. Ostervik, may I ask you something plainly?"

"You've been asking me things plainly since you arrived, Captain. I'd be disappointed in a change."

Something moved behind his eyes — not amusement, exactly. Recognition. "Your late husband served as harbormaster for twenty-two years. In that time he will have known every man in this port, what they owe, what they smuggle, whom they hate. The ledger your council keeps — the civic ledger — records names. Debts. Births." He set his gloves on the desk, one atop the other, precisely. "Books like that are how wars are won without firing shots. I have seen a town emptied by a single volume. So when I am told the ledger needs *three more days* — that pages must be *reconciled* — I begin to wonder what reconciliation means in Vellamare."

"It means clerks are old and slow," Maren said. "It means the book has thirty years of entries and some of them are crossed out and some are in two hands and one of them is in ink that's gone to mold. If you wanted speed, Captain, you should have requisitioned a town that lies about itself."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he collected his gloves, thanked her for the registry, and wiped his boots again on the way out, though there was nothing on them.

She stood at the window until he was gone from the square, and then she let her hands shake, briefly, the way you let a sail luff before trimming it. Then she put on her coat and went to find Oscar Nakamura.

* * *

"He counted the boats," she told him, in the cellar, under the smell of ink and lamp oil. "Not the ledgers. The boats. He took my registry and he stopped at the *Solveig* like it owed him money. He knows a boat is leaving, Oscar. He doesn't know when, or what it carries, but he's narrowed it to water."

Oscar sat among the type cases with a composing stick in his hand and ink to the second knuckle, and he looked, Maren thought, like a man who had been awake for two days and had decided sleep was another country he couldn't get visas for. The true ledger lay open beside him, and next to it the forged one, half-grown, its pages still smelling of fresh size.

"He searched the tanner's row yesterday," Oscar said. "And the church. Ferro heard the patrol talking — Reyne has men going shop to shop starting tomorrow. Doors open, papers read."

"Then we're done buying time."

"We have tonight."

"No." She said it flat, and he looked up. "You have less than that. Listen to me the way you listen to a customer who's wrong about his invoice. Reyne gave you three days. He's already spent his patience. Tomorrow he walks through this town reading whatever he finds, and what he finds cannot be this." She put her palm flat on the true ledger, on thirty years of her neighbors' names. "This goes to sea tonight. Not dawn. Tonight, on the dark tide, before the harbor watch changes."

"The *Solveig* can't clear the point in the dark—"

"My husband cleared that point in the dark forty times. I've crewed her since I was sixteen. The channel markers are still where he left them." She heard her own voice go hard and didn't soften it. Hardness was a tool. It kept the other thing underneath from showing.

Oscar was quiet. Then: "Who sails her?"

"I do."

"No." He said it fast, standing up. "Absolutely not. Reyne knows your boat. He stopped at your line in the register — if the *Solveig* runs the harbor the night the ledger disappears, he won't need proof, he'll need rope."

"And if your boat runs it? What boat, Oscar? Whose hands? Who else do we trust with thirty years of names?" She let that sit. "There's no one. There's you, there's me, there's a deserter in your cellar, and there's a dead man's boat. That's the whole conspiracy."

"Then wait for the morning tide. The fishing fleet goes out at first light — she's one hull among twelve, she's smoke and nets and ordinary, and Reyne can't board twelve boats on suspicion alone—"

"He can board one." Maren took a breath. "But you're right about one thing. It should be the morning fleet. Hidden in plain sight, nets wet, fish stinking, nothing worth a soldier's attention." She looked at the boy — no, not a boy anymore; she kept catching herself making that mistake, and Erik had made it too, right up until the end. "You finish the book tonight. Every page. Every correction. And at first light it goes out on the *Solveig*, buried under herring, and I take her past the point myself, and I hand her to Joren Voss at the offing — he owes Erik his life and he's bound for exile waters anyway. He carries it south. Nobody in Vellamare touches it again."

Oscar stared at her. "You've thought about this."

"I've been counting things for eight months," she said. "Barrels, nets, coins, days. This is just a longer list."

"And you?" His voice changed. "You hand it off and come back — into the harbor, past Reyne, with your boat empty?"

"That's the plan."

"Maren—"

"Don't." She said it quietly, and it landed harder than shouting. She turned away, busied herself with the lamp wick, which did not need doing. When she trusted her voice again she spoke to the flame instead of to him. "Erik kept that harbor for twenty-two years. Every hull that came in, every man he lied for in the margins of his registry, every storm he stood through on that walk because someone's son was overdue. That harbor is the last place he was mine. I'm not leaving it to be governed by a man who wipes his boots where you can see."

She heard Oscar sit back down. The composing stick resumed its small clicking work. After a while he said, "Joren Voss. What do I write in the ledger about tonight?"

Maren almost smiled. "Nothing. Some entries are for the margin only."

* * *

She went home at dusk and did the thing she had been avoiding all week: she opened Erik's sea chest.

His oilskins smelled of him still — tar and salt and the ghost of pipe smoke — and she folded them into a canvas bag along with flares, the good compass, dried cod, a jug of water. She took down the framed chart of the coast that hung over the hearth, the one with his pencil annotations in the margins — *shoal shifts after NE gales*, *keep off the white rock in a swell* — and she rolled it tight and packed it too. Someone in exile waters should have his handwriting on the coast. Someone should benefit from twenty-two years of knowing.

At the bottom of the chest, wrapped in oilcloth, she found what she'd known was there and had not let herself touch: the little notebook he kept in his breast pocket, the private one, with tide tables and debts forgiven and one line about her on the first page, dated the year they married. *M.O. reads my hand better than I do.* She put it in her coat, against her ribs, where it made a small square warmth.

Then she wrote the note. It took four attempts. The final version said only:

*If the Solveig doesn't come back, I chose the cargo. Don't argue with a dead woman. Finish the book. — M.*

She left it on the type cases where he'd find it, and she stood a long moment in the cellar door watching Oscar work, bent over the two books like a priest between altars, lamplight catching the ink on his fingers. Above them, boots crossed the square in pairs. Somewhere a soldier laughed at something. The tide was turning; she could feel it in her teeth, the way sailors' wives always claimed they could, and she had finally become one.

Tomorrow at first light she would carry thirty years of Vellamare out past the point and give it to the sea's keeping. And then she would turn around and sail back into the harbor, empty-handed, past the black hull of the *Vigilant*, and smile at whatever officer boarded her, and answer whatever questions he asked, because that was the price of the cargo, and she had stopped pretending prices weren't real.

She blew out the cellar lamp on her way up, and in the dark she heard Oscar keep working, the small faithful click of metal setting words in order, and she thought: *he doesn't know yet what it costs to stay.* But he would. Staying was the whole inheritance.

Above the cellar, a fist hammered on the shop's street door — three blows, hard, and then a voice calling for the printer by name.

Maren went very still on the stairs.
