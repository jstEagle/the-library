## 8. Three Days, Two Books

The forgery was nearly perfect, which was the problem.

Oscar held the last sheet up to the lamp and compared it against his memory of his father's hand. The old ledger's entries had a slight forward lean, a hurry in them, as though Aldous Nakamura had always been writing against something — closing time, failing eyes, the approach of death. Oscar's forged entries leaned too far. They looked eager. A book that wanted to be believed.

He shaved a hair's width off the italic nib with his graver, blew the metal dust from the type case, and tried again. *Bren Ostrand, cooper. Debt of four crowns, forgiven.* The words settled onto the page with the right reluctance now. He let the ink dry and did not admire it. Admiration was a luxury for men whose work would not be inspected by an officer with a sword at his hip and a suspicion behind his eyes.

Above him, through the floorboards, he could hear the soldiers in the square. Not marching tonight — just talking, boots scraping, someone laughing badly at a joke that wasn't funny. They had been billeted there eleven days. He knew their rhythms now: the change of watch at midnight, the corporal with the cough who walked the perimeter twice before sleeping, the hour around three when even sentries grew honest and slow.

Three days. Reyne had given him three days to deliver the ledger, and tomorrow morning — no. Tonight. Tonight was the last night of those three days, and by the time the tide turned he would have to walk two books out of this cellar and surrender only one of them.

He turned back to the true ledger, hidden beneath the false bottom of the composing stone, and read the entry he'd been avoiding since sundown.

*Tomas Ferro, listed dead, winter of the famine year.*

His father had written it in the same hand as everything else. No flourish, no guilt. Just ink. And somewhere in the hills above Vellamare, the man named in that line had been alive ever since — breathing under a false date, eating bread bought with another man's name. His father had committed murder on paper and called it mercy.

Oscar dipped his pen. In the true ledger, beneath the old entry, he wrote in his own hand: *Alive. Present in Vellamare, this season.* Then he stopped, because the next thing to write was the reason, and the reason was a debt his father could never repay and Oscar had not yet decided how to carry.

*Escorted Aldous Nakamura to prison,* he wrote instead. *Debt outstanding.*

It was not forgiveness. It was accounting. But his father had taught him that all mercy begins as arithmetic — you cannot forgive what you refuse to first record.

* * *

Maren Ostervik came down the cellar stairs at half past one without knocking, because knocking was a habit for people who hadn't spent twenty years married to a harbormaster woken hourly by weather. She carried bread wrapped in cloth and an expression Oscar had learned to distrust, because it preceded either good news or catastrophe and she delivered both the same way.

"You should see the harbor," she said. "The ship's moved. Anchored off the customs pier. That's a ship preparing to load, not a ship waiting."

"The tide—"

"Tides don't matter to Captain Reyne. Tides are for fishermen." She set the bread down and pulled the lamp closer to the forgery, studying it the way she'd once studied charts. "Your father's hand has gotten better since he died."

"That's the idea."

"Reyne will know." She said it flatly, no cruelty in it. "He's been in this square eleven days. He's watched you carry your ledger box to the council hall and back twice. Men like him don't read books, Oscar. They read people carrying them."

"Then I'll carry it like it weighs nothing."

"No." She sat down across from him, and for a moment she looked very tired — a widow's tired, the tiredness of a woman who had buried her husband and then buried the version of herself that waited for anyone to come home. "You'll carry it like it shames you. Like you're handing over something you failed to protect. Officers understand shame. They collect it."

She reached over and turned the forgery toward herself, and with the pen — her pen, a cheap one she kept behind her ear like a carpenter keeps a nail — added a single blot near the margin. A clerk's accident. A human error.

"There," she said. "Now it's been kept carelessly by a man who resents the work. Which is true, so it costs you nothing to show."

* * *

The knock came at four in the morning, and it was not Maren's knock.

Oscar had perhaps ninety seconds between the first boot on the stair and the second, and he used them the way his father had taught him to use every emergency: calmly, and with the tools already in their places. The true ledger went into the cavity beneath the composing stone. The forgery went into its box, tied with the cord Reyne's own quartermaster had supplied. Tomas Ferro, who slept behind the paper stores in a space barely wider than a coffin, made no sound at all — Oscar had learned that Ferro's silence was the most professional thing about him.

Captain Aldous Reyne descended the cellar stairs alone. That was the courtesy of him. Six soldiers remained at the top of the stair, but the captain came down into the lamplight himself, unhurried, his hands clasped behind him like a man visiting a museum.

"Nakamura." He glanced at the press, the cases, the drying sheets. "You work at strange hours."

"The ledger doesn't keep itself."

"No. It keeps us. That's rather the point of it." Reyne picked up one of Oscar's practice sheets — a discarded attempt at Bren Ostrand's forgiven debt — and read it without expression. Then he set it down, precisely where it had been. "There was a raid tonight. On the tannery off Rope Walk. We found pamphlets. Seditious material, printed on stock with a watermark my quartermaster recognized." He looked up. "Nakamura stock."

"I print for half this town," Oscar said. "Half this town prints for itself."

"I'm sure that's true." Reyne moved along the shelves, trailing one finger across the type cases without touching them, a man reading braille he already knew by heart. "I'm also sure that the man who printed those pamphlets is either you or someone who knows you, and I find that I no longer have time to determine which. My orders move with the tide, Nakamura, not with your convenience. The ledger comes aboard tomorrow morning. At the dockside. Before the ebb."

"It was to be three days."

"It was to be many things." For the first time something moved behind the courtesy — not anger, exactly. Something wearier. "Do you know what I was before the army? A schoolmaster. I taught boys their letters. I believed, genuinely, that a man who could write his own name could never be made a stranger to himself." He straightened his cuffs. "The war has corrected several of my beliefs. One more day won't correct yours. Tomorrow. Dockside. And Nakamura—" he paused at the foot of the stairs, "—I will be reading it carefully. Every page. Books outlive officers; it's the only immortality we're offered, so we tend to check the arithmetic."

Then he was gone, and the boots went with him, and Oscar stood in the lamplight with a finished forgery and an unfinished truth.

* * *

Ferro came out of the paper stores when the door was barred again. He moved quietly for a big man, and he crouched by the lamp and looked at the forgery for a long time.

"He knows," Ferro said.

"He suspects."

"In the last town, the officer suspected too. Suspected is enough. They took the ledger, they took the names, and six weeks later they came back for the men whose debts were marked forgiven." Ferro's voice was low and even, the voice of a man reporting weather in a country he no longer lived in. "Forgiven debt means someone vouched for them. Someone local. It's the mercies they hunt, Nakamura. The debts are just paper. The mercies tell them who loves whom."

Oscar looked down at the page. At Bren Ostrand's forgiven four crowns. At the birth entered under the wrong name — Anneli Voss's boy, recorded as her brother, because her husband had run and shame was cheaper than scandal. At Ferro's own death, dated eight years stale. Every small kindness his father had ever committed, arranged now in a column for a firing squad.

"Then I can't leave any of it in," Oscar said slowly. "I scrub the mercies out of the copy. Plain entries. Debts owed, debts paid. Nothing forgiven, nothing hidden—"

"And the true book?"

"The true book keeps everything." He heard his own voice steady in a way that surprised him. "That's what it's for. Somebody has to remember that these people were worth lying for."

Ferro studied him for a moment, and something passed over his face that might have been the closest a deserter could come to hope. "Your father wrote me dead," he said. "Best thing anyone ever did for me."

"Tonight I'm writing you alive," Oscar said. "In the only book that matters. If they burn this shop, if they take me, that page survives. That's the whole trade."

* * *

They worked until the sky behind the high window went from black to iron.

Maren forged. Her hand was better than his for the older entries — she had copied her husband's correspondence for thirty years and knew how a clerk's wrist tires across a long page. Oscar cut new sorts for the names that needed altering, pulling letters from the cases his father had assembled decades ago, each sort cold and small in his fingers, each one a decision. Bren Ostrand's debt became *paid in full*. Anneli Voss's boy became a nephew, then a nephew properly recorded, the lie smoothed over rather than exposed. Ferro stayed dead in the forgery and lived in the truth.

By three o'clock Oscar's eyes were failing him. He worked by touch, setting type blind, trusting the muscle memory of ten thousand hours at the case. By four the exhaustion had passed through pain into something else — a clarity, almost holy, in which every letter mattered equally and nothing existed outside the page in front of him. He understood suddenly why monks had illuminated manuscripts in cold rooms, why his father had died at this desk rather than in a bed. Craft was not resistance. Craft was the only form of prayer the army couldn't requisition.

At quarter past five, Maren set down her pen.

"It's done," she said. "Both of them."

Oscar laid the two ledgers side by side. To look at them, identical — same binding, same water stain on the rear board, same weight in the hand. Only the contents differed. One book full of the town as the army wished to read it: flat, obedient, harmless. The other full of the town as it actually was: indebted, merciful, misnamed, alive.

"One goes to Reyne," Maren said. "One goes to the boat."

"Yes."

"And you stay."

"Yes."

She didn't argue. She had made her own arrangement with absence years ago, and she recognized it in others. She only took the forgery, wrapped it in oilcloth, and held it against her chest the way you hold something you're about to throw into deep water.

"The tide turns at dawn," she said. "And Oscar — Reyne will count the pages. He told you he'd check the arithmetic."

Oscar looked at the true ledger, at the eight extra pages he had added to complete it — eight pages of corrections, of restored mercies, of a life written back over a convenient death.

"Let him count," he said. "This book is supposed to weigh more."

Outside, above the cellar stairs, the boots were changing watch, and somewhere past the point the requisition ship was making ready, and dawn — the hour when all debts come due — was coming up gray over Vellamare.
