## 9. The Morning of Names

I had not slept. The ink was still tacky on the last page when I closed the true ledger at a quarter past four, and my hands shook so badly I had to wrap them in Maren's kitchen cloth to still them before I could bind the forgery.

Two books lay on the workbench in the lamplight, and they were twins. That was the horror of it — that I had made them twins. Same calfskin boards, same brass corners pried from a ledger my father had retired twenty years ago, same three cords stitched through the spine. Inside, the same hands: mine for the older entries, Maren's perfect clerk's script where the original had been her husband's, Ferro's blunt capitals for the years he'd spent as the garrison's record-keeper before he deserted. Ten days of cutting, copying, aging paper with tea smoke and candle soot, breaking the new bindings with a mallet until they cracked like old grief. If you opened either book, it would tell you Vellamare had existed. Only one of them told the truth about who had lived in it.

Maren came down the cellar stairs without knocking. She'd stopped knocking on the fourth day.

"The tide turns at half past six," she said. "The *Petrel* can't wait past seven or Ostervik's cousin loses the fishing grounds."

"Reyne comes at six."

"I know what Reyne said." She set a cup of chicory on the bench beside the ledgers, careful not to spill near the type cases. In ten days she had never once spilled anything near the type cases. "I'm telling you what the tide said."

Above us, boots crossed the square. The patrol had thinned after midnight — Reyne pulling men toward the granary, toward the quay, toward the ship that had sat in our harbor for nine days like a knife left on a table. But not all of them. Somewhere up there a soldier was stamping his feet against the cold, and every stamp fell through the floorboards into my chest.

"You could go with it," Maren said. Not looking at me. Looking at the books. "There's room. There's always been room. I arranged it that way on purpose, in case it came to this."

"It didn't come to this."

"It came to this on the first night, Oscar. We just agreed not to say so." She picked up the forged ledger, weighed it in her hands the way she must once have weighed her husband's charts. "Which one goes?"

I took the true one from her. It was heavier than the forgery by nothing — by an ounce, maybe, by whatever an ounce of names weighs. "This one. You know why."

"I know why." She set the forgery down. "Say it anyway."

"Because if both books burn, one of them should burn empty."

She looked at me then, and something in her face broke open and shut again, quick as a tide over a rock pool. "Your father would be proud of you," she said, "and furious at you, and those are the same expression on that face of yours," and then she went back up the stairs because neither of us could afford the thing that was happening between us, not this morning, not with soldiers in the square counting the minutes of our town like coins.

* * *

At six o'clock exactly, Captain Aldous Reyne arrived at the print shop door with two sergeants and a lantern he did not need, because dawn had come up gray and wide over the harbor, and everything was visible now. Everything was always visible in the end. That was the whole point of ledgers.

He stepped inside and removed his hat, which was courtesy, and looked around the shop, which was inspection.

"Master Nakamura." His voice filled the room the way cold fills a room. "You've kept me waiting nine days."

"The binding required restoration. My father's ledgers are sewn on cords; a broken cord is a lost page."

"And is anything lost?"

"Nothing, Captain."

He smiled. He had been smiling at me for nine days, a smile like a well-made lock. "Then let us see."

I put the forged ledger into his hands.

What happened next lasted eleven minutes. I know because I watched the clock my father kept above the press, its pendulum swinging through the worst eleven minutes of my life while Captain Aldous Reyne stood in my shop and read my town.

He read the way a man reads who has been trained to find the lie inside the truth — not fast, not slow, but *levelly*, turning pages with two fingers, pausing at random as if guided by chance but never once by chance. He paused at the birth entries. He paused at the debt remissions. He lingered on the page where Tomas Ferro's death was recorded, eight years old now, in my father's hand: *lost at sea off the point, body unrecovered.*

"Unrecovered," Reyne said mildly.

"The sea keeps what it takes, Captain."

"Mm. So does the army." He turned another page. Behind him, through the shop's small window, I could see the quay, the masts, the *Petrel* riding low at her moorings with her nets stacked and her sail furled, and Maren standing at her rail in her widow's black like a figurehead that had outlived its ship.

Somewhere under the floor beneath his boots, in the false bottom of the type cabinet, lay the plates for the pamphlets. Somewhere across the harbor, in a fish hold under salted herring, lay nothing at all yet, because the true ledger was in my coat, against my chest, warm as a second heartbeat.

Reyne closed the book.

"Well?" I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect.

"There are four hundred and twelve names of fighting age in this town," he said. "My clerks will want to verify the count against the parish rolls."

"The parish priest burned his rolls in the autumn, Captain. You'll remember. You ordered the hearths inspected after."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Recalculation. A man doing arithmetic and finding the sum correct but the method unfamiliar. "You keep a clean shop, Master Nakamura."

"My father taught me that a ledger is a promise. Every name entered is a promise that someone existed."

"Yes." He tucked the book under his arm. "Officers learn otherwise. Books outlive us all." And then, at the door, he stopped, half-turned, and said — lightly, terribly, the way a man flicks ash from his sleeve — "Give my regards to Widow Ostervik. Her husband kept the finest harbor charts on this coast. It's a pity no one in her family carries on the trade."

The door closed behind him. I stood in my father's shop and listened to my own blood.

* * *

The handover was at the customs house on the quay. Reyne wanted it public — wanted the town to watch its names marched aboard the requisition ship in a captain's grip, wanted the lesson legible. A file of soldiers lined the dock. The townspeople stood back along the warehouse wall in the gray light, hats off, silent, the way people stand at executions and land auctions.

I walked the length of the quay carrying the forgery, and behind me, thirty yards back, walked Ferro.

We had argued about it until the candles died. He insisted on coming. "If it goes wrong," he'd said, "it goes wrong near me. That's the only use I've been to anyone in eight years." I told him Reyne would know his face. He said Reyne had never seen it — Reyne had arrived with the occupation. But the sergeants might. The sergeants had served in the last war, where Ferro had worn the uniform he now despised more than the rope they'd hang him with.

So we went together, and the crowd parted for us the way water parts for something sinking.

At the customs house steps, Reyne received the ledger formally, a sergeant holding the receipt book, quill scratching. Sign here. Date here. Received, one civic ledger, complete. The requisition ship's launch waited at the steps. Across the harbor, the *Petrel* lay quiet, her crew ostensibly mending nets, her sail still furled, Maren at the tiller now instead of the rail, a woman checking the wind with her whole body while appearing to check nothing.

Reyne signed. He handed the ledger to a lieutenant. The lieutenant carried it to the launch.

And that should have been the end of it. That should have been the moment the tide turned, literally, the moment the *Petrel* cast off and slipped away east with four hundred and twelve true names salted under the herring, and I stood on the quay having won a war nobody would ever know was fought.

Then a soldier on the warehouse roof shouted.

"Sail! Sail on the fishing boat — she's making way!"

Every head turned. Every musket turned a half-second later. The *Petrel* had her canvas up, catching the turn of the tide, sliding from her moorings with the smoothness of a woman leaving a room quietly — but not quietly enough, not with a lookout bored and sharp-eyed and a morning this clear.

"Stop that boat!" The sergeant's voice. Boots hammering planks. A whistle shrieking from the requisition ship.

And Tomas Ferro stepped off the customs house steps into the middle of the quay, tore open his coat to show the faded red of a uniform shirt underneath — a deserter's confession, worn like a target — and shouted, in a parade-ground voice that carried across the whole harbor:

"Here's your runaway! Eight years I ran! Come take me!"

It was the bravest and stupidest thing I have ever seen, and it worked, because soldiers are trained to pursue the man in front of them and not the boat behind him. Six of them hit Ferro like a wave hitting a groyne. He went down swinging, laughing — actually laughing, a sound I will hear until I die — and the sergeant screamed orders, and Reyne stood very still on the steps with his eyes moving from the scuffle to the *Petrel* to me, moving, moving, measuring.

The *Petrel* cleared the mole. The wind took her. She grew smaller against the bright water, and Maren did not look back, because looking back is how boats get caught, and she knew it, and I loved her for it with a love that felt exactly like losing her.

They dragged Ferro past us, bloody-mouthed, arms pinned. For one second his eyes found mine. He winked. Then they shoved him toward the requisition ship's launch, toward the deck where Reyne's clerks would enter him in their own book — *one prisoner, taken, rebel sympathies* — the entry that would decide whether he saw a cell in the capital or a wall at dawn.

Reyne came down the steps and stood in front of me. Around us the quay emptied of drama and refilled with silence.

"A deserter," he said. "In my occupied town. For nine days."

"I don't know the man, Captain."

"No," Reyne said softly. "Of course not. Nobody knows anybody in Vellamare. It's remarkable — a whole town of strangers who all owe each other money." He studied my face the way he'd studied the ledger, levelly, hunting the lie inside the truth. The wind came off the harbor and smelled of salt and herring and distance. Out past the mole, the *Petrel* was a smudge now, then less than a smudge, then water.

He could search the shop. He could tear up the floorboards, break the type cases, shake the town until its teeth rattled. But the ledger was signed for, receipted, aboard his ship, and a captain who admitted his prize was false would have to explain how a printer's son had beaten him — and officers do not survive explanations like that. I watched him arrive at the sum. I watched him hate it.

"Books outlive officers, Master Nakamura," he said at last. "Yours may outlive mine. See that it says nothing foolish." He put on his hat. "Go home."

I went home. Through the streets, past the shuttered market, past the granary, up the hill, with the receipt for a false book folded in my pocket and the weight of the true one already gone from my chest — gone east, gone to sea, gone where names could survive even if the town did not.

Behind me, on the requisition ship, they were deciding what to do with Tomas Ferro.

Ahead of me, in my cellar, under the type cases, the pamphlet plates still waited — and I found, walking uphill in the gray morning with my hands finally steady, that I was not finished.
