## 10. Regiment and Harbor

The regiment left on a Tuesday, the way it had arrived: without asking anyone's opinion.

Oscar watched from the shop window as the column formed in the square. Three weeks of occupation had worn grooves into Vellamare — cart ruts in the mud where the supply wagons had turned, boot-polish smears on the fountain's rim, a doorframe on Tanner's Lane scarred where they'd pried it open looking for pamphlets. Now the soldiers folded themselves back into their marching order as if the town were a coat they were shrugging off. Captain Reyne sat his horse at the head of the column and did not look at the print shop. Oscar was almost certain that was deliberate.

Almost.

He counted them out of habit. Two hundred and eleven, give or take the stragglers, minus the four who stayed in the ground behind the chapel — three of fever, one of something else that the army's surgeon had recorded without comment. One more name that would never be entered anywhere now, unless Oscar entered it himself.

*Later,* he thought. *There will be time for later.*

The column reached the harbor road and began to shrink. Maren Ostervik stood at her door with her arms crossed, watching them go the way a woman watches weather break — not with relief exactly, but with the exhausted attention of someone who has learned not to trust a clear sky. When the last of them passed her gate she looked once toward the shop, once away again, and went inside. That was all the conversation they needed. They had said everything worth saying over ten days and two books, and some things were better kept in the body than repeated aloud.

The requisition ship was gone too. It had sailed on the morning tide nine days ago carrying a ledger full of names Oscar had written himself — every debt inflated just enough to be plausible, every birth date shifted by a season, every "disloyal" rendered loyal or dead or moved to a parish the army would need a month to locate. He had forged his father's hand so well that when Reyne leafed through it at the dockside, the captain had smiled the small smile of a man who suspects he is being lied to but respects the craftsmanship.

"Books outlive officers," Reyne had said, closing the cover. It might have been a threat. It might have been a benediction. Oscar still hadn't decided.

And somewhere behind him on that tide, smaller than a gull from shore, the fishing boat had slipped its moorings with the true ledger in its hold — every real debt, every forgiven one, the birth entered under the wrong name and the correction beside it, Tomas Ferro listed dead for six years and alive for six years after. Sailing east, Maren had said, to a cousin in a port whose name Oscar had been made to forget before he ever heard it. The book would outlive the town that produced it. That was the point. That had always been the point.

*

It took him four days to sweep the cellar clean.

Not because there was much to remove. Because there was so much to decide about. The type cases where he'd hidden the original stood open and empty of secrets now, holding nothing but lead. The copying desk still bore the ghost-rings of ink pots. In the corner, folded under sacking, lay the blanket Ferro had slept on during his eleven nights below — eleven nights while soldiers walked the floorboards overhead and Oscar lay awake doing arithmetic with another man's life. Ferro had gone south the day after the handover, walking, carrying bread and a lie about a road that didn't exist. Before leaving he'd stood in the cellar doorway and said, "Your father never hated me either. I could never work out which of you was the bigger fool." Then he'd touched the doorframe the way men touch things they don't know how to bless, and gone up the stairs into the morning.

Oscar washed the floor anyway. Some rituals don't require an audience.

Then he climbed to the shop proper, unlocked the front door for the first time in a month, and let the light in. The press stood where it always stood. The cases stood where they always stood. On the wall above the cutting bench hung his own tools — the knife, the graver, the eyelet punch — each in its painted outline, the way his father had taught him: *a tool missing from its shape is a tool already lost.*

His father's tools were still wrapped in oilcloth in the drawer beneath.

For three years Oscar had kept them there. Not hidden, exactly. Stored. There was a difference he'd been careful not to examine. His father had died owing money to half the town and loyalty to none of it, a man who believed history happened elsewhere — in capitals, in parliaments, in wars with proper names — and who had spent his life recording other people's births and debts as if keeping score for a game he never intended to play. Oscar had inherited the shop, the trade, the ledger, and the belief, all bundled together like a lot at auction, and he had never once thought to refuse any part of it until the army put a price on the bundle.

Now he took the oilcloth out and unwrapped it on the cutting bench.

The tools inside were older than his memory. The knife's handle worn concave by a grip he knew better than his own face. The graver with the hairline crack in the tang that his father had never repaired because — he'd said once, offhand, the only sentence of philosophy he ever offered — *a flaw you can feel teaches you more than perfection you can ignore.*

Oscar cut new outlines on the wall. Measured twice, cut once, the way you did with everything that mattered. Then he hung the knife beside his knife, the graver beside his graver, the punch beside his punch. Four shapes of old wood and steel next to four shapes of new. From across the room they read as eight points of the same sentence.

He stood back and looked at them for a long time.

It wasn't forgiveness. He wasn't sure it was anything as large as acceptance. But it was accurate, and accuracy was the only religion the shop had ever practiced.

*

Maren came on Saturday, bringing bread she pretended was surplus.

"The council met Thursday," she said, setting it on the counter without ceremony. "About the harbor repairs. About the granary accounts. About whether to petition the provincial governor for compensation." She paused. "About the ledger."

"What about it?"

"Whether to commission a new one. Since the old one is, officially, in the possession of His Majesty's forces." She said the words the way the requisition officer had said them, with the same bureaucratic courtesy, and let the irony sit between them like a third person. "They asked who should keep it."

"And?"

"I told them it should be whoever has been keeping it." She looked around the shop — at the press, at the cases, at the eight tools on the wall — and something in her face shifted, the look of a woman confirming a sum she'd already done in her head. "They'll confirm it formally next week. There will be a document. You'll want to enter yourself properly this time. No wrong names. No quiet corrections."

"No," Oscar agreed. "No quiet corrections."

That was the difference, he understood now, between his father's ledger and whatever this next one would be. Not honesty — his father had been honest in his way, honest the way a river is honest, carrying what it carries. The difference was intention. His father had recorded the town because recording was the job. Oscar would record it because the record was the only weapon that had ever actually worked here, and weapons should be maintained.

After Maren left he cleared the big desk, squared a fresh quire of rag paper, and ruled the first page himself — margin lines drawn true with a straightedge, the header letters spaced by eye and checked against the case twice. At the top he wrote:

**CIVIC LEDGER OF THE TOWN OF VELLAMARE — SECOND BOOK**

Beneath it, smaller:

*Kept by O. Nakamura, printer. Begun in the year the regiment came and went.*

He dipped the pen. And then he sat for a moment with the nib hovering, because the first entry of a ledger is also a kind of oath, and he wanted to get it right.

He wrote the four soldiers buried behind the chapel — name, rank, date, cause, in the army's own spelling, corrected only where the army had been careless. Dead men deserved accurate books no matter whose side had killed them. Then the granary accounts, reconstructed from Maren's memory and the baker's, cross-checked against the invoices the occupation had left behind like shed skin. Then the harbor repairs, item by item, with costs, because a town that doesn't know what it lost can't argue for restitution.

Then he turned to a fresh page and began the register of persons, entry by entry, from the parish rolls and his own memory, which had turned out — he discovered with something between pride and grief — to be the finest instrument in the shop.

When he reached the Fs he paused.

Ferro, Tomas. Born [parish unknown], year uncertain. Listed dead, former ledger, in error; correction appended, former ledger, folio 214. Current whereabouts unknown. Status: alive.

He wrote *alive* and felt the word go down through the pen into the page like a nail into good wood.

Outside, the light was going long and gold across the square. Somewhere a hammer was already working — Halvard's boy rebuilding the fence the cavalry had broken, the blows arriving steady as a pulse. Vellamare was knitting itself closed around its absences the way towns do, filling the gaps with new wood and new gossip, forgetting at the speed of convenience.

Let it forget, Oscar thought. Forgetting was a kind of mercy too, and the town had earned its mercies.

But somebody had to remember accurately enough to contradict the forgetting when contradiction became necessary. Somebody had to keep the second copy. Somebody had to be the man who, when the next regiment came — and one would come; regiments always came — could open a book and say: *this is who we were; you will not revise us.*

His father had believed history happened elsewhere.

Oscar set the pen down, looked at the eight tools hanging in their paired shadows on the wall, and understood at last that his father had simply been wrong about the geography. History happened wherever someone wrote it down and refused to let the writing be corrected by force. It had happened in this cellar. It was happening right now, in his own hand, on this page.

He took up the pen again and worked until dark, and lit the lamp, and worked past dark, entering the town into the book one true name at a time — and when he finally blew out the lamp near midnight, he noticed, pinned to the inside of the front door where it must have been slipped sometime during the evening, a single sheet of paper printed in a hand he almost recognized.

A notice. A call. Something beginning, *To the printers of the coast towns—*

He held it up to the lamplight and began to read.
