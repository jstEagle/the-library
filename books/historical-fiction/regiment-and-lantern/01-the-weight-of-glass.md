## 1. The Weight of Glass

The first thing Rosalind Zhou had ever learned about a lighthouse was that it was mostly cleaning. Not the romance the mainlanders imagined — no wreaths of storm-spray, no dramatic rescues, no lonely souls staring nobly at horizons. Just glass. Forty-eight panes in the lantern room, each one to be wiped down twice weekly with vinegar and newspaper because Father had sworn by it and Grandfather before him, and heaven help the keeper who tried to substitute a proper commercial cleaner. "It streaks," Father had said, with the gravity of a man discussing treason. "Streaks cost lives."

Rosalind was thirty-four years old and could still hear him saying it every time the vinegar stung a hangnail.

They worked clockwise, always clockwise — pane one through pane forty-eight, then the brass fittings, then the lens itself, which was not glass at all but a ringed cathedral of it, a first-order Fresnel assembled in France before anyone alive had been born. You did not wipe a Fresnel. You breathed on it, gently, and coaxed it with a chamois, and you apologized to it under your breath for the indignity, because Rosalind had decided somewhere around age twelve that the lens deserved courtesy. It was the only honest thing on this coast. It asked for nothing but attention and gave back a beam that had, so far as the logs recorded, never once failed.

*Oct 14. Wind SW 15 kt, backing S by evening. Sea moderate. Barometer steady at 1012. Lamp trimmed, lens polished, all panes clean.*

Rosalind wrote it in the logbook with the fountain pen Mother kept refilling out of stubbornness, blotted it, closed the book, and stood for a moment listening to the house below. Gullwatch Point station was less a lighthouse than an argument between a tower and a cottage that had lost: two rooms and a kitchen huddled against the tower's base like a boat gone aground, plus a cellar that flooded when the moon felt ambitious. In the kitchen, Mother was doing what Mother did every morning at this hour — arguing with a radio that only received half of anything.

"—and I'm telling you, Mei, they cut the shipping forecast again, third time this month—"

"Then listen to the half we get," Mother said, "and imagine the rest. You're good at imagining."

"I am *not* good at imagining. That is precisely my complaint."

This was the liturgy. Every morning, the same exchange, word for word, and if either of them deviated even slightly the whole day sat wrong, like a picture hung a finger's width off true. Rosalind came down the spiral stairs, took over the kettle before it whistled, and kissed the top of Mother's head, which smelled of camphor and the eucalyptus oil she rubbed into her wrists against the damp.

"Anything from the world?" Rosalind asked.

"The world has been cut for budgetary reasons," Mother said darkly. "Also there's weather coming. The man said 'a system.' They always say 'system' now instead of just saying *storm*, as though naming it politely will keep it off the doorstep."

"Everything's a system now." Rosalind set out three cups out of habit, caught themself, and put one back. Edmund wasn't due until the supply boat brought him — if the supply boat brought him; eleven years of silence made a person's arrival more rumor than plan — and putting out his cup daily was either hope or superstition or spite, depending on the morning. Today it felt like arithmetic. Three cups meant three people, and three people meant the question Rosalind had been not-asking since the letter came in September.

The kettle sang. Outside, the sea did its patient gray work against the rocks.

* * *

The supply boat *Cormorant* came Thursdays, weather permitting, and this Thursday she came wallowing in behind the point with her paint peeling and her skipper, Denny Okafor, wearing the expression of a man delivering news he'd rehearsed badly. Rosalind met him at the jetty with the hand truck for crates, and noticed immediately that he wouldn't quite meet their eyes.

"You've got mail," he said. "Official-looking."

"Everything official-looking is bad-looking. That's how you know it's official."

He laughed, but it came out sideways. He handed over a fat envelope wrapped in plastic against the spray, stamped with the Coastal Authority crest — the gull over the wave, drawn by someone who had clearly never seen either — and while Rosalind turned it over, Denny busied himself with the crates in a way that suggested he wanted to be anywhere else, including underwater.

Inside: a letter, a pamphlet, and a form.

The letter was two paragraphs long and written in the tone of a dentist confirming an appointment. *Dear Keeper,* it began, though Rosalind was not technically the keeper anymore, having been reclassified eighteen months ago as a "site attendant" in a cost-saving exercise that had also reclassified the foghorn as "non-critical infrastructure." *In accordance with Phase Two of the Regional Consolidation Programme, residents of Gullwatch Point and adjacent holdings are advised that seasonal evacuation arrangements are now in preparation. Please find enclosed the relevant schedule and declaration of household requirements. No immediate action is required.*

*No immediate action is required.* Rosalind read that sentence four times. It had the specific chill of bureaucratic language — the phrase meant *we have already decided everything and are merely informing your furniture.*

The pamphlet was worse. Glossy, cheerful, full of photographs of smiling families on the mainland holding keys. *Your New Home Awaits!* it announced, in a font that bounced. There was a section titled "What Can I Bring?" with helpful icons: a suitcase, a cat, a photograph frame. There was no icon for a Fresnel lens. There was no icon for forty-eight panes of glass, or a tower, or a father buried up on the headland under a stone that read *HE KEPT THE LIGHT*, or a mother's garden, or eleven years of ledgers in a cabinet, or the particular way the kitchen smelled on baking days, which was most days.

"What is it?" Denny asked, unable to help himself.

"Evacuation notice." Rosalind held it up. "Casually worded."

"Ah." He looked at the sea. "They gave the same to Halfway Rock last month. And to the Terns." The Terns being the family on the outer skerries who had kept goats and grudges in equal measure. "Whole coast's going quiet, Roz. By spring there'll be nothing burning between here and the harbor light but gulls and regret."

"Somebody has to be the regret."

Denny grinned despite himself, then sobered. "You'll come across, though? When the bridge closes?" He said it lightly, but Rosalind heard the real question underneath, the one everyone on the mainland asked now: *are you staying or going*, as if a person were a tide table.

"That depends on the berth count," Rosalind said, and folded the letter away, and smiled, and changed the subject to diesel and flour and whether Mrs. Adeyemi at the chandlery still stocked the good chamois, because some conversations you can only have once you've had time to be frightened in private.

* * *

That evening Rosalind did something they hadn't done in years. They took a notebook — a fresh one, not the log, the log was for truth about weather and this was for truth about a different kind — and sat at the kitchen table after Mother went up to bed, and began to make lists.

Column one: **What can be carried.**

Mother's medicines, obviously. The good blanket, the green one from Grandmother Zhou. Photographs — but which photographs? The albums were heavy as ballast stones; you couldn't take all of them, so you took pages, and choosing pages meant deciding which years mattered, which was its own cruelty. The ledgers, all of them, every volume back to 1903. That was non-negotiable. If the house went, the ledgers were the house. Father's sextant, though it hadn't been used seriously since Rosalind was sixteen and he'd caught them taking a noon sight off the gallery rail and instead of scolding had simply corrected their grip, saying *you hold it like you mean it or don't hold it at all*. The tea caddy. Mother's sewing box. One lamp. Two changes of clothes.

Column one filled a page and a half and weighed, Rosalind estimated, rather less than the lens alone.

Column two: **What cannot.**

The tower. Obviously. But also: the marks on the cellar doorframe where Father had measured Rosalind's height every birthday, pencil lines marching upward like rungs, stopping abruptly at fourteen when measuring stopped mattering to either of them. Also the doorframe itself. Also the smell of the place, which was salt and paraffin and old bread and could not be boxed. Also the view from the gallery at dawn, which Rosalind had watched roughly four thousand times and would happily watch four thousand more, and which no photograph had ever survived contact with the original. Also the graves on the headland, which would stay regardless, because stone does not evacuate, and someone should mow around them in summer, and no one would.

Also, Rosalind wrote, and then stopped, pen hovering.

Also the deed.

It lived in the strongbox in the cellar, in Father's handwriting, in Father's lawyer's officious language, and it said what it said. Rosalind didn't open the column-two entry further. Some things you list without reading, the way you log a storm by its pressure without looking directly into it.

The fire ticked. Upstairs, floorboards announced Mother turning over. Out on the water, the buoy bell rang its slow bronze note, three seconds on, seven seconds off, the coast's oldest clock.

And here was the thing Rosalind noticed, writing those columns, the thing that sat in the chest like swallowed ballast: the notice had used the word *household*. Household requirements. Declaration of household. As if a household were a simple unit, countable, divisible by boats. But a household was three people who had not been three people for eleven years, becoming three people again in six weeks' time, and the pamphlet's helpful icons — suitcase, cat, photograph frame — did not include any symbol for *one berth short*, because no committee had ever had to draw one.

Edmund was coming on the next *Cormorant*. Mother had his letter pinned to the corkboard by the stove, his careful handwriting announcing, after a decade of nothing, *I'll come help with the move, it seems sensible, we should discuss the property matters face to face.* Property matters. Eleven years away and he opened with property matters, and Mother had read that letter aloud four times with her chin lifted as if it contained warmth, and Rosalind had nodded along, and neither of them had said what they both knew, which was that *property matters* meant the deed, and the deed meant the cellar, and the cellar meant everything Father had signed and everything Rosalind had done since.

Rosalind capped the pen. Closed the notebook. Carried the lamp to the stairs and began the climb, ninety-one steps, counting them as always, past the watch room with its charts curling at the corners, up into the lantern room where the great lens waited in the dark like a patient queen.

They lit the wick, wound the clockwork, and watched the beam swing out over the black water — sweep, sweep, sweep — touching nothing, promising everything, keeping its appointment with ships that might or might not be out there.

"Forty-eight panes," Rosalind told it quietly. "I counted today. All present."

The light turned. Below, the notebook lay shut on the table, its columns waiting, and outside the barometer — though Rosalind couldn't see it from here — had begun, very slowly, to fall.
