## 2. Orders Come at Low Tide

The notice arrived on the Tuesday supply boat, folded inside a copy of the *Coastal Gazette* that was three weeks old and smelled of diesel and someone else's lunch. Rosalind found it while looking for the tide tables, which is to say they found it by accident, which is how all the important things in their life had arrived: sideways, damp, and inconveniently timed.

*NOTICE OF SEASONAL BRIDGE CLOSURE,* it said, in a font that suggested the matter had been discussed in a meeting where nobody raised their voice. *Effective the first of December, the Gullwatch Causeway Bridge will close for scheduled winter maintenance. The final guaranteed crossing will depart from the east landing at 0800 hours on 28 November. Passengers are advised that berths aboard the relief vessel are limited to two (2) per household. Early booking is strongly encouraged.*

Rosalind read it twice. Then a third time, because the phrase *two per household* kept rearranging itself in their mind like furniture in a room that was too small.

They were three people. The arithmetic did not care about anyone's feelings.

Outside, the light turned its slow patient circle, as it had done every eight seconds for sixty-one years, indifferent to paperwork. Rosalind set the notice down on the kitchen table, weighted it with the salt cellar, and went back up the tower to log the barometer, because the barometer did not care about anyone's feelings either, but at least it was honest about falling.

* * *

By evening, the whole household knew. This was not because Rosalind told them so much as because Mei Zhou had the instincts of a customs officer and had gone through the mail before Rosalind finished winding the clock.

"Two berths," Mei said, when Rosalind came down. She was sitting in her chair by the stove with her feet up — doctor's orders, though she treated the orders as suggestions from a promising but inexperienced junior colleague — and the notice lay open on her lap like something she'd already cross-examined. "Two. For three of us."

"The bridge authority's arithmetic has always been creative," Rosalind said. "Remember when they billed us for two lamps because the tower casts one reflection?"

"That was forty years ago."

"They're consistent."

Mei smiled at that, briefly, and then stopped smiling, which was worse. She looked out the window toward the water, where the last of the daylight was going gray and apologetic. "Edmund should have one berth," she said.

And there it was. The first stone laid down on a path nobody wanted to walk.

"And you'd take the other," Rosalind said carefully.

"I'm saying nothing yet." Mei smoothed the notice flat with both hands, an old habit from her shopkeeping days, when smoothing a thing flat had been the first step to deciding what it was worth. "I'm saying Edmund has been away eleven years and he came back, which counts for something. I'm saying I am seventy-four years old and stubborn as a barnacle and this house has held me together through worse than weather."

"You fell in October."

"I sat down quickly in October," Mei corrected. "There is a difference, and your brother will confirm it, since he witnessed it and made a great deal of noise."

"He made a great deal of noise because he thought you'd broken your hip."

"He thought I'd broken my hip because he panics beautifully. It's his best quality." She said it with real fondness, and Rosalind felt the strange small ache of hearing their mother be tender about Edmund — eleven years of silence, one storm-season return, and already she defended him like he'd never left. Perhaps that was simply what mothers did. Perhaps love kept a ledger of its own, with different columns than anyone else's.

"Mother," Rosalind said, and then didn't know how to finish the sentence, so they finished it with the kettle instead, filling it and setting it on the ring, buying time in units of boiling water.

Mei watched them do it. She had watched Rosalind buy time in units of chores for forty years; she could probably price it.

"We'll discuss it properly tomorrow," she said at last. "When your head is cool and mine is too."

"My head is perfectly cool."

"It's steaming," said Mei. "I can see it from here."

* * *

The trouble, Rosalind reflected later, hauling the evening lamp oil up the tower stairs, was that everyone in this house had spent decades perfecting the art of not saying the true thing aloud, and now the true thing had arrived in government print and there was no polishing over it.

The true thing was simple: three people, one household, two berths, and a closing date. Someone would stay. And every one of them had privately decided, without consultation, that the person who stayed would be themselves — and each of them believed, with equal private certainty, that they alone were being reasonable about it.

Rosalind knew they were reasonable about it. They were the keeper. The light required a keeper; the evacuation order had explicitly exempted essential personnel, a clause the authority had included with the enthusiasm of people who had discovered a way to keep a lighthouse running without paying anyone extra. Rosalind staying wasn't sacrifice. It was a line item.

Except that it wasn't, and Rosalind knew that too, somewhere under the competence, in the place where they kept the older, less tidy truths. The truth that they had stayed on this coast not only for the light but because leaving would have meant admitting what the deed had already decided — that the house, the land, the future belonged to Edmund, sole heir, and Rosalind had merely been keeping the premises warm for him across eleven years of silence. Staying had felt like devotion. Some nights, in honest hours, it had also felt like refusing to hand over the keys.

Now the government was going to decide who handed over what, and Rosalind found they hated being told almost as much as they hated choosing.

At the top of the stairs, the great lens waited in its housing, glass upon glass, catching even the dim stair-light and returning it doubled, trebled, made enormous. Rosalind polished the brass fittings out of habit more than need. There was comfort in the work. Glass did not lie about what it was. You gave it a flame and it gave the sea a promise, and no deed or berth allocation could alter the terms.

From below came the sound of the kitchen door, boots on the flagstones, and Edmund's voice — still carrying mainland vowels after four months home, like a coat he hadn't quite taken off. He was talking to their mother, low and cheerful, and Rosalind caught the word *booking* drift up the stairwell, followed by a pause of exactly the wrong length.

Rosalind stood very still beside the lens.

So. Edmund had read the notice. Edmund, who had come back ostensibly to help and actually — everyone knew, nobody said — to establish his claim before the coast was condemned and the compensation letters started arriving. Edmund, who had been surprisingly good about the flooding and the firewood and their mother's medications, and who had never once, in four months, mentioned the deed out loud, which was its own kind of mention.

He wanted a berth. Of course he did. And he deserved one, and their mother deserved one, and the mathematics of the thing were pitiless and printed in bold.

*Two per household.*

Downstairs, the kettle whistled. Rosalind finished the polish, logged the weather — wind southwest, freshening, pressure dropping the way everything in this family dropped, gradually and then all at once — and went down to lie convincingly about being fine.

* * *

Supper was fish stew, which Mei made when she wanted everyone obedient, and it worked as it always worked. Edmund ladled. Rosalind baked the bread because Mei's hands ached in cold weather, and if anyone noticed that the keeper of Gullwatch Point had become very good at small acts of service performed silently and without acknowledgment — well, that was rather the point of them.

"So," Edmund said, on his second bowl, in the tone of a man opening negotiations he hoped would feel casual. "The twenty-eighth."

"Mm," said Mei.

"Two berths." He glanced between them, quick as a man checking exits. "I've been thinking—"

"Don't think," Mei said. "Eat. Thinking on an empty stomach is how families ruin themselves."

Edmund ate. But Rosalind saw his jaw working on something that wasn't fish, and after a moment he set the spoon down anyway, because he had never once in his life obeyed their mother on the first instruction, and said: "Whoever stays, it can't be you, Mum."

"Can't it?" said Mei, pleasantly, in the voice she used before winning arguments.

"You fell in October."

"I *sat down quickly* in October—"

"And there's ice coming, and the road down to the landing, and the generator fuel run — Mum, honestly—" He turned to Rosalind, appealing, and for one unguarded second his face was fifteen years old again, asking Rosalind to fix something, the way he always had. "Tell her."

Rosalind looked at their mother. Their mother looked back, and behind the iron of her expression was something Rosalind had rarely been permitted to see: fear, worn thin, like paint on the seaward rail.

"I think," Rosalind said slowly, "that we should write to the authority tonight. Ask whether the berth count is fixed or whether exceptions are possible. Essential personnel, medical grounds, that sort of thing."

"Bureaucrats love exceptions the way gulls love umbrellas," said Edmund.

"Then we'll ask anyway. In writing. With Mother's medical records attached." Rosalind kept their voice level, keeper's voice, the one that could be heard over engines. "And until we have an answer, nobody decides anything."

It was a coward's peace and everyone recognized it as such, and everyone accepted it gratefully, because a coward's peace was still peace, and there was stew left.

Later, washing dishes while Edmund walked their mother to her chair, Rosalind dried the same bowl three times and admitted the thing they'd been not-admitting since the notice arrived:

If the answer came back *no exceptions*, Rosalind would give up the berth. Not out of nobility — or not only — but because the alternative was watching the light go dark, and some inheritances you don't receive. You refuse to abandon them, and that refusal becomes yours.

But their mother had looked at Rosalind over supper with that thin-worn fear, and had said, quietly, in the space where only Rosalind could hear it:

"Take the boat, child. Promise me nothing. Just take the boat."

And Rosalind, drying the bowl a fourth time, had said the family's oldest and most fluent sentence:

"We'll see."
