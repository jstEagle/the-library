## 3. What Edmund Carried Back

The ferryman charged Edmund forty dollars and a look of contempt, which he suspected was the going rate for anyone arriving at Gullwatch Point after an absence of eleven years.

"That's the Zhou boy," the ferryman had said to no one in particular, untying the line. "The one who left."

Edmund had considered correcting him — he was thirty-four, not a boy; he hadn't left so much as relocated; there was a difference, legally speaking — but the wind took his breath before he could arrange the words, so he simply nodded like a man agreeing to terms he hadn't read.

The crossing took twenty minutes. The mainland receded behind them in a gray smear, and ahead the point rose out of the sea like something that had been waiting for him specifically and was not pleased about it. The lighthouse stood at its crown, white paint gone the color of old teeth, and beside it the house with its blue door that their father had repainted every spring until the year he couldn't, after which Rosalind had evidently taken up the tradition without breaking stride.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed, and it bothered him more than he expected: the house looked *kept*. He had prepared himself for decay. He had, if he was honest, counted on a certain amount of it. There was a version of this homecoming he'd rehearsed on the train — the sagging porch, the salt-ruined shutters, Rosalind worn thin and defensive, grateful for help. In that version, his arrival looked like rescue. In that version, the deed folded in his breast pocket was practically a mercy.

Instead the porch boards were sound under his boots. The hinge on the gate swung without complaint, freshly oiled. Someone had staked the rosemary against the winter wind with twine tied in neat, deliberate knots.

* * *

Rosalind opened the door before he knocked, which meant they'd seen the boat from the tower, which meant Edmund's grand entrance had been observed and catalogued from four hundred feet away.

"You're thinner," Rosalind said, by way of hello.

"You're grayer."

"I'm thirty-six."

"Gray at thirty-six is a choice, Roz."

"It's a hereditary condition. You'll find out." They stepped back to let him in, and there it was — that flat, patient look Edmund remembered from childhood, the one that made him feel like a specimen someone else had already finished studying.

Inside, the house smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and, faintly, of ginger, because their mother was cooking, because their mother cooked the way other people breathed, and eleven years apparently hadn't changed that either. Everything was exactly where memory said it would be and simultaneously better than memory claimed. The banister gleamed where a hand had polished it ten thousand times. The clock in the hall kept time. Someone had re-glazed the window over the stairs.

"Mom's in the kitchen," Rosalind said. "She made too much food. She always makes too much food when she's nervous, so you should eat quickly before she starts a second batch."

"Is she all right?"

"She's fine. She's angry with you, but she's fine. Those aren't mutually exclusive in this house."

* * *

Mei Zhou hit him at the kitchen door at approximately the speed of grief. She held his face in both hands, turned it left, turned it right, pronounced him too thin, fed him within ninety seconds of arrival, and did not once mention the eleven years, which was somehow worse than if she had screamed. Edmund ate three bowls of noodles and understood that he was being forgiven provisionally, the way a bank extends credit, and that the full review would come later, probably at night, probably over tea, probably in Cantonese so he'd have to work for it.

Rosalind ate standing up, one eye on the window, and excused themselves to check the weather barometer twice in twenty minutes.

"Does it do anything interesting?" Edmund asked when they came back the second time.

"The barometer?"

"You keep looking at it like it owes you money."

"Falling," Rosalind said. "Fast. We'll get weather tonight or tomorrow." And then, because they were still Rosalind: "There's soup if you want more. Mom will be hurt if you don't have more. It's not real forgiveness until you've had seconds."

* * *

He found the ledgers that first evening, though "found" was generous — they lived on the study shelf in plain sight, fourteen of them, cloth-bound, spines labeled by year in a small precise hand. He told himself he was looking for the station's maintenance records. That was the official story he'd constructed somewhere around the third bowl of noodles: a man reviewing documents, gathering information, being practical. Practical men did not feel things. Practical men certainly did not stand in their childhood study at nine o'clock at night running a thumb down columns of figures with their throat tight.

Because the ledgers were immaculate. Not tidy — *immaculate*. Fuel consumption logged daily. Lamp hours. Lens wear. Every storm noted, every repair dated and costed, every supply order placed and received and reconciled. Fourteen years of it, and every page in the same steady hand, and the hand was Rosalind's.

He did the arithmetic standing there, the way you can't stop yourself doing arithmetic. Fourteen years. Their father had died thirteen years ago, which meant the first ledger predated the funeral. Rosalind had been twenty-two. Twenty-two years old, signing their name at the bottom of a page like a person who intended to be held to it, and then holding to it, alone, for longer than Edmund had been gone.

In the margins — this was what undid him — in the margins were notes. Small ones, domestic, unbusinesslike. *Mother's cough worse in November — ordered extra coal.* *Fixed the gate hinge, used Father's oil recipe, worked.* *E. sent postcard. No reply yet.* Dated. Filed between fuel tonnage and lens inventory as though a brother's silence were simply another line item to track.

He touched that last note longer than he meant to.

"You're reading my margins."

Rosalind stood in the doorway with a hurricane lamp, and the light threw their shadow long across the floorboards. They didn't look angry. They looked like a person confirming a fact they'd already recorded elsewhere.

"These are remarkable," Edmund said, and hated how it sounded — like a man appraising livestock.

"They're a job." Rosalind set the lamp on the desk. "You're welcome to look. It's all there. Every year. Some people find it boring."

"Some people are wrong."

"Mm." A pause. Outside, the wind had begun testing the shutters, one soft push, then another, like a burglar checking locks. "The deed's in the cellar, if that's what you came for. Bottom of Father's strongbox. I moved it there after the roof leak in '09. It's dry now. I fixed the leak."

Each sentence arrived with its own small hammer. *I fixed the leak. It's dry now.* Eleven years of repairs delivered as casual inventory, none of it offered as accusation, all of it landing as one.

"I came to help," Edmund said. "With the evacuation. With Mom. Whatever needs doing."

"I know," Rosalind said. "You said that in your letter."

And then they picked up the lamp and left him alone with fourteen years of his own absence, filed chronologically.

* * *

The storm arrived at two in the morning, the way storms do — all at once, having pretended all evening to be merely considering it. Edmund lay awake in his childhood bed listening to the house take hits and give ground and hold, and around three he gave up on sleep and went downstairs for water and found Rosalind already dressed, already at the door with oilskins over one arm.

"There's a loose shutter on the north face," Rosalind said. Not a request. An assignment. "If we don't secure it, it takes the window, and if it takes the window, we lose the parlor floor to rain by dawn. Hold the ladder. Don't argue about whether you're staying — you're here, so you're carrying the other end of something. That's how it works in this house."

So Edmund stood at the bottom of a ladder in a gale, hauling on rope in the dark while his sibling — his brother? his sister? Rosalind had never made it simple and Edmund had stopped needing it to be, which was maybe the only uncomplicated thing between them — worked above him with cold hands and total authority, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the train began, very slowly, to loosen.

Not enough. Not enough to change what was in his pocket.

Because here was the thing he circled all through the next day, through the cleanup, through lunch, through his mother watching him with those patient black eyes: the deed named him sole heir. Their father's signature, notarized, iron-clad, drawn up in the year Edmund left — drawn up, he now admitted in the honest hour of three a.m., *because* Edmund left, a bribe disguised as a blessing, an anchor thrown to the child swimming away. And Rosalind knew. Of course Rosalind knew; Rosalind read everything in that house, filed everything, reconciled everything. Rosalind had known for thirteen years and had never once raised the subject, had simply gone on keeping the light and logging the coal and noting *no reply yet* in the margins, and that knowledge — that Rosalind knew, and stayed anyway, and never asked — was a weight Edmund found he could carry nowhere except directly in front of his chest, folded in a pocket, next to his heart.

After dinner on the second night, Mei pressed a cup of tea into his hands and said, quietly, in English, so there could be no pretending later: "Your sister has not slept properly in a month. Since the notices came." She smoothed his sleeve, an old gesture, automatic. "Whatever you carried back across that water, Edmund — put it down while the house still stands. Things get decided in storms. Your father knew that. It's why he wrote what he wrote when he wrote it."

The barometer needle, Edmund noticed, was still falling.
