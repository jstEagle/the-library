## 4. The Ledger and the Deed

*Rosalind*

The barometer had been falling since Tuesday, the way a man falls down stairs — slowly at first, then all at once with a sound like furniture breaking.

By Thursday morning Rosalind had logged it three times an hour and stopped pretending to be calm about it. The needle sat at 28.6 inches and trembled there like something small and frightened. Out past Gullwatch Point the sea had gone the color of unpolished pewter, and the swell was coming in long and wrong, the way swells come in before they mean business.

"Storm glass says misery," Rosalind announced to the kitchen generally, tapping the little brass instrument on the mantel. The camphor mixture inside had gone cloudy and feathery overnight — crystals standing up in it like dead grass.

Mei looked up from her tea. "Your father's said the same thing for forty years. Storm glass says misery, barometer says misery, and then he'd go out and check the moorings anyway."

"And then he'd go out and check the moorings anyway," Rosalind agreed. Some sentences in this house were load-bearing. You didn't remove them; you just kept living under them.

Edmund was at the window with his coffee, watching the water. Eleven years away and he still stood like their father when he watched weather — feet planted, weight forward, as though the sea might be reasoned with if you confronted it properly. He'd been back nine days now. Nine days of careful politeness, of Edmund saying *I'll take that* whenever Rosalind lifted anything heavier than a teacup, of both of them orbiting the subject of the deed like two boats avoiding the same rock.

"The radio says it makes landfall tonight," Edmund said. "They're calling it a bomb cyclone now. Very dramatic word."

"They have to call it something," Rosalind said. "It doesn't care what we call it."

The work of preparation filled the day and was almost a mercy for it. Shutters latched. The boat winched up to the high cradle and double-lashed. Oil topped off, batteries checked, the spare lamp glass wrapped in wool and put high on a shelf where floodwater couldn't reach it — because whatever else happened, whatever the sea took, the light would burn tomorrow night. That was not negotiable. That had never been negotiable.

It was Mei who sent them down to the cellar.

"We'll lose power by midnight," she said, in the tone that ended discussions before they began. "Take the ledgers down. All of them. And the strongbox." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that Rosalind couldn't read. "Everything in the strongbox."

Rosalind hadn't opened the strongbox since Father died. It sat on its shelf like a sleeping animal everyone agreed not to wake. But there was no time for agreement now; the wind was already worrying at the eaves like a dog at a door.

* * *

*Edmund*

The cellar smelled the way it had always smelled: cold stone, lamp oil, brine soaked so deep into the walls that no amount of lime would ever get it out. Edmund came down the stairs behind his sister with an armload of logbooks and felt eleven years fall away from him all at once. He'd spent half his childhood down here, doing exactly this, hauling records out of the tide's reach while their father's voice counted him through the dark: *Careful with those, boy. Paper is memory, and memory is the only thing the sea can't take unless you let it.*

He'd forgotten that voice. Standing in the cellar with the storm beginning overhead, he got it back entire.

Rosalind set the ledgers on the dry shelf above the old flood line and started arranging them by year, spines out, with the automatic precision of someone who'd done it many times. Edmund watched. There were more of them than he remembered. Far more.

"How far back do those go?"

"Nineteen years," Rosalind said, without turning around. "Since I was sixteen. Complete run. Weather logs, oil consumption, lens maintenance, vessel sightings, supply inventories." A pause. "There are duplicates of everything in the tower office. Fire protocol."

Nineteen years. Edmund did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do for nine days. Nineteen years of winters. Nineteen years of nights like the one assembling itself over their heads right now. And every page of it, evidently, in one hand.

He pulled a volume at random — 2014 — and opened it under the hanging lantern. The handwriting was small and exact, each entry ruled off neatly, margins annotated in a different ink where corrections or observations had been added later. *Barometer erratic. Checked moorings twice.* In another margin, in pencil, faded: *Mother's medicine ran short — stretched supply until the 14th, boat late.*

"You wrote all of these," he said. It wasn't a question, but it came out like one.

"Somebody had to."

The words weren't sharp. That was the worst of it. They were simply true, laid down flat as an entry in a ledger, and they went into Edmund like a splinter he hadn't known was there.

He'd spent eleven years telling himself a story. It had been a comfortable story, well-furnished, the kind of story you could live in: that the station had carried on fine without him, that Rosalind had *chosen* to stay, that staying was its own reward for people of that temperament, and that his own leaving had cost no one anything because nothing here had ever needed him. The story had let him sleep in city apartments and sign contracts in good conscience.

The ledgers were nineteen volumes of evidence against it.

The wind chose that moment to arrive in earnest. The house overhead groaned — a long, structural complaint, timbers flexing — and somewhere above them a shutter broke loose and began banging with the rhythm of a drum. The lantern light swung on its hook. Then the lights went out altogether, and the cellar went black except for the flame, and Mei's voice came thin through the floorboards: *"Down there? Are you all right?"*

"All right!" Rosalind called back. "Stay away from the windows!"

And then the sea hit the point, and even down in the stone belly of the house they heard it — a boom like a door slammed by God — and spray came sighing up through the old ventilator grate in a fine cold mist.

* * *

*Rosalind*

They rode out the first hour of it sitting on upturned crates with the lantern between them, shouting conversation over the storm the way you shout across a river. Edmund told a story about a ferry in Hong Kong harbor that Rosalind suspected was mostly invented, and it was almost — *almost* — like being children again, hiding from weather in the one room that never frightened them.

Then the crate Rosalind was sitting on shifted, and the old board beneath it cracked, and the corner of the shelf above sagged, and the strongbox — dislodged by the shudder of the house — slid forward with a scrape of iron and dropped its contents across the floor.

Papers. A bundle of letters tied in string. And a document, heavy stock, folded in thirds, that skated across the stones and fetched up against Edmund's boot.

He picked it up. Unfolded it. And Rosalind watched his face do something complicated in the lantern light.

"I thought this was lost," Edmund said quietly.

"So did I."

Their father's deed. The original — not the fair copy kept in the county records but the working draft, the one drawn up the winter before he died, with the surveyor's annotations still in the margins and the ink gone brown at the folds. Edmund read it aloud, or began to. His voice made it halfway down the second paragraph before it failed him.

"*...to be conveyed wholly and indivisibly to his son, Edmund Zhou,*" he read, "*in consideration of his succession to the keeping of—*"

He stopped. Looked up.

"Wholly," he said. "Not shared. Wholly. To me."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "I know. Mother signed it. I watched her sign it. I was seventeen."

"Why—" Edmund's voice cracked and he steadied it. "Why didn't you say anything? Ever? Nineteen years, Rosalind, you never once—"

"Say what?" And here Rosalind found, to their own surprise, that they weren't angry. The anger had been burned off years ago, down to something harder and calmer underneath. "Say *the deed names you and yet here I am*? Say it to whom? You were in Singapore. Father was dead. Mother was signing papers because he asked her to from his deathbed and she'd spent thirty years never once refusing him anything." Rosalind picked up one of the scattered ledgers — 2009, the winter of the triple storm — and held it out. "So I said it to the paper instead. Every day. That's what a logbook is, Edmund. It's a witness."

Edmund took the volume. Opened it. And in the margin beside the entry for March 3rd — *Lamp lit 17:42, wind SW force 9, glass falling* — Rosalind knew what he would find, because they had written it themselves, years ago, in a hand steady with fury: *Kept the light. Kept the house. Kept her. Noted for the record.*

Page after page. Year after year. The margins full of it — small penciled testimonies, receipts of labor no contract acknowledged, wages no deed paid. *Repointed the seawall alone, six days.* *Hauled mother to mainland clinic in the dinghy, sea state 4, doctor visit missed otherwise.* *Fixed the lens clock. Again.*

"This is—" Edmund turned pages with hands that weren't quite steady. "You documented *all of it*. Like a claim."

"It wasn't a claim," Rosalind said. "It was a record. There's a difference. A claim asks for something. A record just refuses to lie."

The storm screamed over them. Water sighed through the ventilator. Edmund sat looking at the open ledger as though it were a mirror he'd walked into without noticing, and Rosalind watched him understand, volume by volume, that the inheritance question had never actually been about the deed at all — that the deed was a piece of paper, and paper, as their father used to say, was only memory, and memory was the only thing the sea couldn't take unless you let it.

Except the sea had taken it. For nineteen years. Quietly. Without ever raising its voice.

"Rosalind." Edmund's voice was very low. "Why did you stay? After he died. After it was signed. You could have walked away and nobody—"

Above them, Mei's footsteps crossed the kitchen floor, slow and deliberate, moving toward the cellar door. Rosalind heard them stop.

"Ask her," Rosalind said. "She's the only one left who knows the whole of it."

The cellar door opened, and Mei came down the stairs into the lantern light with the storm howling behind her like something let out of a cage — carrying, in both hands, a second bundle of papers that Rosalind had never seen before.
