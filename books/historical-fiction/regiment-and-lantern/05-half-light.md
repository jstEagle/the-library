## 5. Half-Light

Mei Zhou had learned, over seventy-one years, that the truth arrived the way weather did — you could not summon it, only prepare for it, and when it came you had better already know where you'd left your boots.

She was preparing now. She sat in her chair by the kitchen window with her knitting in her lap — a scarf she had begun for Edmund eleven years ago and never finished, because he had left before she could decide whether to forgive him — and she listened to the two of them argue about firewood.

"We stack it green side down," Rosalind said.

"You stack it green side down. That is not a law of nature, that is a preference."

"It's physics."

"It's stubbornness wearing physics as a coat."

Mei smiled at the window. The rain ran down it in long silver threads, and beyond the glass the headland grass lay flattened under the wind like hair combed by a firm hand. Her children were fighting about firewood. It was, she thought, the most peaceful they had been since the boat came back.

Her hands were not what they used to be. The needles trembled unless she pressed them against the chair arm, and her chest had developed opinions about stairs — loud ones, delivered at inconvenient moments. Dr. Ferro on the mainland had used the word *management*, which Mei understood perfectly well was a polite word for *counting*. She had been counting too.

Two berths on the last boat. One household of three.

The arithmetic sat in the room with them like an unpaid bill.

* * *

In the winter of 1979 — no, 1980; the deed was signed in March, after the ice went out — a man named Aldous Crane had come up from the county seat carrying a briefcase and an umbrella he never once opened.

Mei remembered him chiefly by his shoes. They were brown leather, city shoes, and he picked his way across their muddy yard as though the mud had personally offended his ancestors. He sat at their kitchen table — this table, the same one, its grain worn into rivers by fifty years of elbows — and spread papers across it while her husband Wei stood at the stove with his back turned, stirring tea that nobody drank.

"The station's a good asset," Crane said. "But assets need protecting. If anything happened to you both — and the sea being what it is—" He made a small gesture toward the window, toward all that grey violence, as if introducing them. "The bank would want clarity. A single name on the deed. Clean succession."

"Clarity," Wei said to the teapot.

"A single heir," Crane said gently. "It's standard practice now. Families resist it, but families also lose lighthouses to lawyers. You have two children. Only one can carry it forward."

Mei had looked at the papers. She could read English well enough — she had taught herself from Rosalind's schoolbooks, sitting up late with a dictionary like a student cramming for an examination — but the legal language swam past her, dense and cold as deep water. She caught phrases: *sole beneficiary*, *primogeniture*, *in perpetuity*.

Edmund was nineteen that spring. Rosalind was seventeen.

"Why Edmund?" she asked.

Crane folded his hands. "Tradition, Mrs. Zhou. Sons inherit. It simplifies matters."

"Rosalind has kept the light every night Wei has been ill," she said. "Three hundred nights last year. I counted."

"I'm sure that's very commendable." He said it the way one commends a dog for retrieving. "But the deed isn't about who works. It's about who owns. Work follows ownership, not the other way around. Now — if you'll sign here, and initial there."

Wei still had his back to them. Mei watched his shoulders, and understood, in the way wives understand things that are never spoken aloud in front of strangers, that he would sign whatever was put in front of him. That he had already decided, or been persuaded, or simply exhausted himself into surrender. The storms that winter had nearly broken him. There had been a night in February when the light failed for eleven minutes and he had climbed the tower in wind that should have killed him, and come down white as paper, and sat in this chair shaking until dawn.

He wanted it settled. He wanted it off his shoulders. And so the easiest thing — the traditional thing, the thing the man with clean shoes recommended — was to hand the future to the son and let the daughter keep working, because the daughter would keep working regardless. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Rosalind's reliability was precisely what made her expendable. You do not secure the loyalty of the ox with a deed. You secure the plow.

Mei signed. Her signature came out small, cramped, apologetic — three characters and a scrawl that didn't look like her at all. She remembered thinking: *this ink will outlast me.*

She was right. It had.

* * *

"Mother. You're drifting."

Rosalind stood in the doorway with an armload of split birch, cheeks pink from the cold, watching her with that level, patient attention that always made Mei feel like a specimen under glass. Behind them, Edmund's voice carried from the yard: "I still say the tarp goes over the east pile first!"

"The east pile leaks," Rosalind called back, without turning. Then, more quietly, to Mei: "Your tea's gone cold again. Third time today."

"My tea and I have reached an understanding."

"Mm." Rosalind set down the wood, took the cup, and poured it out the back door into the drain without comment. Fresh water went on the stove. This was Rosalind entire, Mei thought — no complaint, no ceremony, just the quiet replacement of everything broken or neglected. Eleven years of cold cups poured out and refilled, and never once a word about it.

That was the thing she could not make peace with. Not the signing itself — cowardice, alas, comes naturally to mothers; it masquerades as protection. No, what haunted her was the years after. Watching. Counting, as she counted now. Rosalind at twenty-two, climbing the tower in a gale with a cracked rib and saying nothing. Rosalind at thirty, turning down the transfer to Harborlight because *someone has to be here, Ma.* Rosalind reading the deed — Mei knew Rosalind had read it, had found it years ago in the cellar with the tax records — and coming upstairs afterward with a face like closed weather, and making supper, and logging the barometer, and never saying one word.

Not even to accuse her. That was the cruelest part. An accusation would at least have been an opening. Rosalind had left her nothing to answer, no door to knock on, just that infuriating competence, that ox-like devotion, hauling the family's whole weight year after year in silence, as though silence were simply the price of the work and had never been anyone's choice at all.

"Rosalind," Mei said, while the kettle gathered itself toward boiling. "Sit. For a moment. The wood won't escape."

"It might. Edmund's involved."

"Sit."

Rosalind sat. Forty years old and obedient as a girl, hands folded, waiting. Mei found she had prepared three speeches and could remember none of them.

"When the boat comes," she began.

"Don't," Rosalind said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're going to say take the berth. I can hear it assembling."

"Then hear the rest of it first." Mei leaned forward. Her voice, these days, had a whistle in it on certain vowels, and she hated that whistle, and used it anyway. "I am your mother. I have buried a husband, survived two wars' worth of worry, and signed away my own daughter's inheritance with a shaking hand in front of a man with filthy manners and clean shoes. I am owed perhaps five minutes of being listened to. Take them."

The kettle climbed toward its note. Neither of them moved.

"You watched," Mei said. "All those years. I know you watched me watch you, and neither of us said anything, and I have decided that ends today. So. Here is what I know." She held up one trembling finger. "One: the deed was wrong, and I helped make it wrong, and no amount of knitting repairs that." A second finger. "Two: Edmund came back for paper, not for us, whatever he tells himself by the woodpile." A third. "Three: I am dying on a schedule I did not approve, and I will not spend my last crossing of that water knowing my child stayed behind on my account."

"Then don't stay on my account," Rosalind said quietly. "Stay on the house's. Stay on yours. You love this house more than either of us does."

"That is true," Mei admitted. It cost her something to admit it, and she saw Rosalind register the admission — the small surprise, quickly smoothed away. "I love this house. But a house is a thing I can grieve. A child is not."

"Ma—"

"One berth, Rosalind. Two people who cannot use it: a mother with failing lungs and a brother with a life elsewhere. And one person who can, and does, and has, every night, without thanks." She reached across the table and put her hand over Rosalind's — the keeper's hands, scarred and steady, glass-polisher's hands, lamp-trimmer's hands. "You think staying is the loyal thing. Staying is the easy thing. Staying is what the deed expected of you. Going — letting yourself be carried — that is the hard thing, and I am asking you to do it, and I am asking badly, and I will ask again tomorrow, and the day after, until that boat comes or I run out of asking."

For a moment — half a moment, less — Mei saw something crack behind Rosalind's eyes. Not anger. Something older and wetter, a spring thaw under ice. Then it sealed over, smooth as the lens glass, and Rosalind squeezed her hand once and stood.

"Tea's ready," Rosalind said.

And that was that. The door opened; the cold came in with Edmund and an argument about tarps; the kettle sang; the ordinary machinery of the household resumed, grinding on toward the storm.

* * *

That night Mei could not sleep. She lay in her narrow bed listening to the wind work at the shutters, and she thought about signatures — how small a mark a person leaves on a page, and how large a mark a page can leave on a person. She thought about Wei, who had died owing everyone apologies and paying none. She thought about the ledger in the cellar, Rosalind's ledger, the meticulous columns of labor nobody had asked her to keep — as if some part of Rosalind had known, all along, that the day of accounting would come, and had spent forty years preparing the evidence.

Perhaps, Mei thought, that was her punishment: not to be accused, but to be documented. To be audited by her own child's patience.

Toward dawn the wind changed, and with it came the radio — the marine band crackling up through the floorboards from the room below where Rosalind slept with one ear open, as always. Mei caught fragments through the boards. Static. Then a voice, clipped and official.

*...advisory upgraded... storm force... relief schedule suspended pending...*

Then Rosalind's low voice, repeating it back, calm as a tide table.

Mei lay in the dark and felt the old dread settle over her like a second blanket. The sea, it seemed, intended to vote on the matter of berths as well.
