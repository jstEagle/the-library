## 6. The Relief Boat That Turned Back

The *Meridian Star* was due on the ninth of December, and by the seventh of December I had already polished her into existence three times.

That is a keeper's sickness, Edmund would tell you if you asked him — the way we see weather as a personal negotiation. I had watched the barometer all morning like it owed me money. It sat at 29.1 and sulked there, neither rising nor falling, which on Gullwatch Point meant only one thing: the sea was thinking about it, and the sea's thoughts were not kind.

"Still watching the glass?" Edmund asked from the doorway. He had taken to wearing one of Father's old oilskins around the house, indoors, as if the storm might come through the kitchen wall unannounced. It made him look like a scarecrow that had been promoted.

"Somebody has to."

"Mother says if you stare at it any harder it'll file a complaint."

"Mother," I said, "should be resting."

"Mother," said my mother's voice from the parlor, "can hear every word you say through a wall built in 1911." A pause, then, softer: "And Mother is resting."

I let the curtain fall. Out past the point, the water was the color of hammered tin, and the swell was coming in long and wrong — not the short chop of a local wind but the deep, patient rollers that are born a thousand miles away and travel here carrying news. You learn to read them the way other people read letters. These said: *something large is on its way, and it is in no hurry, because it already knows it will win.*

* * *

At noon I climbed the tower for the noon check and found the radio already hissing at me before I touched it.

The set was an ancient Marconi-adjacent beast that predated both my parents' marriage and, I suspected, the invention of patience. It had survived four decades of salt air by the simple strategy of failing in new and inventive ways each winter. That morning its voice came through wrapped in static, like a stranger shouting down a well.

"—Gullwatch, do you copy, Gullwatch—"

I keyed the mic. "Gullwatch copies. Reading you two by five. Say again your last."

More hiss. Then, fragmentary, a voice I recognized as young Mr. Calloway from the Relief Office — a man whose entire professional personality was the phrase *as per schedule*.

"—advising... *Meridian Star*... diversion... seas exceeding... schedule revised... stand by..."

"Say again?" I said. "Calloway. Say again your last. Is the boat diverted?"

Static. A long, insulting stretch of it. Then, faintly, almost apologetic:

"—will advise—"

And then nothing but the ocean's own frequency, which is a sound like the inside of a seashell if the seashell were having second thoughts.

I stood there a while with my hand still on the key. Below me, the lens turned in its bath of mercury, steady as a heartbeat, keeping faith with ships that were, tonight, mostly hypothetical.

Down in the kitchen, Edmund looked up when I came in. He read my face the way he used to read Father's — quickly, defensively, bracing.

"They turned back?"

"Diverted. Seas too rough. They'll advise."

"They'll *advise*." He laughed, one dry note with no music in it. "Eleven years I'm gone and the mainland's still talking like a train station announcement. There's no boat, is what they mean. There's no boat until they decide there is."

"The schedule said the ninth—"

"The schedule," Edmund said, "is a letter people write to themselves so they can feel organized right up until the moment the world disagrees." He pulled out a chair, didn't sit in it. Paced instead, oilskin whispering against itself. "Rosalind. What does the barometer read?"

"Twenty-nine point one and sulking."

"And falling?"

"It hasn't decided. That's worse."

He stopped pacing. And I saw it happen — saw the thing move behind his eyes that I'd been dreading since the day he stepped off the supply skiff with his city shoes and his lawyer's envelope. The arithmetic. Three people. One berth, whenever the berth finally came. And now no date on the arithmetic at all.

"I have contacts," he said carefully. "On the mainland."

"You have contacts."

"Harbor commission. Two of them owe me — owed me — favors from the firm days. If I could get word out, raise someone on a working band, maybe charter something—"

"With what money?"

"That's not—" His jaw worked. "It's not about the money."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "It never is. It's usually about whose name goes where on things."

He flinched. Good. I hadn't meant it as cruelly as it landed, but I have learned not to apologize mid-blow; it only confuses everyone.

"Edmund." I softened it as much as I knew how. "If you can raise the harbor commission on that radio, you're a better man than Marconi. But try, if it helps you sleep. There's a working channel list in the log room drawer."

He went. I heard him in the log room for two hours, calling into the static, his voice getting smaller each time, a man shouting down a well of his own.

* * *

By evening the sea had finished deciding. The barometer fell to 28.8 and kept going like a stone dropped off the point, and the rollers arrived in earnest — green-black, house-high at the outer ledges, breaking white over the low rocks that hadn't seen spray since March. The tower hummed. Not dangerously. Just the way an old dog hums when thunder's coming: a sound in the bones of the building that says *I remember this.*

Mei sat by the stove with a blanket around her shoulders, mending a sock she did not need mended, because her hands required employment the way mine required the light. Her cough had settled into a low, wet tenor underneath everything, a bass note under the storm.

"Sit," she said, without looking up. "You've checked the light twice in an hour. It knows how to turn by now."

"It likes supervision."

"It likes nothing. It's glass and mercury." She bit her thread. "You, however, like supervision, and there's nobody left to give it to you but yourself."

I sat. For a while there was only the wind finding new notes in the chimney — it played the stack like a pipe organ in December, everything from a low hymn to a whistle sharp enough to sting.

"Mother," I said finally. "When the boat comes—"

"When the boat comes," she said, in the tone of a woman cutting a card game short, "we will discuss it then."

"There may not be a discussion. One berth."

"One berth," she agreed, serenely, pulling another thread tight. "So we should be very careful who deserves it."

"That's exactly the conversation I'm trying to avoid."

"I know." She looked up then, and her eyes were bright and tired and utterly immovable, the way they'd been across forty years of arguments she had won by refusing to have them. "Which is why I am having it first. You always did let other people go through doors ahead of you, even when the door was on fire. Your father was the same. It's not humility, Rosalind. Sometimes it's just stubbornness wearing Sunday clothes."

Before I could answer, Edmund came in from the log room, and whatever he'd been doing to his face in the hallway, he'd done it badly. He was pale in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

"Anything?" Mei asked.

"Channel sixteen is dead air. Harbor commission isn't answering — probably isn't *manned*, this time of year, or their aerial's down, or—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "There was one voice. A trawler out of Penhallow, maybe twenty miles south. I raised them. Told them our situation."

"And?"

"They said—" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "They said *good luck, friend*, and signed off. Twenty miles. Twenty miles and they wouldn't even—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I spent eleven years building a life over there. Names in books, people who'd take my call at midnight. And the one night I need it, the whole mainland is a closed door with the lights off."

The wind took the chimney organ up into its highest register. Down in the cellar, I knew, the tide gauge would be climbing past the red pencil line Father drew in 1962.

"Come sit by the fire," Mei said to him, gently, and something in her voice — some note I'd never heard her spend on him before — made him obey.

He sat. He put his head briefly, boyishly, against our mother's shoulder, the way he hadn't since he was small enough that Father could carry him up the tower stairs in one arm. Mei stroked his hair once, twice, and looked at me over his bent head with an expression I will be unpacking for the rest of my life.

Outside, the sea stood up and began, in earnest, to knock.

* * *

I went up at midnight because someone had to, and because the alternative was standing in the kitchen being loved, which at that particular hour felt more dangerous than ninety-knot wind.

The catwalk gave me the full theater of it. The storm had made landfall early — the leading edge of the great front, the vanguard before the true body arrived — and it came across the water like an army with its lanterns lit, if the lanterns were lightning and the army intended to stay for a week. Rain moved sideways. The swell hit the point with a concussion I felt through my boots, through my teeth.

In the lamp room, the lens turned. Steady. Unbothered. Four tons of glass and brass doing the one job it was made for, without opinion, without complaint, without once asking whether anyone was grateful.

I put my hand flat on the housing, the way I've done ten thousand nights, and I felt the vibration of the mechanism and, beneath it, the deeper shudder of the tower taking the storm's shoulder against its hip.

And that was when I saw it — far out, south by southeast, a single white star where no star should be at that bearing, rising and falling with a rhythm no sky ever taught anything. Running lights. A vessel, small, fighting up-sea toward the point. Toward us.

Toward the lee of Gullwatch, or toward us, or simply toward anywhere that wasn't open water — and in this sea, the difference between those things was about to become somebody's whole biography.

I grabbed the microphone.

"Gullwatch Point to unidentified vessel, unidentified vessel, this is Gullwatch Point. I have your lights bearing south-southeast, range approximately four miles. Be advised the point shoals hard at half a mile; come north-northwest for the deep channel. I'll hold the light steady for you. Come on."

Static answered. The white star rose, and fell, and rose —

and began, very slowly, to grow.
