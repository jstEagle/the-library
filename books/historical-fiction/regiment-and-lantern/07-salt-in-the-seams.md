## 7. Salt in the Seams

Edmund woke to the sound of the sea coming indoors.

Not metaphorically — he had learned in eleven years away that people on this coast spoke in metaphors the way other people breathed, and he had trained himself out of it — but literally. The sea was in the house. He could hear it moving furniture.

He came down the stairs with a candle he would later be ashamed of — Rosalind kept storm lanterns stocked at every landing, labeled in their small square handwriting, and of course he hadn't thought to look — and found water sheeting across the kitchen floor, black and gleaming in the candlelight, carrying a spoon past his feet like a small determined swimmer.

"Cellar's flooding," Rosalind said from somewhere inside the dark. They were already in oilskins, already ankle-deep, already calm in a way Edmund found frankly offensive at three in the morning. "The south wall took a stone from the point. We need to get the logbooks up before it reaches the shelving."

"The logbooks," Edmund said. "The house is drowning and you're worried about—"

"Yes," Rosalind said simply, and handed him a crate.

* * *

Here is what Edmund Zhou had planned to do with his night: nothing heroic. He had planned to hold a door, perhaps, or pass buckets with the weary competence of a man demonstrating that he was, despite everything, useful — the way one demonstrates anything to a sibling, which is to say defensively.

Instead he spent four hours hauling ledgers up out of a cellar that sounded like a drowned church bell, and somewhere around the second hour he stopped thinking about the deed entirely, which frightened him more than the water did.

Because the ledgers were heavy in more than paper weight. He'd opened one by accident — 1889, the year their father bought the station — and found not just weather entries but lives. *Lamp glass cracked, replaced from petty cash. Mrs. Aldous brought cod, repaid in oil.* And in the margins, in that same small square hand that was unmistakably, maddeningly Rosalind's, decades of continuation: coal deliveries logged through winters Edmund had spent in cities where nobody knew his surname; repairs itemized down to the nails; a note from 1903 reading simply *Mother better today. Ate a whole egg. She says the gulls are gossiping again.*

"You wrote all of these," he said. It wasn't a question. The question had been sitting in his chest for weeks and it finally fell out: "All these years. It was you."

Rosalind took the ledger from him without ceremony, wrapped it in oilcloth, and set it on the high shelf. "It was whoever stayed," they said. "That's how stations work." Then, because they were Rosalind: "Mind the third step. It floats now."

* * *

By dawn the water had begun its retreat, leaving the lower rooms salted and stripped like a beach at low tide. The kitchen smelled of brine and lamp oil. Their mother slept through all of it upstairs, which Rosalind insisted was a mercy and Edmund privately suspected was strategy — Mei had always possessed the gift of sleeping precisely when her children most needed an audience.

They worked in the gray light, wringing out rugs, propping furniture on bricks. Edmund found the great lens from the spare lamp assembly crated in the parlor, half-submerged, and hauled it out with both arms while Rosalind directed him with the serenity of a harbor master.

"Careful — it's ground glass, not your pride. It can't be replaced. There isn't another within two hundred miles."

"Why do you even have a spare?"

"Because the sea breaks things," Rosalind said, "and I intend to still be here when it does."

And there it was — the sentence Edmund had been circling for weeks, the whole argument of his brother's life laid out in nine words, said without heat, without appeal, as though stating that water was wet. *I intend to still be here.*

He set the lens down harder than necessary. "You could have sold. Any of the last ten years. Father's been dead fifteen, the mainland buyers circled twice—"

"I know. I read their letters."

"They were good offers."

"They were offers," Rosalind agreed, wringing a rug with a motion like milking a cow. "The light isn't an offer. It's a shift. Somebody has to stand it."

"And it had to be you? For fifteen years? Without a word, without—" He heard his own voice climb and hated it. "You let me think the place ran itself."

"No," Rosalind said, and looked at him then, directly, with the tide-gray patience that had always made Edmund feel like a boy caught skipping chores. "I let you think what was easier to think. That's not quite the same thing."

* * *

They did not speak for an hour after that. But they worked together — Edmund hauling, Rosalind sorting, the two of them moving around each other in the ruined kitchen with something almost like rhythm, and Edmund noticed, with the reluctant tenderness the storm was forcing on him like medicine, that he knew this rhythm. His body remembered it. Fifteen years ago they had stacked firewood in exactly this counterpoint, arguing about nothing, and his hands had not forgotten.

"So the cod woman," he said at last, because silence and Edmund Zhou had never gotten along. "Mrs. Aldous. Is she still—"

"Died in '04. Her granddaughter trades fish for kerosene now. Same arrangement."

"You have arrangements."

"I have a coast," Rosalind said. "A coast is mostly arrangements. You should see my arrangement with the cormorants. It's tense but stable."

Despite himself — genuinely against his will — Edmund laughed. It came out cracked from disuse and salt air, and Rosalind glanced over with an expression carefully arranged into neutrality, which is how siblings say *I'm glad* without surrendering anything.

They carried the last of the logbooks up to the tower room together, where the light waited under its canvas shroud like a patient animal. Rosalind uncovered it, checked the wick with two fingers, and Edmund watched them move around the lamp the way other men moved around their children, and understood suddenly, completely, the thing he had sailed eleven years to avoid understanding:

Rosalind didn't keep this station because of the deed. The deed was paper. Rosalind kept it because if the light went dark, some boat full of strangers would hit the point in the dark, and Rosalind was constitutionally incapable of allowing that. It wasn't inheritance. It never had been. It was a post, and Rosalind had stood it alone while Edmund built a life on the proceeds of a signature Mei had been pressured into — and the worst part, the part that sat in him like swallowed seawater, was that the labor in those margins had never once been billed.

"Rosalind." His voice came out strange. "About the deed—"

"Don't." Rosalind trimmed the wick. "Not tonight. Tonight we got the books dry and the lens safe and Mother slept through a flood. That's a good night, Edmund. Let it be one."

* * *

But the sea was not finished being generous with its opinions. Near noon, while they ate bread and tinned fish standing up like soldiers, the radio — salvaged to the tower room, crackling through static — coughed out a fragment from the mainland relay. Edmund caught only pieces through the interference: *...final relief sailing... revised manifest... remaining berths...*

Then the voice dissolved into the storm-band hiss, and Rosalind turned the dial slowly, twice, and did not find it again.

"One berth," Edmund said. The words tasted like the flooded kitchen smelled. "It said one berth, didn't it."

Rosalind was quiet long enough that the wind filled the pause. Outside, the greatest storm of the year was assembling itself beyond the point, patient as paperwork.

"We'll know tomorrow," they said, and began polishing the glass, which was what Rosalind did instead of trembling.

Edmund looked at his sibling — at the small square hand, the unbilled decades, the light burning behind them — and felt something rearrange itself in his chest with the finality of a tide turning.

Tomorrow, someone in this house was going to refuse that berth. He already knew who. He was already certain, with a dread he could not explain, that so did Rosalind.
