## 8. One Berth

The message came in on the 6 a.m. schedule, crackling through the static like someone reading a shopping list at a funeral.

*Gullwatch, Gullwatch. Final relief vessel *Meridian* departing Thursday, oh-six-hundred. Berth allocation revised. One passenger per station. Confirm by Wednesday. Over.*

Rosalind stood with the logbook open and the pen still wet from the last entry — *wind SW 22, barometer falling, glass steady* — and read the transmission three times, as though repetition might rearrange the arithmetic.

One.

The word sat on the page of their mind the way a stone sits in a shoe: small, hard, impossible to ignore once noticed.

Behind them, the kitchen smelled of ginger and scorched rice. Mei cooked the way she always had in crisis — too much food, as if abundance could argue against scarcity. Edmund was at the table with his sleeves rolled up, attempting to fix the pump handle that had been broken since October, mostly because he needed something to do with his hands that wasn't shaking.

"Radio," Rosalind said.

Both heads turned. Mei set down her spatula with the deliberate calm of a woman who had survived worse than weather. Edmund did not set down anything; he simply stopped moving, wrench suspended mid-air, like a photograph of himself taken at the exact moment hope curdled.

"One berth," Rosalind said. "Final boat. Thursday."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing the house had produced all winter, and the house had produced some considerable silences — the night the cellar flooded, the night Edmund's old dog died, the eleven-year silence between brother and sister that had finally cracked open over a deed in a storm.

"Well," said Mei, wiping her hands on her apron. "Obviously it's yours."

"It's not mine."

"Rosalind—"

"You can't climb the tower stairs anymore, Ma. Your knees gave out on the third landing in November. You told me it was the cold. It wasn't the cold."

Mei's chin lifted a fraction — the same stubborn angle Rosalind had been arguing with for forty years. "I have lived through two typhoons, one war, and your father's cooking. I will live through a winter."

"The boat doesn't care what you've lived through." Rosalind kept their voice level, keeper's voice, the voice they used for reporting conditions. "Thursday. One berth. That's the whole equation."

Edmund set down the wrench. It clinked against the pump base, absurdly loud. "Then take it," he said. "Both of you are arguing about who deserves it more, and neither of you is listening to me, which is traditional."

"We're listening," Rosalind said.

"No, you're performing. There's a difference." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked less like the polished man who'd arrived from the mainland in November and more like the boy who used to fall asleep in the lamp room during summer watches. "One berth. Three of us. Someone stays, and we all know—" His voice caught, and he hated it, you could see him hating it. "We all know who's staying. I just got here. Eleven years late. I don't get to be rescued first."

Nobody moved. Outside, the gulls were screaming at the tide the way they did before bad weather, which this year was every day.

* * *

That evening Rosalind found Mei in the parlor, mending a coat that belonged to no one currently in the house — Edmund's old one, from before. She sewed the way she prayed: steadily, without asking anything of heaven she didn't intend to supply herself.

"Ma. Sit down. We need to talk about Thursday."

"I am sitting."

"You're sewing."

"Sitting and sewing. Multitasking. A skill your generation has lost." But she set the coat aside, and folded her hands, and looked up at Rosalind with eyes that were suddenly, terribly clear. "You will take the berth."

"No."

"Yes."

"Ma—"

"I signed that deed, Rosalind." The words came out quiet and terrible, like water finding a crack in a wall. "Your father put the paper in front of me and I was tired and frightened and I signed it, and I watched them hand everything to Edmund, and I watched you pick up the lantern the same week and never say one word of complaint, not one, for thirty years. Thirty years. Do you know what that does to a mother? It rots something. Quietly. Like damp in a beam." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands had knotted themselves in her lap. "So no. I am not taking that berth. I am an old woman with bad knees and a finished life. You are a keeper without a light to keep if you leave. Take the boat, take the mainland, take a life that isn't measured out in oil and glass. That is not a request, Rosalind. That is a repayment."

Rosalind crouched in front of her — knees protesting, mirroring hers — and took those knotted hands gently apart, held them flat between their own.

"You want to repay me?" Rosalind said. "Then get on that boat alive. That's the whole debt. That's the entire ledger, Ma. You cross that water on Thursday and you let me keep this light, and we'll call it even." They smiled, because it was true and because humor was the only currency the family had never debased. "Besides. If you stay, Edmund will try to cook. Have you seen him with the pump? He fixed it so well it now pumps backwards."

Despite herself — despite everything — Mei laughed, a short sharp bark that turned into something else halfway through, and she gripped Rosalind's hands back, hard.

"You are your father's stubbornness," she said, "with none of his cowardice."

"I'll have that engraved somewhere."

* * *

But Edmund had heard the radio too, and Edmund had spent the day being quietly hollowed out, and at ten o'clock that night Rosalind found him standing in the lamp room, hatless in the rotating glare, watching the beam sweep the black water like a slow lighthouse-shaped heartbeat.

"They offered me things, you know," he said, without turning around. "On the mainland. After I left. Positions. Partners. My old firm wants me back — sent a letter last month, before the bridge closed. Very warm. Very generous." He laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "Eleven years I built a life out there, Roz. And do you know what I thought about when the radio said *one berth*? Not the firm. Not the partners. I thought about whether the ledgers were still in the cellar drawer."

"They are."

"I know. I helped carry them up during the flood." He turned then, and the passing beam lit his face in slices — light, dark, light. "Here's the thing I figured out at about four o'clock this afternoon, staring at that broken pump. I came back for the deed. I told myself it was to help. I packed a bag and told myself it was family duty. And the truth — the ugly, simple truth — is I came back to collect. And instead I found thirty years of your handwriting keeping this place alive, and a mother with ruined knees, and a light burning for boats that stopped coming years ago, and I thought: *this is what inheritance looks like, and it isn't paper.*"

He reached into his coat and pulled out the deed itself — their father's original, the one naming him sole heir, which he'd been carrying around in his breast pocket since the storm like a talisman he couldn't decide whether to worship or burn.

"So I'm giving you the berth," he said. "Both of you. However you sort it out between you — Ma crosses, or you cross, or you draw straws, I don't care. I stay. I'm the one who left. Let me be the one who stays. It balances."

"Edmund—"

"Don't argue. I'm better at it. I had practice."

Rosalind looked at their brother — at the hollowed-out, half-rebuilt man holding a piece of paper that had caused thirty years of silence — and felt something shift in their chest that had no name yet but would, eventually.

"You'd hate it here," Rosalind said. "By February you'd be talking to the foghorn."

"I already talk to the foghorn."

"That's my foghorn."

"See? Practicing."

They almost laughed. Neither quite managed it. The beam swept past again, and in its wake Edmund held out the deed — not dramatically, not as theater, just held it out, the way you hand someone a heavy thing you're tired of carrying.

Rosalind didn't take it.

"Keep it," Rosalind said quietly. "Not because it means what Father wanted it to mean. Keep it because Thursday morning, when the boat comes, you're going to put our mother on it, and you're going to sit beside her, and you're going to cross that water and build whatever you build next with her safe on the other end of it. That's the payment. That's everyone's payment. And then—" Rosalind's voice did something it hadn't done in front of Edmund in thirty years; it shook, just slightly. "And then come back in spring. Come back when the survey boat comes. Come back and see the light still burning, and tell me I was right to stay, because I will need someone to tell me that, Edmund. I will need it very much."

The lamp turned above them. Below, Mei's door stayed closed, though anyone could hear she wasn't sleeping.

Edmund folded the deed slowly and put it back in his pocket.

"Thursday," he said. "Oh-six-hundred."

"Thursday," Rosalind agreed. "Oh-six-hundred."

Neither of them mentioned that the forecast, updated at dusk, showed a low pressure system forming off the shelf — the deepest of the year, the kind the old-timers named like hurricanes.

Neither of them mentioned that *Meridian* would be sailing straight into the edge of it.

Outside, the gulls had gone suddenly, completely silent.
