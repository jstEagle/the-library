## 9. The Last Crossing

The boat came at dawn, as promised, which was the only thing that winter that had kept its word.

Rosalind heard the engine before the light — a low, stubborn chug through the gray, like a kettle arguing with itself. They had been awake since three, not from anxiety but from habit, and had used the hours productively: the lamp room scrubbed, the logbook squared away, the last of the bread turned into toast because Edmund claimed anxiety tasted better with butter.

"Engine," Rosalind said, coming down the spiral stairs.

Mei was already standing at the window, wrapped in two blankets and the particular dignity of a woman who has decided how the morning will go and is merely waiting for the world to comply. "I know," she said. "I've known since four."

"You haven't packed anything."

"I packed in November." She nodded toward the small canvas bag by the door, which had indeed been sitting there for weeks like a patient guest who understood, eventually, it would be going home. "Everything I need is in there. Everything else is yours now, and you may do what you like with it, though I'd prefer you didn't sell Father's chair to buy more brass polish."

"No promises," Rosalind said, and their voice cracked on the second word, which they covered by checking the barometer they did not need to check.

* * *

The argument happened on the shingle beach, because arguments about departure always happen where the departing can see the water.

"You're taking the berth," Rosalind said.

"I am doing no such thing." Mei's breath made small furious clouds. "I have lived sixty-eight years on this coast. The sea and I have an arrangement."

"The sea has no arrangements, Ma. That's rather its defining feature."

Edmund stood slightly apart, holding his own bag — the one he'd arrived with in autumn, still half-packed, as if some part of him had never quite committed to being anywhere. He opened his mouth. Rosalind raised a hand.

"Not yet. Let me lose this argument properly first."

"You're not losing it," Mei said. "You're winning it so thoroughly that I'm embarrassed for you. One berth, Rosalind. One. And you're my child same as he is, but you are the one who knows how to be alone, and God forgive me for saying it, but that's not a compliment."

"It wasn't taken as one."

"I mean—" She stopped. The wind took her scarf and she wrestled it back with the efficiency of long practice. "I mean that I watched you stay for thirty years without ever once asking whether you wanted to stay. So now I'm asking. Do you want to?"

And there it was, the question at the bottom of the winter, finally surfaced like something the storms had dredged up and refused to bury again. Rosalind looked out at the boat — small, black-hulled, rising and falling with an insolence that suggested it knew exactly what it was costing them.

"Yes," Rosalind said. "But wanting isn't the unit of measurement here. Someone keeps the light. That's the whole job. It was always the whole job."

* * *

It took force of will, in the end, and a certain amount of physical maneuvering that neither of them would later describe accurately.

Mei dug her heels in on the wet stones — literally, heels, like a mule — until Rosalind pointed out that the alternative was the boat leaving with nobody aboard, which would render the entire moral debate moot and also get the crew drowned. Then Edmund, of all people, took his mother gently by both shoulders.

"Ma," he said. "Come on. You'll catch your death, and then Rosalind will have to log it, and they'll write *cause of death: stubbornness, maternal*, and it'll go in the permanent record."

Mei laughed despite herself — a short, wet, defeated sound. "You've gotten funny," she accused him. "Eleven years away and you come back *funny*."

"Had time to practice."

She let them walk her to the waterline. At the rail she gripped Rosalind's hands hard enough to leave marks and looked up into their face with an expression Rosalind would spend the rest of the storm learning to read.

"You were the one," she said simply. "All those years. I signed that paper because your father frightened me, and then I let you carry it because you made carrying things look easy. It wasn't easy. I knew it wasn't easy. I'm sorry takes less ink than that, but it's all I have this morning."

"Take the berth, Ma."

"I'm taking the berth."

"Then we're square."

"We are not remotely square," Mei said, "but we're even enough to sail on." She kissed them once, hard, on the cheek — salt and wool and camphor — and then climbed aboard with the brisk competence of a woman boarding a ferry to market, as if the crossing were routine, as if the squall building on the horizon were weather and not an ending.

* * *

Edmund lingered. Of course he lingered. Eleven years of lingering compressed into forty seconds at the rail.

He looked terrible, Rosalind thought with a surge of affection. He'd lost weight he didn't have, his hands were scabbed from the flood work in the cellar, and his eyes had the raw look of a man who had spent a season discovering that the ledger of his life did not balance and could not be made to balance by any entry he possessed. He looked like someone who had come home to claim a deed and found out, instead, what a deed was worth against a person who stayed.

"I wrote to Harrowgate," he said abruptly. "Before Christmas. About the mainland connections. There was a position — surveying, inland, decent money. I told myself if I secured it first, I'd have something to bring back. Something to justify—"

"Edmund."

"—coming back empty-handed. Which is what I did. Come back empty-handed." He laughed, without much humor in it. "They never answered. Probably the office closed. Probably the whole street closed. Probably—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way he'd learned to stop himself all winter. "It doesn't matter. None of it matters. Here's what matters."

He reached into his coat and pulled out the deed — their father's original, water-stained, the paper soft as cloth now from handling. Edmund sole heir, in ink gone brown as old blood. He held it out over the water between them.

"Take it," he said. "Burn it, keep it, feed it to the gulls. It's yours. It was always more yours than mine; I just hadn't done the arithmetic."

Rosalind looked at the paper flapping in the wind and found, to their mild surprise, that they felt nothing much at all about it. A piece of paper. Their father's fear written in his careful hand. Thirty years of margins full of lamp oil and flour and mended glass, and here was the document that had tried to contain all that in two signatures.

"Keep it," Rosalind said.

Edmund blinked. "What?"

"Keep it. Burn it when you get across, keep it in a drawer, I genuinely don't care. But I'm not taking it, because taking it means it was ever mine to take, and it wasn't. It was never the deed, Edmund. It was the keeping." Rosalind stepped closer to the rail, close enough that the spray off the hull dotted their face. "Here's the only thing I want to give you before you go, so listen, because I won't say it twice and the wind's picking up."

Edmund waited. The boat rose and fell beneath him. Behind him, Mei watched with the stillness of someone witnessing a thing she had prayed for and not dared name.

"I forgive you," Rosalind said. "Not for the deed — that was Father's sin and Mother's silence and mostly just bad luck wearing a legal costume. Not for leaving — everyone leaves; it's practically a coastal tradition. I forgive you for coming back. Because you didn't have to. Because every sensible instinct you had told you to send a lawyer and a check, and instead you got on a boat and pumped bilge water all night beside me and learned to hold a lantern steady in a gale, badly, but steadily. Coming back was the hardest thing you did and you did it late and imperfectly, which is the only way anyone does anything hard." They paused. "That's it. That's the whole speech. Now get off my point before I say something sentimental about childhood."

Edmund's face did several things at once, none of them orderly. "You absolute—" he managed, and then gave up on language entirely and grabbed Rosalind's collar and pressed his forehead against theirs, brother-fashion, the way they'd done as boys on this same shingle when the world had been smaller and kinder and the deeds unwritten.

"Keep the light," he said into the wind.

"Always do."

* * *

The boat pulled away at 6:47, and Rosalind logged the departure before it finished happening, because that was who they were. *6:47 — Relief vessel departed with two passengers, Z., M., and Z., E. Weather deteriorating. Visibility poor. All well.*

The last phrase they wrote slowly, testing it. *All well.* It was not precisely true — nothing was well, everything was ending, the greatest storm in living memory was assembling itself on the southern horizon like an orchestra tuning up for catastrophe — and yet it was true in the way that mattered. Two people safe. One person willing. A light that needed keeping.

They watched the boat shrink into the murk. Once, near the mouth of the channel, it seemed to pause — or perhaps that was only the swell lifting it, holding it up for a moment like a hand raised in farewell — and then the squall came down between Rosalind and the water like a curtain drawn, and the boat was gone, and the sound of its engine lasted another full minute after it vanished, fading the way a voice fades when someone walks away down a long corridor, and then the sea had the whole horizon to itself.

Rosalind stood on the shingle a moment longer, letting the rain find them, letting it be ordinary rain for as long as it could manage.

"Well," they said aloud, to no one, in the tone they usually reserved for a jammed wick. "Just us, then."

* * *

The climb up the tower took longer than usual, because the tower was leaning into the wind like a drunk friend and the stairs had opinions of their own. By the time Rosalind reached the lamp room the storm had made landfall in earnest — the great one, the one the radio had been warning about in increasingly apologetic terms for three days, the one the old keepers' logs referred to, in earlier centuries, with names and small prayers.

Rosalind lit the lamp. Checked the rotation. Wiped the first salt spray off the glass, knowing there would be ten thousand more wipes before morning.

Below, the sea stood up and began to throw itself at the rocks with the wholehearted malice of a thing doing exactly what it was born to do. Above, the light swung out once, twice, into the boiling dark — steady, patient, absurd, indispensable.

Rosalind sat down at the log desk, opened the book to a fresh page, and began to write.

Somewhere out in that violence, a small boat was carrying their mother east. Somewhere beyond the squall, a town was emptying, a bridge was closing, a world was deciding what this coast had been worth. And here — four hundred candles' worth of stubbornness in a glass drum, turning and turning against the night — was the answer Rosalind intended to give until the ink ran out.

The storm hit the tower full on. The windows groaned. The light held.

Rosalind dipped the pen, and kept the watch.
