## 10. Regiment and Lantern

The spring survey boat came up the channel on a morning so clear it felt like an apology.

Rosalind saw it first as a smudge against the headland, then as a white hull with *Coastal Commission* stenciled along its flank, and finally — through the glass — as two officials in matching orange vests who stood at the rail looking up at the tower with the expressions of people who had expected to find either a ruin or a ghost and were not sure which would be worse to find alive.

Rosalind put the kettle on. There was no sense meeting bureaucracy uncaffeinated; that was a lesson learned over forty years of inspectors, auditors, and one memorable man from the Admiralty who had tried to tell them their fog signal was "non-standard" until Rosalind's father had recited the entire maintenance manual from memory, standing in his undershirt, daring the man to check page by page. The man had checked three pages and left.

By the time the dinghy bumped against the landing stage, there was tea for two, biscuits under a cloth, and the logbook open on the chart table to the current week, ink still faintly tacky from that morning's entry: *Wind SW 4, vis. good, lamp trimmed 0540, all well.*

"All well," read the taller of the officials, a woman named Prentiss whose lanyard announced her as *Regional Condemnation & Survey*. She looked around the lamp room, at the brass polished to the color of honey, at the lens turning its slow patient circles, and something in her professional posture loosened by a degree. "You know, we genuinely didn't expect this. The file said the station was abandoned."

"The file," Rosalind said, pouring tea into enamel mugs, "was written by someone who wasn't here."

* * *

They had come, it emerged over biscuits, to close the file. The evacuation of the coast had been completed in February; the condemnation proceedings had run their course through the winter; every structure from Gullwatch Point south was scheduled either for demolition or, in the case of buildings deemed "heritage-significant," for transfer to whatever party could demonstrate lawful interest. The station, being both heritage-significant and structurally sound — Prentiss consulted her tablet — qualified for the latter category, but the process required an heir.

"There were three names on the original deed," she said carefully. "We've been unable to reach—"

"You'll want the paperwork." Rosalind went to the shelf beneath the chart drawers, where eleven logbooks stood in a regimented row, spines aligned, each labeled in a steady hand: dates, weather summaries, lamp hours, fuel consumed. Beside them, wrapped in oilcloth, sat a folder of correspondence — the winter's radio transcripts, the supply manifests, the medical notes Rosalind had kept on their mother before the crossing, because even after Mei was safely ashore Rosalind had continued logging her blood pressure readings out of habit, addressed to a doctor on the mainland who presumably found them baffling and touching in equal measure.

Prentiss opened the most recent logbook and stopped turning pages very quickly, the way one does when entering a church.

The entries were immaculate. Not merely complete — *immaculate*, each one dated, timed, initialed, with marginal notes in the same hand recording things no regulation required: *gull nesting on gallery rail again, left him to it.* *Fresnel lens, 3rd order, polished per schedule; she sings better in cold weather.* *Storm force 11, gusts higher. Held.*

"Held," Prentiss repeated.

"That was the night of the twenty-third of January," Rosalind said. "The worst of the year. I'd have written more, but my hands were occupied."

There was a silence in which the only sound was the clockwork of the lens turning above them, and Prentiss looked up into the light — which was burning, mid-morning though it was, because Rosalind had never entirely trusted the automatic systems and kept the lamp lit through any weather worth lighting it for — and when she spoke again her voice had shifted registers, from *Regional Condemnation & Survey* to something more human.

"Ms. Zhou. May I ask you something plainly?"

"You may try."

"Why? Everyone else left. The commission offered relocation assistance, inland postings, a pension. You stayed through a storm season alone in a condemned building on an evacuated coast. Forgive me, but from where we sit — in offices with heating that works — this looks less like duty than like stubbornness."

Rosalind considered the question honestly, because it deserved honesty, and because it had taken Rosalind most of the winter to answer it too.

"My father used to say there were two kinds of inheritance," they said at last. "Regiment and lantern. The regiment is what you're given — orders, obligations, the name on the deed, the expectation that you'll fall in line. And the lantern is what you choose to keep lit, whether or not anyone ordered you to, whether or not anyone's watching, whether or not there's a single soul on the water who needs it." They turned the mug in their hands. "For a long time I thought the deed was the inheritance. It took me fifty-odd years and one very bad winter to understand that the deed was just paper. The lantern was always mine."

* * *

It was Prentiss's junior, a young man named Okafor who had spent the climb up the tower visibly regretting his career choices, who found the letter.

He was cataloguing the archive room off the lamp room — the old storage space where charts, spare wicks, and decades of accumulated station life lived in careful disorder — when he called down, "Ma'am? Ms. Zhou? There's something tucked in the back of the 1978 logbook. It's — I think it's addressed to you."

Rosalind knew before opening it. Knew by the paper — the good airmail stock, blue-lined, that Mei had hoarded since the seventies — and by the handwriting, which had grown shakier over the winter aboard the mainland but remained unmistakably, defiantly upright, a woman who had signed her surrender to the deed in 1979 and had apparently decided, somewhere across the water, to sign something else.

*Rosalind,*

*Your brother tells me you will be angry that I wrote this instead of telephoning. He is correct. But anger keeps the blood moving, and at my age one takes one's exercise where one finds it.*

*Edmund has been staying with me. He cooks badly and talks too much about property values, which I remind him is how our family got into trouble in the first place. He has also, without being asked, fixed the loose stair in my building and argued with the pharmacy until they filled my prescription early, so I am forced to conclude he is not entirely ruined, only bent, the way a fence post bends — still doing its job if you squint.*

*I enclose what I should have given you thirty years ago, and could not, because giving it meant admitting what I had done, and I was a coward about that for longer than I was a keeper of anything. Read it or burn it. Both are your right. But know this: everything the deed says belongs to Edmund, and everything I say now belongs to you, and between a piece of paper and a mother's word, I am asking you to believe the word.*

*The house was never his. The keeping was never acknowledged. I watched you absorb both wrongs with a straight back and I told myself you were strong enough to bear it, which was a lie strong people are told so the weak don't have to fix anything.*

*Keep the light. Not for the commission. Not for the sea, which owes nobody anything. Keep it because it is yours — it was always yours — and because when I am gone, I want to know that somewhere in the world there is one honest thing still burning that I helped make, however late I made it honest.*

*Your mother,*

*Mei*

*P.S. The biscuits you sent in December were too dry. Use more butter. This is not negotiable.*

Tucked behind the letter was a single sheet of the old blue-lined paper: a handwritten statement, dated, witnessed by a mainland notary whose stamp Rosalind recognized from the supply-boat paperwork, affirming that Mei Zhou, of sound mind, did hereby attest that the conveyance of 1979 had been executed under duress and misrepresentation, and that her true wish — her lifelong, unspoken, cowardly, and now finally spoken wish — was that Gullwatch Point and all it contained pass to her child Rosalind, the only one who stayed.

Rosalind read it twice. Then folded it along its original creases, sat down on an old crate of spare wicks, and laughed once, wetly, at the postscript.

Okafor, wisely, had developed a sudden fascination with the horizon.

* * *

Prentiss spread the paperwork on the chart table that afternoon: the condemnation buyout schedules, the transfer forms, the options. "Realistically," she said, tapping a column of figures, "the commission's preference is that you relocate. Inland posting, assistant keepership at the training facility — it comes with a salary, benefits, colleagues. Or—" she hesitated, "—there's the other path. The station's condemned status can convert to private ownership via buyout, provided the owner demonstrates capacity to maintain it. It's irregular. Frankly, it's unprecedented in this district. But the logs—" she touched the row of them almost reverently, "—the logs make a case no lawyer could argue with. You've demonstrated capacity for forty years. The last six months were done alone, in a storm season, without relief, on a coast everyone else fled."

"And if I take the buyout?"

"Then Gullwatch Point is yours. Outright. No heirs contesting it, no commission holding the mortgage of your labor. The light stays lit under license, you remain the registered keeper, and the government pays you — modestly, I warn you — a stipend for maintenance of an active aid to navigation." She smiled, small and dry. "You'd be the poorest landowner on the coast and the only one who owns exactly what they've earned."

Rosalind looked out the gallery window. The sea lay flat and glittering, empty in the way it had been empty all winter — no fishing boats, no ferries, no wake but the survey boat's own, already fading. Somewhere past the headland, the bridge stood closed and rusting. The world had moved inland and left the water to the gulls and the wind.

And yet the light turned. All winter it had turned, for no one, and Rosalind had come to understand that this was the whole secret: a thing tended faithfully in emptiness is not wasted. It is *ready*. Some spring — maybe not this one, maybe not for years — a boat would come up this channel in bad weather, some fool or fisherman or lost soul, and they would see the light, and it would be there, because it had never stopped being there.

"I'll take the station," Rosalind said.

Prentiss nodded as though she'd known before asking. Perhaps she had. She uncapped her pen. "Sign here. Initial there. And Ms. Zhou — one condition of the stipend. Quarterly inspections. Someone has to verify the light's being maintained."

"Send whoever you like," Rosalind said, signing. "I'll put the kettle on."

* * *

That evening Rosalind climbed the tower at dusk, as always, and began the ritual: trim the wick, check the fuel, wipe the lens with the soft cloth kept solely for her — the lens, whom Rosalind's father had insisted on calling *she*, a superstition Rosalind had inherited along with everything else that actually mattered.

Before lighting her, Rosalind pinned two papers to the corkboard beside the log shelf, where the winter's radio transcripts hung in their neat rows. The first was Mei's statement, copied fair in Rosalind's steadiest hand — the original would go to the lawyers, but the board should hold the truth, the way it held the tide tables and the emergency procedures. The second was a fresh sheet of airmail paper, on which Rosalind had begun a reply:

*Mother —*

*The butter situation is resolved. Edmund was right about one thing, which I will deny ever writing: the house was never the point.*

*Come in June. There's a berth on the supply boat now that the channel's open, and the gulls are nesting, and the light needs showing off. Bring the good tea. Bring him too, if he can survive a week without property values to discuss. I have work for his hands and a loose stair of my own.*

*All well.*

Below it, Rosalind wrote the date, the weather — *wind SW 2, vis. excellent, sunset clear* — and initialed it, the first entry in the newest logbook, the one that belonged to no commission and no deed but to the person holding the pen.

Then Rosalind struck the match, and the lens caught the flame and carried it round, and round, and round — out over the empty shining water, past the closed bridge, past the headland, out to the far dark edge of the sea — a small stubborn gold circle turning in the dusk, kept by choice, kept by love, kept.

Outside, the first evening gulls wheeled through the beam and cried out, indignant and alive, and inside the tower the clockwork ticked on, steady as a heart, lighting the way home for anyone — someday, anyone at all — who needed it.
