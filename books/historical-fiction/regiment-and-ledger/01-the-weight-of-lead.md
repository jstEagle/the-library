## 1. The Weight of Lead

The graver sang against the steel at a pitch only printers understood, and Theo Delacroix had long ago stopped hearing it as anything but her own pulse.

She worked by lamplight in the back room of the shop, where the air smelled always of lead and lamp oil and the ghost of her father's pipe tobacco, though he had been three years dead and the pipe broken longer than that. The punch before her was half-cut — an *f* with too generous a spur, which she was correcting by lamplight because daylight belonged to customers, to invoices, to the fiction that Delacroix & Son still had a son who kept it.

Delacroix & Son. Her father had painted the sign himself, on a Sunday when Marcel was fourteen and full of promise, standing beneath it with his collar starched and his chin lifted as though the letters were being cut into him. *The boy will carry it forward,* their father had said, hand heavy on Marcel's shoulder. And Theo had stood to one side with ink to her elbows, saying nothing, because at eleven she had already learned that the press forgave everything except hesitation, but fathers did not.

The letterform took shape under her file: the stem true, the crossbar level, the counter — that negative space, the silence inside the letter — opened just enough to breathe. Her father used to say you judged a typeface by its counters, not its strokes. Anyone could make a mark, he said. The art was in the emptiness you left behind.

Theo measured the finished punch against her gauge, blew the filings from its face, and held it up to the flame. It gleamed like something alive.

"Mademoiselle Delacroix." The bell over the shop door rang through the partition, and she set the punch down carefully, the way her father had taught her — never laid flat, never hurried — and went out front.

The morning's first customer was Madame Roussel from the chandlery, wrapped in a gray shawl despite the mildness, wanting five hundred handbills for her husband's candidacy to the merchants' council. Behind her waited old Père Aubry, who bought his newspaper twice a week and read it aloud in the café whether anyone wished it or not.

"The merchants' council," Aubry said, with the contempt he saved for things he considered beneath even his scorn. "As if the harbor cares who sits on it. The harbor has other business this spring."

"What business?" Madame Roussel asked, though her tone said she regretted it immediately.

Aubry leaned across the counter toward them both, lowering his voice as though the type cases might repeat what they heard. "Grain shipments diverted. Bread up another two sous since Lent. The garrison's been doubled at the arsenal gates — you've seen them? And they say—" he glanced toward the window, where the street lay bright and ordinary, washerwomen and fish carts and a lieutenant of the gendarmerie strolling with his hands clasped behind him like a man admiring architecture— "they say there are pamphlets coming up from the south. Printed ones. Calling for the workers to strike when the fleet puts in."

"Pamphlets." Madame Roussel sniffed. "Paper doesn't frighten anyone."

"Paper frightened the last king," Aubry said. "Ask him how he liked it."

He bought his newspaper and left, and Madame Roussel ordered her bills in the smallest type Theo stocked, so the price would seem less, and left also, and the shop settled back into its quiet — the tick of the clock, the creak of the floorboards above the cellar where the paper was stored, the smell of ink that Theo had decided, somewhere around her ninth year, was the smell of safety.

It did not smell safe this morning. Or rather, it smelled the same, and she was the thing that had changed, listening now for boots on cobblestones, counting soldiers the way she once counted ems.

*Keep your press cold.* That was what Marcel had said at Christmas, the last time he'd come in uniform. Not *be careful*, not *the times are troubled*. Keep your press cold — printer's language, the language of their childhood, delivered with an officer's flatness. As if the press were a stove and winter a season that would pass.

She looked at the handpress against the far wall, her father's press, iron-framed and older than either of his children, its platen polished by forty years of pulling. Cold. It sat cold now and had for weeks, and every day it sat cold was a day the shop earned less than it spent, and every night she sat cutting type for other people's orders while her own designs — the specimen book she'd dreamed of since girlhood, the typeface she'd already named *Vellacourt* in her head — gathered dust in a drawer.

And every day she signed the ledgers *T. Delacroix*, and every customer who cared to know knew that T. stood for Théodora, and that Théodora had no legal existence as a master printer, and that the license on the wall bore her brother's name because the guild would not bear hers. When the inspector came — and he came monthly now, the inspections growing longer, more curious — she showed him Marcel's license and Marcel's signature and spoke of *my brother's establishment* in a voice she'd practiced until it sounded bored.

Her brother, who wore a captain's uniform now. Her brother, who enforced with a saber the same streets their father had inked with a roller.

The bell rang again at midday, and this time it was him.

Marcel Delacroix filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway — broad, correct, his blue coat brushed, his boots gleaming with the particular shine of a man inspected daily by men who outranked him. He had their father's height and their mother's coloring and, Theo thought as she watched him scan the shop before letting himself smile, something new in his eyes that she could not name yet and did not want to.

"Sister." He kissed her cheek. His lips were cold. "You're pale. Are you eating?"

"I'm eating. You're early. I thought officers dined at their mess."

"I told them I had family business." He said it lightly, but he said it facing the window, watching the street, and Theo felt the dread arrive ahead of any reason for it, the way weather arrives ahead of wind.

"Family business," she repeated. She poured him the cheap wine she kept for herself, not the good bottle, because the good bottle was for celebrations and she wanted no celebration of whatever this was. "Sit. Tell me why a captain of the garrison risks his colonel's displeasure to drink my bad wine."

Something crossed his face at the word *colonel* — quick, quickly mastered, but not quickly enough. Vassey. Colonel Vassey, whose name had begun appearing in conversations the way rot appears in fruit: not discussed, merely avoided.

"Theo." He sat across the worktable from her, and for a moment he was eight years old again, sitting exactly so while their father explained the difference between a ligature and a diacritic, his whole face open with the hunger to understand. Then the moment passed, or was put away, and the officer remained. "There is going to be trouble in this city. Soon. Weeks, perhaps days."

"You sound like Père Aubry."

"Aubry is a fool who is right." Marcel turned his glass but didn't drink. "There are lists being made. On both sides — that's what no one understands. The ministry makes lists of agitators. The agitators make lists of informers. And the men in between, the tradesmen, the printers—" his eyes touched the press, briefly, the way a man's eyes touch a wound— "the printers are on everyone's list. A press that works can serve either side, so a press that works gets watched. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you want me to starve quietly."

"I want you to *live*." The word cracked out of him harder than he intended; she saw him rein it. "Print the wedding invitations. Print the funeral notices, God knows there will be enough. Print Roussel's handbills if you must. But nothing political. Nothing unsigned. Nothing that isn't paid for in advance with a name attached. Promise me."

"Or what?"

"Or the next time an inspector comes here, he won't be an inspector." Marcel drank finally, all of it. "Theo, I sit in rooms where these decisions are made. I have seen the orders. When the trouble comes, there will be no careful distinctions made between a pamphleteer and a woman who happens to own type metal. There will only be presses smashed and questions asked afterward."

She studied him across the table — her brother, her only blood, the boy who had taught her to set her first line of type upside down and backward because that was how the world looked to letters. *Everything true must be composed in reverse,* their father said. *You build the mirror image, and the impression gives you the truth.*

"And you?" she asked. "When the trouble comes. Where will you stand?"

He didn't answer at once. Outside, a drum began somewhere near the harbor, practice or warning, impossible to tell. Marcel listened to it with his head tilted, and Theo watched her brother count beats the way she counted picas, and understood that he lived now inside a different system of measurement entirely — ranks, orders, survival — and that she could no longer read his figures.

"I'll stand where I'm ordered," he said finally.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one they've left me." He set down the glass and stood, and the officer's posture closed over him like a door. "Keep the press cold, Theo. Please. For Father's sake if not mine." He kissed her forehead, and his hand lingered a half-second on her shoulder — the exact spot their father's hand had rested under the painted sign, twenty years ago, on the boy who would carry it forward.

Then he was gone, and the bell's sound faded, and the shop was quiet again except for the drum, still beating faintly from the direction of the water.

* * *

She did not keep the press cold.

She told herself it was necessity first — Roussel's five hundred handbills would not print themselves, and rent did not forgive politics — but that evening, after she had locked the door and shuttered the windows and lit the lamp over the stone composing table, she took out the drawer she never showed inspectors. Inside lay the forme she'd been setting in secret for a month, a single page, twelve by eighteen picas, and she looked at it now with her heart going hard against her ribs.

It was an article. Unsigned. Unpaid. Set in her own experimental type, the *Vellacourt* face, cut by her own hand over three winters — every character in it drawn from her memory of this city, the curve of the harbor bridge in the *b*, the tilt of the fish-market awnings in the italic *e*. It was about the grain shipments. About the diverted cargoes and the bread prices and the names of the warehouse owners who grew fat while the dockworkers' children went hollow-eyed. Names she had collected the way other women collected buttons, patiently, over years, from customers who talked too much in front of the woman at the type case.

It was, she understood perfectly well, the most dangerous object in Vellacourt.

Her father's voice, in memory: *You judge a typeface by its counters. The art is in the emptiness you leave.* And what was this but that? An article about everything the city did not say. Truth built out of deliberate silence.

Theo locked the shop door again though it was already locked. She washed her hands though they were clean. Then she sat down at the case and began to set the final paragraph, the stick filling line by line in her fingers, each piece of type sliding home with its small definite click — the sound, she thought, of a decision being made one letter at a time.

Outside, the curfew bell rang ten o'clock. Somewhere beyond the shutters, boots passed in step — a patrol, four men by the sound, moving through streets that had grown used to the sound the way a body grows used to a fever.

She was fitting the last word of the last line when she heard the pounding.

Not on the door. On the shutter. Fists against wood, rapid, desperate, arrhythmic — nothing like the knock of a customer or the formal rattle of the law. Three doors down, a dog began to bark.

Theo stood very still in the lamplight, one hand flat on the forme she should not have been setting, and listened to the night try to decide what it was becoming.

The pounding came again, weaker now, wet-sounding, as though the knuckles were bleeding.

She blew out the lamp.
