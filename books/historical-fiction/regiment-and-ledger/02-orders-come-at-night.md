## 2. Orders Come at Night

The knock came at midnight, and it was not a knock at all.

It was a fist against the shop's iron shutter — three blows, then three more, the rhythm of a man who had forgotten how to be quiet because he no longer had the strength to remember. Theo sat up in the loft above the press room, her hand already on the candle before her mind caught up with her body. In this city, in this year, midnight sounds sorted themselves into kinds: the drunk kind, the patrolling kind, and the dying kind.

She lifted the shutter's viewing slit and looked down into the Rue des Fondeurs, and the man standing there looked back up at her as if he had been waiting his whole life for exactly that slit of lamplight to find him.

He was tall and stooped at once, the way a man is when something inside him has broken and the outside has given up pretending otherwise. Blood ran from his temple into his collar, black in the dark, and his left arm hung wrong, cradled against his ribs like something borrowed. Under the other arm he carried a bundle — and Theo's breath stopped, because the bundle moved.

Not a package. A child.

"Madame." His voice was gravel dragged over stone. "I know it's late. I know what I'm asking."

"You're asking nothing yet," Theo said, and hated how steady she made her voice sound when her hands were not.

"Open the door."

"Curfew began two hours ago. If a patrol—"

"If a patrol finds me," he said, "they'll find you too, standing at your own window talking to a bleeding man. So open the door, or don't, but do one of them quickly."

He swayed. She saw him catch himself on the shutter's edge with his good hand, saw the effort cost him something he could not easily pay again. And the bundle stirred, and a small voice came out of it, thin and sleep-slurred: "*Papa?*"

Theo was moving before she decided to move. That was the thing about decisions like this one — she would understand later that she had made hers in the space between the child's voice and the bolt sliding free, and that everything afterward was only consequence.

She hauled the shutter up enough to pull him through, and he came through it heavier than any man she'd ever handled, all his weight surrendering at once, so that they went down together onto the floor of the print shop among the scattered quoins and the smell of ink and lamp oil. The bundle rolled free, and out of it emerged a girl of perhaps four years, dark-eyed, solemn, wrapped in a man's coat with the sleeves knotted under her chin. She did not cry. She looked around at the cases of type, the great iron press looming in the dark like a sleeping animal, and then at Theo, with the expression of someone taking inventory.

"Papa fell down," the girl announced.

"So I see," Theo said.

* * *

She got him into the chair by the cold stove, got the coat off him, got the bleeding slowed with a rag and water and the last of her father's brandy, which she poured more generously over his head than into him. He let himself be tended the way a horse lets itself be bridled — enduring it, watching her the whole time with eyes that never softened and never quite focused either.

Above his left ear the scalp had opened in a crescent, and beneath the torn shirt she found the bruise already blooming across his ribs, purple going black, and she knew without asking what kind of blow does that. A boot does that. A rifle butt does that.

"They took the others," he said at some point, when the brandy had reached whatever part of him still spoke honestly. "I was carrying the accounts. They wanted the accounts. I gave them my blood instead." He almost smiled. "It wasn't enough blood to satisfy them. Clearly."

"Who took whom?"

"Madame." He closed his eyes. "Don't ask me questions. Every answer you get becomes a debt you owe, and I have seen how this regime collects its debts."

The girl had climbed onto the type cabinet near the stove and arranged herself among the paper stock like a cat claiming a warm sill. She watched them with those grave dark eyes. When Theo brought her bread and a little honey scraped from the bottom of the crock, the girl ate with terrible efficiency, as though meals were events that could be canceled at any moment and should never be wasted when they arrived.

"What's your name?" Theo asked her.

The girl considered the question with the seriousness of a magistrate. "Bird," she said finally. "Papa calls me Bird. Because I talk too much."

"You don't talk much at all," Theo said.

"I'm saving it."

Something in Theo's chest cracked along an old fault line. She turned back to the stranger before he could see it.

Outside, the curfew streets lay silent as held breath. Then they weren't.

Boots. Many boots, coming up the Rue des Fondeurs from the harbor end, with the loose unhurried rhythm of men who owned the night and knew it. Lantern light swung across the shutter slats in long yellow bars.

The stranger's eyes opened. He didn't sit up; he simply became still in a way that living men are not usually still, and Theo understood in one cold flash that this man had done this before — gone silent, gone small, gone somewhere else while death walked past close enough to touch.

"There's nowhere," she whispered. "The cellar's full of paper stock, the loft has one stair, the front—"

"The press," he breathed.

She didn't understand until she understood. The old double-crown hand press stood open, its platen raised, its bed pulled out for cleaning as she'd left it that morning. Beneath the bed, in the space where the carriage ran, there was a gap — a foot and a half of darkness between iron and floor, meant for the mechanic's knees, big enough for a man folded wrong. It was a terrible hiding place. It was also the only one.

She stuffed him into it in nine seconds flat, threw the rag and the brandy bottle into the wash bucket, kicked the girl's coat-wrappings under the type cabinet, and had just straightened when the first fist hit the street door.

"Open! In the name of the Provisional Authority!"

She opened it. What else. She opened it wearing her father's old apron over her nightdress, ink to the elbows, and put on her face the particular blankness that women of Vellacourt had spent this whole year perfecting — the face of a person with no opinions, no politics, no memory, no name worth writing down.

Three soldiers and a sergeant filled her doorway. The sergeant was young, which was worse than cruel; young men with power were still surprised by it, and surprised men overcompensated.

"Shop's closed," Theo said. "Curfew."

"We're not shopping." The sergeant pushed past her, lantern high. The light swept the press room — the type cases, the paper racks, the cold stove, the girl sitting motionless on the paper bin with her hands in her lap. "Who's the child?"

"My niece. Her mother's ill. I keep her nights."

"Her mother's ill at midnight?"

"That's usually when illness keeps its appointments, sergeant."

He looked at her. She looked back with the blankness holding, though behind it her heart was doing arithmetic: the wet rag in the bucket, the smell of brandy, the blood — God, the blood, there was blood on the chair, on the floor, a smear on the stove rail—

The lantern swung toward the chair.

"Bird," Theo said lightly, "come here, sweetheart, it's cold by the window."

And Bird — four years old, wrapped in a stolen man's coat, possessing exactly the quantity of sense God gives to sparrows — slid off the paper bin and walked directly between the sergeant and the chair, and looked up at him, and said, in a clear carrying voice:

"Do you know your letters?"

The sergeant blinked. "...What?"

"Letters." She pointed at the type case against the wall, its hundred small compartments of brass-bound lead. "Papa says if you know your letters you can read anything, and if you can read anything nobody can lie to you. Do you know yours?"

For a moment the room balanced on something finer than a knife's edge. Theo heard her own pulse. Behind her, under the press, a man was not breathing.

The sergeant's mouth twitched — not kindly, but distractedly, the way a man twitches when something reminds him of a life he had before the uniform. "A, B, C," he said, mocking, and then, because the absurdity of interrogating a four-year-old had punctured whatever suspicion had brought him, he straightened and became official again. "Lock your door, madame. There are criminals abroad tonight. Dangerous ones. Anyone comes to your door, you send for the garrison. Understand?"

"Perfectly," Theo said.

He dropped a printed notice on the counter — a curfew proclamation, cheap paper, bad imposition, she noted professionally even as her stomach clenched — and they clattered out, boots fading down toward the harbor.

Theo locked the door, put her forehead against its cold boards, and allowed herself ten seconds of shaking.

Then she knelt and let Luc — though she did not yet know his name — crawl out from under the press.

* * *

He would not stay till morning. She saw that in him as soon as he was upright, swaying against the press frame, one hand pressed to his ribs.

"They'll come back," he said. "Or worse ones will. I've brought you nothing but danger, and I'm sorry for it, but I couldn't—" His voice failed and restarted. "There was nowhere else. Everyone else is taken, or bought, or dead. Your father's shop was the one place in Vellacourt I trusted."

"You knew my father?"

"I knew his work. Everyone in this city who reads anything true knows his work." He looked at the press with something like reverence. "And I know the son runs it now."

"The brother," Theo said. Something in her voice must have carried, because his eyes flicked to her face and away.

"The brother," he agreed carefully.

He crouched, wincing, and unslung the leather satchel he had somehow kept through everything — through the beating, through the flight, through the crawling under her press. From it he drew a book. Not a pamphlet, not a notebook. A ledger, bound in scarred brown calfskin, its corners worn round, its spine cracked from use. Heavy. Full.

He held it out to her.

"No," Theo said.

"You have to take it."

"I don't even know your name. I don't know what's in it. I have a child asleep on my paper stock and a business that survives because nobody important remembers it exists, and you're asking me to—"

"He'll come back for it." The stranger glanced at Bird, and his face did something complicated and unbearable. "In a few days, if I live. If I don't—" He swallowed. "Then she needs someone. And the book needs someone. And you're the same someone, madame. That's why I'm here. I've thought about this for six days, walking, hiding, bleeding, and every road ended at this door."

"Take her with you, then."

"With me?" For the first time he laughed, and it was a horrible sound, a hinge rusting. "Madame, look at me. I am a letter being delivered. Letters don't carry children. Either I reach my destination or I'm destroyed in transit — but she doesn't have to be either, if she stays here."

Theo looked at the ledger in his outstretched hands. Calfskin, worn soft. She had grown up around books of account; her father had kept his orders and wages in ledgers just such as these, and she had learned her letters copying headings in them. This object was familiar as bread. And yet it seemed to radiate something — weight, heat, menace — like a stove banked for the night, still dangerous hours after the fire dies.

"Forget my face," the stranger said quietly. "That's the price of the book. Whatever happens, whoever asks, however they ask — you never saw me. You found the child on your step. You found the ledger beside her. You know nothing else. Can you swear that?"

"Why?" she asked. "Whose names are in it?"

He was silent a moment. The lamp gutted and steadied.

"Names," he said, "that people killed to keep out of print."

He set the ledger on the counter beside her father's old cutting gauge, squared it neatly with two fingers — the unconscious gesture of a clerk, a man of orderly habits driven into disorder — and stepped back.

"Days," he said. "Maybe a week. If I haven't come by the time the fleet sails, burn it. Burn it and forget everything, and raise the girl, and live. Promise me that too."

"I promise nothing," Theo said. "Promises are also debts."

He looked at her then — really looked, the first time — and something moved behind his eyes, some weary recalculation ending in a conclusion he didn't share. He bent to Bird, who had woken fully now and was watching him with her grave sparrow eyes, and kissed the top of her head, and murmured something into her hair too low for Theo to hear.

Then he was at the shutter. Then he was through it. Then the night had him, and the Rue des Fondeurs was empty, and the only evidence that any of it had happened was a smear of blood on her floor, a child on her paper stock, and a ledger on her counter, breathing its own strange heat into the dark.

* * *

Bird went back to sleep the way children do — instantly, completely, curled in the nest of coats Theo built her in the loft, thumb finding mouth, breathing gone small and even. Theo tucked the blanket twice and sat a long time watching her, listening to the silence reassemble itself over the Rue des Fondeurs.

Then she went down and cleaned the blood from the floor, because that was who she was — a woman who cleaned, who ordered, who met the day with her accounts balanced. She scrubbed the boards. She rinsed the rag. She banked the stove embers. All the while the ledger sat on the counter where he had squared it, and she did not look at it, and looking-not-at-it took more strength than all the rest together.

At last there was nothing left between her and it.

She lit a fresh candle, trimmed the wick, and set the book before her. The calfskin was cold. She opened it.

The first pages were innocent — inventory columns, dates, sums in a fine cramped clerk's hand, entries for cloth and wine and coach hire. Smuggling, she thought, or graft, or both; Vellacourt's commerce had always run on twin sets of books. She turned pages. Payments to names, payments from names, initials and figures, a private shorthand threading through the columns like wire through wax.

On the ninth page she found the first entry that stopped her breath: a date, a sum, and beside it, in the margin, in the same clerk's hand but pressed harder, deeper, angrier: *paid — settled in full.*

Beneath it, a name. A name she knew. A fishmonger from the Rue Basse, a broad laughing man who used to give Bird-sized children scraps of salt cod. A man who had disappeared in March, whose stall had been reassigned within the week, whose wife had asked questions until she too stopped being seen.

Theo's hands went cold on the page edges.

She told herself it might mean anything. Ledgers were ambiguous; entries were not verdicts; a merchant's book could record a debt discharged as easily as a life extinguished. She turned the page telling herself this, and the next page, and the next, and the entries accumulated like silt, dates and sums and names and that hard-pressed marginal hand growing more frequent, more certain, until she reached a page near the middle of the book where the clerk's neat script broke formation entirely, and across the top of the column, underlined twice, stood a single word:

*Executions.*

Below it, three names. Below the names, a figure — a sum of money, entered with the same dispassion as the cloth and the wine. And below the figure, authorizing it, closing the transaction the way a signature closes any contract in the civilized world —

a signature she had known since before she could read.

She had learned her letters tracing it. She had watched their father guide their small fingers through its loops on the backs of delivery notes, saying, *Marcel writes his M like a bird in flight, Théo, see the wings.* She had set that signature in type herself, carved a facsimile of it in soft lead at fourteen years old as a birthday joke, and Marcel had kept the little block of type in his coat pocket for years, through school, through the academy, through everything — *so I can stamp my things,* he'd said, *and everyone will know they're mine.*

M. Delacroix.

The candle flame leaned in a draft and righted itself. Somewhere above her, Bird sighed in her sleep, a small settling sound, utterly trusting.

Theo sat very still in the circle of lamplight with her brother's name lying under her fingertip like a blade laid flat on the page, and understood that the door she had unbolted at midnight had not been the shop's door at all, and that there was no version of the world now in which she could go back and choose not to open it.

She turned to the next page, and began to read.
