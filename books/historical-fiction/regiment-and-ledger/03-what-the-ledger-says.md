## 3. What the Ledger Says

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width by the time Theo locked the shutter and turned the bolt twice.

She did not do it because she believed a lock would stop what had come to her door at midnight. She did it because her father had always done it, every night of his life, and because the ritual of it — key in iron, iron turning — was the last ordinary thing she owned. The stranger was gone now, swallowed whole by the curfew streets with his bloodied sleeve pressed to his ribs and his voice still ringing in her skull like a dropped typecase: *forget my face.* As if a face could be forgotten that easily. As if she were a woman who forgot anything.

Upstairs, in the room that had been hers as a girl and was now a stranger child's by accident of war, Bird slept on. Theo had heard nothing from her since the small body had curled into the cot — no whimper, no cry for anyone. A four-year-old abandoned twice in one night, once by the street and once by whatever had come before the street, and she had simply closed her eyes and gone down into sleep like a stone into a well. Theo found it unbearable and admirable in equal measure, and could not decide which feeling to feed.

She carried the candle back down to the shop.

The press sat under its dust sheet like something sleeping, or something pretending to. Her father's forme rack along the east wall. The type cabinets, oak and iron, each drawer labeled in his hand — *Long Primer, Pica, Brevier* — the labels faded but legible, the way dead men's handwriting stays legible long after their opinions have stopped mattering to anyone. She had spent the evening cutting new matrices by lamplight, three orders of wedding announcements and a funeral notice for the harbormaster's mother, measuring her hours in ems the way other women measured theirs in loaves or prayers.

Now she set all of that aside and laid the ledger on the composing stone.

It was a printer's ledger. That was the first thing she understood, before she'd read a single word — she knew it in her hands, in the weight and grain of it, the way you know a fellow tradesman across a market square. Leather covers gone soft at the corners. Sewn binding, good thread. The kind of book her father had kept forty years of accounts in, and the kind of book that outlives the man who keeps it. Someone had cared for this object. Someone had trusted it.

She opened it.

The first pages were almost disappointingly mundane. Columned entries in a clerk's neat copperplate: dates, sums, abbreviations. *V., rec'd. 400 fr. — harbour dues, adj.* *M.L., pd. 120 fr. — silences, two.* Theo read the phrase *silences* twice, telling herself it meant advertising space withheld, or subscriptions cancelled, or any of the hundred anodyne things a ledger might record about a city where everyone had grown careful with their words.

Then she reached the third page and found the cipher.

Not a full cipher — that would have been almost a comfort, a wall she could name and therefore plan against. It was worse than that. It was a working code, the kind clerks use when they must write plainly enough for their masters to read quickly and obscurely enough that a seized book yields nothing useful. Numbers standing in for names. Greek letters marking categories. And threaded through it, recurring like a signature motif in bad music, the abbreviation *ex.* followed by nothing at all — no sum, no explanation, just the bare notation and then a blank space where the accounting should continue.

Theo was the daughter of a printer. She had been solving codes since she was seven years old, because her father had taught her that a compositor reads everything backwards and forwards both, and that language is only a machine, and machines can be taken apart.

*Ex.* She said it aloud to the empty shop, testing the shape of it. Then she stopped saying things aloud.

She worked through the night. This is what she would remember afterward, in the weeks when remembering was all she had: not fear, not even yet grief, but the pure mechanical absorption of the work itself — pencil stub, scrap paper, the candle trimmed twice, her father's old magnifying glass lifted to the page. The numbers resolved first. They were dates, mostly, and amounts, and once — her stomach turning slowly over as she decoded it — a figure in francs beside the notation *head, delivered,* which she chose, for the span of one hour, to read as bounty hunting. It took the second pot of ink and half the candle's life to admit it was not bounty hunting. It was payment for testimony that sent men to the firing range behind the citadel, and the payments were itemized like freight charges.

The Greek letters were simpler. *Α* for officials. *Β* for payments made. *Γ* for payments received. *Δ* —

She set down the pencil when she reached Δ.

Δ was executions. Not payments *for* them, not arrangements *around* them. Executions themselves, entered as line items, dated and initialed, the way her father had entered completed print runs: job done, client satisfied, account settled. There were eleven of them since autumn. Eleven *ex.* notations, eleven dates, and beside each date a coded name that took her twenty minutes apiece to break because whoever built this code had built it well, and because her hands had begun to shake somewhere around the fourth name and shaking hands are poor instruments for close work.

She recognized none of the names on the first pass. Strangers, or names dressed up as strangers. She told herself that too, and got as far as page nineteen before the lie collapsed.

Because there, in the margin of an entry dated the sixth of November — *ex., approved, V.* — were three initials she knew instantly, intimately, the way you know your own street's cobblestones by the feel of them through your boot soles.

*T.M.*

Théodore Marchal, who kept the rope walk near the salt warehouses and had sung bawdy songs at her father's funeral until Marcel laughed so hard he had to sit down on a crate.

*A.R.*

Alphonsine Roux, who sold coffee and chestnuts two doors down and had slipped Theo free cups all through the winter after her father died, saying a shopkeeper feeds her own street or she's no shopkeeper at all.

*É.G.*

Émile Garnier. A compositor. A *compositor* — trained at the case like her, fingers stained to the second knuckle, who had set type for the liberal broadsheets back when such things were printed openly, and who had disappeared in January the way a match disappears: suddenly, completely, with only a smell left behind.

Three neighbors. Three names out of perhaps four thousand souls in the harbor quarter, chosen by chance of geography, and every one of them marked *approved* in a hand Theo now knew better than she wished to.

Because the approving initial was not V.

V appeared elsewhere — Colonel Vassey, she assumed, though the ledger never said so outright; power signs itself least often. But these three, these particular three, these *neighbors* — their execution entries bore a second mark beneath the approval. A counter-signature. The acknowledgment of the officer who had verified the identification, confirmed the arrest, witnessed the sentence. Standard military procedure. Theo knew it was standard procedure because the ledger explained it, in one decoded marginal note, with the crisp pedantry of a man recording how sausage is made.

And the counter-signature, written small and precise in the clerk's cipher, decoded to:

*M. Delacroix, Lt.*

The candle guttered. Theo did not relight it. She sat in the shrinking circle of light with the ledger open on her knees and felt nothing at all for a long time, which frightened her more than any feeling would have.

Marcel.

Her brother, who had learned his letters at this same composing stone. Who had held the stick wrong for a full year and gotten ink in his hair and sworn, at nine years old, standing on a crate so he could reach the press, that when he grew up he would print the truth so loud the whole country would hear it. Their father had laughed and said the press would be his someday. Marcel had kissed their mother's cheek and said no — the truth would be his, and the press was only how you threw it.

He had joined the reform officers' circles at nineteen. Theo had sewn the tricolor cockade onto his coat herself, sitting cross-legged on this very floor while he paced and rehearsed speeches about liberty of the press — *liberty of the press, Théo, imagine it, our trade finally legal instead of merely tolerated.* He had marched in 'the spring of hope,' the year the barricades went up along the Rue Neuve, the year their father printed ten thousand pamphlets overnight and hid the formes under the floorboards when the dragoons came. Marcel had stood on those barricades. She had seen him there, sleeves rolled, shouting himself hoarse.

And now his initials sat in a secret ledger beside the names of Théodore Marchal, who sang at funerals; Alphonsine Roux, who fed her street; Émile Garnier, who set type with beautiful hands.

They hadn't vanished. They had been *processed.* Verified. Approved. Settled. And her brother had signed the settlement.

Theo became aware, gradually, of two facts arriving together. The first was that dawn was coming — a thin grey line under the shutters, the gulls beginning their argument over the fish market. The second was that she had not moved in a very long time, and her legs had gone numb beneath her, and the candle was a puddle of tallow and smoke.

She looked at the ledger differently now. Before, it had been evidence — a thing to be hidden, survived, maybe passed along to someone braver. Now it was a mirror angled at her family, and she could not stop looking into it. She turned back through the pages with new eyes. How many of the Β payments had flowed to M.? How many of the coded names, once broken, would resolve into faces she'd known since childhood? The work of answering stretched ahead of her like a dark street, and she understood, with the flat certainty of the sleep-deprived, that she was going to walk down it regardless. There was no unreading this. There was no version of tomorrow morning in which she set type for wedding announcements again with clean hands and a quiet heart.

A floorboard creaked above her.

She froze, ledger half-closed, listening. Footsteps — small, unsteady, the unmistakable stagger of a very young person navigating an unfamiliar room in the dark. Then silence. Then the footsteps again, descending this time, finding the stairs one cautious step at a time.

Bird appeared in the doorway wrapped in the blanket Theo had put over her, dragging half of it behind her like a tail. Her hair stood up on one side. Her face was swollen with sleep and utterly calm.

She crossed the shop without hesitation, climbed onto the stool beside the composing stone as if she had been assigned that stool by some authority, looked at the open ledger, and said:

"Letters."

Theo blinked. "What?"

Bird pointed one finger at the page, at the dense columns of cipher. "Letters," she repeated, with the patient insistence of the very young confronted by adult slowness. Then she yawned enormously, leaned her whole warm weight against Theo's arm, and within thirty seconds was asleep again, mouth open, breathing the slow breath of a child whose conscience contained nothing heavier than a stolen blanket.

Theo sat very still.

The child of a dead or fled revolutionary, most likely. The stranger's daughter, or his cargo, or his reason. She knew nothing except that this small warm weight against her arm was asleep in a city that executed printers, and that her own brother had countersigned three of the names in the book lying open six inches from the child's sock feet.

She closed the ledger gently. She lifted Bird — the girl didn't stir — and carried her back upstairs, past the press, past the forme rack, past her father's labeled drawers, and laid her in the cot. Bird's hand caught her finger in sleep and held it, briefly, the way drowning men are said to hold things, and then let go.

Downstairs again, Theo washed her face at the basin, cold water, again, again. In the small specked mirror she watched her own expression assemble itself into something she did not recognize: not the shopkeeper's pleasant neutrality she wore for customers, not the sister's softness she wore for Marcel. Something harder being forged underneath, still glowing at the edges.

She thought about Émile Garnier setting type with his beautiful hands, and about the last time she'd seen him, buying paper stock off her at a fair price and joking that soon there'd be no honest presses left in Vellacourt, only honest graves.

She thought about Marcel's face at Sunday dinner, two days from now, if Sunday dinner happened at all.

And she thought about the ledger's final entry — the one she had decoded near dawn, the last line item before the blank pages. Not an execution. Not a payment. A single notation, dated eight days ago:

*Fleet departs — tide, morning. Manifest sealed. V. instructs: nothing leaves the harbour uncounted.*

Something was leaving Vellacourt on a morning tide, in eight days' time, and Colonel Vassey wanted it counted. Gold, probably. Stolen, certainly. And a clerk had died bleeding on her doorstep rather than let it go unrecorded.

Theo blew out the last stub of candle, unlocked the shutter bolts, and opened the shop to the grey morning light and the smell of the harbor.

She needed help. Not muscle — knowledge. There was exactly one person in Vellacourt who could crack a cipher built by clerks, because there was exactly one person who had spent thirty years printing for the men who built them. Odile Ferrand had buried a husband, a press, and possibly a son to this regime, and she had never once, in all that time, been seen to flinch.

Theo put on her coat, tucked the ledger inside it against her ribs, and stepped out into the waking streets — toward the widow Ferrand's door, and whatever debt waited on the other side of it.
