## 4. Marcel's Uniform

He knocked the way he always had — twice, then once more, a rhythm their father had invented for them as children so they could signal each other across the workshop without shouting over the press. Theo stood in the middle of the shop with her hands still smelling of ink and lye, and for one absurd moment she was nine years old again, wiping her palms on her apron before she answered.

She made herself wait three breaths. Then she opened the door.

Marcel filled it in his uniform — the dark blue coat of the Provisional Guard, brass buttons polished to a hard shine, the new epaulettes that said lieutenant where last month there had been nothing at all. He had shaved close. He had grown broader through the shoulders since winter, or perhaps the tailoring simply insisted on it. The sword hung from his hip with the casual weight of something he had learned not to notice.

"Sunday," he said, holding up a bottle of Vellacourt red by its neck like an offering. "As promised."

"As promised," Theo echoed, and stepped aside to let him in.

Bird was sitting on the floor behind the compositor's frame, sorting type scraps into a chipped bowl — Theo's invention to keep the girl's quick fingers occupied and away from the delicate matrices. She looked up when the door opened and studied the stranger in blue with the frank, unblinking assessment of a four-year-old deciding whether the world had produced something interesting.

Marcel crouched down at once, which Theo had not expected. "And who is this?"

"Bird," the girl said. "I'm Bird."

"Are you." He smiled, and it was their father's smile, the easy crooked one, and Theo felt something twist under her ribs. "A fine name for a print shop. Do you know what we make here, Bird?"

"Letters."

"That's right. Letters." He held out his hand, palm flat, and after a moment's consideration Bird placed one of her sorted pieces of type in it — an *m*, Theo saw, lowercase, worn smooth at the shoulders. Marcel turned it over gravely. "An honorable letter. It does more work than almost any other."

"You can put it back now," Bird said, and held out her hand until he did.

* * *

The dinner was small because the dinner was always small: a hen that had cost Theo too much, potatoes roasted in dripping, bread bought fresh that morning from the baker on Rue Saint-Clair who charged her double since the crackdown and met her eyes with an apology he didn't dare speak aloud. Marcel carved at the table because their father had carved at the table, and he did it well, and Theo watched his hands move and hated how much she loved him still.

"The city's quieter," he said. "You've noticed."

"I've noticed fewer customers."

"People are careful with their money." He set down the knife. "That's all it is. Careful seasons come and go. By autumn the port will be busier than ever — there's a contract coming through for naval charts, actually, government work. If you wanted steady commissions—"

"I have steady work."

"Theo." He said her name the way he'd been saying it since he got his commission: gently, with a patience that was worse than argument. "Father built this place with his hands. I know what it means. But the world outside the door has changed, and shops that don't bend—"

"Shops that don't bend break. Yes. You've quoted the colonel to me before."

Something flickered across his face — fast, quickly smoothed, but she caught it. Guilt, maybe. Or the irritation of a man whose borrowed convictions were showing their seams.

"Vassey is not my colonel," Marcel said. "I serve the Provisional Authority. There's a difference."

"There is?"

"He restored order after the riots. Order, Theo. You weren't at the docks in March. You didn't see what the mob—" He stopped himself. Picked up the wine. Poured. His hands were steady; his hands were always steady now. "You keep your press cold, like I asked?"

"My press keeps itself busy enough with wedding notices and shipping lists."

"Good." He drank. "Keep it that way a while longer."

* * *

It happened between the roast and the cheese, the way these things always happen — sideways, without ceremony, while no one is watching for it.

He mentioned the names.

He didn't have to. That was the terrible thing. She hadn't asked, hadn't led him anywhere near it, and yet he set down his glass and said, in the tone of a man discussing weather, "There was trouble this week. Two arrests in the tanners' quarter. And the Rousset brothers, from the cooperage — you remember them, they used to buy their broadsides from Father—"

Theo's hand went still around her fork.

"—they were taken up Tuesday night. Sedition charges. They'll be processed through the tribunal like anyone else." A pause, brief as a skipped stitch. "If they're found guilty, they'll be transported. It's cleaner than it sounds. Better than what would happen to them if the mobs had their way."

Cleaner than it sounds. Theo looked at her brother across the table — the polished buttons, the close-shaved jaw, the voice that had learned to say *taken up* instead of *arrested* and *processed* instead of *tried* — and she thought of the ledger hidden inside the false bottom of the paper chest not six feet from where he sat. Three entries she had read by candlelight until her eyes burned. *R., Étienne. R., Baptiste. Paid. Settled.* Beside each name, in a hand she had known since she was five years old, learning to draw her letters on scrap stock while their father watched: *M. Delacroix.*

"They had children," Theo said. "Étienne Rousset. Four of them. Baptiste's wife is expecting."

"Then let's hope they kept their heads down." He reached for the bread, tore it, buttered it. His appetite was good. His appetite was always good now. "The ones who suffer are the ones who listen to pamphlets and tavern talk. If a man works and holds his tongue, nothing touches him."

"Is that what you believe? Truly? Or is it what you've decided must be true, so you can sleep?"

Marcel looked at her then, really looked, and for a half-second she saw something move behind his eyes — some older boy, some ghost of the young man who used to climb onto the press bed at fourteen and declare that ink was the only honest army. Then it was gone, shuttered, and the officer was back.

"I believe in keeping our family safe," he said. "That's all. Whatever else you think of me, believe that."

Bird, who had finished eating and slid down from her chair, chose that moment to crawl under the table and attach herself to his boot like a burr. She began sorting the buttons of his coat by touch, humming to herself, and Marcel laughed — genuinely laughed — and let her.

Theo watched her brother look down at the child at his feet with an expression of unguarded tenderness, and she thought: *he is not lying because he enjoys it. He is lying because lying is the uniform under the uniform.* And she thought, in the same breath, colder: *and the Roussets are gone regardless.*

* * *

Afterward, while Marcel examined the shelves out of old habit — running one finger along the cases of type, reading the spines of their father's manuals — Bird wandered toward the paper chest.

"No," Theo said, sharper than she intended.

Bird froze, lower lip trembling. Theo crossed the room in two steps, knelt, gathered her up. "Not that chest, little one. That's Papa-work. Here—" she lifted the girl onto her hip and carried her to the compositor's frame, where the sorted scraps waited in their bowl, "—your letters are here. Yours alone."

From across the room, Marcel said, "Papa-work?"

"Ledgers," Theo said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Accounts. Invoices. The dull arithmetic of staying solvent." She turned, smiling, with Bird balanced on her hip. "You wouldn't find a single interesting word in it."

"Mm." He had stopped at the chest. His hand rested on its lid, lightly, absently — the way he used to rest his hand on the tympan of the press while thinking. "Since when do you keep accounts locked?"

"It's an old habit of Father's. The lock came with the chest." She shifted Bird's weight. "Wine?"

"In a minute." He lifted the lid.

The world narrowed to a single point of sound — her own pulse, loud as the press drum. Beneath the loose papers on top, beneath the invoice stack she had arranged that morning as camouflage, the leather spine of the ledger lay flat against the false bottom, and the false bottom was *closed*, she knew it was closed, she had checked it twice—

Marcel lifted out a handful of invoices, glanced at them, set them back. "Shipping lists," he murmured. "You're right. Dull as ditchwater." He closed the lid. The latch clicked, soft as a bone setting.

He turned and smiled at her. "Wine, yes. One glass. Then I'm wanted at the barracks by eight."

* * *

She walked him to the door with Bird on her hip. Outside, the street lay blue in the early evening, lamplighters working their way along the quay road, a patrol's boots ringing somewhere past the fish market — measured, unhurried, the sound of men who owned the hours.

"Lock your shutters early," Marcel said. He touched Bird's head with two fingers, lightly, a benediction. "Both of you."

"Marcel." He paused, hand already turning away. "Do you ever think about the spring? Before the Authority? When you and Odile Ferrand's boys ran those leaflets off Father's press at night — 'Liberty or Lead,' remember? You were sixteen. You signed it *M.D.*, and Father pretended not to know, and you thought you were going to change the world with a hand-pulled lever and forty copies."

For a moment he stood very still in the doorway. The lamplight caught the brass at his collar.

"I was a boy," he said quietly. "Boys believe ink can stop bullets." He kissed her cheek — dry, quick, their father's gesture exactly — and stepped down into the street. "Keep the press cold, Theo. Please. For once in your life, just do as you're told."

Then he was walking away, blue coat fading into the blue dusk, and Bird waved at his back with her whole arm until Theo turned inside and threw the bolt.

* * *

She did not open the chest that night. She couldn't. Instead she banked the lamp low and sat in the rocking chair by the cold stove with Bird asleep against her shoulder, and she let herself remember — because grief demands its due, and better to pay it now than at three in the morning.

Their father had been a patient man with metal and an impatient man with everything else. He'd taught them both at the same stone, the summer Theo was seven and Marcel eleven: *here is the face of the letter, see, the printing face, and here the nick, and here the groove — the groove lets air in, so the type won't suck free when you lift it. Everything honest has a groove in it somewhere, Théo. A place for the truth to breathe.*

Marcel had set his first line at nine — *THE PRESS IS THE PEOPLE'S VOICE*, misspelled, reversed in the stick so it printed backward and they had laughed until their ribs ached. Their father hadn't corrected the spelling for a full day. Let the boy live in the error, he said. He'll never forget it that way. And he never did. Twenty years later, standing in a tribunal antechamber waiting to be decorated, Marcel had told the story to Colonel Vassey himself, laughing, and Theo had heard him through the door.

She remembered the nights of '44, when the three of them worked the press by turns to run off the reform petitions — Father at the lever, Marcel feeding sheets, Theo inking, the four of them (counting the press, Father always counted the press) moving like one body. She remembered Father's hand on Marcel's shoulder afterward, ink to the elbow, saying: *This machine will outlive us all, and everything we put through it will still be true when we're dust. Mind what you make true.*

*Mind what you make true.*

And now her brother wore the uniform of the men who burned presses. Now he said *taken up* and *processed* and *cleaner than it sounds*, and tore his bread with a good appetite, and lied about cooperage men with four children between them as easily as he'd once set a line of type.

But — and here was the thought she kept circling, the splinter under the skin — he had hesitated. Half a second at the table, when she'd asked if he believed it. Half a second at the door, when she'd said *Odile Ferrand's boys*. The brother she remembered was still in there somewhere, grooved deep like the letterform, a place where the truth could still breathe. She had to believe that. Not for sentiment. For strategy.

Because tomorrow — or the day after, when she could arrange it, when Odile could help her decode what the cipher pages held — she was going to need to know which brother would answer when she laid the ledger open on this table and said *look at your own signature*.

Bird stirred against her shoulder, murmured something about letters, slept on.

Outside, far off toward the citadel, a drum began — the night patrol changing watch, ten o'clock, regular as a press cycle. Theo rocked slowly in the dark and listened to the rhythm of the occupation keeping time, and she thought about lead: how soft it was in the hand, how heavy in the form, how it took any impression pressed upon it and held that impression forever, unless it was melted down and cast again.

Everything honest has a groove in it somewhere.

Her brother's groove, she suspected, was deeper than his polish. She meant to find it — and if she couldn't, she meant, God forgive her, to cut one.

The lamp guttered. In the paper chest across the room, three names waited under lock with her own family name written beside them, and outside, the drums kept their cold time, counting down toward Sunday's end.
