## 5. The Compositor's Union

The address Odile Ferrand had given Theo — scratched in pencil on the back of a pawn ticket, pressed into her palm three nights ago by a woman she'd never seen before and hadn't dared to follow — was not an address at all. It was a door. Rue Saint-Aubin, between a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and a shuttered wine merchant whose windows still bore the chalk ghosts of prices no one could pay anymore. The door itself was blue once, now gray, with a brass knocker shaped like a fish that had been rubbed featureless by decades of hands.

Theo stood before it for a long moment, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth beneath her shawl, flat against her ribs like a second heart. She had rehearsed what she would say. She had rehearsed it on the walk over, past the barricade rubble on Cours Napoleon that the city crews had cleared too quickly, as if the stones themselves embarrassed the authorities, past the new garrison posters that flapped wetly in the morning wind: ORDER RESTORED. REWARD FOR INFORMATION.

*Information.* The word had followed her like a dog all week.

She knocked. Nothing. She knocked again, harder, and heard — or imagined she heard — movement above, the creak of a floorboard weighted just so.

"The shop is closed," said a voice through the wood. Old, dry, unhurried. "It has been closed since 1834."

"I need a compositor," Theo said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "My father was Henri Delacroix. Of Delacroix & Son, Printers, Quai des Salines."

A pause long enough to count one's own heartbeats in. Then, from somewhere behind the door, the dry voice again:

"Henri Delacroix set his first forme at fourteen and never learned to leave his politics out of the type case. They hanged a man in Lyon for less than your father printed." Another pause. "Come in, then, before someone sees you standing there like a confession."

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

Inside was not a shop. It was a tomb that had been pretending to be one for fifteen years. Dust lay over everything in a fine gray velvet — over the stacks of paper gone soft-edged with damp, over the iron frame of a hand press that stood against the far wall like a sleeping animal, over the glass-fronted cases where compositors' tools hung in their places, each handle aligned to the same angle, kept true by a hand that no longer used them but could not bear to see them crooked.

Odile Ferrand closed the door and threw the bolt, then turned to look at her visitor.

She was smaller than Theo had expected, a woman built like a sparrow, all bone and quickness, with white hair pinned up carelessly and eyes that were the color of the dust itself — pale, watchful, missing nothing. She wore a widow's black gone greenish with age, and her hands, when she folded them, bore the specific calluses of fifty years at the trade: the ridge along the right forefinger from the composing stick, the burn scars on the left wrist from hot metal, the ink that lived in the creases of her knuckles no matter how she scrubbed.

"You're the daughter," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the printer." Theo unwound her shawl, then thought better of it and pulled it tighter instead. "Or I have been, under my brother's name. Since my father died."

"Since your brother went into the uniform, you mean, and the family needed a man's name on the license." Odile's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Yes, I know. I know more than you'd think, girl, and less than you'd hope. Sit down. That chair holds; the other one is a liar."

Theo sat. The chair held. From somewhere in the back rooms came a faint scratching — a rat, or a board settling, or something else entirely — and Odile cocked her head toward it without alarm, as if it were a clock striking.

"Now," she said. "You've brought me something to read."

Not *something to show*. Something to read. Theo felt the ledger's weight shift against her ribs.

"How did you—"

"Because nobody comes to this door after fifteen years of it being dead unless they're carrying something they can't carry anywhere else. Because you looked left and right twice before knocking, which means you're being followed in your mind even if you aren't being followed in fact. And because your hands are shaking and you're hiding them in your skirt like a schoolgirl who's torn her hem." Odile dragged a stool to the middle of the room with her foot and sat down across from Theo, knees nearly touching. "So. Show me."

Theo hesitated one moment longer. It was not fear exactly — or not only fear. It was the strange reluctance of a person about to hand over the last private thing she owned. For four days the ledger had been hers alone, its terrible arithmetic hers alone, Marcel's name beside those three vanished neighbors hers alone to carry. Once it left her hands, it would belong to whatever this was — this dusty tomb, this sharp-eyed widow, this network of pamphleteers she had only been told existed in whispers.

Then she remembered Bird asleep that morning, curled in the paper bin with a fistful of sorted type scraps clutched like treasure, and Luc Arnaud's face the night he'd said *forget my face*, blood sheeting down from his scalp into his collar. She remembered the three names. Madame Roux, who sold fish. The Vasseur brothers, who ran the rope walk. Gone since autumn, and the whole street careful not to say their names aloud, the way people step around a body.

She unwrapped the ledger and put it in Odile Ferrand's lap.

* * *

The old woman read the way she did everything else — fast, precise, without wasted motion. Her cracked lips moved slightly over some entries. At others her fingers hovered above the page as though the numbers might be felt rather than seen. Theo watched her face for the shock she herself had felt on the first night, the cold drop when she'd found Marcel's signature, but Odile gave nothing away except a slow tightening around the eyes, like a sail taking wind.

"Cipher," she said at last. "Numbers paired with letters, letters keyed to a word. See here — the recurring group, *seven-three-one-nine*, appearing at the head of every entry? That's the key indicator. The cipher turns on a word, probably French, probably common, chosen so any clerk could remember it without writing it down." She tapped the page. "Whoever built this book wanted it readable by half a dozen men and no one else. Which means the key word lives in six heads, and we only need one."

"Can you break it?"

"Given time? Almost certainly. Given time and a sample of known plaintext, certainly. Given—" she paused, and her pale eyes came up and fixed on Theo with uncomfortable weight, "—given that I decide to spend the time. Which brings us to the part of the evening where I ask you questions and you answer them honestly, because everything after depends on it."

"Ask."

"Who wrote this?"

"A clerk named Luc Arnaud. He copied it from the originals. He says the originals are Colonel Vassey's — records of payments, of estates confiscated from executed prisoners, of gold moved through the harbor under requisition papers." Theo heard her own voice go flat and careful, reciting facts to keep the fear underneath from rising. "He was wounded bringing it out. He left it with me because—" she stopped.

"Because a print shop is the one place in this city where a book can be made into ten thousand books," Odile finished. "Yes. Your father would have understood the request immediately. So would mine." She turned a page, stopped. Turned back. "This signature. Delacroix. Is that your Marcel?"

Theo's throat worked. "Yes."

"And do you understand what that makes you, in the eyes of anyone who learns this book exists?"

"Yes."

"Do you?" Odile leaned forward, and for the first time there was heat in her voice, banked coals raked open. "Because I don't think you do. You think you've found a dangerous document. You haven't. You've found a debt. Knowledge is money in this city now, girl, and like all debts it gets collected — with interest, and by whichever creditor reaches you first. Vassey will collect it if he finds it. His enemies will collect it. The tribunal will collect it. Even the revolution, if it comes, will collect it, and the revolution collects with a rougher hand than the colonel does, whatever the pamphlets say. Everyone who reads these pages becomes collateral. Everyone who helps you becomes collateral. The child you're keeping — is that the clerk's child?"

"No one knows whose child she is. He wouldn't say."

"Then she's yours now, and she's collateral too." Odile closed the ledger gently, both hands, the way one closes a coffin. "I'm not telling you this to frighten you off. I'm telling you so that when the bill comes due — and it will come due, within the month, I promise you that — you cannot say no one told you the price."

Theo sat very still. Through the dusty window, high and small, a bar of morning light had crawled across the floor and now lay across the sleeping press like a blade laid down.

"My father," she said slowly, "printed a pamphlet in 'thirty. After the riots here. He printed it in one night, two thousand copies, and by noon every gendarme in Vellacourt was looking for him, and by evening he was setting type again for next week's run. My mother begged him to stop. He told her — I was small, but I remember — he told her that a press that only prints what's safe isn't a press, it's a decoration." She looked up. "Is that why you opened the door? The name Delacroix?"

Odile studied her for a long moment. Something in her sparrows' face shifted, softened by a degree, the way frost shifts before it melts.

"Your father carried my husband's forme to safety the night the dragoons came for our shop in 'thirty-four. Edmond was already dead — fever, nothing romantic — but they wanted his type, his press, his subscriber lists. Henri Delacroix hid forty pounds of lead and a man's whole life's work under sacks of flour while soldiers drank his coffee in the next room." She rose, stiffly, joints cracking like her door hinges. "So yes, girl. Debts run both directions in this trade. Sit there. Touch nothing. I'm going to fetch paper and ink, and we are going to begin."

"Begin what?"

"Breaking your cipher." Over her shoulder, already moving toward the back rooms, her voice dry as the dust on her press. "And then, God help us both, deciding what gets printed when we do."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the gray beginning of evening. Odile's method was patient and merciless: she copied recurring number-groups onto strips of paper, laid them side by side like a compositor sorting sorts, hunted for the repetitions that betrayed a keyed alphabet. Theo transcribed, checked, re-copied. Twice Odile sent her into the cellar for candle stubs and once, when Theo's hand cramped, made her flex her fingers and recite the alphabet backward until the cramp passed — *a craftsman's hands are her capital, girl, and you let them rust at your peril*.

The key, when it began to surface, surfaced the way wreckage surfaces — a shape here, a shape there, meaning assembling itself out of noise.

"It's a word cipher built on a liturgical calendar," Odile murmured, squinting at her paper strips in the failing light. "Clever. Every clerk in the garrison offices knows the saints' days by rote. Look — the seven-three group corresponds to the feast of Saint Michael. September twenty-ninth. The entries cluster around quarter-days and holy days. Payments made when the bells ring." She laughed once, short and bitter. "They laundered blood through the Church calendar. Men like this always do."

By full dark they had enough to read fragments. Not the whole — Odile was explicit that fragments could lie, that a broken cipher half-guessed would tell you what you feared most and dress it as fact. But the fragments were enough. A payment of twelve hundred francs entered under a name Theo didn't recognize. An instruction, *the rope-walk matter to be concluded quietly*. The word *quietly* appearing eleven times in nine pages.

Eleven times. Theo counted them herself, twice, because she wanted to be wrong.

"That's the Vasseur brothers' entry," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "That's the entry near Marcel's signature."

Odile said nothing for a moment. In the candlelight her face was carved from the same material as her press — iron, patience, weight.

"There's a question you're circling," she said finally, "and you'll sleep better tonight if you ask it now instead of at three in the morning, which is when it will come anyway."

Theo's hands lay flat on the table, ink-stained, trembling only slightly. She made herself say it.

"If my brother signed those entries — if his name is on orders that ended with the rope-walk brothers disappearing — then when we print this book, when we break it open for the whole city to read—" She stopped. Started again. "Am I destroying him?"

"You're asking me whether truth has a discount for family." Odile gathered the paper strips together, squared their edges with a practiced tap, and slid them into a tin box. "No. It doesn't. But hear me, and hear me carefully, because this is the one thing I know for certain after sixty years and a dead husband: a man who signs his name to a thing has already answered for it. You would not be destroying your brother. You would be letting him be what his own hand made him." She stood, took up a candle, and nodded toward the door. "Now go home. Check the streets before you step out — left, then right, then left again. Take the long way. And come back Thursday, after dark, with the ledger. There are men I need to show this to, and they only meet after midnight, and they will want to know whether the daughter of Henri Delacroix has the stomach for what comes next."

Theo wrapped the ledger back in its oilcloth. At the door, her hand on the bolt, she paused.

"Madame Ferrand. The pamphleteers. Your network. If I do this — if we print — what happens to us?"

Odile smiled then, fully, and it was the most frightening expression Theo had ever seen on a human face: tenderness and iron in equal measure.

"What always happens," she said. "Something ends, and something begins, and neither one is what we expected. Go home to your child. Mind the corners."

Theo stepped out into the dark of Rue Saint-Aubin, checked left, checked right, and began the long way home through streets that seemed, tonight, to have grown new shadows at every corner. Behind her the blue-gray door closed and bolted. Ahead, somewhere across the sleeping city, a church bell rang the hour — and she found herself counting, involuntarily, matching each stroke to a saint's day, a payment, a name.

She was still counting when she reached her own door, and it was only inside, with the bolt drawn and the lamp lit and Bird stirring sleepily in the back room, calling her name in a voice thick with dreams, that Theo admitted what she had known since the moment Odile Ferrand's pale eyes had fixed on hers across that dusty table.

There was a light burning in the window of her own shop. A lamp she had not lit.

Someone was waiting for her.
