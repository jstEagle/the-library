## 6. Bird Learns the Shop

The child woke before the gulls did.

Theo heard her through the floorboards — small feet padding down the stair from the loft, the careful negotiation of each step, hand on rail — and by the time Theo came down with the lamp still lit against the grey of dawn, Bird was already standing at the composing stone in her borrowed nightgown, staring up at the cases like a congregation at an altar.

"You'll wake the neighbors," Theo said.

Bird didn't turn around. She had a way of not turning around that was less disobedience than gravity; whatever held her attention held all of her. "Which one is A?" she asked.

Theo set the lamp on the stone and looked at the type case, its hundred small compartments, its brass labels worn to ghosts by her father's thumb. Twenty years she'd known that face of the case the way other people knew their own hands. "The upper left," she said. "Capital A. Lowercase lives two rows down."

"Show me."

It was not a request a four-year-old should have been able to make with such authority. Theo lifted her down onto the stool anyway, wrapped her father's old apron twice around the small body so it wouldn't drag in the ink, and put Bird's fingers on the compartment edge. "Feel how the type faces up? The letter is on the *foot*, do you see — no, the top, the printing face. We never touch the face. Oil from skin ruins it. We hold it here." She guided the child's thumb and forefinger to the correct grip. "Like a little bird. Not too tight or it can't sing."

"What does it say?"

"It says nothing until it's with the others. One piece of type alone is just metal. Set them together and they speak."

Bird considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. Then she reached into the A compartment, extracted a single sort with the exact clumsy care Theo had shown her, and held it up to the lamplight. "*A*," she announced, as though she had pulled a living thing out of the earth.

Outside, Vellacourt was waking badly. The fish carts came late now, and the harbor bells rang wrong — two strokes where three belonged, some signal Theo didn't yet know how to read. Soldiers had been seen at the Rue des Ancres. Bread was short. But inside the shop the air smelled of lead and oil and yesterday's ink, and a child was holding the letter A up to the light like a coin she'd found in the street, and for perhaps half a minute Theo let herself forget everything else.

Then the kettle went on the stove, the shutters came open, and the day began.

* * *

By seven Bird had learned three things: that A lived upper-left, that the letters were called sorts and not "the little metal words," and that if she sat very still on the stool with the apron around her like a tent, no one would make her go back upstairs. By eight she had learned to sort.

It happened by accident, which was how most true things in that shop happened. Theo was setting a jobbing order — invoices for the chandler on Quai Neuf, forty copies, dull work but honest — and the galley of broken type beside her had grown into a chaos of mixed sorts: e's jumbled with t's, a stray ampersand, three em quads rolling loose like marbles. Bird watched her distribute them, finger flying case to case, and after the third pass said, "I could do the round ones."

"The round ones?"

"O. And the ones like O but fat."

"B and D," Theo said. "And Q has a tail."

"I could do those."

Theo should have said no. There was a voice in her — her mother's voice, dead these nine years — that said children belonged in lofts and kitchens, not among the tools of trade. But her father's shop had never once turned away a pair of willing hands, and there was something in the girl's face, that absolute gravity again, that made refusal feel like closing a door on weather.

"Only these five compartments," Theo said, tapping them. "O, B, D, P, Q. Nothing else. If you're not sure, it goes back in the pile."

Bird sorted for two hours. Her method was slow and solemn and almost entirely wrong — the B's migrated, the Q's lost their tails somewhere behind the stone — but her hands were quick and her attention did not wander, and by the time the chandler's boy came for the invoices, a rough order had emerged from the scrap pile that was better than nothing. Theo corrected it after she left, quietly, without comment, and found she was smiling at the type case like a fool.

She stopped smiling when she counted the till and remembered what the week looked like. Eleven francs, and rent due in nine days. Two jobs on the books. The regime's censors had taken most of the commercial work off independent presses since spring — anything with more than a page required a stamp now, and stamps cost money and questions — and what remained was invoices, funeral notices, and prayer cards. Enough to keep the press cold, Marcel would have said. Exactly enough to starve slowly instead of quickly.

And then there was Bird. Bird who ate like a sparrow but ate every day. Bird who needed shoes by winter, whose nightgown had come to her already worn through at the elbow, who had arrived on the doorstep eleven days ago wrapped in a stranger's coat with a ledger full of dead men's names. No papers. No parish record Theo could find. No claim anyone official would honor — a woman alone, unmarried, keeping a print shop under a dead brother's name, petitioning to keep a child nobody owned? The magistrate's clerk would laugh her into the street, or worse, start asking why a printer's sister had a strange child at all. Questions moved downhill in Vellacourt now, and they ended at Vassey's desk.

Guardianship, Theo thought, sweeping the floor while Bird napped in the paper bin — she'd claimed it herself, dragging in the soft misprints like a cat arranging straw — guardianship was a word men used when they meant property, and women used when they meant fear.

* * *

Madame Roux came at noon for her husband's memorial cards — third reprinting, the good stock, because Aldous Roux had been a churchwarden and his widow had standards — and stayed, as she always stayed, to conduct her survey of the street.

"They took the Lemaire boy Monday," she reported, settling onto the customer's bench with the sigh of a woman who enjoyed bad news properly delivered. "Gendarmes, plainclothes, in daylight. His mother screamed at them and they only smiled. Smiled, Théo! Like it was a joke they were all in on."

"Was he—"

"Printing. Of course printing. Little pamphlets, someone said. Stupid boy." Madame Roux fanned herself with the proof slip. "And soldiers at the end of the quay asking after ships' manifests. My cousin's son is in the harbor office; he says they want to know what leaves port and when. Everything's being counted. People, paper, bread. Counting is what they do before—" She stopped, glanced at the bin where Bird slept, and lowered her voice to the register women used for dangerous subjects near children. "There's talk too. About this street. About this shop."

Theo kept her hands moving, washing the ink plate, though the water had gone cold hours ago. "What kind of talk."

"A man came into the confiserie yesterday. Officer's coat, not local. Bought sugared almonds — sugared almonds, at a time like this — and asked Madame Gallet, ever so pleasant, who printed her wedding announcements. And then whether the Delacroix shop was still taking work. Whether the press ran at night. He asked about your brother, too." Madame Roux's eyes were sharp over the fan. "By name."

"Everyone knows Marcel. He grew up on this street."

"He grew up on this street wearing a schoolboy's coat. He wears a captain's now, and captains don't get asked about by strangers buying sweets unless somebody above them wants to know the answer." She rose, gathered her cards, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame — and for a moment her face softened into something older than gossip. "Your father printed my wedding announcement, God rest him. Forty-one years I've walked past this door. Whatever trouble is coming to it, you send that child up to me first. You hear me, Théo? First. Before you're brave."

She left. Theo stood a long while with her wet hands and her cold water, looking at the door.

Before you're brave. As if bravery were a thing you chose to be, like early rising. As if she hadn't spent eleven nights sleeping with a leather ledger under the loose hearthstone and a knife under her pillow, telling herself both were temporary arrangements.

* * *

The afternoon brought rain, and with the rain, no customers, and Theo gave the hours to Bird and the type.

It was her father's old method, she realized — the one he'd used on her, on Marcel, back when the shop was warm and the worst thing in Vellacourt was the price of Spanish paper. He'd taught them letters through the case itself. *A is a ladder, M is two mountains, S is a snake that decided to behave.* Bird took to it with an appetite that frightened Theo a little, the way hunger frightens you when you realize how long it's been fed. By four o'clock the girl could pick out a dozen letters by shape alone, holding each sort up to the window light, reading the world backward the way printers did.

"Why is it backward?" Bird asked, squinting at a capital R.

"So that when it kisses the paper, it comes out right."

"Everything in here is backward?"

"Everything worth saying."

Bird chewed on that. Then: "Is Papa in here?"

Theo's hand stilled on the stone. Eleven days, and the question had waited, coiled, patient as a trap. "What do you remember about your papa?"

Bird shrugged, four years old, devastating. "Big coat. He smelled like the sea and like blood sometimes. He said hush a lot. He said—" her brow worked, "—he said the lady with the letters would keep me. He said that on the stairs. Before the knocking."

The knocking. Theo had told herself she'd forgotten the sound of it — fists on shutter at midnight, the patrol's boots in the lane, the stranger's blood on her hands as she pushed him through the cellar hatch. Apparently the child had kept it too, filed away behind those grave eyes, waiting for a rainy Tuesday to spend it.

"Yes," Theo said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "He said that."

"Are you the lady with the letters?"

"I'm the lady with the letters."

Bird nodded, satisfied, and returned to her sorting, and Theo turned to the wash sink and stood with her back to the child for a long moment, doing nothing, letting the rain fill the silence.

A woman with no standing and no money, keeping a promise made in blood on her own doorstep by a man she couldn't name to authorities she couldn't trust. If the officers were already asking after the shop — and they were; Madame Roux's sugar-almond man was only the first ripple of something larger — then Bird was not a secret Theo was keeping. Bird was evidence. A child unregistered in a printer's house, in a quarter under watch, with a brother in Vassey's uniform and a ledger under the hearthstone. Every tenderness was also a risk now. That was the arithmetic of the year: love weighed in danger, like everything else.

But watching Bird hold a lowercase *e* up to the grey light, tongue between her teeth, utterly certain she was doing important work — watching that, Theo found the arithmetic refused to balance any way except forward.

* * *

At dusk she climbed to the loft where Bird slept and sat on the stair while the child drifted off, and afterward she went down alone and pried up the hearthstone.

The ledger lay where it always lay, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as a debt. She didn't open it. She had learned that much about herself in eleven days: she could not open it in the evenings anymore. Its ciphered columns waited for Odile Ferrand, for a decoder's patience she hadn't yet summoned the courage to seek out — because seeking Odile out meant becoming something more than a woman hiding a book. It meant joining the network of pamphleteers, the underground Odile was rumored to run out of her dead husband's old bindery, and knowledge, everyone said, was a debt that got collected.

Instead Theo sat by the banked stove with the ledger's weight against her knee through the cloth, and thought about the officer buying sugared almonds, and about the Lemaire boy who had printed pamphlets and smiled at from the street by men who would hang him by Friday if the rumors held, and about her brother, whom she had not seen in six days, whose last visit had ended with him standing in this same doorway saying *keep the press cold, Théo, I mean it* in a voice she had never heard him use before — not commanding. Afraid.

A knock came at the shutter.

Her whole body went to iron. Rain ticked on the glass. The knock came again — twice, soft, civilian. She crossed the room, took the knife from behind the flour crock, and eased the shutter open an inch.

Odile Ferrand stood in the rain with her shawl over her head, sixty years old and built like a bollard, and her eyes went past Theo's shoulder into the shop with a professional's speed — the cold press, the scattered sorts, the paper bin with its blanket still shaped by a sleeping child.

"You've got the girl still," Odile said. Not a question.

"For now."

"Then listen fast, because I won't say this twice on an open street." Rain ran off the brim of her shawl. "A friend of mine works the guard post on the Rue des Ancres. Tonight's rotation includes a list. Shops to be visited, warrants pending. Yours is on it. Not for tonight — maybe not this week — but it's on the list, Delacroix, written down in a colonel's own hand. They're calling it a paperwork inspection." Her mouth twisted. "Vassey's paperwork inspections find pamphlets in bakeries and muskets in christening fonts. Whatever you're sitting on under that hearthstone — and don't insult me, I've watched this street thirty years and I know a woman carrying weight when I see one — whatever it is, it needs to move, or you need to move, and neither happens by lamplight and luck."

Theo gripped the shutter's edge. Through the wall she could hear Bird breathing in her sleep, small and even, a metronome counting down something.

"Why tell me?" Theo asked. "You don't know me. You knew my father. That was twenty years ago."

Odile's face, wet and seamed in the lamplight, did something complicated — grief and assessment and a kind of grim recognition passing across it like clouds.

"Because your father hid three of mine in that cellar in 'thirty and never asked their names," she said. "And because I've been waiting eleven days for you to come to me, and you haven't, and I'm too old to wait for pride to starve. Tomorrow morning. The old Ferrand bindery, off the Tanner's Walk. Come before eight, come alone, and bring the thing you can't open." She stepped back into the rain. "Or don't, and take your chances with the paperwork inspection. But decide tonight, Delacroix. In this city now, deciding slowly is the same as deciding wrong."

She melted into the dark, and the rain closed behind her like water over a stone.

Theo stood in the doorway a long time, knife still in her hand, listening to the breathing behind the wall. Above the shop the clouds broke for a moment and the harbor lights showed far off, counting themselves — ships, soldiers, names on lists. Somewhere in that arithmetic was a colonel's handwriting with her shop in it.

Tomorrow, then. Before eight. Alone.

She closed the shutter, banked the stove, and touched the hearthstone once with her foot, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you were about to wake.
