## 7. The Man Who Forgot His Face

Luc Arnaud had learned, in the Ministry's service, that a man's name was the first thing taken from him and the last thing returned.

For eleven days he had been no one at all — a shape moving through Vellacourt's back lanes with his collar up and his hat pulled low, sleeping in the loft of a cooperage that smelled of oak shavings and stale beer, eating bread bought from three different bakers so no single one would remember him. He had learned to walk like a man going somewhere dull and unavoidable, never hurrying, because hurry drew the eye of patrols. He had learned that his own face, seen in dark windows, looked like a stranger's already: cheekbone bruised yellow-green where the corporal's baton had found it on the first night, jaw rough with a beard he'd never worn in his life as a clerk with clean hands and a desk by the east window.

He had been Émile Fournier's assistant for six years. He had been nobody. That was the whole point of him — a man whose signature appeared nowhere, whose presence in rooms was furniture-like, who carried papers between offices and was not expected to have opinions about what the papers contained.

Then Émile had died with a bayonet wound in his belly and a name list in his coat, and Luc had understood that being nobody was no longer protection. It was only a slower way to die.

* * *

He came back to the print shop on the Rue Saint-Aubin on the eleventh night because he had run out of every other night.

The curfew bell had gone at nine. He moved along the walls of shuttered houses, keeping to the black seam where the lantern light didn't reach, and the whole city felt like a held breath — doors barred behind him, dogs silent, even the harbor gulls quiet as if they too had been ordered indoors. Twice he heard boots: a patrol crossing the Place des Orfèvres, their lantern swinging a slow pendulum of light across the cobbles. Both times he stood inside a doorway with his heart hammering against his ribs like a fist on a locked door, counting seconds, waiting for the light to pass over him and move on.

The wound in his side had opinions of its own now. The knife had gone in shallow — he'd been lucky, or unlucky; he hadn't decided which — but it had festered. Walking, each step sent a hot wire of pain up through his hip into his lower back. His shirt clung to him, stiff and stinking. Fever came in waves that made the streets swim and straighten and swim again, so that he wasn't always sure whether the shapes ahead of him were men or shadows or memories.

And still, beneath all of it, beneath the fever and the fear, there was the ledger. He could feel it the way a man feels a debt — not on his body but in it. Forty-one names. Eleven estates. The neat columns in Vassey's own hand, or near enough his hand that no court would question it. Executed men's property, seized before their bodies were cold, sold at auction to officers' cousins for a tenth of worth, the difference flowing into accounts Luc himself had balanced, page after page, year after year, without once asking where the dead men had gone.

He knew where they had gone. That was the thing he could not stop knowing now. He had spent six years not-knowing it, professionally, elegantly, and one evening in October Émile had laid the ledger open on his desk and said, *read it, Arnaud, read it all, and tell me you can keep balancing these figures*, and the not-knowing had ended forever.

Émile was dead. The ledger had come to him. And so the debt had come to him, transferred like an inheritance he'd never wanted.

The print shop's shutters were closed, as they were every night, and above them the painted sign caught what little light the sky gave: DELACROIX & FILS, IMPRIMEUR. He stood across the street in the shadow of a cooper's doorway and looked at that lie of a sign — *and sons*, when everyone on the street knew the sons were one man in a uniform he'd put on four years ago, and the shop itself survived on the hands of a woman whose name appeared nowhere at all.

A woman whose name appears nowhere, he thought. A compositor. Someone who sets other people's words in metal and lets the words take the credit. Someone who knows how to make a thing true and durable and multiplied past any power's ability to burn every copy.

That was why her door. Not chance. He'd chosen it the way Émile would have chosen it — deliberately, with a clerk's cold arithmetic. She had the skill. She had the press. She had, as far as he could learn, nothing left to lose except the shop, and a shop was a smaller thing than forty-one names.

And she had already kept his secret once. Eleven nights ago, bleeding on her doorstep, he had pressed the bundle into her arms and said the words he'd rehearsed — *forget my face* — and when the patrol came down the lane with their lanterns, she had not screamed, had not given him away. He had heard it all from the coal cellar where she shoved him: boots on her floor, the sergeant's voice, her own voice steady as a set line of type, lying beautifully about a sick sister and a locked cellar full of damp paper.

She had lied to soldiers for a stranger. There were maybe five people in Vellacourt who would do that, and he had stumbled onto one of them in the dark.

Now he crossed the street.

* * *

His knock was weaker than he intended. He meant three sharp raps, urgent but controlled; what he produced was two soft sounds, like a man tapping at a door in a dream. He tried again, and the fever chose that moment to crest, the lane tilting under him, the painted letters of the sign swimming — *IMPRIMEUR* stretching and folding like paper in water. He put his forehead against the wood and breathed.

Above him, a floorboard creaked.

"Who is there?" Her voice, low, careful. Not afraid — measuring. He remembered that voice from the cellar night, steady as a plumb line.

"The printer," he managed. "Madame Delacroix. It's — I need—"

Silence. Then, colder: "There is no printer here at this hour. Go away."

"It's me." He swallowed blood-taste and fever-dryness. "The man from your coal cellar. I'm sorry. I told you not to expect me. But there's no one else."

Another silence, longer. He heard the small domestic sounds of someone deciding: a chair pushed back, a candle lifted, a child stirring — yes, there was the child; he heard her murmur in sleep, a small sound like a bird settling, and something twisted in him that had nothing to do with the knife wound. He had handed a man's whole catastrophe to a woman who already had a child at her breast, a child that wasn't hers, and he had not asked. He had simply left the weight on her doorstep and walked into the dark, and told himself it was strategy.

"I'm coming down," she said finally. "If there are soldiers with you, I will scream, and my neighbors will come, and you'll wish the soldiers had found you instead."

"No soldiers," he said. "Only me. What's left of me."

The bolt drew back. The door opened a hand's width, then wider, and candlelight spilled out across the threshold, and there she was — Theo Delacroix, ink-stained to the second knuckle, hair pinned up carelessly, eyes that went over him the way, he supposed, she went over a galley of type: quickly, precisely, missing nothing. He watched her catalog the fever-bright eyes, the ruined shirt, the way he leaned on the doorframe. He watched her decide.

"In," she said. "Quickly. Before God and the whole street see you."

* * *

She got him down into the type cellar because it was the only room with a door that locked, and because — he understood this later, when his head was clearer — it was the room she had already lied about once and preferred to keep her lies consistent. She sat him on a crate beside the composing stone, propped a pillow she must have brought from upstairs behind his back, and held the candle close to his side until he flinched.

"Take off the shirt."

"I can—"

"You can't. You couldn't walk up three steps without holding the wall. Take off the shirt."

He took off the shirt. It cost him more than he wanted her to see. The bandage he'd wrapped around himself eleven days ago, drunk on cheap brandy and shaking, had soaked through and crusted and soaked through again, and when it peeled away from the wound he heard himself make a sound he hadn't made since he was a boy — something small and involuntary, bitten off half-finished.

Theo Delacroix did not comment. She fetched a basin, boiled water on a spirit lamp she apparently kept down here among the cases of type, and cleaned the wound with hands that were steadier than most surgeons' — quick, economical, unsentimental. The knife had entered below his ribs on the left and exited — barely — at the back. It had been sewn once, badly, by his own hand in a mirror in the cooperage loft, and she unpicked those stitches and redid them while sweat ran down his face and he fixed his eyes on the wall of typecases and counted the drawers to keep from disgracing himself.

Forty-one drawers, he counted. No. Thirty-eight. He started again.

"You've lost blood twice," she said, tying off the last stitch. "Once to the knife and once to fever. Another day out there and you'd have been found dead in a gutter, and whoever found you would have searched the body, and whatever you're carrying—" her eyes flicked up to his face "—would be on the Colonel's desk by noon."

"I'm not carrying it anymore."

"I know. I have it." She said it flatly, without accusation, which was somehow worse than accusation. She snuffed the spirit lamp and sat back on her heels, studying him in the candlelight. "Eleven days. I thought you were dead in a ditch, honestly. Or arrested. I thought about burning it, the book. Did you know that? Three separate nights I stood at the stove with it in my hands."

"Why didn't you?"

She was quiet a moment. "Because I read it."

The words fell into the cellar like lead into a forme. Luc closed his eyes. Of course she had. He would have. Anyone with a brain and a candle would have, and he had known it when he left it — had known he was handing her knowledge the way you hand someone a lit fuse, and had done it anyway because there was no one else, and because some part of him, the part that had spent six years being nobody, wanted desperately for somebody to know. For the knowing to be shared, so that it stopped being only his.

"Then you understand," he said, "why I couldn't go anywhere else. And why I can't stay long."

"Understand?" Something moved in her face — not anger exactly; grief wearing anger's clothes. "I understand that there are three names in that book I set type for. Wedding announcements. Funeral notices. The Marchand girl's marriage contract, printed right there on my father's press, and her father's name is in your ledger under a payment dated the week after he vanished." Her voice stayed level. That was the frightening thing about it. "So yes. I understand. Sit up. Drink this."

She gave him watered wine, and he drank, and the warmth of it spread through the fever like a rumor through a barracks.

"My name is Luc Arnaud," he said.

"I didn't ask."

"I know. But you should have it anyway. If they take me, and they ask you under — pressure — whether you know the name of the man who left the book, I'd rather you had a real one to give them or withhold. A name is evidence, Madame Delacroix. I've spent my life handling evidence. I know how it behaves." He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and she waited him out with the patience of someone who had stood beside a press through long runs and knew that everything eventually worked itself out or broke. "Luc Arnaud. Clerk, third class, Bureau of Estates and Requisitions. Six years balancing Colonel Vassey's accounts without once looking up from the column of figures."

"And now you've looked up."

"Now I've looked up." He set the cup down. His hand was not steady. "Do you want to know what I found when I looked up, Madame? Do you want the whole of it, since you've already read the book?"

"I read entries. Ciphered ones I couldn't read at all. Tell me the whole of it."

So he told her. He told it the way he'd composed it in his head a hundred times in the cooperage loft, in fever, in the dark — orderly, itemized, a clerk's confession, because that was the only shape he trusted himself to give it in.

The executions were real, he said. That was the foundation of everything, and it had to be said first or the rest sounded like mere theft. Men arrested for sedition, for pamphleteering, for nothing at all sometimes — for a name on a list supplied by informers paid per name — tried in an afternoon, shot within the week. Legal, on the surface. Sanctioned. The tribunal stamped its papers, the regiment filed its reports, and the state washed its hands.

But between the shooting and the paperwork, there was the estate. A dead traitor forfeited everything — that was law. And law, Luc had learned, was a machine, and machines have gears, and gears can be turned by whoever stands closest to them. Vassey stood closest. The confiscated properties — houses, workshops, fishing boats, vineyard shares, a glassworks, two warehouses on the harbor — passed through the Bureau of Estates, where they were appraised by appraisers Vassey appointed, and auctioned to buyers Vassey approved, at prices Vassey's clerks recorded in ledgers that were shown to the Ministry's auditors once a year and shown to no one else ever.

The difference between what the estate was worth and what the record said it sold for — that difference had a destination. Luc had traced it, over months, in the margins of his working hours, using the one gift his anonymity gave him: access. Eleven thousand francs in two years, routed through a shipping agent, through a notary in Rouen, through an account that ultimately answered to a name Luc had copied into the ledger's final ciphered pages in a code of Émile's devising, because plain text would have killed them both a month sooner than it did.

"They killed Émile for less than the whole of it," Luc said. "He'd only begun copying. They caught him with fourteen pages. If they'd known I existed — if they'd known the copy continued—" He stopped. Started again. "They don't know yet. That's the entire margin I live in. They killed Émile thinking they'd stopped the leak. Every day they believe that is a day I breathe."

Theo listened without interrupting. When he finished she was quiet for a long time, turning her wedding-ring-less ring finger — no, not a ring; an old callus, a compositor's callus, he realized, the groove where the composing stick rests.

"The fleet," she said at last. "You said, that first night — 'until the fleet sails.' What fleet? Why does it matter?"

"Because on the morning the fleet sails, Vassey's gold sails with it." He watched her face change. "Not metaphorically. The final pages of the ledger — the ones in cipher — they include a shipment manifest. Eleven crates, marked as naval stores, consigned aboard the frigate *Lionne*, departing Vellacourt on the morning tide of the twenty-sixth. The gold leaves France legally, under military escort, as ballast. Once it's past the breakwater it ceases to exist as far as any audit is concerned. Vassey retires rich to wherever retired colonels retire, and the forty-one names stay dead, and the books balance forever."

"And if the ledger becomes public before the twenty-sixth?"

"Then the gold doesn't sail. Or it sails and the scandal sinks him anyway — either way he's finished, but only if it happens *before*. After the twenty-sixth, the evidence points at a man who's beyond reach, and we're just criminals with a stolen book." He leaned his head back against the crate. The fever was ebbing now, leaving behind the particular exhausted clarity that comes after. "Twenty-two days. I need somewhere to heal, somewhere to finish verifying the last pages against the original, and then I need the contents of that ledger in front of ten thousand pairs of eyes before the *Lionne* clears the harbor. Pamphlets. Broadsides. however many copies this town can choke on."

"And you came to a print shop."

"I came to the only press in Vellacourt whose owner has already lied to a sergeant's face for a stranger." He met her eyes. "I know what I'm asking. I know better than anyone alive what it costs, because I've watched the cost assessed and collected, entry by entry, for six years. So I won't dress it up. If you help me, Madame Delacroix, and we fail, you will very likely die for it. If we succeed, you may still die for it — success only changes which men come for you. I have no right to ask. I have less than no right; I'm the reason Émile is dead, because he showed me the ledger instead of burning it, because I was too curious, too decent, too something, and my decency got a good man opened up like a butcher's cut."

"Stop," Theo said.

He stopped.

"You didn't kill Émile. Men like Vassey kill people; the rest of us only choose which side of the ledger we stand on when it's too late to matter." She rose, gathered the bloody basin, the stained bandages, movements brisk and final. At the foot of the stairs she paused, candle in hand, and did not look back at him. "The twenty-sixth. Twenty-two days. Heal, verify, and then we'll talk about presses. Tonight you sleep in the cellar, and tomorrow you are my cousin from Rouen, a journeyman compositor convalescing from a fall, and if you can't hold a composing stick convincingly by Wednesday I'll know you're lying about being a clerk, because no honest man's hands are that idle."

"Madame Delacroix." His voice cracked on it. "Why? You owe me nothing. Less than nothing — I brought a death sentence to your doorstep and a child you never agreed to raise. Why do you even leave the door bolted behind me instead of behind me and gone?"

She stood a moment in the candlelight, and he saw her weigh the answer, saw her decide to give it.

"Because I read it," she said again. "And now I know what you know, and there's no version of me that un-knows it. You didn't give me a burden, monsieur Arnaud. You gave me a share. Those aren't the same thing." She began to climb. "Sleep. I'll bring broth at dawn. And Luc—" the use of his name, deliberate, settled something in his chest he hadn't known was loose, "—the next time you nearly die on my street, knock louder. My nerves are not what they were."

Her steps faded overhead. The candle she'd left him guttered low beside the composing stone, throwing the great ranks of typecases into shadow, each drawer a small coffin of alphabet, every letter in the French language waiting in the dark for someone to set it free.

Twenty-two days, Luc thought, and slept, and dreamed of columns of figures that turned, row upon row, into lines of type, and the lines of type into voices, and the voices into a sound like the sea — like a fleet sailing, or a city rising, he couldn't tell which, and woke once in the black cellar with his heart pounding, certain he'd heard the curfew bell tolling thirteen times.

In the morning, Theo brought broth and worse news with it.

"Soldiers at the corner of the Rue Saint-Aubin," she said quietly, setting down the bowl. "Two of them. Not patrolling — posted. Asking after strangers." Her eyes were steady, but her knuckles were white around the empty basin. "Someone has told the Colonel that his missing clerk isn't missing anymore."
