## 8. Vassey's Men

The bell above the door had a cracked note — it had rung that way since before the Republic fell, since before the Empire of the bayonets rose in its place, and in all those years Theo Delacroix had never once flinched at it. It rang on a Tuesday morning in late October and she did not look up from the galley tray she was setting, because a customer was a customer, and customers were bread.

"Mademoiselle Delacroix."

The voice stopped her hands.

She knew it without knowing it. Not from any meeting — she had never met the man — but from Marcel's careful silences, from the way his jaw tightened whenever the name came up over Sunday dinner, from the way he'd said *Colonel Vassey* the way other men said *the plague*. A voice you learn by absence.

She turned.

He filled the doorway without filling it — that was the first uncanny thing about him. Colonel Vassey was not a large man. He was neat, silvering at the temples, dressed in civilian blue rather than uniform, with a walking stick he clearly did not need. He might have been a notary. He might have been anyone's uncle come to commission a funeral notice. Only his eyes moved like a man counting exits, and only his smile was wrong — too symmetrical, held a half-second too long, like a portrait that had learned to breathe.

"You have the advantage of me," Theo said, which was a lie, and watched him know it was a lie, and watched him enjoy it.

"I think not." He crossed the shop floor at an unhurried pace, his stick tapping the boards between the type cabinets. "Delacroix & Fils. Established 1819. The father died — my condolences — and now the son runs it. Marcel Delacroix. Captain Delacroix, I should say." His gaze drifted over the wall of specimen cards, the framed broadsides, the great iron press standing silent under its dust sheet. "A family of printers. There is something admirable in that. Continuity. Craft." He picked up a composing stick from the bench and turned it over as though appraising a horse's hoof. "In these times, continuity is worth more than gold."

"We're honored," Theo said evenly. "Was there something you wanted printed?"

*Please let him want something printed.* Let this be what it appeared: commerce. A colonel with a vanity project. She could print his memoirs in gilt if it put him out her door.

"In fact, yes." He set the stick down precisely where he'd found it, aligned to the bench edge, and Theo hated how much that small orderliness frightened her. "I'm told you produce fine work. I require two hundred copies of a proclamation. Official business." He produced a folded sheet from inside his coat and laid it on the counter with two fingers, the way one lays down a winning card. "The municipal authorities wish to remind the citizens of Vellacourt of the curfew regulations, and of the penalties attending their violation."

Theo unfolded it. The type was dense, officious, stamped with the prefecture seal. *Persons found abroad after the ninth hour... liable to detention... repeat offenses punishable by—* Her eyes snagged on the last clause and she made herself read it flatly, the way she read any copy that came across her counter, because a compositor who reacted to text was a compositor who didn't last.

"Punishable by deportation," she said. "To the labor camps."

"To the colonies," Vassey corrected pleasantly. "Deportation sounds so grim. Many men thrive in Algiers."

"When do you need them?"

"Friday would suit." He glanced around the shop again, and this time the glance had weight to it, ballast, a ledger's worth of inventory taken behind those pleasant eyes. "You work alone? I understood there was an assistant."

"My brother assists when his duties permit."

"Ah. Yes. His duties." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile; a recalculation. "Captain Delacroix has distinguished himself. The commandant speaks highly of him. A young officer of real ambition." He pronounced the word slowly, tasting it. *Ambition.* "He will go far. Men like your brother understand that the times reward decisiveness."

"He's always been diligent," Theo said, and heard her own voice come out thin as tissue paper.

"Diligent." Vassey laughed — softly, courteously, the way one laughs at a child's joke. "Yes. Diligent is one word for it."

He moved then, drifting along the specimen wall, and Theo's heart climbed into her throat because three steps past the broadsides, half-hidden behind a display of mourning cards, stood the shelf where she kept her jobbing cases — and among them, wedged carelessly behind a font of 14-point Bodoni she hadn't sorted since Luc Arnaud bled on her floor, sat a case whose compartments held not letters but cipher fragments, Odile Ferrand's transcriptions, copied out in her own hand.

Vassey's fingers walked along the shelf's edge.

Theo counted her own heartbeats. Four. Five. Six.

"The mourning trade must be brisk," he remarked.

"It keeps the lights lit."

"Death always has." He withdrew his hand from the shelf and turned back to her, and for one unguarded instant the courtesy dropped away entirely and what looked out of his face was nothing civil at all — it was appetite, cold and patient, the look of a man who has already decided how a thing ends and is merely enjoying the interval. Then it was gone, shuttered behind the notary's smile. "Two hundred copies. Friday. You'll send the bill to the prefecture — ask for Captain Delacroix's section. I'm sure he'll see you're paid fairly." He tapped the proclamation with his stick. "Family should look after family. In both directions, Mademoiselle. Both directions."

The bell gave its cracked note as he left. Theo stood with her hands flat on the counter until his footsteps faded on the cobbles, and only then did she realize she'd been holding her breath long enough to ache.

* * *

She locked the door. She never locked it during working hours — a printer's shop lived by foot traffic — but she shot the bolt and leaned against it anyway, and let her hands shake now that there was no one to see.

*Both directions.*

He had not searched the shop. That was the thing she kept turning over, the splinter in the mind. He had come in, commissioned printing, browsed, and left — and every second of it had been the search. He had wanted her to know he could have torn the place apart board by board, and had chosen instead to show her that he didn't need to. That the threat itself was the message, delivered free of charge, wrapped in civility.

And the message underneath the message: *I know who your brother is. I know what he is to me.*

She thought of the ledger hidden beneath the floorboards under the drying rack, wrapped in oilcloth, its cipher pages waiting for Odile's key. Three names with Marcel's signature beside them. Perrin. The Aubry brothers. Men she'd sold almanacs to. Men whose wives still came in on market days and bought nothing, just stood in the warm smell of ink because the shop had been their husbands' shop too.

She had told herself — God help her, she had actually told herself — that the signature meant little. That officers signed things. That Marcel's name beside those entries might be clerical, incidental, the pen of a staff man doing staff work, the way a compositor's hands set type he never reads. She had built a small shelter out of that reasoning and lived in it for two weeks.

But Vassey had called him *ambitious*, and had said it the way a man says a word he owns.

She saw it suddenly, whole, the way a forme comes together on the stone — each element separate until the moment the lockup tightens and the image appears. Marcel at Sunday dinners, lying smoothly about the disappearances while Bird played at his boots — not lying badly, not squirming, but *well*, with the ease of practice. Marcel's warnings to keep the press cold — not fear for her safety alone, but fear of exposure, of anything that might draw the prefecture's eye toward a shop bearing his name. Marcel rising through the ranks of a regime he had once sworn to pull down, and rising *fast* — too fast for mere diligence, fast the way men rise when they are useful, when they make themselves useful, when they bring things to the table that others cannot.

He wasn't trapped in Vassey's machine. He was *climbing* it.

The realization did not arrive as rage. It arrived as coldness, spreading from somewhere behind her sternum outward, the way ink spreads through damp paper — slow, total, irreversible. She had spent weeks grieving a brother she believed corrupted by circumstance, a good man bent by bad necessity. But necessity did not explain the ease of his lies. Ambition explained the ease of his lies.

She went to the window and looked out through the gap in the shutters. The street ran quiet in the gray light. Two soldiers stood at the corner by the chandler's, bored, stamping against the cold — ordinary soldiers, garrison soldiers, the kind who checked papers and stole apples and dreamed of home. They were not there for her. They were just there, the way weather is just there.

Behind her, Bird sat cross-legged on the floor by the stove, sorting type scraps into a wooden box — *her* job, her proud employment, letters sorted into families the way Theo had taught her to sort buttons. The child worked with fierce concentration, tongue between her teeth, occasionally holding up a piece for inspection.

"This one's got a tail," Bird announced, brandishing a Q.

"So it does. Where does the Q live?"

"With the O people. Because they look alike."

"That's right. They're cousins."

Bird considered this gravely, then returned the Q to its cousins. Four years old, and already she knew more about the anatomy of language than most magistrates. Luc had not come back since the night he'd named himself and begged her to hold the ledger until the fleet sailed — nine days now — and every day Theo expected either his return or his corpse floating in the harbor basin, and every day neither came, and the not-knowing had begun to feel like a kind of companionship, terrible and familiar.

She crouched beside the child. "Bird. If anyone ever comes to the door and asks about me — asks where I am, or what I keep in the shop — what do you say?"

"Nothing. I'm just a little girl."

"And what are you doing here?"

"Helping my auntie Theo."

*Auntie Theo.* The word went into Theo like a blade goes into a lock — something turning over, something opening that could not easily be closed again. She had no legal claim on this child. No papers, no standing, no money beyond what the shop earned and the shop earned less every week the city smoldered. On paper, Bird belonged to no one, and children who belonged to no one belonged to the state, and the state had plans for such children that involved institutions with high windows and long silences.

"If the tall gentleman comes back," Theo said quietly, "the one who smells like tobacco and talks very nicely — you go straight to the cellar stairs and you wait there. Don't hide. Just stand where I can find you quickly. Can you remember that?"

"Tall like Papa?"

Luc again. The child carried the stranger around with her like a pressed flower in a book. "Tall like Papa," Theo said. "Can you remember?"

"Yes, Auntie Theo."

She kissed the top of the girl's head, which smelled of wood smoke and cheap soap, and stood up, and went back to the bench, and began to set the curfew proclamation.

Because that was the obscenity of it, she thought as her fingers flew through the case — *e-t-i-o-n*, the familiar muscle-memory of treason rendered as civic duty — that she would print this thing. Two hundred copies of a document that sent hungry men across the sea in chains. She would set it beautifully, cleanly, with tight register and even impression, because her father had taught her that work done shoddily dishonored the worker regardless of the work, and because refusing was not yet survivable, and because —

— and because, said a voice in her that sounded like Odile Ferrand, *a printer who can set their lie perfectly can also set the truth. Every skill cuts both ways. Learn the weapon by cleaning it.*

She worked through the afternoon. The forme came together page by page: the seal, the clauses, the penalties, the prefect's signature block. When it was done she locked it up, and stood looking down at it — the whole machinery of cruelty arranged in lead and wood, orderly as a garden, ready to reproduce itself ten thousandfold at the pull of a lever.

*Ambition,* Vassey had said.

She thought of their father's hands, black to the second knuckle, guiding four-year-old Marcel's small fingers onto the press lever. *Gently, mon fils. The press is honest. Whatever you feed it, it gives back exactly. Never feed it anything you couldn't bear to hear read aloud at your funeral.* Marcel had pulled the lever and the roller had passed and their father had laughed, and little Marcel had looked up glowing, drunk on the sound, on the making, on the power of a machine that turned intention into a thousand identical truths.

He had never gotten over being drunk on it. That was the whole of it, perhaps. Some men drank wine and some men drank applause and some men — men like Colonel Vassey, men like her brother — drank consequence. The power to sign a name and watch the world rearrange itself around the signature. Their father had meant the lesson as a warning. Marcel had heard a promise.

Theo wiped down the stone. Outside, the light was failing and the curfew hour crept closer, and somewhere across the city Odile Ferrand was bending over the cipher pages by candlelight, coaxing out the names, and somewhere Luc Arnaud was alive or dead, and somewhere Marcel was dining with officers, ambitious, diligent, going far.

She covered the press, banked the stove, and lifted Bird, half-asleep, into her arms. The child murmured and hooked a fist into Theo's collar and held on.

At the door, bolting it for the night, Theo paused. Tacked to the frame at eye level, so neatly it might have grown there, was a small card of good stock, printed in fine italic:

*The prefecture thanks the tradesmen of Vellacourt for their cooperation.*

No signature. None needed.

It had not been there that morning.

Theo stood a long moment in the darkening street, the sleeping child heavy against her shoulder, reading the card until she had memorized its every curve of type — and recognized, with a compositor's eye she could not switch off, the exact face it was set in. Bodoni. Fourteen point.

The same font that sat, three compartments short, on the shelf in her own shop.

Someone had stood at her specimen wall while Vassey smiled at her, and had cut a sliver of her type — a calling card, proof of access, proof that they had touched everything — and had walked it out the door under the colonel's courteous wing. Tomorrow there would be a patrol at the corner all day. She would count them. And tonight, before curfew's ninth hour, the ledger was coming out of the floorboards, because a shop that had been measured once could be entered twice.

She pulled her shawl over Bird's head and walked into the streets, toward the rue des Anciennes Casernes, toward Odile Ferrand's door, moving fast through the blue dusk while the bells began to count the hour.
