## 10. Brother Against Brother

Theo closed the shutter herself at half past six, though the light had not yet failed. It was a signal she had learned from Odile — a shop shut early was a shop that wished to be undisturbed — and she had sent the boy who swept for her around to Rue des Tanneurs with a scrap of paper folded no larger than a type slug: *Come tonight. Alone. After dark. Come as my brother or don't come at all.*

She did not know which brother would answer.

Bird sat on the floor by the stove, sorting misprints into a biscuit tin, humming something tuneless and small. Theo watched her over the rim of the composing stick in her hands, not composing anything, just holding it, the way one holds a rosary without praying. Outside, Vellacourt went about its evening as if it were any other evening: a fishwife crying the last of her sardines, a drum somewhere in the distance — garrison drums now, nightly, the regime's lullaby.

The knock came at nine. Three raps, evenly spaced. Marcel's knock, the one their father had taught them both: *firm enough to be heard, soft enough not to wake the lead.*

Theo opened the door before he could knock again.

He stood in the doorway in civilian clothes that fit him like a costume — a plain brown coat, no insignia, his hair damp with fog. He had tried to look like a printer's son and had almost managed it. Only his boots betrayed him: officer's boots, polished to a black mirror, gleaming even here in the dark of the threshold.

"You said alone," he said quietly. "I am alone."

"Are you?" Theo stepped back to let him in. "Or does Colonel Vassey know where you are tonight?"

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, swallowed. "Theo."

"Shut the door, Marcel."

* * *

Marcel

The shop smelled the way it had always smelled — oil, ink, the cold mineral breath of lead — and standing inside it Marcel felt, absurdly, eight years old again, standing on a crate so he could reach the lever while Papa counted down. *Three. Two.* The whole world reduced to a sheet of paper and the moment before impression.

He made himself stop looking at the press. He looked instead at his sister, who stood with her arms crossed beside the type cases, watching him the way one watches a document suspected of forgery.

"You got my note," he said. "About sailing."

"I got your note." She turned away from him and lifted the lamp from the counter, carried it to the table in the back room, set it down. "Sit."

He sat. The chair was Papa's chair, the one with the worn cane seat, and that too was an accusation.

"Theo," he began, "whatever you've heard—"

She put the ledger on the table between them.

Marcel stopped breathing.

It lay there under the lamplight, fat and scarred, its leather cover cracked along the spine, and he knew it instantly, completely, the way a man knows his own handwriting on a threatening letter. He had last seen it in Luc Arnaud's hands, in the counting room above the harbor, three nights before Arnaud vanished. He had told Vassey the book was lost. He had told himself the same thing, and for two days he had almost believed it, because belief was a skill he had been practicing for years.

"How," he said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Where did you get this?"

"A man left it at my door. Bleeding. With a child." Theo sat across from him, and she did not take her eyes off his face. "He said forget his face. I haven't. Have you forgotten yours, Marcel? Because the man in this book signs like you. Same hand. Same flourishes. You used to sign your school copybooks that way, and Papa said a signature should be honest, because ink remembers."

"Ink remembers," Marcel repeated. It was the first thing Papa had ever taught them, before ems, before picas, before anything. *Ink remembers what men want to forget.*

"Yes." She opened the ledger. Turned pages. Her fingers were steady; he noticed that with a strange, detached pride, and then hated himself for noticing. She stopped at a page and rotated the book toward him. "Explain this to me. Explain it slowly, and explain it like I'm stupid, because I must be, to have spent thirty-one years loving a man I apparently never met."

Marcel looked at the entry. He didn't need to read it. He could have recited it. *R. Aubry, cordwainer, rue Saint-Léonard. Disposition: removed. Fee disbursed, 40 francs. M. Delacroix.* Removed. As if a man were a stain on a ceiling.

"Aubry owed money to the wrong people," Marcel said. "People above Vassey. Aubry talked. Aubry was going to talk more. And on the fourteenth of March, Aubry was taken from his bed and put on a cart, and his wife was told he'd been conscripted south, and she believed it, because believing is easier than digging." He heard his own voice and was disgusted by its calm. It was the voice he used in briefings. "That's what the entry means."

"And your name beside it."

"My name beside it."

"Because you took the forty francs?"

"Because I certified the disbursement." He held her gaze because looking away would be worse than anything. "Someone has to sign, Theo. That's the machine. Not cruelty — clerks. Signatures. A hundred honest little marks of a pen, and at the end of them a man is gone and no one is guilty, because everyone only signed a piece of paper."

"Then be the one who doesn't sign."

"It isn't—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles against his mouth. When he lowered his hand he made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness if nothing else. "If I stop signing, someone else signs. Someone with no sister in a print shop, no reason to ever look twice at a ledger. I signed so that I'd be inside the room. Inside the room is the only place a man can do anything at all. You can't steer a cart you're tied beneath."

"That's a fine philosophy for the horse."

"Theo—"

"René Aubry taught me catechism." Her voice cracked, just once, and then hardened over the crack like cooling wax. "He had a daughter. She's eleven. She still writes letters to the south. They come back unopened. Forty francs, Marcel. You cost forty francs and a girl gets to keep writing letters to nowhere."

Marcel looked at the lamp flame until his eyes watered. "I know what it costs."

"Do you? Do you actually? Because from where I sit, it costs you nothing. It costs you dinners and Sundays and the shape of your face when you look in the glass. It costs everyone else everything."

* * *

Theo

She watched him absorb the blow, and she hated how much of her wanted to take it back — to cross the table, take his head in her hands like when he was feverish at fourteen, tell him none of this was his fault, that the times were at fault, the war, the colonel, the century itself. There was always something to blame instead of the man. That was the family sickness. Papa had blamed the censors, the tariffs, the paper shortages. Never once had either of them said aloud the simplest sentence: *we choose.*

"We're not done," she said. "There's more in this book than Aubry."

"There's always more."

"There's gold." She turned to the later pages, the ones Odile's cipher had opened like a lockpick. "Shipments. Prisoners' estates seized and sold, the proceeds routed through a shipping agent, loaded aboard a naval transport on the morning tide, three weeks hence. Your signature appears on the manifest authorizations too. So let me ask you the question I've been carrying around for six days like a stone in my boot." She leaned forward. "Is Marcel Delacroix a coward, a thief, or a believer?"

The silence stretched long enough for Bird, asleep in the back room, to turn over in her cot. Theo heard the straw shift. Marcel heard it too; his eyes flicked toward the sound.

"Whose child is that?" he asked.

"Answer my question."

"Whose child, Theo?"

"A dead man's. A living question. Answer mine first."

Marcel exhaled through his nose, a soldier's exhale, the one that preceded bad news delivered in order of rank. "A believer," he said finally. "Once. In the academy, in the cellars where we read contraband newspapers by candle-ends — you remember, you found one of our pamphlets once and Papa burned it and I cried. I believed the Republic was a press, Theo. You set the truth in type and you pulled the lever and the people read it and rose up and it was beautiful. Then the rising happened, and it ate its own children, and the men who came after weren't printers. They were colliers. They shoveled whatever burned hottest into the furnace, and the furnace was us, all of us, and I learned the first law of surviving a furnace." He spread his hands. "You become fuel that steers."

"Fuel doesn't steer. Fuel burns."

"Fuel that's useful lasts longer."

"Is that what they teach at the garrison?" She laughed, and it was an ugly sound, and she was glad of it. "Last longer. René Aubry lasted exactly as long as it took a cart to reach the barrier at Montreuil."

"And what would you have me do?" He leaned forward now too, matching her, and for a moment they were children again across the kitchen table, arguing over whose turn at the lever, except the stakes had grown teeth. "Tell me. Concretely. Walk into Vassey's office tomorrow morning and say 'Colonel, I resign, also here is a list of your crimes'? By nightfall I'm in a cellar at the citadel with my boots off, and by the following nightfall you're explaining to soldiers why your press is warm. They know about the shop, Theo. They've known since the winter. The only thing keeping it cold and standing is that the officer whose name is in your ledger has asked them — politely, repeatedly — to leave his sister's little business alone." He said it flatly, without pride, offering it up like evidence of a disease. "I am not your protector because I'm good. I'm your protector because I'm compromised. It happens to be the same hand."

"So every name in that book bought protection with someone else's blood."

"Yes."

"And you'll keep buying."

"As long as I can afford it."

* * *

Marcel

She was crying now, he realized. Not sobbing — Theo never sobbed — but the tears ran steadily down her face while her voice stayed level, and somehow that was worse than weeping, that discipline of hers, learned at their father's knee alongside the trade. *Never let the form see you shake,* Papa used to say. *The type takes the tremor.*

He wanted to tell her everything. That was the trap of this room, of this family: the old instinct toward confession, as if the shop were a confessional and ink were absolution. If he told her everything — about the night Aubry was taken, about the sound the cart wheels made on the cobbles, about the fact that he had lain awake every night since listening for that same sound coming for himself — she would forgive him. That was the terrible thing. She would forgive him, and forgiveness would make him smaller, because it would mean his crimes weighed less than her love, and he had built his whole rotten architecture on the assumption that nothing weighed more than survival.

So instead of confessing, he did the unforgivable thing. He gave her the truth shaped like a warning.

"Theo. Listen to me." He reached across the table; she didn't take his hand, but she didn't withdraw hers either, and he rested his fingertips near the ledger without touching it. "Whatever you think you're going to do with this book — whoever gave you the idea that printing it changes anything — stop. Stop tonight. Look at me." He waited until her eyes came up. Red-rimmed. Unbowed. God, she looked like Papa. "Vassey is not a clerk. Clerks can be embarrassed. Vassey is the kind of man who solves embarrassment with fire. If ten thousand copies of this ledger appear in Vellacourt, he will not search for the printer. He will not send polite sergeants. He will burn the quarter. He will burn the shop, and anyone found in it, and he will call it an accident of the riots, and the papers he controls will print his accident, and no one — no one — will ever pull a single page of proof out of the ashes." He made his voice gentle, which was the cruelest register he owned. "You'd die for a pamphlet. Martyrs are cheap here. We grow them in barrels. What this city needs isn't another martyr. It needs one person who kept a press running through the whole war so that afterward — afterward, Theo — there's someone left to print the truth when truth is survivable. Be the press that survives. Not the page that burns."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the patrol drums changed rhythm, and they both listened to it the way farmers listen for weather.

Then Theo drew her hand back, wiped her face once with the heel of her palm — a compositor's motion, economical, leaving no smudge — and said:

"Testify."

"What?"

"At the tribunal. When it comes — and it's coming, Marcel, the whole ring is rotting from the inside, Odile Ferrand's people aren't the only ones who know — testify. Stand up in front of the court with your own mouth and say the names. Not anonymously. Not through a signature on someone else's ledger. Say them yourself, out loud, and I will burn this book tonight and never speak of it again, and when it's over you can sail east and I'll follow with the child and we'll open a shop in Trieste or Alexandria or anywhere with salt air and no Vassey." She leaned in, and her eyes were dry now, blazing. "One testimony, Marcel. That's the price. That's the whole price. You've already paid more than that for less."

Marcel sat very still.

Because the offer was real. He could feel that it was real, and underneath his fear he felt something older and more dangerous stir — the eight-year-old on the crate, the academy cellars, the boy who cried when Papa burned a pamphlet. For one full second he was that boy again, and the word *yes* was in his mouth like a coin.

Then he thought of the citadel cellars. Of the carts. Of the specific quality of silence in a room full of men deciding whether you exist.

And he thought: *she's asking me to die in daylight.*

"They'd shoot me before I finished the third name," he said quietly. "You know that. And you'd lose the shop within the week. Both of those things are certainties, Theo. Not risks. Certainties." He stood. The chair scraped. He straightened his coat with both hands, a gesture so like their father buttoning his apron that Theo flinched. "Keep the press cold. Keep the book hidden. Sail on the tide when the berth comes — I'll get you the berth, I swear it on Papa's grave, that much I can still do." At the doorway to the front room he paused, his back to her, and the lamplight caught the officer's shine of his boots. "And Theo — if soldiers ever come asking what your press prints, you show them wedding announcements and cargo manifests and nothing else. Because I cannot protect you from what happens next if that book ever touches ink."

"Goodnight, Marcel."

He let himself out. The door closed softly, firm enough to latch, soft enough not to wake the lead.

* * *

Theo

She sat a long time in the lamp's small circle, listening to his footsteps fade up the rue — unhurried, measured, a man walking home inside his certainty.

Then she rose, went to the case, and slid open the drawer of twelve-point bodoni. Their father's favorite. She set a stick in her left hand and began to set, letter by letter, upside down and backward, the words that Marcel had refused to say aloud so that she might say them for him:

*COLONEL VASSEY HAS ROBBED THE DEAD.*

The type took no tremor. Ink remembered.

In the back room, Bird murmured in her sleep, and far off across the rooftops the patrol drums kept their patient time — counting down, Theo thought, to something.
