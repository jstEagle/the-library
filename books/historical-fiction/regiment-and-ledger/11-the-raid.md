## 11. The Raid

Theo knew the sound before she knew what it was: boots, many of them, out of step with the morning rhythm of the Rue des Fondeurs. Soldiers did not walk like printers or fishwives or priests. They walked like weather coming.

She was sorting type at the front bench when the first fist hit the shutter.

"Open! In the name of Colonel Vassey!"

Her hands went still over the case, a *k* and an ampersand caught between her fingers like small cold bones. Across the shop, in the corner where the paper bins stood, Bird looked up from her game of arranging letter-scrap by color — she sorted by shape, not by letter; she was four — and read Theo's face the way children do, instantly and without mercy.

"Bird." Theo kept her voice low and even, the voice she used for customers. "You know our game. The quiet game."

Bird nodded.

"The cellar stairs. Behind the paper. Go now, and you don't come up until I sing."

Behind her, from the shadowed mouth of the type cellar — a stone room below street level where the lead was cast and the old formes slept under sacking — Luc Arnaud had already gone pale. He'd been there three days, his wound festering despite Odile's poultices, moving between the cellar and the loft above the shop only at night. He opened his mouth.

"Don't," Theo said. "Not one word. Down. Now."

He hesitated half a heartbeat too long — a man who had spent months being hunted learning that hesitation kills — then took the stairs two at a time. Theo swung the paper rack across the cellar door, dragged the bin of damp quires in front of it, and heaved a crate of unsorted type onto the top. It wasn't clever. It was all she had time for.

The pounding came again, harder. Somewhere down the street a shutter banged closed, and a dog began to bark with the hysterical fury dogs reserve for uniforms.

She lifted the bar.

* * *

They came in like water finding cracks. Six men in the grey-blue of the garrison regiment, then a sergeant behind them — a broad man with a face like a dropped loaf, flour-pale, with small eyes that had learned to enjoy their work. His name, stitched on a scrap of cloth at his shoulder, was Perrin. Theo would remember that name longer than she wanted to.

"Sergeant." She stepped into the middle of the floor and made herself a wall made of a woman. "This is a print shop. My father's. There is nothing here but lead and ink and bad poetry."

"That's what we'll see." Sergeant Perrin turned a slow circle, taking in the press, the cases, the composing stone, the drying broadsides pinned along the wall — funeral notices, price lists, a handbill for a lost dog. His eyes were not admiring. They were inventorying. "Colonel Vassey's compliments. We're looking for a fugitive. Clerk's build, dark hair, wound in the left side. Might be calling himself anything. Might be calling himself nothing."

"I keep no lodgers. I sell print."

"You sell more than print." He picked up a funeral notice, read it upside down with the laborious care of a man whose literacy was recent, and let it drop. "Everyone sells more than print these days. Search it. All of it."

The soldiers moved through the shop the way they moved through everything, Theo thought — as if the world were already conquered and this was merely mopping up. One overturned the ink barrel before anyone told him not to; black spread across the floorboards like something spilled out of the century itself. Another ran his bayonet through the bales of rag paper, shredding, listening to the tear of it the way boys listen to the crack of ice. A third climbed to the loft and she could hear him up there kicking apart her cot, her father's old coat, the tin box where she kept Marcel's letters from the academy — letters she should have burned years ago and hadn't, because they were the last ones in which he sounded like himself.

Bird had not gone to the cellar door. Thank God for the child's instinct for theater, because Bird had done something better: she had climbed into the big paper bin near the window, pulled quires around herself like a nest, and gone back to sorting her colored scraps as if the world were a large dull thing happening elsewhere. A child hiding in plain sight. The soldiers glanced at her the way men glance at furniture.

"And that?" Perrin jerked his chin at her.

"My apprentice's daughter. Orphaned." It was nearly true. "She stays where I can see her."

Perrin looked at Bird a long moment. Bird looked back with the frank, guileless stare of the very young, held up a red *b* as if presenting evidence, and returned to her work. Something in the sergeant's face flickered — Theo couldn't name it and didn't trust it — and he turned away.

"Cellar?" he said.

The word landed in Theo's chest like a dropped forme.

"Lead storage," she said. "Casting room. You're welcome to it, though mind the third stair, it's rotten, my father never fixed it—"

But he was already moving, and two soldiers with him, and Theo understood with terrible clarity that the next thirty seconds would decide whether Luc Arnaud died in her cellar or lived to see the fleet sail. She thought about throwing herself down the stairs ahead of them, about fainting, about fire — there was no fire — about the lie she should have prepared and hadn't, because she had believed the shutters and the sacking and the damp smell of lead would be enough.

Perrin put his boot on the first stair.

And Bird sang.

* * *

It was not much of a song. It was, in fact, the alphabet — the one Theo had taught her without thinking, sitting on the composing stone evenings while Luc slept below, turning letter scraps into a game because a child must learn her letters somehow and the shop was full of them. Bird belted it out from the paper bin at the top of her four-year-old lungs, tuneless and triumphant, and every head in the wrecked shop turned toward her, including the sergeant's, including the heads of the two men already descending toward the cellar door.

"A, B, C, D—" She got to G, lost her place, laughed at herself, started over. "A, B, C—"

"What in God's name," Perrin said.

"She's learning to read." Theo heard her own voice come out steady and was almost proud of it. "In this house we learn letters before prayers. My father's rule. Letters are the only things that can't lie to you."

That was a dangerous sentence and she saw Perrin weigh it — saw him decide whether a woman who said things like that was worth a second look — and Bird saved her again by climbing out of the bin, marching to the type case, and tapping it solemnly with one finger. "That one's my favorite. The round ones."

"The round ones," Perrin repeated.

"O," said Bird. "And Q. Q has a tail."

For a moment — one suspended, absurd, unbearable moment — the sergeant crouched beside the child in the wreckage of a raided print shop while his men waited on a staircase, and Bird showed him the difference between O and Q, and the war paused around a four-year-old's lesson in typography. Theo stood with her heart going so hard she could feel it in her teeth, doing arithmetic of seconds: how long since Luc had heard them coming, how long to wedge himself behind the casting furnace, how long before someone noticed the paper rack dragged imperfectly across the doorframe, the scratch marks on the floorboards, the truth of any hiding place, which is that it takes one curious man thirty seconds to find what it took a desperate woman all night to conceal.

"Enough." Perrin straightened. "Downstairs. Now."

The two soldiers went down. Theo counted. Ten seconds. Fifteen. From the cellar came the scrape of the crate being shifted off the top of the pile — she had stacked it wrong, of course she had, three minutes is not enough to hide a man properly — and then the long silence in which a man lifts a sacking and looks behind it.

Twenty-five. Thirty.

"Nothing here but metal and spiders," one called up. "Smells like a grave."

"It is one," Theo said before she could stop herself, and immediately wished she hadn't, and Perrin's small eyes came back to her and stayed this time.

* * *

They wrecked the rest of it thoroughly. That was the thing no one warned you about raids — that finding nothing is its own punishment, that a search which fails takes payment anyway. They pulled the type cases off their shelves and dumped them, forty fonts of her father's life sliding into a single undifferentiated sea of lead, ten thousand letters that had taken thirty years to set in order, and Theo stood watching a *pica* of Bodoni mingle with jobbing gothics and thought: I can sort this. It will take me a month, and I can sort this, because I have sorted worse.

She was lying to herself and knew it, and did it anyway, because the alternative was screaming.

They broke the glass of the cabinet where the finished work was displayed — wedding announcements, mostly, the sweetest inventory in Vellacourt — and one soldier pocketed a silver-framed memorial card with a skull engraved on it, perhaps as a joke, perhaps not. They slit the pillows in the loft. They poured out her bottle of gum arabic and trod it into the ink on the floorboards until the whole shop smelled of vinegar and ruin. Through it all Perrin supervised with the mild attention of a landlord inspecting drains, and twice he asked questions that had nothing to do with a wounded clerk:

"Business is good? For a shop run by a woman?"

"It survives."

"Your brother. Delacroix. Officer now, isn't he? Up in the Colonel's office." He said it lightly, tasting it. "Must be proud."

"Proud enough."

"He doesn't visit?"

" Sundays."

"Doesn't he." Perrin smiled with the bottom half of his face only. "Well. Give him the Colonel's regards."

When they finally went — and it was the bell of the cathedral striking eleven that moved them more than any order — Perrin paused at the broken threshold and looked back at Theo with an expression she would carry to her grave, the expression of a man marking a ledger entry.

"We'll be back, Madame Delacroix. Shops like this always have something in them worth finding. Usually it's the woman."

Then they were gone, boots fading down the Rue des Fondeurs, the dog still barking, the street exhaling one shutter at a time.

* * *

Theo barred the door and did not move for a full minute. Then another. Her hands, when she finally looked at them, were shaking so badly the ampersand was still caught between two fingers — she had been holding it the whole raid, she realized, a small iron & clenched like a talisman — and she pried it loose and set it on the composing stone with exaggerated care, because if she handled anything quickly she would shatter.

"Théo?" Luc's voice floated up the cellar stairs, cracked and careful.

"Not yet. Wait." Because the street had ears; because a raid that finds nothing comes back; because she needed one minute inside her own skull before she became brave again. She crossed to the paper bin, knelt, and gathered Bird against her. The child smelled of paper dust and ink. She was utterly calm, already reaching for her scattered colored scraps.

"They broke your letters," Bird observed.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because they're afraid of them."

Bird considered this with the seriousness she gave to everything. "Of little ones?"

"Especially the little ones. Big lies need big letters. Little truths fit anywhere."

Theo held her a moment longer than necessary, feeling the small heartbeat against her own, and understood with a new and colder clarity what guardianship cost — that she had just watched a child be used as a shield and a sword within the same hour, that Bird had distrusted the sergeant on instinct and played him like a fiddle, and that someday the girl would understand exactly what she had done that morning and what it meant that a four-year-old had to be the bravest person in the room.

Then Theo sang — the little rising phrase that was their signal — and Luc came up the cellar stairs blinking, gray-faced, his bandage spotted fresh red, and took in the devastation with the expression of a man reading his own death warrant in someone else's handwriting.

"They found nothing," he said.

"They found nothing today." Theo righted a stool, sat, and finally let her hands shake where no one could see. "But Perrin asked after Marcel. By rank and by name. And he left me his regards like a debt collector leaves a card." She looked around the ruined shop — the ink lake slowly spreading, the fonts drowned together, the shredded paper drifting like dirty snow — and felt the fear in her chest finish its transformation into something harder and more useful. "They've marked me, Luc. Which means we have less time than we thought. Whatever Odile needs to finish the cipher — she finishes it tonight."

Luc lowered himself onto the wrecked stool opposite her, wincing, and met her eyes.

"There's something else you should know," he said. "About what the last pages say. About the date."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a cannon spoke once — a single flat note rolling across the rooftops, the city clearing its throat — and neither of them moved.
