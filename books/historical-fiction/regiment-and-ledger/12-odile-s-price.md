## 12. Odile's Price

Odile Ferrand had buried two husbands and one revolution, and she had learned from all three funerals that the dead are honest in ways the living cannot afford to be.

She kept the honesty downstairs, in the cellar of the shuttered wine shop on Rue des Tanneurs, where the barrels had long ago gone empty and been replaced by paper — reams of it, wrapped in oilcloth, stacked to the low vaulted ceiling like cordwood. Above her, the front room still smelled faintly of vinegar and old oak, and the sign on the door still read FERRAND & FILS, though there was no fils and had not been for eleven years. Gaspard had died at the barricades in '37 with a compositor's rule in his pocket instead of a pistol, because he had believed to his last breath that words were ammunition enough. She had spent a decade learning how wrong he was, and how right, and the learning had cost her everything she'd had except the cellar.

Tonight the candle burned low between her and Theo Delacroix, and Odile watched the younger woman across its flame the way she once watched a forme locked too tight — knowing that pressure, unrelieved, finds its weakest point and breaks something.

"You're certain," Theo said. It was not quite a question. Her hands were flat on the barrel-head they used as a table, and her knuckles had gone white, and she did not seem to know it.

"I am certain the way you are certain when the press pulls crooked," Odile said. "I can show you the misprint."

She reached into the ledger satchel at her hip — not Delacroix's leather book; that stayed hidden where no courier could find it — and drew out a slip of paper no larger than a prayer card. On it, in a hand Odile knew better than her own dead husband's, was a list of six names and six hours.

"Camille took this route Tuesday." She laid the slip down but kept two fingers on it, an anchor against the wind she felt coming. "Six drops. Rue Saint-Aubin, the cooper's yard, the fish market, the barracks wall, the harbor taverns, and back by midnight. Camille has run that route since spring. Never late. Never questioned."

"And Tuesday?"

"Tuesday she was three hours late, and she came in through the courtyard instead of the alley, and she would not look at me while she gave me the collection." Odile turned the slip over. On the reverse, in her own hand, were the names of the people those drops connected: a lieutenant who passed along garrison orders, a dock clerk who counted crates, a laundress who heard what officers said over their shirts. "By Thursday, the lieutenant had been transferred without explanation. By Friday, the dock clerk didn't open his stall. This morning I sent a boy past the laundress's door. The soldiers were already carrying out her things."

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, in the street, a patrol went by — boots, the dull jingle of kit, voices too low to catch. They waited until it faded. They had gotten good at waiting.

"The fish market drop," Theo said slowly. "That's where your people collect the harbor gossip. That's where Luc said—" She stopped herself.

"Where your stranger said Vassey's men have been asking about pamphlets." Odile nodded. "Yes. And here is the arithmetic that keeps me awake, girl: Camille knew every name on this slip. Every one. Not because I told her all of them — because a courier only needs her own route — but because she collected from each of them, week after week, and memory is a thing no cipher protects. Six contacts. Three gone silent inside four days. You do not need to be a colonel to read that pattern."

"Maybe they ran. Maybe they got frightened."

"Gaspard used to say the same thing in '37, right up until the week the arrests came." Odile's voice did not harden; it simply settled, the way ash settles. "Frightened men run loud. These ones vanished quiet. Quiet is what the army buys."

Theo pulled the slip toward her, and Odile let her take it, because there was no longer any point in holding it back. The young woman read it twice, her lips moving slightly, and Odile saw her arrive at the same door Odile had been standing before for two days.

"It isn't just the network," Theo said. "It's the ledger. If Vassey has Camille, he has whatever Camille carried. Has anything of mine—"

"Not yet. Your pages never touched Camille's route. That was luck, or God, or my own stubbornness — I split the copies three ways after you brought me the cipher key, and none of them traveled together." Odile folded her hands. "But luck is not a plan, Theo. And here is the rest of it, the part I have put off saying because saying it makes it real."

* * *

Odile rose and took the candle to the far wall of the cellar, where the paper stacks ended and a hanging blanket covered a rack of wooden frames. She pulled the blanket aside with the tenderness other women reserved for shrouds.

Behind it hung the network's whole anatomy, drawn in chalk and pinned scraps: routes in red thread, contacts marked with pins, the harbor quarter dense as a spider's belly. She had built it over ten years, pin by pin, widow by widow, printer by ruined printer — everyone the regime had broken who still had hands and a grievance. She had thought of it, in her less humble hours, as the true government of Vellacourt.

Now she took down three pins. Red thread dropped and hung loose.

"Camille's route," she said. "And these two, because they fed into hers. What remains touches what's lost in nine places. If Vassey has Camille's testimony, he can walk this map blindfolded. In a week, perhaps two, there will be nothing left to compromise because there will be nothing left at all."

"So we fold it up. Disperse. Wait him out."

"Wait him out." Odile laughed, and it was an ugly sound in that stone room. "Child, I have waited out a king, an emperor, and two prefects. Waiting works when the other side tires. Vassey does not tire. He is selling executed men's estates and shipping the gold out on the fleet, and every week he waits, the fleet gets fuller and the evidence gets thinner. We do not have weeks. We may not have days."

She turned back to face Theo, and she made herself say the rest plainly, because plainness was the only inheritance she had left to give.

"There is one way to spend what we have before it is taken from us. Print openly. Once. Everywhere at once. Ten thousand copies if the type will bear it, thrown into the markets and the barracks gates and the harbor taverns in a single morning, so fast that by noon it is in every hand in Vellacourt and burning it accomplishes nothing. No couriers to arrest. No routes to unwind. No cipher for his clerks to chew on. Just ink, in the open, under the sun."

The silence after that had weight, like wet snow on a roof.

"They'll know whose press," Theo said quietly.

"They'll know within the hour. Perhaps sooner." Odile met her eyes. "A press is loud, Theo. A press smells. Soldiers can find a working press faster than they can find a runaway horse. Whoever prints this signs their name whether they mean to or not. Which is why I did not ask you here to assign the work. I asked you here to tell you the price first, and then to ask."

* * *

They went upstairs afterward, because Odile's knees were bad in cold cellars and because some conversations go better with a view of the street you're discussing the death of. Through the grimy window of the old taproom, Vellacourt lay gray under a low sky, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold. The harbor masts looked like a forest of bare trees. Out there somewhere was the fleet, loading gold that belonged to dead men whose families hadn't been paid a sou.

"My father bought our press the year I was born," Theo said. She was holding a chipped cup of chicory coffee Odile had boiled up without asking; she hadn't drunk any of it. "He used to say a press is the only tool a man can own that thinks. You feed it truth and it multiplies it. Feed it lies, same thing. It doesn't judge. It just repeats."

"He was a wise man, your father."

"He was a careful man. He survived the Restoration by printing wedding announcements and almanacs and keeping his opinions in the drawer." Theo's mouth bent — not quite a smile. "Marcel and I grew up pulling proofs for almanac farmers. Weather predictions. Saints' days. He used to let Marcel set the front page because Marcel had the quicker eye, and I set the tide tables because I was more patient. Do you know what a tide table is, Madame Ferrand? It's a promise. You print it months ahead, and you're staking your reputation that the sea will keep its schedule. Father said it taught humility. You learn that some things come in whether you're ready or not."

"The tide comes in," Odile agreed.

"And the fleet sails on the morning tide, Luc says. Nine days." Theo finally drank the coffee and grimaced at it, which Odile appreciated; it meant the woman was still anchored in her body, still capable of being offended by bad chicory. "If we wait for the network to die and then try to rebuild, there's no time. If we print small, through what's left of the routes, Vassey strangles it piece by piece and we've spent lives for pamphlets nobody saw. You've already done that arithmetic, haven't you. You've known since Friday."

"Since the laundress," Odile said. "Marguerite Lourdes. Forty-one years old. Two sons, one drowned, one in the colonial regiment. She washed officers' shirts and remembered everything she heard over the collars, and she did it for eight years for nothing but the pleasure of being useful to something." Odile found her voice had gone rough and did not trouble to smooth it. "When they carry a woman's washbasin out into the street, girl, you stop counting the cost of the alternative."

Theo set down the cup. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — not louder, but denser somehow, the way lead is denser than tin.

"Then use my press."

* * *

Odile had expected it. She had even, in the privacy of her own conscience, arranged the evening so that Theo would arrive at it herself rather than be pushed — a compositor's trick, locking the chase so the type can only sit one way. But hearing it said aloud still landed like a weight on the tympan.

"You understand what you're offering."

"I understand exactly what I'm offering." Theo stood, and in the gray light from the window Odile saw how much the last month had carved out of her — the shadows under the eyes, the hands that no longer fidgeted but held still with deliberate force. "You're telling me the soldiers will come to the shop. That I'll hang for it, or worse. That Bird—" Her voice caught on the child's name, and she mastered it. "That I need to arrange for the child. That my brother, who already knows what I am, will be the one they send, or the one who has to watch it happen."

"Yes. All of that."

"Then hear what I'm telling you." Theo came around the table and stood close, and Odile smelled ink on her — real ink, worked deep into the skin, the smell Odile had worn for forty years and would wear into the grave. "My father kept that press cold for twenty years to keep us safe, and safe got us nothing. My brother joined them to keep us safe, and look what safe made of him. I cut type under a dead man's name for four years because a woman's name wasn't worth lead. I am done paying safety's bills, Madame Ferrand. The press is warm tonight. Let it stay warm."

For a long moment Odile could not speak. She had recruited despairing men and furious widows and one defected priest, and she had never once heard it put that way — not as sacrifice, not as martyrdom, but as a bill coming due that someone had finally agreed to pay.

"Then we do it properly," she said at last, and the craftsman in her took over because the mother in her was too near tears. "Not tonight. Tonight we plan. Your press — how many can she pull in a night, honestly?"

"With fresh type, clean rollers, and me feeding her? Four hundred an hour if the formes are set in advance and I don't stop. Eight hours, maybe ten. Three thousand, four thousand copies."

"Then we need every hour of darkness, and we need the formes set before dark, and we need helpers who don't know the address until the last moment." Odile was pacing now, the plan assembling itself out of old habit, old raids, old narrow escapes. "We distribute at dawn, all quarters at once, before the patrols change shift. Market women, fish porters, boys who sell papers — they carry bundles under their ordinary goods. By the time Vassey understands what's happened, it's in ten thousand pockets."

"And the content?"

"The ledger. Names, sums, dates — decoded, plain French, no cipher for his clerks to hide behind. Vassey's profits off the executed. The estates sold. The fleet's cargo." Odile paused at the window. Below, the street was empty except for a dog nosing along the gutters, and beyond the rooftops the harbor cranes stood against the sky like gallows. "And we sign it. All of it. A press that prints truth anonymously is a rumor. A press that signs its work is a witness. Gaspard believed that. I was too careful to believe it for ten years. I believe it now, with whatever time I have left to believe things."

Theo was quiet for a moment. Then: "There's a risk you haven't named."

"Name it."

"Marcel." The word came out flat, pressed smooth, like a sheet pulled off wet type. "My brother is Vassey's protégé. When this breaks, when your ten thousand copies hit the barracks gates, he won't just be compromised — he'll be finished, unless he helps finish it himself. Unless he burns my shop with his own hands to prove where he stands." She looked down at her own hands, the ink-stained fingers, and Odile watched her close them into fists. "I need to talk to him. Before the presses turn. I owe myself that much, and I owe him the chance to stand somewhere on purpose for once in his life."

"That's dangerous."

"Everything left is dangerous. That's the whole point of your arithmetic." Theo lifted her chin. "Give me two days. Set the plan. I'll talk to Marcel tomorrow, and whatever he does with it — warn Vassey, or warn me, or stand there like a man — we'll know what we're printing against."

Odile studied her for a long time. Then she crossed the taproom, opened the old strongbox behind the bar, and took out the thing she had sworn she would never entrust to anyone living: a single galley of type, wrapped in cloth, set years ago and never distributed. Gaspard's last composition. She had pulled one proof and locked the metal away like a relic.

She put it in Theo's hands.

"What is it?" Theo unwound the cloth. Her fingers, professional without thinking, read the type by touch before her eyes could — and Odile saw her stop breathing.

*THE TRUTH NEEDS NO LICENSE,* it read. Beneath it, smaller, in letters Gaspard must have cast himself: *SET AND LOCKED BY GASPARD FERRAND, WHO DIED BEFORE HE COULD PULL THE SHEET.*

"He set it the night before the barricades," Odile said. "Eleven years it's sat in that box. Pull it for me, Delacroix. Pull it first, before anything else, and let that man finally see his own words in daylight."

Theo held the galley the way one holds a sleeping child. Outside, the light was failing, and somewhere across the city a church bell rang the hour — five strokes, flat and unhurried, marking time off like a man counting out coin he knows won't cover the debt.

Two days, Odile thought. Two days to set ten thousand truths and survive the setting.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, the sea was already turning toward the tide.
