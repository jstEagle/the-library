## 13. Setting the Type

The last customer had gone home before dusk, and Theo turned the sign on the door of Delacroix & Son to face the street — *Fermé*, the word her father had painted in gold leaf thirty years ago, when the shop was young and so was she, and the name on the sign still told a truth no one thought to question.

She locked the door. She drew the shutters. Then she lit every lamp in the composing room at once, all six of them, until the little room glowed like a chapel, and stood for a moment in the middle of it with her hands hanging empty at her sides.

Tonight she would set treason one letter at a time.

Luc sat at the workbench with Odile's cracked cipher spread before him and the ledger open beside it, his bandaged shoulder propped against the wall where she'd wedged a cushion. The fever was down but not out; she could see it banked in his eyes, the way a fire banks under ash, waiting. He had slept four hours on the cellar cot and refused to sleep more. "Tomorrow they'll be looking for us," he said. "Tonight is the only night we get."

"Then we don't waste it." Theo pulled her apron from its nail and tied it on. The gesture was so ordinary it steadied her — twenty years of tying that apron, twenty years of standing at this case, and the type did not know what words it would carry tonight. Lead was loyal that way. Lead only wanted to be handled well.

Bird's bed had been made in the paper bin since the raid, after she'd declared the cot "too flat for birds," and there she lay now among the folded stock, one fist curled under her chin, breathing in the slow deep rhythm of a child who trusted the room she slept in. Theo looked at her a long moment. *That's the arithmetic*, she thought. *Every line we set tonight buys her a morning.*

She took up the composing stick.

* * *

They began with the names.

Odile's cipher key — the substitution grid she'd worked out over three nights by candlelight, muttering to herself like a woman arguing with God — reduced the ledger's elegant shorthand to plain French, and plain French was worse than any cipher. Theo had read the decoded pages once, all of them, and then made herself read them again because Luc needed her to check his transcription and she would not let him see her hands shake twice.

Colonel Vassey. Prefect Roussel. Judge Aubertin. Eleven officers, three magistrates, two priests, a shipping magnate whose barges carried confiscated goods to sea while their owners' widows queued at relief offices. And beside each name, in the ledger's cold columns: sums. Sums paid. Sums taken. Sums owed for men shot at the barricades in March, entered as *expenses of order*, as though the bullets were candles for a dinner party.

And Marcel. Marcel's signature, seven times now she knew of, the last dated three days ago.

"You're hesitating," Luc said quietly. He didn't look up from the ledger. He had learned to hear her hands the way musicians hear an orchestra — the small stutter of a compositor's doubt traveling through the stick.

"I'm not hesitating." She slid a *v* into place. It was true; she wasn't hesitating. She was simply aware, with the whole surface of her mind, that the letters passing through her fingers were the same letters her brother's fingers had signed, that lead was lead whether it built a wedding announcement or a gallows speech, and that tonight the shop smelled exactly as it had smelled every night of her childhood — oil, ink, the mineral breath of metal — and that her father was dead and could not see what his press would say by morning.

"Read me the next passage again," she said instead. "The estates."

Luc found it with his finger. "'Confiscated holdings of prisoners executed under Article Nine, liquidated through the harbor trust, proceeds distributed per schedule.'" His voice went flat and careful on *executed*, the way a man walks flat and careful across ice. "There are forty-one names in the schedule. Theo — forty-one families."

"Then forty-one lines." She squared the first galley and reached for the next. Her father's voice lived in her hands: *A line badly set is a lie told slowly, Théo. Take the time to tell it true.* She could hear him so clearly tonight it was almost unbearable — old Gaspard Delacroix leaning over her shoulder in this very room, correcting her spacing, telling her that print was the only promise a man could make that outlived him. *You put a thing in ink, it stays put. That's the whole religion of it.*

Well, Papa, she thought. Tonight your press keeps a different kind of promise.

* * *

Midnight came and went. The lamps burned low and she trimmed them. Outside, the curfew patrols passed on the hour, boots on cobbles, and each time they did Theo lifted her hands from the type and stood motionless until the sound faded, as though the letters themselves could be arrested if caught moving.

It was on one of those silences — the third or fourth, she'd lost count — that the note came.

Not through the door. Through the gap beneath it, folded tight and weighted with a pebble, sliding across the floorboards like something alive. Bird stirred in the bin. Luc's head came up sharply, and for one heartbeat neither of them breathed.

Theo crossed the room and picked it up. Plain paper, unsealed, no mark. She unfolded it and the handwriting hit her before the words did — that upright, disciplined hand, every letter a soldier at attention. She'd known it her whole life. It had addressed her birthday letters. It had signed their father's funeral notices.

*Théo.*

*I know what you have. I know what you mean to do. I will not pretend I can stop you, which should tell you how far things have moved beyond me. But hear this once and believe it: Vassey has the printers' quarter listed for tomorrow evening. Every shop. Yours is third on the list, which means he already suspects, which means you have until dark and not one hour more.*

*A ship sails on the morning tide. Luc will know it. There is one berth secured — I arranged it weeks ago for reasons that seemed important then. Take the child. Take Luc. If there is any way left to take yourself, take it.*

*Sail tomorrow, or be arrested with the rest. Those are the choices as they now exist. I am sorry they are so small.*

*M.*

Luc read it upside down as she held it, and when she lowered it he said nothing for a moment. The lamp flame ticked.

"He's warning us," Luc said at last. "Why?"

"Because he loves me." Her voice came out strange, distant. "Or because he wants to be able to say he warned me. Or because some part of him still believes the ledger can be survived if only the right people run fast enough." She refolded the note along its original creases. "He thinks this is a flood, Luc. Something you get ahead of."

"And you think?"

Theo looked at the galley on the stone bench — twelve lines of the prefecture's payments, locked and level, waiting for the chase. She thought about the word *arrested* sitting in her brother's careful hand, and about how it had frightened her less than the word *executed* had, reading it off the ledger page. She had been afraid of many things this month. She discovered, examining herself now the way she'd examine a misaligned form, that fear had been refined into something else. Something with edges.

"I think a man who sets type knows the difference between escaping a sentence and finishing one," she said. "Put the paper away. We're behind."

* * *

Two o'clock. Three. The work acquired its own gravity, its own weather.

This was the part outsiders never understood, Luc realized — or perhaps Theo realized it, watching him watch her, because she was doing most of the watching even while her hands never stopped. Composition looked like nothing. A woman standing at a case, picking up letters, putting them down. An hour could pass and produce four inches of type. But inside that hour was the entire architecture of the argument: where the names went so they struck hardest, where the sums sat beside them like witnesses, how a paragraph broke so the reader fell into the next one before he could decide to look away. Every em quad was a decision. Every space between words, measured and deliberate, was the difference between a mob's shout and a court's verdict.

"The sums go directly under the names," Theo said. "Not in a separate table. Underneath. So no one can skim past them."

"They'll say it's forgery."

"They'll say everything. Set it anyway."

Luc dictated; she composed; the galleys filled. There was a rhythm to it that became almost liturgical — his voice reading the decoded entries in that flat clerk's diction, her hands answering in lead, the press waiting silent in the next room like a congregation. Once, reaching into the case for a capital V, her fingers brushed the compartment where she kept the ornaments, and she paused. Her father had cut that V himself, back when he cut his own display faces, back when the shop made things instead of merely printing them. The letter was worn smooth at the shoulders from thirty years of use.

She set it in Colonel Vassey's name. It fit perfectly.

Around three, Bird woke — not crying, just surfacing, the way children do, blinking at the lamplight with the solemn confusion of the very young. Theo went to her without deciding to, the way her body simply redirected itself. Bird's hair was flattened on one side and her cheek bore the imprint of a fold of wrapping paper.

"Why aren't you sleeping?" Bird asked.

"Because I'm working."

"At night?"

"Some work can only be done at night." Theo smoothed the girl's hair down. Bird considered this with the gravity she gave all pronouncements, then reached up and patted the wooden case at the edge of the bin — the one holding the type scraps she'd spent weeks sorting into a tin by size, wrong half the time, treasured entirely.

"Will you need my f's?" she whispered. "You can borrow my f's."

Theo laughed — a short, wet sound that surprised her — and kissed the top of the small head. "I may indeed, little bird. Keep them ready." And Bird, satisfied, burrowed back down among the papers and was asleep before Theo had straightened.

She stood a moment longer than she needed to. *Sail tomorrow,* Marcel had written. She pictured the berth: a narrow bunk, salt air, a horizon with no colonels in it. She pictured Bird on it, safe, growing up in some port where the worst thing a printer's daughter feared was a paper cut.

Then she went back to the case, because the prefecture's accounts would not transcribe themselves, and because some promises could only be kept in lead.

* * *

By five the forme was nearly full.

It lay on the imposing stone like a city seen from above — columns and blocks, the dense black neighborhoods of text separated by the white streets of margins, and threaded through it, unmistakable, the names. Forty-one estates. Eleven officers. Three magistrates. Two priests. One colonel, set in her father's own hand-cut capitals, large enough that a man could read the name across a market square.

Theo locked the last lines with furniture and quoins, tightened the chase, and stood looking down at the whole assembled page. This was always the moment — the quiet one, the private one, the moment composites never spoke of because it sounded like vanity and wasn't. The moment the form became true. Not the words — the words were Luc's and Odile's and the dead men's. The *form*. The thing made level and fixed and real, ready to give one impression and then ten thousand identical ones, so that what had existed in one leather book in one hiding place would exist everywhere at once, in every hand that took a copy, unburnable, unsilenceable.

*You put a thing in ink, it stays put.*

"Papa," she said softly, to the empty room, to the smell of oil and metal, "look what your press says tonight."

Luc had fallen asleep at the bench somewhere around four, mid-sentence, his cheek on the cipher pages, and she let him sleep. The sky beyond the shuttered windows was going grey at the seams. In an hour the runners would come — Odile's people, slipping in through the yard door two at a time, carrying the galleys out to three other presses across the quarter so that no single seizure could kill the print run. In two hours the tide would begin to turn. Somewhere on the water a ship waited with one berth, and somewhere in a barracks her brother waited with whatever was left of his career, and somewhere in the fortress on the hill Colonel Vassey was, at this moment, being told that the printers' quarter would burn by evening.

Third on the list. He'd written *third on the list*, which meant he'd seen the list, which meant her brother stood close enough to Vassey to read death warrants before they were signed.

Theo wiped the ink plate, laid fresh ink, and began to prepare the press for the proof pull — one copy, first, to check the form before the runners came. Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a kind of wonder. All night they had not shaken once.

She was reaching for the tympan when the knock came — soft, twice, the signal Odile had given her, and then, before she could answer it, a second knock. Different. Heavier. From the front.

From the street.

Through the gap in the shutter, grey light showed her a figure in civilian clothes standing too still in the doorway, and pinned to the doorframe beside him, fluttering in the harbor wind, a printed notice — fresh ink, official seal, the new decree she could read even upside down from six feet away:

*BY ORDER OF COLONEL VASSEY: ALL PRINTERS TO PRESENT THEIR FORMES FOR INSPECTION AT SEVEN O'CLOCK THIS MORNING.*
