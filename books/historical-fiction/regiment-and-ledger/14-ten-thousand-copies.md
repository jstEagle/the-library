## 14. Ten Thousand Copies

The first bundle went out before the gulls woke.

Luc Arnaud stood in the alley behind the print shop with his arms full of newsprint and his heart hammering like a boy's, watching Odile Ferrand's couriers take up their loads one by one. There were nine of them in the grey half-light — a fishwife, two dockhands, a seminarian who had abandoned his collar for a porter's smock, a girl of perhaps fifteen with quick eyes and quicker hands. They said nothing. They had been told not to. Each took a wrapped stack of forty sheets, tucked it beneath oilcloth or fish or firewood, and melted into the streets of Vellacourt like water into sand.

Ten thousand copies. Theo had set the type herself through the night, her fingers black to the second knuckle, her eyes red-rimmed but absolutely steady. When the last forme was locked she had stepped back from the press and looked at it the way other people looked at altars.

"Go," she had said. And now he was going.

Luc carried his own bundle against his chest, under a borrowed coat that smelled of tar and someone else's sweat. His ribs still ached where the soldiers' boots had found him in March; his shoulder throbbed where the newer wound had not quite closed. He had stopped cataloguing his injuries sometime around the hour he first saw the ledger's cipher cracked open on Theo's worktable. Pain was currency now, and he was rich enough.

The market at Saint-Aubin opened at dawn, as it always did, with carts and cries and the smell of blood from the butcher stalls. Luc walked its length twice before he made his drop — a bundle left atop a barrel of salt herring, another slid beneath a fallen awning, a third pressed into the hands of the old woman who sold chestnuts and who, he would learn later, had been Odile's eyes in the market for eleven years. By seven o'clock, sheets were changing hands faster than coins. He watched a stevedore read the first lines and go very still, the way a man goes still when the ground shifts under him.

*By order of Colonel Étienne Vassey, Commandant of the Port District: payments rendered. Prisoner Émile Gorse, executed 12 January. Estate auctioned 20 January. Proceeds diverted.*

*Prisoner Marguerite Solane, executed 19 January—*

He had written those transcriptions himself, dictating to Theo line by line while the press clacked below them like a mechanical heart. He knew every name by heart now. That was the thing no one warned you about: knowing names was a weight, and ten thousand copies was ten thousand chances to put the weight down.

A woman near the fountain began to read aloud.

She had a plain voice, unremarkable, the voice of a woman who shouted at children across courtyards. But the square quieted around her anyway, the way it quiets for a hymn. *Payments rendered. Executions ordered. Estates sold. Gold shipped.* When she reached the third name — a name everyone in Saint-Aubin knew, because the man's widow still sold lace two stalls away — someone swore aloud, long and filthy and reverent, and then the reading was taken up by a man with a better voice, and then by a boy standing on an overturned crate, and the words moved through the crowd like fire moving through dry grass.

Luc bought a cup of wine he did not want and stood in the corner of a tavern doorway and let himself feel, for exactly one minute, the thing he had chased across two provinces and a river of blood: it was working.

Then the church bells began to ring out of sequence, which meant the barracks gates, and the minute ended.

* * *

At the garrison of the Fourth Line Regiment, the broadsheets arrived folded inside loaves of bread.

This had been Odile's idea, delivered with the flat pragmatism of a widow who had buried a printer and raised two sons on suspicion. The regimental bakery took its flour from three mills; one of the millers owed the network a favor older than the war. By the time the morning mess was served, a hundred soldiers were holding the same paper their officers were trying to confiscate in the city.

Luc did not see this himself. He heard it, hours later, from the seminarian-porter, whose real name was Anselme and who talked too much when he was frightened, which was always.

"They wouldn't post guard on the north gate," Anselme whispered, hunched beside Luc in the back room of a chandlery off the Rue des Forges. "Twelve men, sergeant included. They stacked their muskets and sat down and read. Read it all, every entry. Then the sergeant — a Breton, big fellow, hands like hams — he folded his copy very carefully, like it was a letter from home, and put it inside his jacket. And they just stayed there. Sitting. Nobody gave the order to move and nobody asked for one."

"What happened to them?"

"Nothing yet. Vassey sent a captain. The captain stood there shouting about mutiny and the sergeant said — Anselme's voice dropped as though the words themselves might be overheard — he said, *'Sir, we were told we fought for France. Show me France in this book.'*"

Luc closed his eyes. In the darkness behind them he saw the ledger pages again, the columns of figures, each number a man shot against a wall so that his farm or his shop or his wife's dowry could be signed over and sold and the gold crated aboard a ship flying honest colors. He had copied those numbers once as a clerk, obediently, telling himself that clerks record and do not decide. He understood now that recording was also a decision. Every silent entry had been a brick in the wall, and he had laid them neatly, in rows.

"You're smiling," Anselme said, uneasy.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrible. Smile properly or don't."

So Luc smiled properly, and it felt strange on his face, like wearing another man's coat.

* * *

By noon the barricades had begun.

They rose the way they always rose in Vellacourt — not from any plan but from the city's own muscle memory, forty years of uprisings stored in its stones. First the Rue Basse, where the fishwives overturned a cart and dragged a hogshead into the gap. Then the crossing at Saint-Michel, where shipwrights tore up paving stones with crowbars and sang while they worked, the old song, the one about the baker's daughter and the tyrant's head, which mothers had been singing to children for three generations without anyone in authority quite daring to forbid it.

Luc crossed the city twice more with fresh bundles, and everywhere he went the air had changed texture. Morning had been exhilaration — the bright, breathless joy of a secret escaping. Noon was something harder. Men were arming themselves with boat hooks and reaping hooks. Women were carrying children up to upper floors and shuttering windows and coming down again to haul stone. A barber had hung a black ribbon from his pole. Somewhere a drum was beating, irregular, amateur, insistent.

At the harbor taverns the broadsheets were nailed to doors. Luc watched a gang of stevedores stand before one in silence, and then, without a word passing between them, walk off the job in a body — four hundred men, maybe five, leaving cranes loaded and holds half-filled, leaving the port itself stopped like a struck clock. A customs officer started to protest and thought better of it. The gold Vassey meant to ship could not ship if there were no hands to load it. Luc understood this before he fully understood anything else that day: they had not just printed truth. They had printed a strike.

In the Tonneau Noir, the oldest of the harbor taverns, a one-eyed pilot stood on a table and read the final page aloud — the list of officials in Vassey's pay, magistrates and quartermasters and two aldermen — and at each name the crowd answered with a wordless sound, a low collective growl, and when the last name fell the whole room turned toward the door as one animal, and Luc slipped out ahead of them because he knew what a crowd looks like at the moment it stops being a crowd.

It looks like an army that hasn't chosen its officers yet.

* * *

He was in the chandlery again when the drums changed.

All afternoon the city had rung with the regime's own signals — recall drums, assembly drums, the arrogant tattoo of cavalry mustering on the Place Royale. Then, near dusk, a new rhythm rolled down from the citadel: slower, doubled, wrong. Anselme, leaning from the loft window, went pale.

"That's the martial law call," he said. "I heard it in 'thirty-one. My father described it." He corrected himself, quietly. "I heard my father hear it."

Martial law. Luc climbed to the window and looked out over the rooftops. Smoke stood up from three quarters of the city — cook-fires multiplied a hundredfold, barricade fires, and here and there something else, something with a building under it. The bells of Saint-Rémy were ringing continuously now, all of them, which was either alarm or celebration depending on which side rang them, and probably both.

Proclamation bills were already pasted up along the Rue des Forges, slapped onto walls by municipal men working fast and departing faster. Luc tore one down and read it by the last light:

*BY ORDER OF COLONEL E. VASSEY, COMMANDANT OF THE PORT DISTRICT, MARTIAL LAW IS DECLARED THROUGHOUT THE CITY AND APPROACHES THEREOF. ASSEMBLIES ARE FORBIDDEN. THE STREETS WILL BE CLEARED BY NIGHTFALL. ANY PERSON FOUND DISTRIBUTING SEDITIOUS PAPERS, OR HARBOURING THE PERSONS RESPONSIBLE FOR THEIR COMPOSITION AND PRINTING, WILL BE SHOT WITHOUT TRIAL.*

*A REWARD OF TWO THOUSAND FRANCS IS OFFERED FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO THE ARREST OF THE PRINTER KNOWN AS "THE HAND OF SAINT-RÉMY," AND FIVE HUNDRED FRANCS FOR EACH ACCOMPLICE.*

Two thousand francs. Enough to live on for years. Enough, in this winter, to buy a dozen betrayals before supper.

"The Hand of Saint-Rémy," Anselme murmured. "They don't know her name."

"No," Luc said. "And we keep it that way."

But even as he said it he was doing the arithmetic that had kept him alive as a clerk in a corrupt office: names leak. Ten thousand copies meant ten thousand hands, and some fraction of ten thousand hands belonged to people who were frightened, or greedy, or both. Someone had seen couriers leaving the alley behind Delacroix's shop. Someone always saw. The reward didn't need to catch the printer; it only needed to make every neighbor a possible informer until the shop suffocated in suspicion.

And there was a second arithmetic underneath the first, colder: Marcel Delacroix. Theo's brother, Vassey's protégé, his signature drying in the ledger's margins three days before they'd broken the cipher. If the colonel connected the press to the Delacroix name — and he would, because Marcel was the thread that ran from the ledger straight into the commandant's own household — then everything Theo had built would burn for the sin of sharing her father's name with a guilty man.

* * *

Full dark came down on a city that refused to obey the curfew.

From the chandlery loft, Luc watched the patrols move through the streets below — lanterns swinging, muskets slung, boots striking in that deliberate cadence armies use to remind cities who owns the night. At the Rue Basse barricade the patrol halted. A challenge was called. Something was thrown down from the barricade — a paper, white in the lantern light, fluttering like a wounded bird. One of the soldiers picked it up. Luc saw the man hold it close to his comrade, both heads bent together over it, and then the patrol moved on without firing, without clearing anything, and the barricade stood.

Word traveled the way it does in sieges and celebrations, mouth to mouth, window to window: the Third Battalion had refused to advance on the fishmarket. Two companies of the Fourth were sitting on their arms at the north gate. A lieutenant had torn off his epaulettes in front of his own sergeant. The naval yard had gone dark and silent, and no order could relight it.

Vassey still held the citadel, the arsenal, and the telegraph to the capital. He still had artillery on the heights. A cornered regime with cannon is not a defeated regime; it is a regime deciding how much of your city it is willing to spend.

Around midnight the first answer came: a single gun spoke from the citadel, and somewhere in the Saint-Michel quarter a house lost its roof. Not aimed at the barricade. Aimed at the idea of the barricade. The boom rolled across the harbor and came back off the water, and after it, in the ringing silence, the city did the most frightening and magnificent thing Luc had ever witnessed.

It sang.

From a hundred streets, ragged and defiant, rose the old song about the baker's daughter and the tyrant's head. The soldiers on the barricades were singing it too. So, Luc discovered, was he, his voice cracking on the high notes, tears running freely down a face he had once ordered the world to forget.

He thought: *We have won.*

He thought, a half-second later, with the clarity that had kept him breathing through two provinces of pursuit: *No. We have started something. Winning is a different country, and the road there is paved with what tomorrow costs.*

Because tomorrow the tide turned, and the fleet sailed — the fleet whose departure date sat in the ledger's final pages beside the manifest of smuggled gold. Tomorrow Vassey would either be a fugitive or a conqueror, and cornered men with artillery do not negotiate. And tomorrow, if the colonel's intelligence was worth the fortune he'd paid for it, the trail of white paper leading through every market and gate and tavern in Vellacourt would be traced backward, street by street, courier by courier, to a lamplit print shop on the Rue du Puits where a woman was even now cleaning ink from between her fingers, and where a child named Bird slept in a bin of paper scraps, dreaming whatever four-year-olds dream while the world catches fire around them.

Luc pulled the proclamation from his pocket and read the reward line again. Two thousand francs for the printer. Five hundred for each accomplice.

Then he folded it, very carefully, the way the Breton sergeant had folded his copy — like a letter from home — and put it inside his jacket, over his heart, where the next bullet would have to earn its passage.

Below, the fires burned. Above, the citadel's searchlight swept the harbor and found, riding at anchor in the roads, the masts of the fleet, black against the last stars, waiting for the tide.

There were six hours of dark left. Luc went downstairs to warn them.
