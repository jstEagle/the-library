## 15. The Berth Question

The harbor smelled of pitch and panic.

Theo had not been down to the quay since the broadsides went out — since the city caught fire in the way that paper catches fire when ten thousand hands strike a match at once — but Luc had sent word through one of Odile's surviving couriers, a boy of perhaps fifteen with soot on his knuckles and fear worn smooth into habit: *the print shop, after dark, come alone.* She came with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs and Bird's hand in hers, because coming alone had stopped meaning anything three days ago, and because she could not leave the child in the shop with the shutters half-burned and soldiers' boots audible on the cobbles at all hours like a second tide.

They picked their way along the breakwater road, past warehouses with their doors stove in, past a barricade of overturned fish carts that boys were reinforcing with cobblestones pried up bare-handed from the street. Someone had nailed a broadsheet to the customs house door — her broadsheet, her type, her father's ink — and it flapped in the sea wind like something alive trying to get free. Theo did not stop to look at it. She knew every line by heart. She had set each one at two in the morning with her fingers bleeding from the brass edges and Luc dictating corrections in a voice gone hoarse, and she knew that somewhere across the city Colonel Vassey was doing arithmetic of his own: how many men it would take to burn the printers' quarter to the waterline.

"Mama." Bird said it absently, the way children say things they have decided are true, tugging her hand. "There's the ship."

There was the ship. The *Perdrix* rode at anchor beyond the mole, a brigantine with patched sails and a hull that had carried salt cod for twenty years and would carry something far more dangerous tonight if the wind held: witnesses. Names. A copy of a ledger that could never be burned because it would be sleeping in the hold of a ship bound for ports where Vellacourt's colonels had no reach.

One berth left. That was what the boy's message had said, and Luc confirmed it an hour later in the back room of a cooperage that smelled of oak shavings and old sweat, where he sat propped against a barrel with his wounded side bandaged and his face gray as unprinted paper.

"One berth," he said. "Captain Marrec owes me from before all this — I settled his accounts when his clerk ran off to the army, and he remembers debts even now. One berth, paid in full, plus this." He set a canvas purse on the barrel head between them. It made a sound like a dead thing dropping. "Coin enough to live three months anywhere with a coast. Enough to start again."

"Where is the ship sailing?"

"Amsterdam first, then wherever the wind and the money agree. There are printers in Amsterdam who'd weep to have your hands, Theo. They'd give you a shop inside a year."

She looked at the purse. She looked at Luc — at the man who had forgotten his face so thoroughly that he had nearly succeeded, who had crawled back to her door twice bleeding, who had slept in her type cellar among the cases of twelve-point bodoni like a man sleeping in a coffin he'd ordered himself. And she did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since the boy brought the message, the arithmetic that was not arithmetic at all but a knife laid flat across her palm.

Three people. One berth.

Bird, asleep against a coil of rope in the corner, her cheek printed faintly with the weave of the burlap, her breath whistling the small tune she whistled in sleep. Marcel, whose name was now on every tribunal list in the city, whose arrest warrant had been drafted — Odile's people said — before the ink on the broadsides was dry, who had surrendered Vassey's records into Theo's keeping and then walked back out into the streets in civilian clothes like a man walking into his own grave and closing the gate behind him. And herself.

"Say it," Luc said quietly. "You've been not-saying it for two days. Say it and let me answer."

"Luc—"

"Say it."

"The child goes. That isn't a question. That's the whole point of—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, pressed her palm flat against the barrel until the wood bit back. "She's four years old. She has no name anyone will claim, no family anyone has found, and a regime that burns printers' shops does not trouble itself over whether there's a child inside when it sets the torch. Bird goes on that ship. That is settled. It was settled the night you put her in my arms."

"Agreed," Luc said. "I'll go with her. I'm the one who knows the ledger by heart — every cipher, every date, every name. If the copy is questioned, if some customs man in Amsterdam wants proof, I'm the proof. I'm the witness. That's my value now; you said it yourself when you set the type." His eyes, dark and steady and too old for his thirty-odd years, did not waver. "So it isn't three people and one berth, Theo. It's two."

And there it was. The knife turned flat, showing its edge.

Marcel. Or herself.

* * *

She walked out of the cooperage because she needed air or she needed to move or she needed not to be looked at while she thought, and Luc, who had learned her well enough in six weeks of shared treason, did not follow. Bird woke and trotted beside her, asking questions about the cranes, about the gulls, about why the masts moved against the clouds when the ships stood still, and Theo answered in the automatic voice mothers and guardians use, the voice that runs on its own engine while the rest of the person is elsewhere entirely.

Two. One berth.

She tried to make it mathematics. Her father would have approved of the attempt — Alaric Delacroix had believed everything in life could be set like type, measured in ems, locked in a chase, and pulled clean. So: Marcel was guilty. Not of the worst of it — the executions had been Vassey's orders, the profiteering off dead men's estates had been Vassey's design — but guilty of signing, of staying, of ambition dressed as duty, of warning her instead of stopping him. Guilty of three names she had known her whole life: Madame Oury who sold chestnuts by the cathedral steps. The Pellan brothers who repaired nets. Old Tomas, who had taught her brother to whistle.

And Marcel had done the only thing resembling repentance available to a man in his position: he had handed over the rest of Vassey's records, the ones that named every official on the take, and he had offered testimony. Testimony meant the tribunal. Testimony meant standing in front of men with muskets and saying *I did this, and here is how, and here is who else.* It might buy him prison instead of a wall and a firing squad. It might not.

If he fled — if she gave him the berth — the ring lost nothing. Vassey's network would lose one mid-level officer and gain a martyr-in-absentia story it could twist however it liked. The tribunal would proceed without its best witness. And Marcel would spend the rest of his life in Amsterdam or Hamburg or God-knew-where, drinking himself hollow with the knowledge that his sister had bought him out of justice with a ship's berth, and he would hate her for it by the second year, and he would be right to.

If she took the berth herself — Luc's argument, pressed gently, twice, with the patience of a man who had thought it through: *you're the printer, Theo, you're the one they'll hang, your name is on the price notice now, they've offered forty livres for the woman with the press* — then Bird still sailed safe. The ledger still sailed safe. But Marcel faced the tribunal alone, without his sister's voice in the chamber, without the original documents delivered by her hand, without the one person in Vellacourt whose word the defecting officers had begun to trust. And she would spend the rest of her life in a foreign city with salt air and good light, printing other people's truths, knowing her brother had gone to the wall while she watched the coastline shrink.

Both choices saved someone. Both choices buried someone. This was the impossible arithmetic of love: it refused to balance. Love was not a forme you could lock and pull; love was a case of type spilled across the floor, and every piece was someone's name.

"Theo." Bird tugged her sleeve. "Why is that man crying?"

A sailor sat on an overturned crate near the cooperage door, a big man with tarred hands, weeping without any effort to hide it, watching the *Perdrix* at anchor. Theo understood without asking. Half the city wanted on that ship. Half the city had learned in the last three days that the tide was a border crossing and borders close.

"Because the sea is far away," Theo said, "and he loves someone on this side of it."

"Oh," said Bird, satisfied, and asked instead if gulls could swim.

* * *

She went back into the cooperage and found Luc standing at the small window, one hand pressed to his bandaged side, watching the same ship. He didn't turn when she spoke.

"I won't choose tonight," she said.

"Theo—"

"No. Hear it once, all the way through, because I've heard you all the way through twice." She set the ledger on the barrel between them, the oilcloth dark with harbor damp. "Tomorrow the tribunal convenes at dawn — you told me yourself, Odile's last courier before they took her. Marcel testifies or he hangs unforgiven. The tide turns at nine. Those facts don't negotiate."

"And you stand between them like a compositor between two formes," Luc said, "and you think waiting is neutral. It isn't. Waiting is a choice too. Waiting is what people do when they want someone else to make it for them."

"I know what waiting is." Her voice came out low and even, and she was surprised by it, by the calm in it, the way ink lies calm in the ductor right up until it meets the paper. "I waited three weeks before I opened your ledger. I waited through Sunday dinner watching my brother lie to me with a child playing at his boots. I waited through a raid with soldiers' bayonets through my father's type cases while my heart tried to beat its way out through my teeth. I know exactly what waiting costs, Luc Arnaud, because I have paid it in full every time and never once regretted the purchase. So hear me: I will not choose between my brother and myself tonight, on your schedule, in a room that smells of shavings, while a child sleeps in the corner. Tomorrow I will walk into that tribunal chamber with the original ledger and Marcel's records under my arm, and I will read names until the roof comes off, and when it's done — when it's done — I will know which choice is real. Because it isn't three people and one berth. It never was."

"It isn't?"

"No." She touched the ledger's cover, the leather worn soft as skin. "It's what the truth needs and who can carry it. And I won't know that until dawn. Neither do you. You think you're offering me escape. You're offering me exile with better weather."

For a long moment Luc said nothing. Then he laughed — short, rusty, surprised out of him like blood from a cut — and shook his head.

"You know what Odile said to me, the first time? Years ago, before any of this. She said the Delacroix girl would be trouble someday, because her father taught her that type doesn't lie and neither should the woman who sets it." He pushed the canvas purse across the barrel toward her. "Keep it. Berth stays open until the tide. Captain Marrec sails when I say sail, and I say sail at nine, and not one minute after, whatever's unfinished."

"Whatever's unfinished," Theo repeated.

He looked past her at the sleeping child, and something in his face went young and wrecked and honest. "You know I'd give her my berth and stay myself, if staying served anything. If my staying toppled one colonel. But I'm worth more alive in Amsterdam than dead in a ditch outside the harbor gate, and so are you, and that's the whole ugly truth of it. Survival is also a weapon, Theo. Sometimes the sharpest one in the case."

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we'll see who gets to fire it."

She gathered Bird onto her hip — the child heavy and warm and boneless with sleep, her head lolling against Theo's shoulder, her fist closing reflexively in Theo's collar — and stepped out into the night. The wind had come around with the moon, off the land now, carrying smoke and the far-off sound of a crowd singing something ragged and defiant in the direction of the barracks. Behind her, in the cooperage, Luc began counting coin by candlelight, paying a debt to a captain who remembered them.

Above the rooftops, eastward where the citadel stood, a single window burned in the tribunal building — lit already, hours before dawn, the way a pressroom is lit before a run. Someone inside was setting type of their own: warrants, verdicts, lists. Theo stood a moment in the dark with her daughter-not-daughter breathing against her neck and looked at that lit window the way a compositor looks at a locked forme she means to break.

Dawn first, she told herself. Tribunal first. Tide after.

And if the tide came before the verdict — she pulled Bird closer and started walking — then God help her, she would choose, and live on whatever it cost.
