## 16. Marcel's Choice

The uniform had been the hardest thing to take off.

Marcel Delacroix stood in his rented room above the cavalry barracks and looked at it hanging on the door—the dark blue coat with its brass buttons polished by a woman he paid to polish them, the epaulettes that had once seemed like wings. He had worn it eleven years. He had put it on the morning after their father died, when Theo was still in the shop learning which way the letters faced, and he had told himself then that it was armor against a world that ate printers' daughters for breakfast. Somewhere along the way the armor had grown into the man, or the man into the armor, and now he could not say which of them had betrayed the other first.

He dressed instead in a laborer's coat bought from a secondhand stall near the fish market, wool gone gray at the elbows, smelling faintly of brine and someone else's sweat. A cap pulled low. He took the satchel from under the floorboard—his hands shook so badly at the knot that he had to start twice—and weighed it in his palm. Fourteen pounds of paper and string and wax seals. The rest of Vassey's records. Everything the colonel kept locked in the green cabinet behind his desk at the citadel: requisitions that never reached the regiments they were drawn for, estate inventories of men who had been shot before their property could be inventoried honestly, the shipping manifests for gold that left Vellacourt on navy hulls wearing army colors.

He had copied none of it. He had simply carried it out, an hour ago, past two sentries who saluted him because the habit of rank dies slower than honor. Vassey would discover the theft by morning, or by midnight if the clerk on duty counted the cabinet's keys. Either way, the clock had started, and Marcel Delacroix was walking toward the only place in the city where he still had any claim to be a human being.

* * *

The print shop on Rue des Carêmes wore its wounds openly.

He saw it from the end of the street and stopped, because seeing it hurt worse than he had budgeted for. Their father had raised that facade stone by stone in 1819, carved the sign himself—DELACROIX & FILS, the ampersand curled like a sleeping cat—and now the windows were boarded from the inside with planks Theo must have nailed herself, and one board hung loose where soldiers had pried at it during the raid. A scorch mark climbed the lintel where something burning had been thrown and beaten out. The doorstep bore boot prints in dried mud, dozens of them, military-patterned, going in and coming out and going in again.

He had walked past this shop every Sunday for months on his way to her table, and he had never once looked up at the sign. That was the thing about complicity no one warned you about: it didn't require you to do evil. It only required you to stop looking.

He knocked the pattern—their father's old knock, three quick, one slow—and waited in the cold with his heart going like a press at full pull.

Long enough to count to forty. Then the shutter slit opened, lamplight behind it, and Theo's eye appeared, and Marcel understood in that single glance that she had been sleeping in her clothes with a weapon within reach since the raid, and that the weapon might be a pistol or might be a composing stick, and that either could kill him if she chose.

"It's me," he said. Foolish. She knew it was him; she'd have shot anyone else through the boards by now.

"I know what 'me' means," she said quietly. "I don't know yet what it costs." But the bar scraped, the door opened six inches, and he went through sideways like a thief, which was what he was.

Inside, the shop was devastation arranged around islands of order. Type cases overturned, their contents scattered in lead drifts across the floor like gray snow. The big press stood intact but shoved against the wall, its lever tied down with cord. Paper—good paper, paper he knew the price of—lay water-damaged where the soldiers had emptied the damp cupboard onto the stones. And in the middle of it all, a small camp: blankets, a tin cup, a candle stub, and a girl asleep in a nest made from the paper bin, her thumb in her mouth, a handful of type scraps arranged beside her pillow in careful rows.

Bird. He had held her on his knee at Sunday dinners and let her play with his braid and thought her nothing but a burden the stranger had dumped on Theo's conscience. Looking at her now, small and breathing among the wreckage his signature had helped make, he felt something turn over in his chest with a click like type locking into a chase.

"The cellar," Theo said, not asking. "If we're talking about anything that matters."

* * *

The type cellar was cold and smelled of ink and lamp oil and, underneath, the iron-and-earth smell of the foundations themselves. Theo lit two lamps instead of one, set them far apart so neither face would be all shadow, and sat on an upturned crate with her arms crossed—not hostile, exactly, but braced, the way you brace a wall you expect to lean on you.

Marcel set the satchel on the floor between them and did not open it. Not yet. First things first, and the first thing was the hardest sentence he had ever had to compose, and he was a man who had dictated execution orders without his voice changing.

"Vassey knows," he said. "About the ledger. About Luc Arnaud. He's known since the day I dined with you and watched you deflect me over the bread. I told him afterward that your shop was clean. It's the only lie I ever told him that cost me anything."

Her expression didn't move. "Go on."

"He's had Odile Ferrand's network mapped for three weeks. There's a courier—"

"Aldous," Theo said. "We know. Odile told us. We printed anyway."

"We—" He stopped. Of course they had. Ten thousand copies flooding the markets while he slept in his quarters believing his one clean lie had bought them time. "Then you know what comes next. Martial law. The tribunal. My name is in the ledger, Theo. You read it yourself. You've had weeks to burn it, sell it, forget it. Instead you printed my treason alongside Vassey's profiteering, and tomorrow—or the day after, he won't wait longer than he must—I stand trial in front of the garrison council with the whole city reading excerpts off walls."

"Yes," she said. "I know."

"You could have taken just his pages." His voice cracked on it, and he hated that, hated the weakness, hated more that he couldn't tell whether he was accusing her or pleading. "Cut my names out. Set the forme without them. One compositor's discretion, ten seconds of work, and your brother walks free. Why didn't you?"

"Because it wouldn't be true." She said it flatly, without drama, the way their father used to justify a price. "A ledger with holes isn't evidence, it's a favor. And favors rot. Ask Vassey how many men he's saved with favors and how many of them are still useful." She leaned forward, lamplight catching the ink stains ground permanently into her knuckles—he'd forgotten how deep those stains went, how they were less stains than strata, years of them. "You signed those entries, Marcel. Three names I'd served wine to at Midwinter. You want absolution? It's not mine to give and it doesn't live in a galley of type."

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't." He crouched, untied the satchel, and turned it so she could see the wax seals broken, the documents inside fanned just enough to show the green cabinet's distinctive folio stamping. "Which is why I brought you this."

She looked at it a long time. Her hands stayed folded. "What is it?"

"The rest. Everything else in the cabinet. Requisitions. Estate rolls. The shipping manifests for the gold—dates, hulls, escort rotations. And the private correspondence between Vassey and the intendant-general, including four letters that name officers who haven't been arrested because nobody outside this room knows they're in it. Testimony, Theo. Real testimony, witnessed, sealed, dated in his own hand. Not excerpts off a wall. The whole carcass."

"And you're giving it to me why?" Her voice was steady but he heard the tremor underneath, the sister under the compositor. "Because you love me? Because you're frightened? Because you think I can trade it?"

"Because there's one berth on the morning tide ship." He made himself hold her eyes. "Luc secured passage. One berth, plus coin. Bird takes it—I know, I'm not arguing, she takes it, she carries the copy abroad where it can't be burned. But you, Theo. You don't have to stay here and die for a city that will forget your name inside a year. Take the records with you. Publish them abroad, in a country where the presses aren't shell casings. Name every officer in the ring, including me, in a book that outlives the tribunal, outlives Vassey, outlives all of us. That's worth more than testimony given to twelve garrison officers who'll be someone else's officers in five years. That's permanent."

"That's exile."

"That's survival with a purpose."

"It's running." She stood, finally, and walked to the far wall where the foundation stones showed, and pressed her palm flat against the cold granite as if checking whether the building itself still meant to stand. "You want to buy your redemption with my departure. You hand me the evidence, I carry it away, and by the time it sees print in some Amsterdam journal, everyone in this room who could confirm it is dead or scattered. Clean. Tidy. No witnesses who knew you when you were nine and stole lead from the case to cast toy soldiers."

"Theo—"

"And what happens to you?" She turned. "That's the part your plan leaves out. I sail, you testify or you don't, and either way Vassey's people reach you before the tribunal does, or the tribunal reaches you and finds a defendant with no advocate and no family in the city. You're not offering me a mission, Marcel. You're offering me a head start on grieving you."

The words landed the way the ram lands on a door. He had rehearsed this conversation forty times on the walk across town and in every version he had answers ready. In none of them had he accounted for the fact that his sister had spent her whole life reading proofs—finding the flaw in the line, the dropped letter, the word that didn't belong—and that he was, and always had been, a flawed line.

So he stopped performing. He sat down on the crate she'd vacated, put his elbows on his knees, and told the truth in the plainest type he owned.

"I'm not asking to be forgiven. I'm not even asking to be believed. Here's what I am, Theo, and I'll say it once and then never again unless you ask: I joined the revolution at nineteen because our father's shop got closed by censorship and I wanted to break the men who broke it. Then the revolution lost, and the men who won offered me a uniform, and I took it because it was warm and because I told myself I could steer it from inside. Eleven years of telling myself that. Every entry in that ledger, I told myself the same sentence: if I refuse, they'll find someone worse. Do you know what that sentence is? It's the most efficient machine for making cowards that has ever been built. It runs on nothing. It needs no fuel. And I fed myself into it for a decade and called it strategy."

Bird stirred overhead—a small thump, footsteps, silence. They both listened until the shop above settled again.

"When the tribunal calls me," he went on, quieter, "I'm going to stand up and name Vassey, and the intendant, and the four officers in these letters, and then I'm going to name myself, last, in full, with dates. Not because it saves me—it won't—but because a confession spoken aloud in front of the garrison, on the record, cannot be reprinted selectively by anyone, ever. It's the one piece of type I can still set that nobody can reset behind me." He looked up at her. "But confession alone is cheap. Confession plus documents—that topples the ring. So: you take the records. Whether you publish them from this cellar tonight or from a ship or from Amsterdam, that's yours to decide. I surrender them unconditionally. That's the offer. That's all of the offer. There's no condition about the berth hidden in it, whatever I said a minute ago. Keep both if you want. Keep neither. I'm done bargaining with the people I love."

The lamps hissed. Somewhere above, wind worked at the loose board over the window.

Theo came back from the wall slowly. She knelt, opened the satchel fully, and began, methodically, to inventory—her fingers moving over the folios the way they moved over type, counting, weighing, assessing. He watched her read three pages in silence and saw the exact moment the documents became real to her: her breath changed, her shoulders dropped a half inch, and she pressed the back of her hand briefly to her mouth.

"The gold ships," she murmured. "The fleet sails with it. Luc said—"

"Thursday's tide. Two days. After that the manifest trail goes cold in colonial waters and Vassey's accounts close forever." He paused. "And the tribunal convenes Thursday dawn. Before the tide. He scheduled it that way deliberately—settle my case while the harbor's still fogged, so no crowd, no witnesses from the lower town, no theater. A quiet morning's work and then everyone sails away rich."

She looked up sharply. "Dawn. Before the tide."

"Yes."

"So if someone wanted to save you—" she began, and he saw it happen in her face, the arithmetic starting, the impossible ledger opening: one berth, one brother, one tribunal, one tide.

"Don't," he said, and his voice came out harder than he intended, so he gentled it. "Theo. Don't start doing sums with me in the terms. I've seen what that arithmetic does to you already—you've been carrying it since Luc put the ticket on your table. I'm telling you what I told you: no conditions. If you spend your one chance at the open sea standing in a tribunal chamber reading my crimes aloud, then Vassey wins twice—he takes the port back, and he takes you. Print the truth. Sail with the child. Live somewhere the word *Delacroix* means a printer and not a warning."

"And you?" she whispered. "What happens to the word for you?"

He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that it wasn't bitter. "Whatever they write on it. At least it'll be true. That's more than I've managed in eleven years."

She closed the satchel. Held it against her chest. For a moment she looked like their father—same set of the jaw, same stubborn economy of motion—and Marcel felt the grief of that so sharply he nearly had to look away.

"Stay till light," she said at last. "Not for sentiment. Vassey's men will be hunting whoever emptied that cabinet, and you'll not get two streets in the dark. There's blanket space by the press. Bird sleeps through artillery." A pause, and then, grudging as a payment overdue: "She asked about you last week. Whether her officer friend would come back to dinner."

His throat closed entirely. He nodded, because nodding was all he could manage, and followed her up the cellar stairs into the wrecked and candlelit shop, toward the press their father had built, where for one night—for the last night, whichever way the tide took any of them—the three of them would sleep under the same roof.

Above the boarded windows, toward the harbor, the foghorn sounded once, long and low, counting down hours none of them could see.
