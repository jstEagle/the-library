## 17. Vassey Closes the Net

The smoke reached the shop before the news did.

Theo was at the stone sink scrubbing ink from her forearms when she saw it through the window — a gray-brown plume rising from the direction of the Rue des Imprimeurs, too wide to be a chimney fire, too steady to be anything accidental. She dried her hands without knowing she'd done it, and stood with the rag still clenched in one fist while Bird sorted type scraps at the bench behind her, humming the alphabet song Odile had taught her.

"Keep sorting," Theo said quietly. "I'll be back in an hour."

She took the back streets, as she had learned to take everything now — the way Luc moved through the city, hood up, eyes down, counting the distance between doorways. But there was no hiding from smoke. Half of Vellacourt was already in the streets looking at it. She heard the story before she saw the ruin: soldiers had come to Odile Ferrand's house at first light, not with a knock but with an axe to the door, and dragged the old widow out into the street in her nightclothes. The pamphlet cellar beneath her floorboards — three years of presses, paper, and the names of every courier in the network — was carted off in two army wagons. Odile herself had gone into the Citadel without raising her voice once, witnesses said, though she had looked back at her burning stock of paper the way a woman looks back at a grave.

By the time Theo reached the Rue des Imprimeurs, the shelling had stopped. That was the worst of it — that it had *stopped*, that this was simply what the quarter looked like now, in the ordinary light of mid-morning, with shopkeepers sweeping glass and a boy hauling a charred door frame on his shoulder as if it were driftwood. Two buildings had taken direct hits from the field guns Vassey had positioned on the quay under cover of darkness. One of them had been the old Gauthier pressworks, empty for years. The other had not been empty.

They carried out four bodies while Theo stood in the crowd. She recognized two of them: Perrin the binder, who had set type for her father before she was born; and a girl named Sylvie, nineteen perhaps, who had run leaflets between the market and the docks and used to whistle when she ran, so no one would mistake her steps for a patrol's. A man knelt beside Sylvie's body and closed her eyes with two fingers, gently, the way you close a book you intend to open again.

Theo did not cry. Something in her had passed beyond crying sometime in the night, or perhaps years ago, and what remained was colder and more useful. She memorized faces. She counted the soldiers. She noted which windows along the street were watching and which were shuttered, because that was information too, and information was the only currency left in Vellacourt that hadn't been debased.

*He knows,* she thought, walking home with her hands in her pockets like any woman out for bread. *Odile is in the Citadel, and he knows what came off my press.*

* * *

Luc was waiting inside the shop when she returned, standing very still by the cold stove in the way he did when he had bad news and was deciding how much of it to spend. Bird had fallen asleep in the paper bin again, her cheek against a stack of blank stock, one small hand curled around a lowercase *e*.

"They took Odile," Theo said. It wasn't a question; she wanted to hear him say it.

"This morning. And they burned the distribution points — the cooper's loft, the chandlery on Quai Nord." He hesitated. "There's more."

"Say all of it."

"A notice went up on the Citadel gate an hour ago. Official seal. The tribunal convenes tomorrow." His voice flattened. "At dawn."

"Dawn." Theo turned the word over. Outside, the harbor bell rang the half-hour, and she found herself listening to it the way a sailor listens — counting toward something. "And the tide?"

"Turns at seven minutes past eight."

The arithmetic landed in her chest like a dropped case of type, every piece finding its place with a hard, final sound. Dawn tribunal. Morning tide. One berth on the ship Luc had bought passage on with coin he'd earned copying death lists. Marcel tried and condemned before the water even began to move — timed, she understood suddenly, *precisely*, because Vassey was nothing if not a careful man, and a trial finished before the tide meant no rescue, no appeal, no escape by sea. It meant Marcel would hang or face the wall while the ship that might have carried him cleared the breakwater.

"It's not a tribunal," she said. "It's a formality with a firing squad attached."

"It's worse than that. They're trying him for the ledger — our ledger. The exposé named him. Every copy in the city has his signature in it next to those payments." Luc rubbed his eyes; he hadn't slept, and it showed in the gray of his face. "We printed his name, Theo. We made him the visible crime so the invisible ones could be prosecuted. That was the strategy. I argued for it. I have to live with what it buys."

"And what does it buy? Odile in irons, printers dead in the street, and Marcel—" She stopped. Her voice had risen and Bird stirred in the bin, murmuring, and Theo lowered it to almost nothing. "Marcel facing the wall at dawn."

"He can still testify. If he gives them Vassey—"

"He refused. You know he refused. He told me to my face that martyrs are wasted lead." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Except now the state intends to make a martyr of him anyway, and he won't even get to choose what it's *for*. They'll try him for treason against the regime — not for helping us, but for being caught by us. He'll die protecting Vassey's secrets because the only alternative is confessing, and confessing means admitting he sold his own neighbors for a colonel's favor."

Luc was quiet a moment. Then: "There may be another way."

"Tell me."

"The tribunal is public. Vassey wants it public — he wants the city to watch an officer of the exposed ring put down like a dog, to prove the machine still works. Public means witnesses. Public means the chamber will be full of officers, magistrates, clerks — half the men whose names are in the last pages of that cipher." He met her eyes. "You have the original ledger. You have Marcel's surrendered records — whatever he gave you. If those documents reach the right hands at the right moment—"

"You're talking about storming a military tribunal."

"I'm talking about *reading aloud* in a room where reading aloud is the most dangerous weapon anyone has ever brought." His mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "It worked in the streets. Ten thousand copies turned soldiers into strikers overnight. Why not in the chamber itself?"

Because, she thought, because the streets don't have guns trained on the audience. Because Vassey will be sitting there. Because if it fails, everyone in that room dies and the truth dies with them, bound and confiscated and burned like Odile's paper.

But underneath the fear, something else was moving — the same thing that had moved in her the night a stranger bled on her doorstep, the same thing that moved every time she locked a forme and pulled the lever and felt the press speak. Fury, yes. The fury was real and it was enormous and she let herself feel its whole weight for one long breath: fury at Vassey, who shelled sleeping printers; at the tribunal, which would dress murder in procedure; at Marcel, who had chosen survival until survival chose him; and at herself, most of all, for every year she had measured her life in ems and picas and told herself that keeping the press cold was keeping her family safe.

Then the fury settled, the way sediment settles in washed ink, and what remained beneath it was clear.

"If we go," she said slowly, "we don't go to save Marcel. We can't. Even if it works — even if the chamber turns — he testified against us, Luc. He signed those names. Whatever happens tomorrow, he stands trial for what he actually did, in front of the whole city, or he doesn't stand at all." She held up a hand before Luc could answer. "I'm not saying I want him dead. God knows I don't. I'm saying I finally understand what Odile meant when she said knowledge is a debt that gets collected. The ledger collected from everyone in it. Marcel included. Me included."

"And you're willing to pay?"

"I've been paying since the night you knocked on my shutter." She looked down at Bird, asleep among the blank sheets, and felt the old impossible arithmetic turn over in her chest — the child, the berth, the brother, the truth, each one weighing against the others and none of them balancing. "But hear me, Luc. If we do this — if we walk into that chamber with the original book — then everything depends on what happens after. On the ship sailing. On Bird reaching safe water with the copy that can never be burned. I will not trade that child's future for my brother's neck. Not even for his life. Do you understand me?"

"I understand." Luc's voice was rough. "It's why I left the ledger with you and not with anyone braver."

"That's not what brave means."

"It's exactly what brave means. Braver people hand things to soldiers. You kept printing."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon with the shutters closed, preparing for a morning they might not survive, and it was the strangest workday of Theo's life — folding the copied pages that would go to sympathetic officers Luc still trusted, wrapping the original ledger in oilcloth small enough to bind beneath her coat, teaching Bird, in the guise of a game, the route from the shop to the cooper's ruined loft where Luc would wait with her if the morning went wrong.

"Again," Bird demanded, delighted. "Which door has the iron hoop?"

"The third door past the fountain. What do you say if a soldier asks where you're going?"

"I'm fetching thread for my aunt." Bird curtsied. "Aunties always need thread. Nobody checks thread."

"Nobody checks thread," Theo agreed gravely, and turned away quickly to check the forme of nothing in particular on the press bed, because her eyes had decided to betray her.

Near dusk, a boy scratched at the back shutter — the signal Odile's network had used, though Odile was beyond signals now, and whoever was running the frayed edges of the network had evidently kept the code. The boy thrust a folded paper into Theo's hand and was gone before she could thank him.

It was written in a clerk's neat hand she didn't recognize, but the content needed no cipher:

*The tribunal list is posted. Seven names to be tried at dawn, Citadel court, public gallery open. Delacroix, M., second on the list. First on the list is a woman. Ferrand, O., charged with sedition and conspiracy. She is to be sentenced first, so the gallery understands the cost of the press.*

Theo read it twice. Then she read it a third time, and the clarity that had settled in her that morning shifted, ground against something, and re-formed around a new center of weight.

Odile. Odile, who had warned her that knowledge was a debt, and had paid it in nightclothes before the Citadel gate; who had cracked the cipher with fingers bent from sixty years at the case; who had given the network its spine and Theo its purpose. Sentenced first, deliberately, as theater — the old woman's ruin staged to terrify the gallery before Marcel's execution sealed it.

"She's seventy-one years old," Theo said aloud. Luc looked up. "Vassey isn't just closing the net. He's making examples in order. The widow. The brother. And then anyone else he can reach — which is everyone who bought a copy off a cart yesterday morning. Everyone who whistled while they ran."

"So the tribunal becomes two trials," Luc said quietly. "Theirs — and ours, if we let it happen without answering."

Theo stood at the window a long time. Below, the city was lighting its lamps one district at a time, and here and there, in the working quarters, the lamps were staying dark in mourning for the Rue des Imprimeurs — dark windows like closed eyes all across the hillside, a city pretending to sleep while it decided what it was.

Tomorrow at dawn, in a chamber full of the men the final cipher pages had named, an old woman would be condemned and a young officer would be condemned, and Colonel Vassey would sit at the head of it all wearing the law like a borrowed coat.

Unless someone stood up in the public gallery with the original book and read the true list aloud.

"We need more than the ledger," Theo said, turning from the window. "If we walk in with only my evidence, it's my word against the state's. But Marcel surrendered records — he gave me what he had. If those papers surface in that chamber *from his own hand*—" She stopped herself. "No. He won't. He told me futility is a luxury."

"He told you that when he thought he'd live," Luc said. "Men see differently at dawn with a wall in front of them."

"Then someone has to make sure he sees clearly tonight." Theo pulled her shawl from its hook. "Where are they holding him?"

"The Citadel cells. There's a clerk on the tribunal staff — a debtor of mine from the estate days, a man who owes his freedom to the fact that I never entered his name in certain books. He can get a message to a prisoner. Once."

"One message." She weighed it. "Then write it. Write it exactly as I tell you, word for word, because I've spent three weeks learning what this man cannot refuse." She looked at the press, cold and patient in the lamplight, and felt the strange calm of a forme about to be locked — every piece of type placed, nothing left loose, the lever waiting under her hand. "Tell my brother that at dawn, in front of the whole city, he will have thirty seconds in which he is neither Vassey's creature nor his victim. Tell him he can spend those seconds dying quietly for a colonel's secrets, or he can spend them reading his own confession aloud — and take the entire ring down with him."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then he chooses the wall," Theo said, "and I will stand in that gallery and watch, and I will not look away, and afterward I will put Bird on that ship and finish what we started." Her voice did not shake, and she noticed that, and marveled at it. "Fury is easy, Luc. Clarity is the expensive part. I've paid for both. Write the message."

Luc bent over the paper. The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the shutter, steadied, and burned straight.

Outside, the tide was already beginning to think about turning.
