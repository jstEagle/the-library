## 18. The Tribunal at Dawn

The garrison bell had not yet finished striking five when Theo came out of the printers' quarter with the ledger strapped against her ribs and Marcel's records in a canvas satchel that smelled of his boyhood — of glue and gun oil and the tallow candles they had once stolen to read by.

The streets were still smoking in places. Shells had come into the quarter the night before with no particular aim, tearing the face off the cooper's house on Rue Sable and leaving a clock standing in the wreckage that still told the hour to nobody. Theo walked past it without looking up. There was no hour anymore that mattered except this one.

Odile Ferrand had been taken at midnight. Two of her couriers had seen the soldiers load her into a cart like a sack of meal, and one of them — a boy of sixteen with ink permanently under his nails — had found Theo in the wrecked shop and told her where they would take her: not to the citadel, but to the customs hall, where Vassey was convening a tribunal at dawn. Quick, quiet, before the city fully woke. A tribunal of three officers and a clerk who kept his eyes down. The kind of court that existed only to make murder look like procedure.

Marcel would be tried there. Marcel, whose name ran through the ledger in his own careful hand. Marcel, who had come to her three nights ago out of uniform, hollow-eyed, and laid the rest of Vassey's records on her composing stone like a man laying down a wound.

*Take my story instead of my berth,* he had said. *It's all I have left to give.*

She had meant to use it differently. She had meant to trade it — testimony for his life, papers for a pardon, some arithmetic in which everyone she loved survived. That was the arithmetic she had carried through the shelled streets, turning it over and over like type in a composing stick, trying every combination, finding none of them true.

Then Odile's name came out of the customs hall on the boy's lips, and the arithmetic broke, and what was left underneath was simpler and colder and older than love or fear. It was the thing her father had put into her hands when he first set her before the case of type, when she was small enough that a lowercase e filled her whole palm.

*Ink doesn't lie, little one,* he'd said. *That's its whole virtue. Men lie, governments lie, priests lie. Ink lies for no one. Your job is only to set it straight.*

The guards at the customs hall doors were young, and they were not Vassey's men. They were line infantry pulled from the barricades two days ago, boys who had watched their own sergeants hesitate, and Theo saw the hesitation still living in them like an ember under ash. She did not run. Running would have been the end of her. She walked toward them the way she walked into her own shop every morning of her life, as though the building belonged to her, and stopped close enough that they could see the satchel, the ledger's strap, the ink stains on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing ever fully lifted.

"I'm here for the tribunal," she said. "I'm the printer."

One guard looked at the other. The other shrugged the shrug of a soldier whose officers had stopped making sense. And the door opened, because in a collapsing regime the doors open from exhaustion long before they open from mercy.

* * *

The customs hall had been built for cargo manifests and harbor dues, and it wore its borrowed grandeur badly. A raised bench at one end, three officers seated behind it, a clerk's desk below with a nervous man already sweating through his collar at that hour. Gas lamps burned low against the gray coming through the high windows. Perhaps forty people stood along the walls: officers, functionaries, a few civilians drawn by rumor, and at the center of the floor, between two gendarmes, Marcel.

He was out of uniform. They had given him a plain gray coat, and it made him look younger and smaller, the way stripping a press of its brass makes it look like just a machine. His hands were bound in front of him, but he held them steady, the way he had learned to hold them over a forme of type so nothing would shift. When Theo came through the doors he saw her, and something crossed his face too fast to name — relief, terror, warning — and then the president of the tribunal rapped for order and it was gone.

"Name of the accused," said the president. Colonel Aubert, Theo thought; she had printed his wedding announcement once, years ago, back when announcements were the worst thing her press ever produced.

"Delacroix," said the clerk. "Marcel Delacroix. Charged with conspiracy, embezzlement of state funds, complicity in the deaths of—" the clerk's voice stumbled— "of persons unknown."

"Persons unknown." Theo heard herself repeat it, and heard how loud her own voice was in that room, and did not lower it. "Three of them lived on my street. Madame Roux, who sold flowers by the fish market. Ansel Coudert, a stevedore with a bad knee and four children. Émile Brissac, who repaired shoes. They are not persons unknown. They are persons *named*."

Every head turned. The gendarmes moved toward her, uncertain, and the president said, "Madame, this is a closed proceeding—"

"It is a closed grave," Theo said, "and you are burying people in it before the city wakes up. So let me wake you instead."

She unstrapped the ledger and held it up, and the gaslight caught the leather cover, and she watched recognition move through the room like fire through dry grass. Half those officers had seen this book before. Some of them had signed it.

"The original ledger of Colonel Vassey's administration," she said. "Copied in cipher, kept in duplicate, authenticated by the hands of every officer in this room who took his pay. I am Theo Delacroix, printer, of the shop on Rue des Pressoirs, and I set this book in cipher myself three nights ago, every page, and I can read it aloud until the tide turns if that is what it takes for this city to hear its own name."

"That book is stolen state property," said the officer on the president's left — Captain Roche, she knew him now, his signature sat beside eleven payments in the final pages. "Seize it."

"No one moves," said a voice from the wall.

Theo turned. It was Major Lefèvre — old Lefèvre, silver-haired, three months from pension, a man she had never spoken to in her life. He had not drawn his sword. He had simply said it, quietly, the way her father used to say *mind your spacing*, and the two gendarmes who had been moving toward her stopped moving, and then, slowly, deliberately, stepped back to either side of her as if they had been assigned to protect her all along.

Lefèvre took his hand off his sword hilt and folded his arms. "Proceed, madame."

* * *

She read.

She read standing in the middle of the floor with the ledger open on her forearm like a hymnal, and her father's training held her up better than any courage could have. She read the way he had taught her to proof — clearly, evenly, without performance, letting the words do their own terrible work. Names. Dates. Sums. The price paid to the prison surgeon for certifying executions that had happened without trial. The sums recovered from the estates of the dead, itemized down to Madame Roux's flower cart, sold for forty francs and entered as revenue. Ansel Coudert's boat tools. Émile Brissac's last month of rent, collected from a widow who had been told her husband deserted to the interior.

The chamber was very quiet. Outside, somewhere across the harbor, a ship's bell rang the half hour, and Theo marked it without stopping, the way a compositor marks the passing hours at the stone.

When she reached the pages Odile had cracked — the final cipher, the ring of officials in Vassey's pay — she began reading names of the living, and the quiet broke.

Captain Roche stood up. "This is fabrication—"

"Your signature is on page sixty-one," Theo said. "Fourteen thousand francs, dated the ninth of March. Your hand, Captain. I have spent twenty years judging hands. Yours slants left when it's hurried, and you were hurrying that day." She turned the page. "Major Duret, page sixty-three. Commandant Ferriol, page sixty-four. Shall I continue? I have the morning free. I don't imagine the colonel does."

And there it was — the sound she had waited three nights to hear, the sound she had imagined while setting type by candlelight with Bird asleep in the paper bin: the sound of a lie running out of floor. Duret sat down. Ferriol stood and left, actually left, walking fast for the doors, and the guards let him pass, and every man in that chamber understood at once that the walls were no longer holding anything up.

"Where is Colonel Vassey?" Theo asked the room. "Why does he try a junior officer at dawn behind locked doors while his own signature sits on every page I've read? Because Marcel Delacroix is the smallest man he could hang this on. Because the accused is convenient, and the dead are quiet, and the printer who holds the evidence is a woman with no standing whom he assumed would burn with her shop." She let that sit. "He assumed wrong about the shop, too. It's still standing."

"He's lying," Roche said hoarsely, to no one, to the president, to himself.

"Produce the counter-ledger, then," said Lefèvre. "Produce any record. Produce one widow who'll swear her husband deserted. Go on." No one moved. "No?" He turned to the president. "Aubert, I've followed orders for thirty-one years, and this is the first time following them would make me a criminal. I'm done. Read the charges against me whenever it suits you."

Two more officers followed him inside a minute — Theo counted them, the way she counted lines of type, automatically, precisely — and then the president, Aubert, removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with the mechanical calm of a man watching his career dissolve, and said:

"The accused will speak."

* * *

Marcel came forward alone, since no one would escort him now. His wrists were still bound, and he lifted his bound hands slightly, an odd formal gesture, almost a compositor presenting a finished galley.

"I taught my sister the alphabet with printer's type," he said, and his voice carried further than he probably intended in that bare room. "She's the better craftsman. Always was." A breath. "Everything in that ledger bearing my signature is mine. I wrote it. I knew what the payments were. I told myself it was survival, and then I told myself it was ambition, and both things were true and neither thing was an excuse. I signed for money I took and for orders I carried out. Three men died on my authorization and I can name the dates better than she can, because I wrote them."

Theo's throat closed. She had rehearsed his ruin a hundred ways in the dark; she had not prepared for him to build it with his own hands, carefully, the way he'd been taught to build anything worth building.

"I won't testify against the colonel," Marcel went on, "because I have nothing to sell and nothing to bargain with, and because everything I know is already in that book, which is the only honest thing I've produced in two years. Let it stand as my testimony. Let whatever court survives this morning weigh it however courts do. I only ask—" and here his composure slipped, just once, and he found his sister's face across the room— "that the record show my name spelled correctly. Delacroix. My father set that name in type for thirty years. It should be accurate at least once more."

The clerk, the sweating one, wrote it down. Theo saw his pen shake.

* * *

What happened next did not happen according to any law. Later there would be arguments about whether the tribunal had dissolved itself or been dissolved, whether Lefèvre's authority meant anything, whether a mob of officers could convert itself into a commission. None of it was lawful. All of it was real.

By seven o'clock the customs hall contained a new body — self-declared, improvised, half-legal at best — with Lefèvre at its head and a civilian lawyer dragged in from the barricade committee, and warrants going out under the city's actual seal for Vassey, for Roche, for every name on pages sixty-one through seventy. Soldiers were sent to the citadel and came back reporting the colonel's quarters empty, his strongbox open, his aide-de-camp weeping into his hands. Odile Ferrand was released from the cellar under the customs hall before eight, blinking in the gray light, thinner and unbowed, and the first thing she said to Theo was, "You're late. The tide waits for no one, not even justice."

Marcel was remanded — not freed, not condemned, held for a proper court that did not yet exist — and as the gendarmes led him past her he stopped, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the ledger hadn't said. But he inclined his head toward her satchel, toward the records he had surrendered, and mouthed one word: *Straight.* Set it straight. Their father's oldest instruction, passed like type from hand to hand.

Theo walked out of the customs hall into a morning that smelled of smoke and salt, and the city was awake now, and loud, and someone was nailing the previous night's broadside to the cooper's ruined doorframe beside the broken clock. The paper fluttered in the harbor wind. Her typesetting. Her brother's confession. Ten thousand copies loose in the world like sparks.

Down at the quay, she could see the masts of the morning ship above the rooftops, and the tide was turning, and somewhere Luc Arnaud was counting coins against one berth and one child and a copy of a ledger that could never be burned.

Theo started running.

She ran past the broken clock, past the barricades being cheerfully torn down and just as cheerfully rebuilt, past Odile shouting something after her that she didn't stop to hear — and the whole way down the hill she did the arithmetic again, the same impossible sum, and this time, for the first time, she already knew the answer.
