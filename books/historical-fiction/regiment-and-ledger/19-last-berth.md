## 19. Last Berth

The tide was turning when they reached the quay, and the harbor had gone the color of pewter, shot through with the first copper light of morning. The *Meridian* rode at her moorings with steam already breathing from her stacks, a gray ghost against the smoke still rising in thin columns from the printers' quarter. Somewhere behind them the city was singing — actual singing, ragged and off-key, drifting down the hill streets like something spilled. Theo could not tell if it was celebration or warning. Perhaps by now it was both.

She carried Bird on her hip because the child's legs had given out two streets back, worn through with three nights of hiding and one morning of running. Bird slept against her shoulder with her cheek pressed to Theo's collarbone, her small fist closed around a single piece of type — an asterisk, of all things, fished from the wrecked shop floor and never surrendered since. It pressed its small star into Theo's skin through the fabric.

Luc walked beside her, moving carefully, his bandaged side making him list to starboard like a ship taking water. He had argued the whole way down and then, somewhere near the fish market, stopped arguing entirely, which frightened her more than the arguing had.

At the gangway a man in a salt-stiff coat checked papers with the weary efficiency of someone who had been awake all night and expected worse before noon. Luc presented their documents — forged, all of them, Odile's final gift before the soldiers took her — and the man waved them through without looking up.

The berth was real. One berth, paid in coin that smelled of other men's crimes, on a ship that would carry whatever boarded it beyond the reach of anything Vassey or his successors could burn. Theo stood at the foot of the gangway and felt the whole arithmetic of her life compress into this strip of salt-rotted planking.

Luc turned to her. "Theo."

"Don't." She shifted Bird higher on her shoulder. "Don't start again. I've heard it every way you know how to say it."

"Then hear it the last way." His voice was low and rough, scraped raw by the night. "You set ten thousand copies with your own hands. Your name is on half the walls in Vellacourt by now — they've written *the printer* on the barricades, do you understand? They'll be looking for a woman. You are the most dangerous person in this city this morning, and you're standing at a dock holding a child."

"I'm standing at a dock holding a child," Theo said, "which is precisely why I'm not getting on that ship."

Bird stirred. Her eyes opened, unfocused, found the water, widened. She twisted in Theo's arms to look at the harbor — gulls wheeling over the masts, the great slow heave of the swell under the pilings — and for a moment her sleepiness broke open into pure astonishment, the way only a four-year-old can be astonished, as if the sea had been invented overnight just for her.

"Theo," Luc said quietly. "Look at her."

"I'm looking at her."

"That's my argument. That's the entire argument. She goes where the ledger goes. The copy we made — the type-key, the pages, everything — it has to leave Vellacourt today or it dies here with the rest of us. And she can't carry it alone. So someone boards with her who knows what it means. Someone Vassey's people have never seen." He touched his chest. "A dead clerk. I forgot my face once already; I can forget it across an ocean. But you—" he shook his head slowly— "you they will hunt until the day they find you, and they will not stop hunting, and you cannot run forever with a child in your arms."

"And if I send her away," Theo said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, "what happens to her in ten years, in some foreign port, when she asks why the woman who kept her didn't come?"

"She'll ask," Luc admitted. "And you'll answer. In letters. In the truth we print. In the fact that she's alive to ask." He held out his hand, palm up. Between his fingers lay the oilcloth packet, tied with printer's cord, heavy as a heart. "I swear to you on the press itself — on your father's press — that she wants for nothing, learns everything, and knows your name before any other word in French. *Delacroix.* Before *maman,* even, if that's what it costs me."

Theo laughed — a broken, wet sound she hadn't authorized — and turned her face toward the water so he wouldn't see what it did to her.

That was when she saw Marcel.

He stood at the far end of the quay near the customs shed, out of uniform for the first time she could remember since the coup — a plain gray coat buttoned to the throat, hands empty, guarded by two soldiers who looked embarrassed to be guarding him. A sergeant with a ledger of his own — everyone had ledgers now; the city ran on them like blood — was checking names against names. Marcel stood very straight, the way their father used to stand at the press when the forme was locked and ready, that terrible patience of a man waiting for something irreversible.

Their eyes met across forty feet of wet stone.

He didn't call out. He didn't try to come to her. He simply watched, and there was no plea in his face, and no defiance either — only a kind of cleared ground, as if everything built on it had burned down to something honest at last. He had testified yesterday. She had made certain of it: had stormed the tribunal chamber with the original ledger under her arm and read names aloud until the room fractured, until officers she'd known all her life tore their epaulettes off in front of Colonel Vassey's white, disintegrating fury. And Marcel had risen when called upon and damned himself completely, calmly, in a level compositor's voice, naming dates and sums and the three neighbors whose disappearances sat beside his signature in the leather book — and then had asked, in the same voice, that the record show he did so freely.

Freely. As if freedom were something you could requisition retroactively.

The sergeant said something. Marcel nodded. And then, instead of turning toward the prison wagon waiting beyond the customs shed, he asked permission for something — she saw the exchange, saw the sergeant glance at the wagon, at the ship, at the sky, and shrug — and began to walk toward her.

The soldiers let him. Ten feet. Fifteen. They flanked him loosely, bored with mercy.

Theo set Bird down. The child woke fully then, swaying on her legs, blinking at the enormous morning, one hand still knotted in Theo's skirt.

"Sister." Marcel stopped an arm's length away. Up close he looked older than thirty-one ought to allow — gray coming in at the temples, a cut healing along his jaw from the arrest. His eyes went to Bird first, and softened, and then came up to Theo's face and stayed there. "You're not boarding."

"No."

"I told you to sail. I sent the note."

"You sent many notes," Theo said. "You were always good with correspondence."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would have gone if he'd earned one. "The tribunal adjourns at eight. Sentencing follows. They'll commute nothing for me; I made sure of the record. What I did needs to stay on the books, or men like Vassey survive on our forgetting." He glanced at the *Meridian*, at the packet in Luc's hand. "So. It's decided, then."

"It's decided."

"Good." He breathed out, long and slow, the breath of a man setting a line of type he knows is right and cannot take back. "Then I have one thing left to give you, and very little time to argue about how."

From inside his coat he drew a wooden case, small, scarred, dark with decades of ink soaked into the grain. Their father's composing stick. Not the shop's spare — the original, the one Henri Delacroix had held every working day of his life, the one whose brass edge had been worn bright by forty years of thumbs. Marcel had taken it when the shop passed to Theo's name-that-was-not-hers. She had never asked why. She had assumed theft, or sentiment, or both.

Marcel turned it over in his hands once, the way their father used to, checking the knee of the slide, the tension of the screw.

"Do you remember," he said, "when he taught us to justify a line? You were seven. You couldn't reach the case, so you stood on the drawer, and you put too much space between the words, and he said—" 

"*Space is not emptiness,*" Theo said. "*Space is what makes the words readable.*"

"He said it to me first," Marcel said. "But yes. I've thought about that sentence every day for six months. Every day since—" He stopped. Swallowed. "There's no justification for what I signed, Théo. I've searched every argument I ever learned, and none of them holds. Space is not emptiness, and silence is not neutrality, and I knew it while I was doing it. I want that in the record too. Not the tribunal's record. Yours."

He held out the composing stick.

"Theo Delacroix," he said, and it was the first time in two years he had spoken her name as hers, not as a fiction maintained for the safety of them both, "print it all. Print my trial, my sentence, my hanging if it comes to that. Print it in the same size type as everything else. No shame hidden in agate, no glory inflated to display. Just — the truth, justified, even measure, even space." His voice cracked on the last of it, and he let it. "Can you do that? Can you promise me the dignity of ordinary type?"

She took the stick. Its weight went into her palm like a hand closing around hers — their father's hand, forty years of mornings, all of it arriving at once. She could not speak. She nodded.

Marcel exhaled, and some last tension went out of him, and for one moment he was seven years old again standing on a drawer beside her, both of them ink-stained and certain the press was the most important thing God had made.

Then he crouched down to Bird.

The child regarded him warily. She had seen him twice, both times in uniform, both times in the company of fear. But children forgive quickly what adults must work years for, and something in the way he lowered himself — knees cracking, making himself small — reached her. She studied him with her head tipped.

"Hello, Bird," Marcel said gently. "My sister tells me you sort letters."

"Bird sorts," Bird said gravely.

"What's your favorite letter?"

Bird considered this with the seriousness of a scholar. Then she opened her fist and offered him the asterisk.

Marcel looked at it a long moment. When he raised his eyes they were shining and ruined. "A star," he managed. "Yes. Good choice. It means there's more to the story. Footnotes. Things worth going back for." He folded her fingers closed around it again, carefully, and stood, and stepped back into the space where the soldiers waited.

"The wagon's this way, sir," the sergeant said, without relish.

"One moment more." Marcel looked at Theo. "Odile Ferrand — they're saying amnesty by week's end, once the civilian court seats. Don't let them forget her. Put her name in the first issue. Old women get forgotten fastest."

"I won't forget her."

"And Luc—" He extended his hand. Luc took it. Whatever passed between them was brief and wordless and sufficient; two men who had been instruments of the same machine, one who fed it and one who fled it, gripping briefly over the head of a sleeping child. "Guardian," Marcel said. "Keep the title better than I kept mine."

"I will," Luc said. "On my life."

"On hers, rather," Marcel said, almost smiling now. "Yours hardly matters anymore."

The sergeant touched his elbow. Marcel turned. He got perhaps five steps before he stopped, his back to her, and said, without turning around:

"The forme is locked, little sister. Pull the lever."

They took him to the wagon. She watched the door close on him — watched it the way you watch a forme go under the platen, knowing the impression it leaves cannot be undone, only printed true or printed false — and she stood with the composing stick in one hand and her brother's whole life in the other until the wagon rolled away and Luc's hand settled warm on her shoulder.

*   *

They wasted no time, after that. The tide would not wait for grief.

At the gangway, Theo crouched and gathered Bird into her arms one last time. The child smelled of paper dust and wood shavings and the shop — their shop, however brief her tenancy of it — and Theo held her hard enough to feel her heartbeat against her own collarbone, quick as a flywheel.

"Now you listen to me," Theo whispered into her hair. "This man is tedious and stubborn and he eats like a starving dog, but he will keep you safe if it kills him, and it may well kill him, so be gentle with him. Learn your letters. Learn all of them, even Q, which is useless and proud. And when anyone asks who kept you—" her voice failed, recovered, held— "you say a printer. Say it loud. Say it like it's a fine thing to be."

"A printer," Bird repeated solemnly, patting Theo's cheek with one small ink-stained hand. Then, with the terrifying directness of the very young: "You come later?"

Theo pressed her forehead to the child's forehead. "I come later. Or I come in every page. Either way, little bird, I am always arriving."

She handed her over the rail into Luc's arms. He received her the way you receive something breakable and essential — a reliquary, a live coal — and stood with her on the deck as the lines were cast off, and neither of them spoke again because there was nothing left that speech could improve.

The oilcloth packet went into a canvas satchel across Luc's chest. The ledger copy, the cipher key in movable type, the names, the sums, the gold, the graves — all of it sailing east into a world that could not burn it, because burning required possessing it, and possession had just become impossible.

The *Meridian* backed water. Turned. Her stacks laid a smudge of black across the copper morning.

Theo stood on the quay with the composing stick in her hand and watched the ship shrink. Bird appeared at the rail — Luc lifted her, her small body vivid against the gray sea — and one arm went up, the asterisk-star flashing in her fist, held high the whole length of the harbor, held high past the breakwater, held high until the distance ate it.

Theo did not wave back. She could not; her hands were occupied, one with the tool, one with the future. Instead she stood at attention, the way you stand at a funeral and a christening held simultaneously, and she let the ship go.

Behind her the city smoked and sang. Ahead of her the presses waited, cold no longer.
