## 20. The Ship Sails

The tide turned at half past six, and Theo Delacroix stood on the breakwater to watch it go.

The stones were still wet from the night's rain, slick under her boots, and the wind off the harbor carried the smell of tar and salt and, faintly, from the direction of the printers' quarter, the sweet char of burnt paper. She had not slept. Her hands still smelled of ink. She had scrubbed them twice at the quay pump and the smell had come up out of the creases of her knuckles like something living, and in the end she had stopped trying to wash it away. It was hers now. She had earned it line by line.

The ship — the *Marigold*, a merchant brig pressed into passenger service by chaos, her hull patched with fresh pitch that glistened black as sealing wax — was already moving. A tug had taken her rope off an hour before dawn, and now she made her own way down the channel, past the mole, past the customs house with its windows shattered and its flag gone, past the hulks of the fishing boats that had not put out to sea in two months because their crews were striking or dead or afraid.

Theo counted the distance in ems. It was a habit she could not break: the world measured itself for her in units of type. The *Marigold* at two hundred paces was the breadth of her thumb held at arm's length. At four hundred, the length of a bodkin. She watched her shrink toward open water and thought, absurdly, of her father's rule about justification — that a line of type must neither be too loose nor too tight, that every space must be exactly what it needs to be and no more. She had set her life like that this week, space by space, letter by letter, and now the form was locked and the impression pulled and there was no unlocking it, no redistributing the words, no taking back a single character.

At the rail, near the stern where the wake curled white and green, a small hand went up.

Bird.

She wore the coat Luc had bought her from the slop-seller on the Rue des Ancres, a coat meant for a boy of eight that hung off the girl of four like a sail in a calm. Her other hand was gripped in Luc Arnaud's. He did not wave. He stood very straight, the way men stand when they are holding themselves together with discipline rather than hope, and he looked back at the city — not at Theo, or not only at Theo — at all of it, the smoking rooftops and the barricades and the harbor water gray with morning, memorizing it, perhaps, so he could describe it someday to someone who had not seen it. His wound was dressed; she could see the white bandage above his collar even at this distance. He had argued until the last hour that she should take the berth. He had argued with the flat desperation of a man who understood arithmetic better than she did, who knew what a printer's head was worth on the market Vassey had posted, and she had answered him the only way that ended argument: she had kissed Bird's forehead, put the ledger copy in his hands, and said, "You're her guardian now. That's not a favor I'm doing you. It's a job."

He had almost smiled then. Almost.

Now Bird's hand waved and waved, small against the gray sky, and Theo raised her own and held it up and did not lower it, though her arm burned and the wind pushed at her palm as if trying to fold her fingers closed. *Keep your fist around the pen,* her father used to say when she tired at the case. *The hand remembers what the mind forgets.* She held her hand up until the ship was a smudge, until the hand at the rail was smaller than a pica, smaller than a period, until the sea took the whole of it the way a press takes paper — one clean impression, no proof, no correction — and then the horizon closed over the place where the *Marigold* had been, and there was nothing but water and weather and the long gray light of morning coming up over Vellacourt.

Her arm came down slowly. It felt strange, weightless, the way a compositor's hand feels after locking a heavy forme, all that mass suddenly gone and the body not yet believing it.

She was alone on the breakwater. Alone in the way she had asked to be alone, and the aloneness sat on her chest like a stone from the harbor wall.

* * *

She walked back through the city because she could not bear to stand still and because standing still invited thinking, and thinking, just now, invited grief, and grief had work to do later and could not be spent yet.

Vellacourt in the morning after the exposé was a city divided against itself like a page badly imposed — half of it burning, half of it singing, and the seam between them running crooked through every street.

In the Fishmarket quarter, where the network had flooded the barracks gates first, the soldiers of the 3rd Regiment had stacked their muskets at midnight and refused the order to fire on the crowd at the corn exchange. Theo passed their sentries now, boys mostly, standing awkwardly without orders, watching the civilians pass with the expressions of men who have jumped off a ledge and are waiting to find out how far down it is. One of them, hardly older than Marcel had been when their father died, nodded to her. Nodded to *her*, a woman in an ink-stained apron, as if she were somebody. As if the broadside in ten thousand hands had made everybody somebody.

Farther on, near the powder magazine, the city sang. She heard it before she saw it — a ragged chorus rising off the barricade at the corner of Rue Saint-Aubin, forty voices maybe, workers and fishwives and two defected dragoons, singing the old song, the one their father had hummed while he dressed type and never let them sing in the shop because singing shook the hand. They had torn up the cobbles and piled them with cart wheels and furniture and one entire pew dragged from the church of Saint-Roch, which struck Theo as either blasphemy or its opposite, and they had planted a lamp on a pole at the top like a star on a tree, and they were singing with their faces turned east toward the light coming up off the water.

And south, past the canal, the printers' quarter smoked. Odile's street. Not all of it — the fire brigades, such as remained, had saved the row nearest the water — but the block where Odile Ferrand had kept her cellar press for nineteen years was a black tooth in the jaw of the skyline, breathing smoke, and the smell of it followed Theo for three streets. They had shelled it at dawn with a single gun from the citadel, careless of where the shot fell, and the shot had fallen where carelessness always falls: on people. She had heard, secondhand and thirdhand, eleven dead, no names yet, and the not-knowing was a splinter working deeper with every hour. Odile was in the citadel's cells, alive as of yesterday's report, which meant alive as of nothing at all.

A woman selling bread from a handcart — actual bread, at a price that suggested either courage or lunacy — caught Theo's sleeve as she passed.

"You're her," the woman said. "You're the printer."

"I'm a printer," Theo said. "There were ten thousand copies. I didn't carry them all."

"The one on my door," the woman said, "had my husband's name in it." Her eyes were dry and enormous. "Third column. What they paid, and who signed for it, and the date they took him from the harbor road." She gripped Theo's wrist harder. "I read it four times. Then I read it to my neighbors. Then I nailed it to the door so everyone who passes has to read it too. You understand? It's nailed to my door. They'd have to burn my house to take it down."

"That's the idea," Theo said.

The woman released her wrist. She looked at her a moment longer, as if checking the impression against the plate, and then she broke a loaf in half and pushed one half into Theo's hands and would not take payment, and walked on calling her bread, calling it loud, into a street where calling anything loud could get you shot.

Theo ate walking. It was the best thing she had tasted since the trouble began, and she suspected it was not the bread.

* * *

The shop, when she reached it, looked like what it was: a building that had been searched by men who wanted to destroy rather than find. The raid had been five days ago and she had righted what could be righted, but a ransacked print shop cannot be righted, only reorganized. Type lay everywhere in the gutters of the floor like spilled seed. The imposing stone was scarred where a bayonet had skated across it. Her father's portrait — the only likeness of him, done in charcoal by a customer who owed money — had been slashed through the face and hung crooked, and she had left it crooked on purpose, the way you leave a scar, so the room remembers.

She swept. It was the only prayer she knew.

While she swept she thought about Marcel, because sweeping left room for nothing else and Marcel filled any room he was given.

Dawn tomorrow, the tribunal. Dawn tomorrow, and the tide today had carried away the one berth that might have been his — except that it never could have been his, that was the arithmetic Luc had laid out on the quay with his clerk's precision: one berth, three souls, and the ledger copy that had to reach foreign soil or everything they had bled for this week could be burned by noon. She had given the berth to Bird because Bird was four years old and blameless and because the ledger needed a future outside the reach of Colonel Vassey's matches. And Marcel — Marcel had pressed his compositor's hand-tool into her palm on the quay with the handcuffs already glinting on the sergeant's belt behind him, and had said nothing, absolutely nothing, and the nothing had said everything: *I know what I chose. Set me in your book as I am.*

She had not cried on the quay. She was not crying now. But her hands shook on the broom handle, and she set the broom down and stood in the middle of her wrecked inheritance among the scattered letters of the alphabet and let the shaking happen, briefly, privately, the way you let a press pull one proof run you don't intend to keep.

Then she picked up the hand-tool.

It was warm. It had been warm ever since the quay, or she imagined it so — boxwood worn smooth by thirty years of her father's grip and then some months of Marcel's, and now hers, and the grain of it fit her palm as if the wood had been waiting. With it in her hand she was not an orphan or a sister or a traitor's sister or a widow-that-never-was. She was a compositor. The oldest kind of person there is: someone who takes loose words and gives them order, who decides what stands together on the page and what gets distributed back into the case.

She hung her apron on its nail. She lit the lamp over the stone, though daylight was strong enough now through the cracked window — she lit it anyway, because lamplight was how her father had worked and how she intended to.

On the imposing stone, still locked from the great overnight run, lay the forme of the exposé. Ten thousand impressions pulled from it, and the words still stood there, upright, patient, treasonous: the names, the sums, the cipher key hidden in plain sight in a shipping notice, the date the fleet sailed with smuggled gold, the signature of Captain Marcel Delacroix beside three neighbors' names. She ran her fingers along the type. High spots and low. Every letter accounted for.

Someone hammered at the shutter.

She went cold from the scalp down. It was the wrong knock — not Odile's courier's triple-tap, not Luc's pattern, not the customer's polite rap. It was urgent and arrhythmic, fists more than knuckles, and beneath it a voice she knew, hoarse and cracking:

"Theo. *Théo.* Open the shutter. For God's sake — they're behind me—"

Not Marcel. Marcel was in the citadel awaiting dawn. It was the voice of the young courier from Odile's network, Gaspard, the boy with the quick feet who had carried the first bundles to the barracks gates, and his voice was the voice of a man being chased.

Theo looked at the locked forme on the stone. At the hand-tool in her grip. At the shutter, trembling on its hinges.

Every instinct her father had bred into her said *keep the press cold.* Every instinct the last three weeks had bred into her said otherwise.

She blew out the lamp — no, she left it burning; let them see it burning — and crossed the floor, and lifted the bar.

* * *

Gaspard fell through the doorway rather than entered it. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, mud to the knees, one shoe missing, a smear of soot across his cheek where he had crawled through something. Behind him the street lay empty in the morning light, deceptively empty, the way streets are empty when the emptiness is listening.

"They took Odile's list," he gasped. "The courier — Renard — he wasn't sold to Vassey, Theo, it's worse — he *is* Vassey's, he's been his the whole time, and last night he gave them the names of every safehouse between here and the Spanish border. They hit them all before dawn. Everyone who ran is running here. Everyone who can't run—" He couldn't finish it. He bent double, hands on knees, breathing like a bellows with a split seam.

"How many?" Theo asked. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "How many are coming here?"

"All of them." He looked up. His eyes were the eyes of a boy who had stopped being a boy sometime in the last twelve hours. "And Theo — the tribunal. It's moved up. Not dawn tomorrow. Dawn *today*. An hour after low tide. Renard gave them Marcel's name for the docket himself, first name on the list, and Vassey means to have him condemned and shot before the city wakes enough to object."

The hand-tool was still in her grip. She looked down at it, at the smooth worn wood, at the shape her father's hand had left in it and Marcel's hand had deepened and hers now completed.

Outside, faintly, off toward the barricades at Saint-Aubin, the singing started again.

Theo Delacroix set the hand-tool on the imposing stone beside the locked forme, took down her father's slashed portrait from its crooked nail, and turned to the boy in the doorway.

"Then we haven't got an hour to lose," she said. "Tell me where they're keeping him — and start pulling type. We're printing one more edition, and this one goes to the tribunal itself."
