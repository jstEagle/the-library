## 21. Reset the Form

The lock had been changed twice since the raid — once by the soldiers who wanted the shop for a garrison storehouse, once again when the civilian court returned the deed to her with an apology so thin it might have been printed on tissue paper. Theo stood before the third lock now, the original one, the one her father had bought from a locksmith on the Rue des Forgerons in the year she was born, and she worked the key that had spent six weeks hidden inside a hollowed-out quoin in the cellar.

The door swung inward on the smell of ash and cold iron.

She had expected worse. She had braced herself for worse — for the press broken open like a ribcage, for the type cases dumped and trampled into an unsortable sea of lead, for the windows gone and the roof sagging. The soldiers had done their work thoroughly enough: the composing stone lay cracked down its middle, the paper stock was water-ruined where they'd split the casks looking for Luc, and someone — she would never know who, and some part of her was grateful not to know — had bayoneted through every galley of standing type they found.

But the press itself still stood. Old Sainte-Anne, her father had called her, though no one knew why; he'd bought her secondhand from a widow in Toulon and never explained the name. She stood against the back wall where she had always stood, black and patient as a church, and when Theo laid her palm flat against the frame she felt nothing but solid wood and honest iron, and something in her chest unclenched for the first time since the tribunal.

"You're not dead," she told the press. "Neither am I. Let's see what we can do about the rest."

* * *

It took eleven days to make the shop fit to open, and she counted them the way her father had taught her to count everything: in ems. One em to sweep out the glass. Two ems to haul the ruined paper into the alley and watch a rag-man carry it off at scandalous discount. Three ems to re-glaze the front window, which she did herself, balanced on a ladder with putty to her elbows, refusing all help because the help kept asking questions she wasn't ready to answer. Four ems to sort.

The sorting was the worst of it and the best of it. The soldiers had emptied the cases in their fury, and ten thousand pieces of type lay mixed across the floorboards like a spilled alphabet, like a language that had been shot. Theo knelt among them morning after morning with a stick of glue-tipped paper in one hand and a sorting tray in the other, lifting each letter home — *a* to the a-case, *e* to its crowded kingdom, the long-s and the short-s separated like brothers who no longer spoke. Her knees ached. Her fingertips went grey and stayed grey no matter how she scrubbed. And somewhere around the sixth day she realized she was humming, some half-forgotten tune from the kitchen of her childhood, and that Marcel used to hum the same one when he was setting headlines, and that she could not stop, and that she did not want to.

On the ninth day, Odile Ferrand came up the street leaning on a cane she hadn't needed before prison, and stood in the doorway surveying the wreckage with the eye of a woman who had buried two husbands and one insurrection and did not shock easily.

"Well," Odile said. "They left you the walls. Generous."

"They left me the press."

"Then they were fools." She lowered herself onto the one surviving stool and watched Theo work the tray. "You've heard, I assume. The amnesty came through Tuesday. They opened the gates at Saint-Pierre and turned us all loose with an apology and four francs' compensation. Four francs. I paid more than that for the wine we drank the night Vassey's name went up on every wall in the quarter."

"I heard." Theo set a row of *n*'s home without looking up. "I also heard they dissolved the tribunal. Civilian court now. Judges who can read."

"A novel arrangement." Odile's voice was dry as old ink, but her eyes weren't. "And Vassey?"

"Gone. A ship out of Marseille, they say. Spain, or beyond." Theo's hands paused over the case. "Named in absentia. Every charge. Profiteering off executed men's estates — the whole ledger, confirmed line by line. There's a price on him now that makes mine look charitable."

"And your brother?"

Theo set the last *n* down very carefully, as if it might break. "Commuted. Not pardoned — commuted. Public labor. He's repairing the harbor seawall." A breath. "Two streets from here. I could spit from my own doorstep and hit the worksite."

"Do you?"

"No." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Not yet."

Odile studied her for a long moment, and whatever she saw there — the grief, the unfinished sentence of it — she had the grace not to name. Instead she reached into her bag and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the cracked composing stone: a new font, twelve-point roman, still bright from the founder's moulds.

"For the reopening," she said. "From the union. What's left of it. We voted."

"The union is seven people and a widow."

"Eight," said Odile. "I'm counting you."

* * *

She hung Bird's type scraps above the door on the twelfth day.

The girl had been gone five weeks now — five weeks and two days, though Theo made a discipline of not counting past the weeks, because the days led to hours and the hours led to standing at the window at odd moments listening for a ship that had long since cleared the horizon. Luc's letters came when they came, carried by whatever honest captain owed him a favor: La Rochelle, then Portsmouth, then a port whose name Theo had to find on a map. Bird was well. Bird was eating. Bird had learned English words for bread and dog and *no* with a conviction that made Luc laugh in his writing.

With the first letter had come a small parcel, and inside the parcel a handful of metal scraps — mis-struck sorts, broken ligatures, the orphaned fragments that every compositor sweeps into the discard bin at the end of a day. Bird had sorted them. That was the thing that stopped Theo's breath when she first poured them into her palm: the child had sorted them, little piles of three and four and five, grouped by size or shine or some private taxonomy only Bird understood, and Luc had written beneath them in careful ink: *She does this at the inn table every night. Says she is helping you. Says the shop misses its letters.*

So Theo took a length of pine, planed it smooth, and mounted the scraps upon it in Bird's own disorder — the crooked *f*, the snapped ampersand, three dots of period lead arranged in ascending size — and hung it above the shop door where anyone entering would have to pass under it. A sign, of a kind. A blessing, of another kind. Customers asked what it meant, and Theo said, *it means we keep what others throw away*, and let them make of that what they would.

The customers came back slowly, the way birds return to a garden after the cat has learned patience. First the ones who needed wedding announcements and funeral notices, life going on in both directions. Then Mère Aubry from the chandlery, who had bought her broadsheets from Delacroix's for thirty years and pretended, elaborately, not to notice the new font or the crack in the stone or the way Theo flinched at boots on cobblestones. Then, one gray afternoon, a young man Theo didn't know, who bought nothing but stood a moment too long by the window and said, quietly, on his way out: *My sister was on the list. Page nine. Thank you for page nine.*

She printed page nine in her sleep for a week afterward. She did not mind.

* * *

Marcel came on a Thursday, in the last hour of working light, still wearing the coarse canvas of the labor crews with the government stencil stamped at the shoulder. He did not come in at first. She saw him through the glass, standing across the street like a man reading an inscription on a tomb, and she let him stand there while she finished locking the forme in her hands, because some things should not be rushed, and forgiveness — if that's what this was, if that word even applied — was one of them.

When she finally pushed the door open, he looked smaller than she remembered. Prison had pared him down; the seawall work was building him back differently, in the shoulders and forearms, a laborer's body replacing an officer's carriage. His hair had gone thin at the temples. He was thirty-one years old and he moved like her father had moved in his last year.

"Theo." Just her name. It seemed to cost him something to get out whole.

"Marcel."

He glanced at the sign above the door — at the crooked *f*, the broken ampersand — and something crossed his face that she chose not to interpret. "I'm not staying. I have till the evening bell."

"I know how the bell works."

A ghost of the old smile, there and gone. "I brought something." He held out a small cloth bundle. Inside was a composing stick — not hers, not the shop's. An older one, worn silver-bright at the grip, with a maker's mark she knew better than her own handwriting. Their father's. The one Marcel had taken when the estate divided, the one he'd carried through the academy and the barracks and God knew what else, the one she had assumed was lost or sold or buried in some evidence room.

"He'd have wanted it here," Marcel said. "Where it worked."

"Marcel—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, gentle. "I didn't come for absolution. I came because the stick belongs to this room and I belong to the seawall, and that's the honest arithmetic of it. But I—" He stopped. Started again, slower, the words dragged up from somewhere deep and unglamorous. "Every day they hand us hammers and we mend the wall, and every day I think: this is the first useful thing I have done in six years. Maybe longer. Maybe ever." His eyes came up to hers. "You printed the truth and the city rose and I sat in a cell and I thought — she did it alone. She did what we swore together, and she did it alone, because I signed my name on the other page."

"You were a boy when you signed the first oath and a coward when you broke it," Theo said. "Both true. Neither is the whole of you." She took the stick from his hands. It was warm from his pocket. "Father used to say a compositor's worst error isn't a wrong letter. It's leaving a space where a letter belongs, and calling the page done."

Marcel looked at her for a long time. The evening bell rang, far off across the rooftops, and he straightened like a man answering a whistle.

"Will you come to Sunday dinner?" she asked, surprising herself. "There's no dinner. There's bread and there's the shop. But the chair is there."

He nodded once, twice, unable to manage words, and went down the steps and away along the darkening street toward the sound of the sea and the wall that needed mending. Theo stood in the doorway until he turned the corner. Then she carried their father's composing stick inside and set it on the stone beside her own, side by side, and began to set tomorrow's type.

* * *

The broadsheet went to proof on the last Friday of the month. *Truth, Weekly,* the masthead read, and beneath it, in twelve-point roman fresh from Odile's gift: *Printed and published by Théodose Delacroix, Vellacourt.* Her own name. Spelled out in lead, letter by letter, hers.

She was pulling the first proof copy when Odile arrived with the morning post and an expression Theo had learned to distrust across two insurrections and one funeral.

"You'll want to see this," Odile said, and laid a letter on the stone.

The hand was Luc's — she knew it now the way she knew a familiar face — but the seal was different, heavier, official-looking. She broke it and read standing, then read it again sitting down, because the room had begun, politely, to tilt.

*Madame Delacroix* [he always wrote to her as if she were somebody's widow] *— Forgive the formality; the seal required it. I have been asked — persuaded — drafted, choose your verb — to serve as witness-secretary to the commission investigating the Vassey affair in London. They want the ledger. They want the original, authenticated, under my hand, and they want me to bring it personally.*

*I will not lie to you: I said no. Twice. The book belongs to Vellacourt; it was written in your blood as much as mine. But then I thought of page nine, and the young man's sister, and every name in that cipher that is still breathing because the right people finally read it. There are names in other cities, Theo. Other ledgers, almost certainly. The commission believes yours may be the key that opens them.*

*Bird is thriving. She has lost none of her French and gained a sailor's vocabulary I take full blame for. She asks when we are coming home, and I tell her soon, and I am tired of lying to her, and I suspect I am tired of lying to myself about something larger.*

*If you send the ledger, send it with my gratitude and my shame in equal measure. If you send yourself —*

The letter ended there. Not torn, not faded. Ended. As if the man who wrote it had arrived at the edge of the sentence, looked over, and could not yet say what he saw below.

Theo folded the pages along Luc's careful creases and sat for a while in the smell of ink and morning light, the proof drying on the stone, the press waiting behind her like an old friend who already knew.

Above the door, Bird's crooked *f* caught the sun.

She reached for a fresh sheet of paper.
