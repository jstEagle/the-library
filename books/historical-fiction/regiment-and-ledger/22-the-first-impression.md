## 22. The First Impression

The morning came in through the east window the way it always had — slantwise, across the composing stone, catching the dust that rose when Theo unlocked the type cabinets and slid the drawers open one by one. She had counted them once, in the weeks after the amnesty: forty-one drawers, her father's hand-lettered labels gone brown and brittle, each one a small kingdom of lead. She had grown up believing those kingdoms were his. She knew better now. They had been hers all along; she had simply never been permitted to sign the deed.

She lit no lamp. For the first time in her working life she did not need one.

On the stone lay the forme for the first number of *The Vellacourt Ledger* — she had argued with Odile about the name for two evenings over wine that tasted of vinegar and victory. Odile wanted *The Trumpet*. Luc, in his last letter before sailing, had suggested something grander still. But Theo had stood in the wreckage of the shop after the raid, among the spilled sorts and splintered chases, holding the leather ledger against her chest like a child, and she had understood then what the book was worth and what it cost, and there was no other name she could give honestly.

*The Ledger.* Truth, kept in columns. Debts, recorded where they could be read.

The broadsheet was four pages. The first carried the tribunal's full findings — every name, every estate seized from executed prisoners, every franc of Colonel Vassey's profiteering traced through the harbor customs houses, laid out in the plain figures she had set herself over six sleepless nights. Vassey's name sat at the top in fourteen-point bold, fled though he was, named in absentia by a court that no longer answered to him. The second page held Marcel's testimony, printed verbatim as he had given it to the civilian magistrates, unsoftened, including the passages that damned him. He had asked her not to cut them. *Print it all,* he'd said from the dock, his voice carrying in that chamber where he'd once worn his uniform like armor. *A man who reads only his good pages is writing fiction.*

The third page was Odile's: the list of the network's dead, the couriers sold and betrayed, the printers who had died with their presses — names Theo had not known until Odile recited them like a rosary. And the fourth page, the smallest, the one that had taken her longest to compose because it took the most courage and the least lead:

*A notice to our readers. This sheet is published weekly by Théodora Delacroix, printer, of 14 Rue de la Fontaine. It is set, composed, and pulled at her own press under her own name. Corrections will be printed. Complaints will be heard. Lies will be answered.*

No brother's name above hers. No dead father invoked as shield or license. Just the four words she had spent thirty years earning: *printed by Théodora Delacroix.*

She ran her thumb along the locked forme now, checking the quoins, feeling the even bite of the chase. Below, faint through the floorboards, she could hear Bird — no. Not Bird. Bird was at sea, three months gone, somewhere between here and a coast Theo could not find on any map in the shop.

Below her, in the type cellar that had hidden a wounded man from soldiers, Odile was sorting the week's news copy, humming an old union song whose words were half nonsense and half sedition, which was, Odile maintained, the correct proportion for any honest hymn.

Theo lifted the forme onto the press bed and slid it home.

* * *

The letter had come on Tuesday.

She had seen the harbor postmark first — foreign ink, foreign stamp, the paper soft from salt air — and had stood in the doorway holding it for a long moment before she trusted her hands to open it. Luc wrote the way he copied ledgers: precisely, without ornament, every sentence doing exactly the work it was sent to do. He reported the passage safe, the port reached, the ledger copy delivered into the hands of men who wept reading it, which Luc noted with mild embarrassment, as though tears were an administrative irregularity. He reported that excerpts were being reprinted in three cities already, that the gold ships' manifests were being audited, that a committee existed now with a chairman and a seal and the dull magnificent momentum of institutions that intend to outlive their founders.

And then, at the bottom, beneath his signature, a second hand. Round, deliberate letters, pressed hard enough to dent the page, the work of someone who had been taught to write only recently and practiced as if the alphabet might be taken back.

*Dear Tio,* it began, and Theo had to sit down on the composing stool, because Tio was what Bird called her, had called her since the third week, when the girl had decided that *Madame Delacroix* was too many syllables for a person you loved.

*Dear Tio. We live by the sea but it is not your sea. Luc says all seas are joined underneath so really it IS your sea but I looked and I could not see the joining part. I drew it for you. The joining part is under the boat. I am learning letters. I know all of A to M. N is next week. Luc says when I know all of them I can write you a whole letter with no help. I am saving up my words. Love, BIRD.*

Folded inside, a drawing in cheap crayon: a blue sea with white teeth of foam, a ship with more sails than physics would allow, and beneath the hull, rendered in careful strokes of green, a long wobbly band labeled — in letters that leaned like drunks — *THE JOINING PART.* Two small figures stood at the rail. One raised its arm. The label beside them read *ME AND THE SEA*, which was either profound or four years old, and Theo suspected both.

She had laughed, alone in the shop, the sound startling her — she had not laughed like that since before the curfew, before the ledger, before everything. Then she had carried the drawing upstairs and pinned it above her bed, and then, after a night of thinking, had taken it down again and pinned it instead above the door of the shop, facing inward, where everyone who worked there would pass beneath it daily. The joining part is under the boat. Let them make sense of that. Let it be the shop's new motto, better than any her father ever lettered.

Odile had studied it for a long time and pronounced, with the gravity she reserved for scripture and good type: "That child understands distribution."

* * *

Now, Thursday morning, the forme was locked and the press waited, and Theo allowed herself the ritual she had performed ten thousand times and would perform ten thousand more: she walked the perimeter of the machine, her hand trailing the iron, greeting it.

Her father had bought the press the year she was born. She had learned this from the invoice he kept folded in the drawer with his spare eyeglasses — the date in his cramped hand, and beside it, in different ink, added later, a note she had found only after his death and had never shown Marcel: *T. will pull this lever better than M. Tell her so when she is tall enough to hear it.* Marcel had never known. She had never told him. Some inheritances, she had learned, are best delivered late, when they can be received whole rather than fought over.

Marcel came on Saturdays now. Not to the shop — he was not permitted near the press, the terms of his labor forbidding the tools of his old trade — but to the house above it, where Theo cooked a dinner neither of them ate with much appetite and both of them ate completely. He wore laborer's gray. His hands, which had held a sword and a signature with equal confidence, were cracked from quarry work, and he showed them to her sometimes without comment, the way a penitent shows wounds he does not expect to be forgiven for. She did not forgive him. She had decided, in the tribunal chamber, watching him damn himself sentence by sentence with his chin level and his voice steady, that forgiveness was not actually owed here — that some debts should stand, entered in the permanent record, legible forever. But she fed him, and he chopped wood, and once, passing the door, he had stopped beneath Bird's drawing and studied the joining part for a long while.

"Under the boat," he had said finally.

"Under the boat," Theo agreed.

He nodded slowly, as if some clause of his sentence had just come due, and said nothing else, and went down to split logs in the yard like a man performing penance in the only currency he had left.

The bell over the shop door rang. Theo looked up from the press.

A woman stood in the doorway with morning light behind her, a market basket on her arm, hesitating the way people still hesitated at that threshold — half expecting soldiers, half expecting to be turned away. Then she saw the press, and the forme locked in its bed, and the smell of fresh ink that hung in the room like weather.

"You're printing today," she said. Not a question. Word traveled in Vellacourt faster than any courier Odile had ever run, and it traveled especially fast now, in these weeks when the city was learning to speak again after two years of whispering.

"First impression pulls within the hour," Theo said. "Copies at midday. Two sous, or free to anyone who brings me a correction of last month's tribunal record." She had decided on that policy the way she decided most things now: quickly, and on principle. "There will be mistakes. I intend to print those too."

The woman smiled — a careful smile, but real — and set a coin on the counter anyway. "Save me one. My husband was a cooper off the Rue Charpentier." She didn't finish the thought. She didn't need to. Three of the names on the ledger's second page had lived on that street. "He'd have liked knowing someone was keeping accounts."

She left. Theo stood a moment with the coin in her palm, warm from the woman's hand, then dropped it in the till and pulled down her apron.

* * *

Inking the forme was prayer, if you did it right. Her father had taught Marcel the fast way, the showy roll of the brayer that made apprentices gasp. He had taught Theo the slow way: thin coats, cross-rolled, patience layered on patience until every shoulder of every sort wore its film of black evenly, no starved patches, no flooding. *Anyone can put ink on metal,* he used to say, tapping her wrist to slow her stroke. *The craft is putting on exactly enough.* She had resented the lesson at twelve. At thirty-two, standing in her own shop in her own morning light, she understood it was the only lesson that mattered, in type or in truth or in grief: exactly enough. No more than the facts bore. No less than the dead deserved.

She rolled the forme clean, lowered the frisket, closed the tympan over it, and cranked the bed toward the platen.

Through the floorboards, Odile's humming stopped. Footsteps on the stairs — the widow climbing with the creak and deliberation of a woman who had buried a printer and outlived an empire and intended to witness everything remaining. She appeared in the doorway with ink to her elbows and her gray hair escaping its pins, and took up her station at the delivery board without being asked. She had held that station for every clandestine run of the past year; she would hold it now that the runs were legal, because legality, Odile observed, changed nothing about how a sheet ought to be treated.

"Well," Odile said. Her eyes went to the fourth page of the open galley proof pinned above the bench — *printed by Théodora Delacroix* — and lingered there a beat longer than efficiency required. "Pull it, girl. The city's waiting and I'm not getting younger, though God knows I'm trying."

Theo set her hand on the lever.

It was her father's lever, her grandfather's before him, iron worn to a polish at the grip by three generations of palms that were not hers — and now, as she closed her fingers around it, she felt the metal take her print as readily as it had ever taken theirs, indifferent to surname, asking only strength and care. That was the secret of the press, she thought, the thing she had finally earned the right to know: it had never cared who pulled it. All those years she had believed the shop belonged to the men whose names hung over the door, and the shop itself had been waiting, patient as lead, for her to walk in and claim it.

The morning light lay across the press bed like a benediction. Somewhere beyond the rooftops the harbor bells rang the quarter hour, and gulls argued over the fish markets, and the sea went on doing its joined-underneath work, holding all coasts together whether anyone drew them or not. Somewhere on that sea a small hand was learning the letter N. Somewhere in a quarry a broken-handed man was breaking stone and calling it honesty. Somewhere a colonel without a country was discovering that ledgers outlive their thieves.

Theo breathed once, deep, the way her father had taught her to breathe before a difficult pull — *the press knows a hurried heart* — and brought the lever down.

The press spoke.

The sound filled the shop the way it had filled her childhood: that great iron exhalation, the kiss of platen to paper, the small thunder that meant a thing true had been made public. Odile caught the sheet, held it to the light, and read the colophon aloud — *printed by Théodora Delacroix* — in the ringing voice of a woman nailing a thesis to history's door.

"One," said Odile, laying it flat.

Theo cranked the bed back. Inked again — exactly enough — and pulled again.

"Two."

Outside, Vellacourt went about its mending: barricade timber carted away, shutters repainted, a street singer testing a new verse about a cowardly colonel to the applause of fishwives. The smoke had mostly cleared. What remained was ash and scaffolding and, in the shop on the Rue de la Fontaine, the steady rhythm of a press speaking truth at the speed of two hundred impressions an hour, signed at last, at last, at last with the only name it had ever truly belonged to.

By noon there would be five hundred copies in the world. By evening, arguments in every tavern. By Sunday, letters — angry ones, grateful ones, corrections, always corrections, which Theo would print, every one, because that was the covenant now and she meant to keep it.

But for this hour there was only the lever, and the light, and the voice of the press saying over and over, in iron and ink, the first words of the rest of her life:

*Here. True. Signed.*

She pulled again.
