## 1. The Question Too Many

The census taker arrived on a Tuesday, in the grey hour between the milk carts and the first church bells, when the fog off the river had not yet decided whether to become rain. Coral Thornbury heard him before she saw him: a precise knock at the shop door, three raps spaced like the ticks of a well-regulated escapement — even, unhurried, inevitable.

She had been cleaning the bench since dawn, though there was little to clean. Her father had been dead eleven months, and the workbench still kept his habits: the loupe in its leather cradle, the oilers ranked by viscosity, the small brass anvil worn shiny where his elbow had rested for thirty years. She worked alone now under her master's distant supervision, and she had learned that grief, like a mainspring, must be let down slowly or it will snap something you cannot replace.

The man on the doorstep was narrower than his voice. He wore the slate-grey coat of the Census Office and carried a leather folio strapped across his chest like a breastplate. His hat was damp with fog. When he removed it, his hair lay flat and orderly as ledger lines.

"Miss Thornbury?" He consulted a card. "Thornbury, C. Clockmaker's apprentice. I am Mr. Quill, enumerator for this district." He produced a warrant card without being asked, holding it at exactly the distance a careful man holds things he does not intend to surrender. "The national census requires your household particulars."

"Of course." She stepped back to admit him, and noted — the way her father had taught her to note, without appearing to note — that Quill did not step into the workshop so much as enter it, eyes moving over the benches, the half-disassembled carriage clock, the rack of pendulums, with the thoroughness of a man taking inventory rather than interest.

"The premises are your late father's," Quill said. It was not quite a question.

"Leased from the guild trust until my indenture concludes. Two more years." She said it evenly. It was true, and true things are easiest said smoothly.

He wrote. His pen made small, dry sounds, like a beetle in a wall. Name, age, occupation, condition of dwelling, number of persons resident. One. Unmarried. Apprentice. All of it ordinary, all of it ink flowing easily onto the ruled page, and Coral felt the particular relief of routine — the sense that the state, vast and indifferent, had glanced at her and found nothing worth a second look.

Then Quill turned to a fresh line, and the quality of his attention changed.

"A supplementary inquiry." He did not look up. "Standard for households of certain trades. Scientific trades. Cartography, surveying, astronomy." The pen paused. "Was your father ever in correspondence with persons of foreign commission?"

The word *commission* landed in the room and stayed there, ticking.

"Foreign commission," Coral repeated.

"Persons engaged by foreign governments. Scientific attachés. Surveyors under contract abroad. It is a matter of record-keeping only, Miss Thornbury." He raised his eyes then, and they were pale and patient and entirely without warmth — the eyes of a man who believed utterly in forms, and who had discovered that forms could be pointed like weapons. "The Crown wishes to know whose hands have touched the instruments of knowledge. Maps. Charts. Astronomical tables. A map is a weapon, after all, in the wrong drawer."

"My father repaired clocks," Coral said. "Springs, escapements, dials. He corresponded with suppliers in Geneva and with a lens-grinder in Amsterdam. If that constitutes foreign commission, then half the guild would need listing."

Quill wrote something. She could not see what. That was the trick of it, she thought later; the census taker's page was angled away from the citizen as naturally as a dealer angles his cards.

"And no other correspondence? Letters received, letters held?" A fractional tilt of the head. "Perhaps letters left in his keeping by others. Persons of scientific reputation who may have fallen out of favour."

The fog against the window seemed to thicken. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, and Coral used the sound — one full second of cover — to arrange her face.

"I keep his effects," she said. "Invoices mostly. Receipts for brass stock. I haven't gone through everything yet. Grief makes poor bookkeeping."

It was a good lie. It was smooth because it was built on truth the way a fusee is built on its cone — every turn of it true, wound tight around a hollow centre. Her father's papers sat upstairs in a locked chest, and she had not gone through them, and not entirely because of grief. Some drawers, her father had taught her, are better left shut until you have the right tools open.

Quill closed the folio. "Thank you for your cooperation." At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the workshop with that inventory-taking gaze once more. "You will be pleased to know the Office takes an interest in trades such as yours. Precision work is valued. Precision is order." A thin smile, the kind that exists to prove the mouth can do it. "And order, Miss Thornbury, is what the census is *for*."

The door clicked shut behind him. Three raps' worth of silence followed. Then the street resumed its noise, and Coral stood among her father's tools feeling as though a fine probe had been run through the works of her life, testing for grit.

She told herself it was nothing. Census men asked questions; that was their trade, as springs were hers. She swept the floor. She oiled a pallet stone. But her hands were not steady enough for the fine work, and so she did the coarse work instead, and all morning the question ticked inside her like a burr in a train wheel: *persons of foreign commission*. Not *foreigners*, which would be broad and meaningless. Commissioned. Contracted. Professional. Someone had given Mr. Quill a name to hunt, and had dressed the hunt as paperwork.

* * *

The summons came that evening, folded inside the ordinary post along with a guild circular and an invoice for mainspring steel.

She nearly missed it. The envelope bore the seal of the Royal Observatory — the star-and-wheel stamp pressed deep in black wax — and her first thought was that some instrument of theirs needed collection, a routine errand, the kind of commission that paid a shilling and taught her nothing new. She broke the wax standing at the counter with a bread knife, as her father had done, because a proper letter knife was vanity.

The paper inside was heavy stock, printed in formal type:

> **To the Apprentice C. THORNBURY.**
> You are instructed to attend upon the Observatory at your earliest convenience to inspect and repair the Great Regulator, whose going has become irregular. Report to the Keeper of Instruments. This commission is issued under authority of the Observatory Trust.

Below the printed text, in a hand she did not know, ran the authorization. And beneath that, countersigned in small, immaculate script — a signature she recognized, because she had watched him write it that very morning in his leather folio:

**E. Quill, Enumerator.**

Coral set the paper down on the bench, next to the loupe her father had worn smooth, and read it again.

A summons to repair the observatory clock was unremarkable. Every apprentice in the city would envy her it; the Great Regulator was the mother clock, the standard against which the whole city's time was judged, and to put one's hands inside it was a mark of trust few journeymen ever earned. That part made sense. The Observatory had heard of her father's skill, perhaps; his daughter inherited the commission; the guild approved.

But the countersignature did not make sense.

Enumerators certified residence. They counted heads, recorded trades, filed the raw statistics of the nation. They did not — *could* not, by any regulation she knew — authorize technical commissions for the Observatory Trust. Their writ ran to names and numbers, not to clockwork. Unless the census office had taken a sudden interest in horology, Mr. Quill's signature on that page meant one thing only: that the summons had passed through his hands deliberately. That he had wanted her in the tower. Or that someone above him — someone with authority over both censuses and observatories — had wanted her there, and Quill was the instrument through which the order ticked.

She carried the letter to the window, where the last light lay grey on the glass, and held it up as if light might reveal a watermark of intent. Fog was closing over the rooftops. Across the city, unseen, the observatory tower stood on its hill with its great dial lit faintly against the dusk, keeping the hour for everyone, answerable to no one.

Her father had loved that clock. He had climbed to it twice a year for the official regulation, and he had come home those evenings quiet and satisfied, smelling of the tower's cold stone and old machine oil, and once — she remembered it suddenly, the memory surfacing like a coin from river silt — he had come home with a scratch on his wrist and a strange expression, and when she'd asked, he'd said only: *"Even the best movement keeps a secret or two, Coral. Especially the honest ones."*

She had thought it a joke. Horologists' humour. Now the words returned to her wearing a different weight, and she found she could not shake them off.

Silence itself has become dangerous. The thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome, the way a cracked jewel hole announces itself only after the pivot is ruined. A census taker asking about letters. A summons countersigned by the asker. Her father dead these eleven months, unable to say what his silences had been keeping safe.

Outside, the observatory clock began to strike the hour. She counted the strokes out of habit — six, seven, eight — and stopped at nine, cold to the bone despite the fire.

Nine strokes. The Great Regulator, the master of masters, the standard itself, had struck nine at eight o'clock.

Eleven months dead, her father had taught her one final lesson: when the reference fails, everything measured against it drifts, and most people never notice. Coral stood in the darkening workshop holding a summons she should never have received, listening to the wrong hour fade over the city, and understood that she had been summoned precisely because the failure had been noticed — or precisely because it hadn't, and someone needed the tower visited by hands they controlled.

Either way, she would go. An apprentice did not refuse the Observatory.

She wrapped the letter away, banked the fire, and took down her father's tool roll from the high shelf where it had hung untouched since the funeral. The leather was stiff. As she unrolled it, something shifted inside the lining pocket with a dry whisper — and Coral froze, for she knew every tool in that roll, and none of them belonged in the lining pocket.

Whatever it was, her father had hidden it there. Waiting, like the rest of him, for her hands to find it.
