## 2. Eleven Minutes Slow

The tower stairs went up in a spiral of cold stone, and Coral climbed them the way she did everything now — carefully, quietly, as though the walls had ears with a census man's patience. The summons had come that morning, folded into an envelope bearing the seal of the Royal Observatory, and beneath the observatory clerk's neat signature ran a second line, smaller, in ink she recognized from yesterday's doorstep: *Countersigned, E. Quill.*

She had turned the paper over three times before breakfast. There was no reason a census taker should countersign a work order. Census takers counted heads; they did not commission repairs. And yet there his name sat, tidy as a pin, at the bottom of a document sending her alone into the highest room in the city.

*You are imagining things,* her master had said when she showed him, or rather he had said nothing, only grunted over the movement of a carriage clock and told her to be back by four. Mr. Fenwick was a man who believed that suspicion, like dirt, got into everything if you let it. But even he had paused, file hovering, before adding: "Take your father's tools. Not the shop's. Yours."

It was not sentiment. She knew that. Her father's tools were lighter, finer, made for work above stairs where every gram mattered. But standing on the tower's last landing with her satchel against her hip, Coral allowed herself the other meaning anyway: that somewhere in the grain of those worn walnut handles was a hand steadier than her own.

The clock room smelled of dust and old oil and, faintly, of pigeons. Morning light came through the east lancet windows in long amber blades, catching the motes that never settled here because the great pendulum would not let them. The master clock stood against the north wall like a coffin set upright — nine feet of blackened oak case, brass bezels gone green at the seams, and across its face the hands pointing to a lie.

Eleven minutes slow. It had been eleven minutes slow for six days, by the almanac pinned inside the door, and the astronomers were beside themselves. An observatory clock that lied about time was worse than a broken one; it was a dishonest witness.

Coral set down her satchel, rolled her sleeves, and opened the case.

***

The fault announced itself before she found it. A sound — or rather the absence of one. A clock this size should have breathed, a slow tidal sigh between the escape wheel's teeth, and this one held its breath too long at each release. She listened with her ear against the wood, eyes closed, letting the mechanism speak through the case the way her father had taught her when she was small enough to stand on a stool to reach his bench.

"Feel first," he'd say. "Look after. Think last. The clock will tell you where it hurts if you don't interrupt."

She felt it within the quarter hour. The maintaining power — the small weighted lever that kept the train moving while the main weights were wound — had slipped its detent. Every winding, the great weights dropped a fraction further than they should, and the escapement, starved of its proper drop, lost time in increments too small for anyone to catch until they compounded. Eleven minutes over six days. The arithmetic of neglect.

It was a half-hour's repair. She knew it even as she knelt, even as she loosened the first screw. Which meant the astronomers' letters had been right about something else: the fault was recent. Someone had wound this clock carelessly, or clumsily, sometime in the past week — and nobody wound the master clock but the observatory's own keeper.

She filed the thought away and reached up into the weight chamber.

Her fingers found the trouble at once — the maintaining lever, bent a degree out of true, its spring slack. She also found something else. Something that should not have been there.

The weight chamber's rear panel was supposed to sit flush against the oak backboard. Hers met resistance instead: a thickness, a softness where there should have been only wood and shadow. She pressed. The panel gave with a whisper of stitching.

Thread. Someone had sewn the panel shut.

Coral knelt very still in the amber light while the pendulum swung its patient arc behind her, and she thought about the shape of the thing under her fingers. Flat. Rectangular. About the size of a prayer book. Sewn into the body of the most public clock in the city, behind the weights, where no eye would look and no hand would reach except a repairer's — and the observatory sent for no repairer but one.

For her father's shop had serviced this clock for nineteen years. And her father had been dead eleven months.

She took out her knife and cut the stitches.

***

There were fourteen letters. She counted them twice because counting was what her hands did instead of shaking. They were bundled in oilcloth, wrapped around a flat packet of stiff paper — survey sheets, she saw at a glance, coastal charts covered in soundings and bearings in a hand she did not know — and bound together with string tied in a knot her father had taught her when she was nine. He called it a fisherman's secret. *If anyone unties it, you'll know, because it can't be retied the same way.* The knot was intact.

She untied it anyway. Some promises outlive their makers, and some knots are meant to fail in the right hands.

The topmost letter bore a date from four years ago, and the handwriting stopped her breath.

*Coral —*

Not addressed to her. Addressed to *E. Thornbury*, her father, in full formal style — but the opening word of the body was her own name, and for one vertiginous moment she read it as a message reaching across the grave, until sense reasserted itself. No. The writer had simply begun, as men sometimes do, with the person he was really writing to and then corrected himself, struck through *Coral* and written *Edward* above it in the same hurried hand. As if her name lived closer to the surface than her father's. As if, in some other life, these letters might have been hers.

She read.

*Aldous Vane presents his compliments, and his gratitude, which is of the kind that cannot be repaid but only carried. You will recall our conversation of Tuesday last, and the promise you were good enough to make over brandy you pretended not to enjoy. I release you from it now if you wish it. What I ask is not discretion — I would not insult you by asking — but custody. There is a difference, and it may cost you.*

Coral looked up at the pendulum. Looked down again.

*They have taken my charts. That is the plain of it, stripped of the language lawyers will dress it in. Fourteen years of survey along the western coast — every shoal, every soundings line, every harbour that drowns at spring tide — and Magistrate Halloway has entered them at the Admiralty under his own name. My name appears nowhere. When I wrote to object, my letter was returned to me opened and resealed, and two days after, two men came to my rooms with a warrant describing papers I had never owned and correspondence I had never conducted, with persons I had never met, in France.*

*I am to be examined. I know how examinations end for men without friends. Halloway has spent three years buying them — clerks, tide-waiters, a customs officer whose debts I could recite. He does not need me guilty. He needs me gone, and quiet, and the coast mine no longer.*

*What remains is truth, and truth wants keeping. In this bundle are the originals — my field books, my fair sheets, the working drawings in my hand with dates in my hand, which no honest court could mistake and no dishonest one explain away. If I am taken, keep them. Do not give them to any officer of any rank, for the officers are his. Keep them as you kept your wife's ring when the bailiffs came, Edward — somewhere no searcher thinks to look because it is beneath thought.*

*And if I am not taken — burn them, and we shall drink that brandy again and laugh at an old man's nerves.*

*Yours in a debt I cannot retire,*
*A. Vane*

Coral sat back on her heels. The tower was very quiet except for the tick — the wrong tick, the starved tick — and her own pulse, which had picked up the clock's rhythm without asking permission.

She knew the name. Everyone in the city knew the name by now; it had been in the gazette for two years running. *Aldous Vane, enemy agent, detained at His Majesty's pleasure.* She had heard it spoken in her father's shop exactly once, by a customer with a newspaper, and she remembered — with the terrible fidelity memory reserves for what matters — how her father had said nothing. How he had gone on fitting a pallet stone with his head bowed, and how the silence had lasted the length of the customer's visit, and how afterward he had cleaned his bench for an hour though it was already clean.

Which silences keep people safe. He had taught her that too, though he had never once said it aloud. He had taught it in the silences themselves — in what he didn't repeat, didn't answer, didn't write down. She had thought, all these months since the fever took him, that she was grieving a clockmaker. A careful, unremarkable man who mended time and kept his opinions wound tight as mainsprings.

She had been grieving a stranger. Or rather — she understood now, with a clarity that hurt worse than grief — she had been grieving a man she knew perfectly and had never once seen whole. The care had been real. So had the rest, hidden behind it, stitched shut like a panel in a weight chamber.

She read the remaining thirteen letters by the moving blades of window light, and the story assembled itself with the inevitability of a going train: Vane's surveys, Halloway's theft, the forged evidence, the examination, the depot sentence. Her father's replies were shorter — a clockmaker's prose, all function and no ornament — but one line recurred in nearly every letter, and by the fourth appearance Coral had it by heart:

*Still keeping. Still winding.*

Still keeping. Four years of letters, and in the last one, dated five weeks before his death, her father had written: *Still keeping. I have moved it where only I can reach, and soon only my daughter after me. Forgive me the weight of it. You taught me that some things must be carried.*

He had known he was dying. Had he written to Vane afterward? Coral searched the bundle. No reply from the depot. The last letter in the packet was Vane's, dated eight days after her father's burial — a letter her father had never read.

*Edward — I learn from the newspapers what I am permitted to learn, and from silence what I am not. If you have received no word from me these many months it is because my letters are read before they are posted, and so I write nothing worth reading. But if you are still keeping, then know that I do not ask you to act. A promise made in peacetime should not be collected in wartime. Keep it as you keep the clocks — faithfully, and against a better hour.*

*Against a better hour.*

Coral folded the letters along their old creases, wrapped them in the oilcloth, and held the bundle in both hands. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the clock.

Then she did what her hands knew how to do when everything else failed. She repaired the clock. She straightened the maintaining lever, drew the detent to true, adjusted the drop until the escapement breathed properly — that long tidal sigh restored — and set the hands forward eleven minutes to meet the sun. The strike train engaged. High above, in the belfry, the great bell gathered itself and rang the quarter-hour across the rooftops, clean and true for the first time in six days.

She left the letters where they had been? No. She caught herself reaching for the stitching, the old habit of restoration, the instinct to put everything back as found — and stopped with the needle halfway to the thread.

Because the panel had been sewn for a dead man's secrecy. And she was not dead, and the secrecy had just been countersigned by Mr. Quill.

Below her, far beneath the tower, the city went about its morning, ten thousand households giving their names to a man with a ledger. Somewhere in it walked a magistrate who had stolen a coastline and called it loyalty. Somewhere a judge — Ellery, her father had said once, in one of the sentences she had filed away without knowing why, *if it ever comes to it, Ellery still reads his own mail* — sat unread.

Coral sewed the panel shut again, but differently, with a knot of her own devising, and buttoned the letters into her bodice against her heart, where they rode like a second pulse.

The clock above her struck the hour, perfect and loud, telling the whole city a truth it had no idea it had missed.

She picked up her satchel and started down the stairs — and stopped at the landing, because below, crossing the observatory yard with his ledger under his arm and his pen tucked behind his ear like a surgeon's tool, came Mr. Quill.
