## 4. The Census Taker's Rounds

The census book weighed four pounds, and Mr. Silas Quill carried it through the wet streets of the Lower Ward like a man carrying a conscience.

It was a fine book — vellum-bound, ruled in columns of red and black, its corners protected by brass caps that the Office of Enumeration had paid for out of a budget no one audited. Quill had requested the brass himself. A book of this importance deserved to endure, he had told the clerk at the Registry, and the clerk had looked at him with the flat indifference clerks reserve for men who love their work too much, and had said nothing.

Quill did not mind. He had learned long ago that nothing worth doing was done for the approval of clerks.

He walked with his umbrella furled though the rain had not quite stopped — a habit of economy, since umbrellas wore out and his salary, modest on paper, was in practice already spoken for. He walked past the shuttered chandlery, past the cooper's yard where the staves stood in ranks like soldiers awaiting inspection, past the corner where the drain had backed up again and the water sat in the gutter the color of weak tea. He knew every irregularity of these streets. That was the point of him.

People believed the census was about counting. Quill had thought so once, when he first took up the ledger and the inkhorn and the little stamped badge of office, seven years ago. It was not about counting. Counting was arithmetic, and arithmetic any schoolboy could do. The census was about *knowing*. Who lived where, and beside whom; who paid rent and to whom; who had come to the city in the last year and from what direction; whose household had shrunk since the last enumeration, and whether the shrinkage could be accounted for by death, departure, or something the columns had no printed heading for but which Quill, in the privacy of his own mind, called *disappearance*.

A nation that did not know itself would be governed by rumor. Quill had seen what rumor did. He had grown up in a river town where rumor had burned a granary and drowned a tax collector, and he had been eleven years old, and he had watched men who were not evil do things that could not be undone because they had believed a story told by a man they trusted more than they trusted their own eyes. Order was not cruelty. Order was the wall between the fire and the granary.

So he asked his questions carefully, and he wrote his answers neatly, and when a name did not sit right in its column, he underlined it twice and came back.

* * *

The Thornbury house was the fourth stop of the morning, and it had been sitting wrong in his book for six days.

He stood across the lane for a moment before knocking, as was his practice, letting the street tell him what the door would not. Rain dripped from the eaves into a barrel gone green at the rim. A cat regarded him from a windowsill with the contempt cats reserve for all officialdom. The house itself — narrow, three stories, the ground floor given over to the workshop with its painted sign half flaked: THORNBURY & SON, CLOCKS AND WATCHES REPAIRED.

& Son. Quill made a small note against that sign, mentally, as he had done before. Old Thornbury had been dead fourteen months. There was no son. There was a daughter, Coral, twenty years of age or thereabouts, apprenticed — though the apprenticeship papers were another matter entirely — and she kept the shop running alone, or mostly alone. A business trading under a name that described no living person. In most parishes this would be nothing. In a parish where the Registry cross-referenced trade licenses against household returns, it was a loose thread, and loose threads were how order unraveled.

He knocked.

She answered herself, sleeves rolled, a smear of oil along one forearm like a shadow she had picked up somewhere and couldn't put down. She was a plain-faced girl until she moved, and then she wasn't; there was a steadiness in her hands that most grown men never achieved, and her eyes went to his ledger before they went to his face.

"Mr. Quill," she said. "Census day again?"

"Census week," he corrected pleasantly. "Day four of nine. You'll forgive the return visit. Some answers want verifying."

"I gave my answers."

"You did." He opened the book against his forearm, sheltering it from the drizzle out of instinct rather than courtesy, and turned to the page. "Household of one, ground floor commercial, two upper rooms residential. Father deceased, fourteen months past. Mother—" he let the pause sit "—unrecorded."

"Gone since I was six."

"No death record."

"She went west. To her sister's. I was a child; letters stopped. I can't tell you what I don't know, Mr. Quill."

"No," he agreed mildly. "One can only tell what one knows. The difficulty is establishing that one has told all of it." He smiled to take the edge off the sentence, and saw that the smile landed nowhere, and respected her for that. Most people smiled back. Smiling back was how you could tell who hadn't yet understood what the columns meant.

"There's a question I put to you last week," he said. "You may remember it. Whether your late father maintained correspondence with persons of foreign commission."

"He was a clockmaker. His foreign correspondence was with a glass merchant in Rotterdam who sold him watch crystals."

"And did he?"

"Did he what? Yes — crystals. Dozens of invoices. I still have some, if invoices count as evidence of treason."

"Invoices count as paper," Quill said. "Paper is what I deal in." He made the notation. He watched her watch him make it. There — a flicker, very small, at the corner of her mouth. Not fear exactly. Recognition. She had understood that he was not here for the crystals.

"Your tenancy," he said, turning the page as if consulting something. "The freehold is held by a Mrs. Aldersey, who lives in Harwick now and collects rent through an agent who, I find, keeps no proper books. You pay quarterly. In cash."

"That's how Mrs. Aldersey prefers it."

"Mrs. Aldersey is eighty-one and prefers her tea strong. Her preferences are not the law. A commercial premises operating under a partnership designation — 'Thornbury & Son' — with one surviving principal who is herself a minor under indenture—"

"I'm twenty."

"To whom are you indentured?" he asked, and heard the question land the way a key lands in a lock it was cut for. Because this was the thing he had pulled from the files three nights ago, sitting up past midnight with the lamp guttering and his own debts arranged around him like furniture: the Registry held no countersigned apprenticeship record for a Coral Thornbury. Her father had taken her on himself — a father instructing his own child, unregistered, unwitnessed, outside the guild rolls. It happened constantly among tradesmen and meant nothing in itself. But it meant that on paper, legally, she was nobody's apprentice and therefore nobody's responsibility, and a girl trading alone under a dead man's name was precisely the sort of column-entry that Magistrate Halloway's new ordinances had been written to catch.

"I'm indentured to my father's estate," she said. "Until I come of age for full guild standing. The papers are at the workshop."

"I'd like to see them."

Something moved behind her face — quickly, professionally suppressed, but Quill had spent seven years learning to read faces the way she presumably read dials, and he caught it. Not guilt. Calculation. The look of a person deciding, in half a second, which version of the truth she could afford.

"They're at the workshop," she repeated. "You're welcome to them whenever it's convenient."

"Now is convenient," Quill said gently.

* * *

The workshop smelled of brass dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the cedarwood the cases were stored in — a good smell, an honest smell, the smell of things built to outlast their makers. Quill had always liked workshops. There was a morality to them. Every part either fit or did not fit; a wheel was true or it was not; nothing could be argued into working. If more of life were conducted on the clockmaker's terms, he thought, there would be less need for magistrates.

Coral lit the lamp without being asked and went to the tall cabinet at the back, and while her back was turned Quill allowed himself the survey that was technically beyond his warrant and practically his duty: the bench with its jeweler's tools laid out in strict rows; the shelf of movements in various states of undress; the great regulator clock on the wall, its pendulum swinging with that patience clocks have, indifferent to everything; the stack of cases awaiting repair, each tagged in a small precise hand.

And on the desk, a letter tray. And in the letter tray, on top, an envelope bearing the seal of the Royal Observatory.

He had not expected that. His eye snagged on it the way a sleeve snags on a nail.

"The summons came Tuesday," Coral said, turning back with a leather folder in her hands. She saw where he was looking, and instead of hiding it — which would have been wiser, or perhaps less wise, Quill honestly could not decide — she lifted the envelope out of the tray and held it so he could see the superscription. "They've asked me to attend the observatory to service the master clock. Its going is off. Eleven minutes a week, they say, which for an observatory is a scandal."

"Eleven minutes," Quill said. "That's nearly a scandal anywhere."

"The master clock governs the time signals. Ships set their chronometers by it." She opened the folder and laid the documents on the bench between them, smoothing each flat with both hands, and he bent to his reading with genuine attention, because he was genuinely good at this part.

The papers were sound. That was what unsettled him. The indenture instrument was drawn correctly, dated correctly, witnessed — witnessed by a name Quill did not know, a "T. Marsh, chandler," and he made a private mark against T. Marsh, because witnesses who did not exist were the mortar of forged paperwork, but the hand of the document itself, the old man's hand, was confident and consistent and *right*, and forgery was usually wrong in some way a trained eye could feel, the way a false coin feels greasy. This felt like coin.

"Your father drew these himself?"

"He did everything himself. He didn't trust clerks."

"A common sentiment," Quill said, signing his verification slip against the bench edge. "Frequently mistaken." He straightened. "Everything appears in order, Miss Thornbury. I'll note the irregularities as resolved — pending confirmation of the witness, routine matter. And I'll wish you luck with the observatory clock."

"Thank you."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and delivered the sentence he had composed on the walk over, the sentence whose purpose she would understand only later, if she ever understood it at all:

"It's remarkable, the things a house keeps after everyone in it is gone. Papers especially. Paper is patient. It waits in drawers and cases and walls for whoever thinks to look." He tipped his hat. "Good day."

* * *

He finished the ward by dark, seventeen households, forty-one souls, three discrepancies, and then he did not go home.

He went to Halloway.

Not to the magistrate's public chambers — those were for petitioners and defendants, for the theater of justice — but to the brick townhouse on Chandler Row where the lamps burned late behind shutters the color of dried blood. Quill climbed the area steps and knocked at the servants' entrance, because that was how he was received, and did not resent it, because resentment was a luxury and he had learned to live within his income.

Halloway was at his study table with wine he barely drank and papers he read completely, and he listened to Quill's report the way he listened to everything: with his head tilted slightly, as though the words might contain a second meaning audible only at an angle.

"The apprentice attends the observatory tomorrow," Quill said. "Summoned to repair the master clock. On its face, ordinary work. She's evidently competent — the shop survives on her, by all local account."

"On its face," Halloway repeated. He turned a paper square on the blotter a quarter-inch leftward, aligning it with nothing. "Vane's charts have never surfaced. Fourteen months since the old man died, and the originals of those surveys have not appeared at auction, in any registry, in any dealer's inventory. Cartography of that quality does not vanish, Quill. It gets *kept*." He looked up. "You searched the house in the winter."

"Thoroughly. Furniture, cellar, attic spaces, the garden to the depth of a spade. Nothing but stock-in-trade and mourning clothes."

"And now the daughter goes alone into the tower where the city's master clock stands, a clock her father serviced for thirty years, and she carries her tools in a bag no one will inspect." Halloway smiled without warmth, the smile of a man confirming a sum already done. "Paperwork, Quill. Everything comes down to paperwork. Which clocks has that shop serviced in the past year?"

Quill produced his notebook. "Thirty-one entries. Domestic regulators, church turret clocks, two municipal dials, the Custom House chronometer—"

"List them all. Addresses. Dates of service. And Quill—" Halloway's voice dropped into the register he used perhaps twice a year, the one that made even men with nothing to hide stand straighter. "Tomorrow evening, after she's done her work at the observatory, you will walk that tower yourself. Walk it slowly. Touch the machine. Whatever her father hid, it will be where his hands could reach it, and her hands are his hands."

Quill bowed and went out into the rain, and walked home the long way, past the harbor where the masts swayed against the lamplight like a forest of black needles, and he told himself what he always told himself: that he served the wall between the fire and the granary. That order was a kindness. That a girl with steady hands and a dead father's secrets was a splinter in the civic body, and splinters wanted drawing out before they festered.

But beneath the self-told truths, in the place a man keeps the accounts he does not enter in any ledger — the debt to Halloway that had bought off his creditors two winters ago; the promotion that waited on Halloway's patronage; the knowledge, cold and specific as a coin bitten, that the magistrate's interest in Vane's maps had never once sounded like justice — beneath all of it, a small voice noted the fact and refused to file it away:

The daughter had not hidden the observatory envelope. She had held it up to the light.

Nobody innocent did that. And nobody guilty showed you the paper unless they wanted you looking at the tower instead of somewhere else.

Quill sharpened a fresh nib, ruled a new column in his private notebook, and wrote at its head, in his small exact hand: *Observatory. Master clock. Weight chamber.*

Then, after a moment's thought, he underlined it twice.
