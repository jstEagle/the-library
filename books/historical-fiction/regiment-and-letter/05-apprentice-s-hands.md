## 5. Apprentice's Hands

The tissue paper came from Mrs. Alderby's stationery shop on Candle Street, three sheets at a time, purchased over the course of a week under the pretext of wrapping a lady's watch for presentation. Coral chose it deliberately: thin as breath, translucent enough that ink showed through in grey ghosts, and cheap enough that no clerk would remember the sale. She had considered copying onto ordinary paper and burning the originals, but burning was a single act, and single acts left ash, and ash could be found by men like Mr. Quill, who had countersigned her summons to the observatory with a hand she now recognized from the census ledger. No — what her father had taught her, without ever saying it aloud, was that safety lived in multiplication. A secret kept once is a prisoner. A secret kept twenty times is a rumor, and no one hangs a rumor.

So at night, after her master Brammel locked the workshop and his footsteps faded down Harrow Lane, Coral sat at the bench nearest the window with the letters spread beneath a scrap of green baize, ready to be swept into her apron pocket at the first sound of returning boots. She worked by moonlight when the moon obliged and by the glow of a shuttered lamp when it did not, her eyes aching, copying Vane's hand first because it was the clearer of the two.

*The charts are mine,* he had written, in a script that slanted forward like a man walking into wind. *I drew every fathom of that coast with my own instruments, my own eyes, my own frostbitten hands. Halloway has never stood on the North Reach in winter. He could not find those shoals if they rose up and bit his boat. And yet his signature sits beneath my title block, and the Admiralty accepts it, because his word carries weight and mine carries only a foreign grandmother.*

Her father's replies were shorter. He had been a careful man even in ink.

*If I hold them, who comes to me? If I give them away, who betrays whom? Keep your head down, Aldous. I will keep the rest.*

And then, in the last letter, dated eleven days before the fever took him:

*I have placed everything where only a clockmaker would think to look. You taught me that a case is not a hiding place but an argument — that anything built honestly can conceal what must survive dishonestly. If I do not write again, go to the tower. Go to the weight chamber. And forgive me for leaving you the kind of inheritance that cannot be spent, only carried.*

Coral read that line so many times she could recite it with her eyes closed, and some nights she did, like a prayer said backwards, asking not for comfort but for instruction.

She divided the letters the way she would divide a complicated movement: into components, each one functional alone but meaningless without the whole. The pages describing Halloway's claim upon the survey went into one set of copies. The pages naming the Admiralty clerk who had accepted the forged attribution went into another. The final letter — the confession of her father's hiding place, which would lead any reader straight back to the clock — she copied last and handled least, folding it into quarters so small it fit inside the palm of a hand like a moth at rest.

Then she began distributing risk through the city, one clock case at a time.

It was elegant, she thought, in the way that only a mechanism can be elegant. The workshop's ledger listed fourteen timepieces awaiting repair or collection, scattered across the city in parlors and counting houses and one damp vestry where the curate swore the church clock ran fast out of piety. Each case would pass through hands that had no reason to search it. A mantel clock bound for the Widow Sennet's front room. A regulator promised to a shipping office whose clerks changed weekly. A carriage watch so temperamental its owner kept it wrapped in flannel and forgotten. Into each case, wedged behind a strike-train bridge or laid flat against the inside of a backboard where no owner would look for years, she tucked a fragment: a strip of tissue bearing three lines of Vane's hand, or two lines of her father's, dusted with a whisper of brass filings so it would seem like workshop debris to any casual eye.

No single clock held enough to convict anyone. No single clock, if seized, gave up more than a thread. But follow all fourteen threads and you had the whole cloth — Halloway's theft, the forgery, the framing, and the name of the magistrate who had done it, written in the dead cartographer's own furious script.

Her father distributed silences, she thought, folding the Widow Sennet's fragment into its crevice with tweezers meant for balance springs. She distributed paper. Perhaps that was simply how each generation translated the last.

* * *

Brammel noticed on the fourth day.

He was not a perceptive man in conversation; he was a perceptive man at benches. He could tell by the wear on a file which apprentice had used it last, and by the angle of a polished pivot whether the hand that turned it had been patient or hurried. So when Coral burned the midnight oil three nights running and produced, by daylight, a fusee cone with tool marks a half-degree off true, he did not ask her what troubled her. He set the cone aside, picked up his spectacles, and looked at her the way he looked at a movement that ticked but would not run — something alive in it, working against itself.

"You're rushing," he said. "You never rush."

"The light's poor in the evenings."

"The light's been poor since October." He held the cone up between finger and thumb, turning it slowly. "This is good work gone careless. That's worse than bad work, Coral. Bad work doesn't know better."

"I'll redo it."

"You will." He set it down, but he didn't dismiss her, and the silence between them stretched until she felt it pressing on her chest like a weight test. "Your father worked for me four years before he opened his own bench," Brammel said abruptly. "Did you know that?"

"I knew."

"He was the steadiest hand I ever employed. And the last year — " Brammel stopped, adjusted his spectacles, started again. "The last year he made mistakes too. Little ones. Distractions. I asked him once what was eating him and he told me it was nothing, and I believed him, because I wanted to believe him, and six months later he was dead and I still don't know if there was something I should have seen." His eyes, magnified slightly behind the lenses, fixed on hers. "I'm asking you plainly, girl, because I won't make the same mistake twice. Is there something eating you?"

The truth rose in her throat like water in a flooded case. She swallowed it down. *A dead cartographer. A corrupt magistrate. Fourteen clocks full of treason.* What she said instead was: "Only the census, sir. All that form-filling. It puts a person on edge."

"Hmph." Brammel turned back to his bench, and she thought she'd weathered it, until he spoke again without looking up. "Clocks forgive nothing, Coral. That's the first thing I teach and the last thing they prove. Miss a tooth by a hair and thirty years later the whole train is worn wrong and nobody remembers why. People forgive. Clocks don't. Whatever's turning in you — check your escapement before it checks you." He bent over his work. "Redo the cone. Twice."

"Yes, master."

She redid the cone twice, and her hands were steady, because her hands were always steady — it was the rest of her that had begun to run fast, like the curate's pious clock, gaining minutes it could not account for.

* * *

That night she delivered the ninth copy.

The shipping office on Fetter's Quay received its repaired regulator at half past eight, the porter signing the book without glancing at the crate. Coral walked home the long way, along the quay, because the fog was up and fog muffled footsteps, and she had taken lately to checking for footsteps the way other people checked the weather.

At the corner of Rope Walk she stopped. Across the street, lamplit and official, stood Mr. Quill.

He was speaking to the night watchman outside the cooperage, notebook open, pencil moving, and even at this distance she recognized the posture: courteous, thorough, a man collecting the world into columns. As she watched, he closed the book, thanked the watchman with a small bow, and turned — and his gaze crossed the street, crossed the fog, and rested on her for exactly as long as a polite acknowledgment requires. Then he touched his hat brim and walked on toward the harbor, his boots making no sound at all on the cobbles.

Coral stood in the cold until her breath stopped showing. Nine copies hidden. Five to place. One original bundle sewn back into the weight chamber of a clock she had officially certified as repaired.

And a census taker who now, apparently, took evening walks along the very routes her deliveries traveled.

She counted the striking of St. Vedast's bell from the dark doorway — nine strokes, unhurried, correct — and made herself walk home at an apprentice's pace, neither hurrying nor lingering, a girl with nothing in her pockets but tweezers and tissue paper.

Behind her, in the fog, somewhere, she could not help feeling that the whole city was ticking — and that someone had begun, patiently, to count the beats.
