## 6. A Debt in Peacetime

The depot at Greyhaven stood where the land gave up on itself — a shelf of shingle and salt grass between the sea wall and the road, so that the wind arrived without anything to slow it. Aldous Vane had learned its moods the way he had once learned coastlines: by patient observation, by the small honest labor of noticing. In four months he had mapped it entirely, though there was no one left to whom a map of Greyhaven would be worth anything.

He had been a cartographer for eleven years before they took his instruments. He had surveyed the eastern approaches in the King's commission, drawn soundings no man had ever taken, charted the shoals off Marrow Point with such precision that three pilots now living owed him their fathers' lives. Those charts had hung, framed in walnut, in the Admiralty's long room. Then Magistrate Halloway had claimed them as his own discovery, and when Vane had objected — objected in writing, with witnesses, with receipts — the word *treason* had been found waiting for him, like a trap already baited.

They did not call this place a prison. The papers said *depot*, *station for persons of foreign commission*, a phrase that wrapped men like Vane in a fog of bureaucracy until even their crime dissolved into administrative language. He was not accused of anything specific anymore. He simply *was* — an entry, a number inked beside his name in a ledger kept by a depot clerk with chilblains and bad breath. Enemy of the state. The phrase had stopped frightening him around the second month. What frightened him now was subtler: the way his own signature was beginning to look strange to him, the way he caught himself signing his name smaller each time, as if trying to take up less of the page.

*They erase you first from your own hand,* he thought, watching the gulls wheel over the sea wall. *Then the rest is easy.*

* * *

The promise had been made over brandy, in the autumn of '43, in a workshop above a clockmaker's shop in the city — Thornbury's, the sign said, *Clocks Repaired & Regulated*. Vane had come to collect a marine chronometer, and Silas Thornbury had poured him a drink instead, because the rain was coming down like judgment and because Silas was a man who believed that no important conversation should be conducted dry.

They had talked until midnight. About charts, about time, about the strange kinship of their trades — "You draw the shape of the world," Silas had said, "and I keep track of how it moves through it. Between us we ought to manage something like the truth." Vane had laughed, and the laugh had felt like the last easy thing he would do for years.

It was later — near the end, when the candles were guttering and both men were warm enough to be careless — that Silas had turned serious in the particular way of quiet men, which is to say all at once, without warning.

"You've made enemies," he said. Not a question.

"I've made one enemy," Vane answered. "He has friends."

"Halloway." Silas said the name the way other men spat.

"He wants my surveys. The eastern charts. There's money in them — salvage rights, harbor fees, a dozen fortunes resting on those soundings. He means to present them as his own work. And when I refused to sign them over—" Vane had shrugged, and the shrug had cost him more than he knew then. "Men who refuse magistrates tend to find their names on lists."

Silas had been silent a long moment, turning his glass. Then: "If it comes to that — if they take everything — what would you want kept?"

"Kept?"

"Safe. Hidden. Held against the day someone asks the right question." Silas looked up, and his eyes in the candlelight had been steady as a pendulum at rest. "I'm a clockmaker, Vane. My whole trade is keeping things true when everything else runs down. Let me hold something for you."

Vane had protested, of course. It was too much to ask; it put a friend in danger; it was the act of a paranoid man. And Silas had waved all of it away with the same gesture he used to dismiss customers who haggled over repairs.

"A debt in peacetime," he'd said, "is paid in wartime. That's what peacetime is *for*. You drink my brandy now, and someday I'll ask you to remember where you buried a body. Every friendship is a loan against trouble. I'm only asking to hold the collateral."

So Vane had told him. Told him about the original folios — not the copies Halloway paraded before committees, but the true sheets, with Vane's own survey marks and dates in the margins, bearing the Admiralty's own countersignatures. Proof, if anyone ever wanted proof, that the charts were his. He had told Silas where the duplicates lay and what the originals meant, and Silas had nodded slowly, the nod of a man filing something in a very secure drawer.

"Nobody will ever need it," Vane had said.

"Probably not," Silas agreed. "But I'll know where it is."

That was four years ago. Silas Thornbury had been dead fourteen months, and Vane had learned of it eleven weeks late, in a letter from a stranger — a cousin, or an uncle's widow, someone whose relation to the dead man was mostly geographical. *Taken suddenly. His heart, they think. The shop continues under his former apprentice.* Fourteen weeks during which Vane had walked the depot yard every morning not knowing the one person alive who held his name's redemption was already cold.

He had wept that night, quietly, into the coarse blanket, the way men weep in depots: without sound, without witnesses, without relief. Not for himself. For Silas, who had died still keeping faith with a promise, never knowing whether it mattered. And then, in the grey morning, Vane had done what cartographers do with grief: he had fixed its coordinates, marked it, and carried on.

* * *

Petition paper was issued on Thursdays. One sheet per man per month, the clerk announced, as though granting a privilege rather than acknowledging a right so old it predated the office that rationed it. Vane saved his sheets the way other men saved tobacco, and he wrote carefully, because a petition wasted could not be un-wasted.

This Thursday, the eleventh of the month, he sat at the common table beneath the high salt-fogged window and began his last petition — though he did not yet know it would be the last, any more than a clock knows, as it strikes, that it is striking for the final time.

*To the Honourable Commission for the Review of Cases of Foreign Commission:*

*I write again concerning the matter of the Eastern Coastal Surveys, 1841–1844, executed under Admiralty warrant number 2217, countersigned by Captain R. Mowbray and Lieutenant J. Fenn. These documents, in their original form bearing my survey annotations, have been represented to Your Commission as the work of Magistrate Edmund Halloway. This representation is false.*

He wrote steadily, in the small precise hand that drafting had given him — letters like soundings, evenly spaced, impossible to mistake. Around him the common room went about its business: two men arguing over a chess problem, a boy from the northern counties whittling, the eternal scrape of the clerk's pen in the corner. No one looked at him. Men writing petitions were the furniture of the depot.

*My case rests upon documents I cannot produce, being held — I believe — in safekeeping by a party now deceased. I ask the Commission neither mercy nor speed. I ask only that it consider whether a magistrate's fortune and a king's warrant may both attach to the same set of charts, and which of them the charts belong to.*

He paused. Outside, the tide was turning; he could hear the change in the water's voice against the sea wall, the deep intake of the channel. Four years ago he had known every tide table on the eastern coast by heart. Now he knew one stretch of grey water, intimately, uselessly.

There was more he wanted to write. He wanted to write about memory — about how a man stripped of profession, property, and country keeps a country anyway, folded inside him like a chart of a coastline no one surveys anymore. He wanted to tell the Commission that he remembered the smell of the draftsmen's ink, the weight of the brass alidades, the exact blue of the Admiralty's binding cloth, and that these memories were not sentiment but *evidence*, the record of a life that had happened whether or not any ledger acknowledged it. But petitions did not admit such things. Petitions were arguments, and arguments required facts, and his facts were locked in a dead clockmaker's workshop behind a face of enamel and brass.

Or were they? A thought moved through him, slow and cold as the tide. *His former apprentice.* The letter had said the shop continued under a former apprentice. He had met her once — a girl of perhaps twelve, ink-stained, solemn, watching him across the workshop while her master poured brandy. Coral, the name had been. Coral Thornbury. She had asked him a question that night, one question, in the grave voice children use when they have decided a thing matters: *"How do clocks know when to stop?"* And Silas had answered for him, laughing: *"They don't, poppet. That's why we mind them."*

If Silas had hidden the folios — and Silas always kept his promises, that much Vane would stake his eroded name upon — then the hiding place would be somewhere in that workshop. Somewhere a clockmaker trusted. And somewhere a daughter of the trade might find, if she ever thought to look, or was ever given reason to.

He could not say this in a petition. A petition passed through the clerk, through the depot post, through channels that Halloway's friends had fingers in — Vane had suspected since spring that nothing he sent arrived anywhere clean. So he did the opposite. In the final paragraph, he wrote something deliberately plain, almost dull:

*I have no further evidence to offer at this time. I remain, sir, your obedient servant, Aldous Vane, formerly Cartographer to the Admiralty, now of the Greyhaven Depot.*

He signed it — full-sized, taking up the whole width of the line, refusing to shrink — sanded it, folded it once, and left it in the wire tray by the clerk's desk, as eleven identical petitions had been left before.

Then he went out into the yard and stood at the sea wall and watched the light fail over water he would never chart professionally again.

* * *

What he did not see, because no man sees the machinery that moves against him, was the clerk's evening routine: the sorting of the trays, the setting aside of certain correspondence into a separate folder tied with green string, the folder's journey in the morning mail coach to a town house in the city where a magistrate took his breakfast at eight and his information at half past.

What he did not see was Magistrate Halloway unfolding the petition at his breakfast table, reading the paragraph about documents held in safekeeping by a deceased party, and setting down his knife with the deliberation of a man who has just located a loose thread.

And what Vane could not have imagined, standing at the sea wall with the gulls screaming their indifferent mathematics overhead, was a girl in a tower workshop far away, kneeling before a master clock with its case open, holding his own handwriting to the lamplight — learning, at that very hour, the whole shape of the debt her father had paid.

The tide came in. The bell of the depot chapel rang seven, slightly fast. Aldous Vane turned back toward the lit windows of the common room, a name on a list, a list in a ledger, a ledger in a folder bound with green string — and somewhere beyond the darkening water, the thread had already begun to run.
