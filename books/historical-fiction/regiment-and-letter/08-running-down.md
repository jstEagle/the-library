## 8. Running Down

The news came at half past nine in the morning, carried by a boy who delivered watch oil to the workshop and who did not know he was carrying anything else.

"Coppers took Mr. Denby's shop on Cordwainer Lane," the boy said, counting out bottles into Coral's hands. "Whole street turned out to watch. They carried out the long-case with the brass face — you know it? The one what chimes the quarters?"

Coral's hands went still around the oil.

She knew it. She had put three folds of tissue paper inside its case eleven days ago, wedged behind the pendulum bob where only a clockmaker's fingers would think to look — a copy of Vane's letter of the fourth of March, the one naming Halloway's claim upon the coastal charts. One of five copies scattered through the city like seed. She had told herself that distributing risk was wisdom, that her father had taught her no single silence should ever have to carry everything.

She had not told herself what she would do if one of the silences was found.

"Thank you, Tam," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her. "That will be all."

The boy went whistling. Coral stood among the benches with the oil bottles sweating in her grip and listened to the workshop: the tick of forty-odd movements awaiting repair, the rasp of her master Josiah Fenwick's file in the back room, the ordinary morning going on as though the city had not just shifted beneath her feet.

They had taken the clock. Which meant they had taken the letter. Which meant that somewhere in the magistrate's house — or worse, in Quill's neat ledgered hands — there was now paper in a hand the authorities would recognize, describing a crime the state had already ruled impossible.

* * *

She learned by noon how bad it truly was, and she learned it the worst way: through Wren.

Wren Ashby was nineteen, second-year apprentice, quick with a lathe and quicker with a joke, and when Coral came back from the errand she'd invented to get out of the workshop, she found him sitting on the step outside Fenwick's door with his cap crushed in his fists and his face the color of tallow.

"They came to the shop," he said before she could speak. "Two men. Not coppers — civil sort, one of them had Quill's manner about him, all ink-stained and polite. They asked me—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Coral, they asked me about *you*. Where you go of an evening. Whether your father kept papers after he died. Whether you'd been inside the observatory tower."

"And you told them—"

"I didn't know anything," Wren said, and then, horribly, his eyes filled. "I said I didn't know anything, and then the polite one smiled and said that was a pity, because apprentices who knew nothing were worth nothing to a master, and masters with worthless apprentices lost their guild standing, and — Coral, I panicked. I told them about the clocks. The ones you've been servicing all over town since the census. I gave them the list."

The list. She kept it in her head — she had thought keeping it in her head was the safe thing, the careful thing — but Wren had watched her come and go for weeks, and a boy in terror will remember more than he knows he remembers. Cordwainer Lane. The widow Oakes' mantel clock in Salt Row. The chapel regulator off Bell Street. Herself, itemized, distributed across the city like a map drawn by someone else.

"Wren." She crouched so their eyes were level. "Look at me. You are not ruined. Whatever they do next, they do it to me. Do you understand? You gave them addresses. Addresses can be explained — deliveries, repairs, an apprentice's honest confusion. But you must never say the word *letters*, or *Vane*, or *observatory*. Can you hold those three words?"

He nodded, wretched and obedient.

"Then you've held enough." She squeezed his shoulder once and stood, and walked back into the workshop past Fenwick's bench without meeting his eye, because she knew what she would see there — the old man's gaze following her, patient and terrible, a man who mended mechanisms for a living and could hear one running down through a closed door.

At the threshold he spoke anyway, quietly, without looking up from his file. "A clock forgives nothing, girl. Every error accumulates. Every tooth that slips is remembered by every tooth after it." *Scrape.* "Whatever you're carrying — and don't insult us both by denying it — carry it faster than you've been carrying it."

* * *

She waited until the workshop closed at seven, until Fenwick bolted the door and shuffled home through the lamplighters' rounds, until Wren had gone with his crushed cap and his broken secret. Then she let herself out the back and crossed the yards to the observatory hill in the last grey light, moving the way her father had taught her to move through a house at night — heel first, weight committed slowly, never hurrying because hurrying makes noise and noise makes witnesses.

The tower door's lock surrendered to her picks in ninety seconds. Inside, the great clock breathed above her, steady as a sleeping animal, and she climbed the stair with the case key cold in her pocket and her heart doing none of the work her head demanded of it — too fast, too loud, a movement badly regulated.

The weight chamber opened under her hands. The oilcloth bundle sat where she had left it: Aldous Vane's letters, fourteen of them, and beneath them the two sheets in her father's cramped drafting hand — the promise, dated in peacetime, and the inventory of exactly what Magistrate Halloway had stolen and how. The originals. The thing all her clever scattered copies existed to protect.

She should leave them. That had been the design — originals hidden, copies circulating, risk divided. But Cordwainer Lane had fallen, and Wren had given them the map of her rounds, and Quill was a man who followed paperwork the way water follows a slope, patiently, into every low place. If they searched the observatory again — and the fresh oil in the weight chamber would tell them someone had been here, had already been here — the originals were not hidden. They were *stored*. There is a difference, and hunters know it.

She took the bundle. Wrapped it against her ribs beneath her shawl, flat and hard as a second heart, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the escapement tick — *tick, tock, tick* — counting down something she couldn't name yet.

Judge Ellery. It had always ended at Judge Ellery or nowhere. The one magistrate whose signature Halloway could not forge and whose conscience Quill could not purchase; her father had drunk wine with him twice a year for a decade and called him "the last honest clerk in the kingdom," which from her father was nearly a love letter. Ellery held evening audiences on Thursdays at his house on Chancellor's Walk. Tonight was Thursday.

The observatory clock struck ten as she descended the stair. She counted the strokes against her own pulse and stepped out into the city.

* * *

Chancellor's Walk was two miles east, across the river, through streets that at night belonged to the watch and the weather. A brisk walker made it in forty minutes. Coral had perhaps thirty before Quill's tidy mind finished connecting Cordwainer Lane to Salt Row to the observatory hill — and she had less than that, she realized within four streets, because the city itself was telling everyone where she was.

St. Anselm's rang the quarter hour ahead of her, left side. The exchange bell answered a beat later, right. The chapel regulator she herself had serviced last month struck true at the ten minutes, and she flinched from her own handiwork. Every clock in the capital was striking her progress across it, broadcasting her position in fifteen-minute installments to anyone with ears and a grudge — and she understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like laughter, that this was the trap her father had never warned her about: that a city of accurate clocks is a city without shadows of time. She could not be early. She could not be late. She could only be exactly where the hour said she was.

So she used it. At St. Anselm's quarter-chime she cut south instead of north, letting the sound wash over her as cover — anyone tracking by ear would place her near the church, and by the time the exchange bell contradicted her, she was three lanes gone. She ran the straight stretches and walked the corners. The bundle rode against her ribs with every stride, a hard percussion under her heartbeat, and once, crossing Miller's Bridge with the black water sliding below, she permitted herself thirty seconds of memory: her father's hands sewing oilcloth, saying *if it must move, Coral, move it yourself, and never by another's arrangement.*

The eleventh hour began to strike as she came off the bridge.

And there she stopped, because the count was wrong.

Eleven — twelve — thirteen. Fourteen strokes, deliberate, unhurried, from the direction of the Corn Exchange, and Coral stood in the empty square with her breath smoking and counted every one, because she knew that clock, she had oiled that train in September, and its hour wheel carried precisely twelve teeth.

Someone was striking it by hand. Or someone had jammed its detent and let the train run loose — and either way, the whole quarter of the city was now looking up, muttering, opening shutters, gathering in doorways beneath a bell that refused to be governed.

A crowd was a cover. A crowd was also a magnet, and somewhere behind her, following a trail of freshly-oiled movements through the streets like a man reading footprints, came Mr. Quill with his ink-stained politeness and his ledger full of her life.

She ran the last mile with the wrong-struck bell still tolling at her back, thirteen, fifteen, sixteen strokes of beautiful lawless noise, and turned onto Chancellor's Walk as the final stroke died away — to find, halfway down the terrace, a figure standing beneath Judge Ellery's lamp-lit windows, consulting a notebook by matchlight.

Quill looked up. Across sixty feet of frozen cobbles, their eyes met, and Coral Thornbury did the only thing left in her train: she lifted her skirts and pounded on the judge's door as though the house were burning.
