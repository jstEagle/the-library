## 10. What Memory Keeps

The shop on Chandler's Row had been a pie-maker's once, and it still smelled faintly of suet and scorched tin no matter how often Coral scrubbed the floorboards. She had decided she didn't mind. It was an honest smell, the smell of a place that had fed people, and now it was a place that kept time for them instead — which, her father would have said, amounted to nearly the same trade. Both were ways of measuring out what people could not hold.

She had hung her name above the door in letters she painted herself, standing on a borrowed ladder on a cold morning in March: **C. THORNBURY — CLOCKS & WATCHES REPAIRED**. The paint had run a little at the bottom of the *y*. She left it. Perfection, she had learned at considerable cost, was not the same as truth, and she had developed a private loyalty to things slightly askew.

The indenture papers were gone — torn up by Master Fenwick's own hand, laid across his bench like something dead he wished to be done burying. He had not shouted. That was the worst of it, or perhaps the best. He had simply set down his loupe, looked at her across two years of shared work, and said: *You knew what you were carrying, girl, and you carried it anyway. I can't keep an apprentice who keeps secrets from me. Go be honest somewhere else.* And then, as she reached the door: *The Whitby regulator wants cleaning before spring. If you're ever respectable again.*

She thought about that sentence more often than she admitted. It was the closest thing to a blessing he knew how to give.

* * *

Winter had passed the way winters do after a scandal — slowly, then all at once, then with everyone pretending they had always known the shape of it. The trial of Magistrate Halloway never happened; that word itself, *trial*, turned out to be too grand for what occurred. He resigned. A statement was read in a room she was not permitted to enter, full of phrases like *ill health* and *the demands of public service*, and by the following week his portrait had come down from the Sessions Hall and his house on Gilder Street had new curtains in the windows belonging to someone else. He would live comfortably somewhere. They always did. Coral had stopped expecting the arithmetic of justice to balance; she had learned instead to read its remainders.

Aldous Vane was exonerated. The word was printed in the *Gazette*, four lines beneath an advertisement for patent manure, and Coral cut it out anyway and kept it in the drawer where she kept her father's last letter. *Aldous Vane, formerly of the Coastal Survey, is cleared of all charges arising from the proceedings of 1843. The Crown regrets the error.* The Crown regrets the error. As though the Crown were a clockmaker who had fitted the wrong wheel and could simply open the case and set it right. Four years of a man's life, his name, his maps, his coast — *regretted*. Filed under error. Closed without apology anyone had to say aloud.

And Vane was not restored. She understood now that this was the quiet cruelty built into exoneration, the clause nobody reads until it applies to them. His charts remained Crown property. His commission was not renewed — *in light of the passage of time*, said the letter, as though time were a neutral party and not the very thing that had eaten him. A cartographer needs eyes the state trusts, and the state, having once blinded itself, preferred to hire men who had never seen him stumble. So Aldous Vane walked the city a free man with no profession, no post, no sea to measure, and Coral watched him learn what she had learned in the tower with the letters in her hands: that being proven right is not the same as being given back what you lost.

* * *

He came to the shop on a Tuesday in April, in the hour when the light comes low and gold through the front window and makes even cheap brass look valuable.

She heard the bell over the door — a little thing she'd taken off a carriage clock, tuned to a note just below the noise of traffic — and looked up from the bench expecting Mrs. Pell from the bakery with her running-down mantel piece. Instead it was Vane, hat in hand, older than the portraits in the newspapers had made him, with a grey she suspected had arrived mostly in the past six months. He stood a moment just inside the door, looking around the shop the way a man looks around a room he has been told about but never seen — at the cases awaiting repair along the wall, at the great wheel of the Whitby regulator leaning against the far shelf, at her, small behind the bench with a burnisher still in her fingers.

"Miss Thornbury," he said. "I hope I'm not— I was told you'd set up here."

"I was told you'd been walking past," she said. "Twice. Mrs. Pell counts everything."

Something moved through his face that was almost a smile and didn't quite arrive. "I wasn't sure I'd be welcome. The last time we spoke properly you were handing evidence to a judge while a census taker climbed the tower stairs after you."

"You weren't the one climbing them."

"No." He set a watch on the bench between them, gently, the way one sets down something borrowed. "But I've spent four years learning who does the climbing, and I find I'm careful now about whose doors I darken. Old habit." He pushed the watch an inch toward her. "It belonged to my wife. It stopped the week they took me — I don't know whether it broke or whether I let it. It hasn't run since. I'm told you're good with the ones other people have given up on."

Coral opened the case. Inside, the movement was furred with dust and one of the pallet jewels had cracked clean through, and the mainspring had been left wound tight against a dead train for so long it had taken a set, coiled into its own grief like a fist that had forgotten how to open. She had seen watches like this before. She had *been* watches like this before, briefly, in the autumn.

"It can be brought round," she said. "Not to what it was. To what it is now. Those aren't the same thing, Mr. Vane, and anyone who promises you otherwise is selling you something."

"I've had enough of people promising restoration," he said. "I'll take *running*."

* * *

She worked on the watch through the length of April, and because there was no one to tell her she couldn't, she let him sit in the shop while she did it — first on the stool by the door like any customer, then, as the weeks went on, closer, at the second bench she'd bought secondhand and never expected to fill. He talked while she worked. That was the arrangement neither of them announced: her hands busy, his memory unspooling. She learned about his wife, Marianne, dead three years before the arrest, and about the survey — the smell of the salt marshes at dawn, the way he'd taught his chainmen to sing against the cold, the night he and her father had sat over brandy in this very city and her father had said, *if it ever goes wrong for you, Vane, I know a clock that doesn't tell the time honestly, and I know a daughter who will.*

"He said that? About me?" Coral asked, holding the balance cock to the light. "I was twelve."

"He said you'd already taken apart the kitchen clock twice and put it back together better than he could. He said some people are born knowing that a thing must be *true inside itself* before it can be useful to anyone else." Vane looked out the window, toward the tower, barely visible past the rooftops, its dial pale against the afternoon. "I thought it was a strange thing to say about a child. Then your father hid my life's work in a weight chamber and died keeping the secret, and his daughter finished the job, and I thought: he wasn't describing a child. He was describing a clockmaker."

Coral bent lower over the bench. There was dust in her eyes, she told herself, or solder-flux, or something. She let the silence stand a moment, the kind of silence her father had taught her to keep — not the hiding kind, not anymore, but the kind that holds a shape so it doesn't break.

"The escapement's done," she said finally. "I've fitted a new pallet stone. When I let the train down, it'll start, and it won't stop again unless something breaks that I can't mend."

"And if that happens?"

"Then bring it back," she said. "That's what shops are for."

* * *

The watch ran. Of course it ran — she would not have let it leave the bench otherwise — but she made Vane wear it home for a fortnight before she considered the work closed, and when he returned it was keeping time to within a minute a week, and he wore it on a new strap and touched it often, the way a man touches a rail when the ground feels uncertain.

On the evening he came to collect it finally and formally, she climbed the stairs to the flat above the shop and opened the casement, and they stood together in the small square of air and looked east.

The observatory tower rose black against the last of the light, and high in it, the master clock's dial glowed faintly, lit by the lamp the Observatory kept burning behind it — a courtesy, the Astronomer had said, for the instrument that had served the city faithfully through fire, scandal, and repair. At nine o'clock precisely, the bell struck: nine strokes, evenly spaced, each one falling on the true second as though the intervening months had never happened. The escapement she had rebuilt turned in the dark, unseen, unforgiving, exact.

"Perfect," Vane said quietly. "After everything."

"That's why it's perfect," Coral said. "Nothing got into the works. Whatever men did to each other down here, the going train never knew. Escapement, wheels, pendulum — they only know what they're built to know." She paused. "I used to think that was a limitation. Now I think it might be the whole point. Something in this city ought to keep true time regardless of what's written in the ledgers."

Vane was silent a moment. Below them, Chandler's Row settled into its evening noises — Mrs. Pell pulling in her shutters, a cart grinding homeward, a child calling a dog. Ordinary sounds. The sounds, Coral thought, that people fight wars they never hear of in order to protect.

"There's a letter came for me yesterday," Vane said at last, and something in his tone made her turn. "From the Admiralty. They want a survey done — the northern coast, the part Halloway falsified. Properly done this time, with witnesses." He was turning his hat in his hands, an old man's nervous habit on young-ish shoulders. "They haven't offered me the commission. They've offered to *consider* me, pending references and an interview, and I have no references because everyone who could give me one is dead or disgraced, except—" He stopped. Looked at her.

"Except," Coral said.

"They've asked for a character of standing in the mechanical trades. Someone who can speak to whether I'm the sort of man who keeps instruments honestly." He held out the Admiralty's letter, and beneath it, a second paper, blank except for the heading and a line ruled for a signature. "You repaired their master clock, Miss Thornbury. Half the city knows your name now, and the other half knows your father's. You're the only reference I have left. And you'd be vouching for me to the same office that ruined me, using your own name, which they've only just finished forgiving."

Coral took the pen from behind her ear. She looked at the blank line a long moment — long enough for the tower bell to begin striking the quarter-hour, four strokes rolling up Chandler's Row, each one landing true.

Her father had taught her which silences keep people safe. He had never gotten to teach her the other lesson, so she had taught herself: that some words, spoken aloud, in ink, in your own hand, cost exactly what they protect — and are worth it.

"What happens," she asked, signing, "if they say no?"

"Then I walk the coast anyway," Vane said, folding the letter away. "With or without their permission. I've learned the difference between the law and the truth, Miss Thornbury. One of them moves faster than the other, but only one of them keeps going after dark."
