## 1. The Question Too Many

The wind came off the Atlantic that morning like something with an errand. Gideon Yarrow heard it before he saw it — a long, low moan in the foghorn's throat, the sea gathering itself against the headland the way a fist gathers before it falls. He stood at the lamp room window with his tea going cold in his hand and watched the weather stack itself up over the horizon, grey upon darker grey, and thought, as he thought every autumn: *she'll want trimming, this old light. She'll want all my attention before the week is out.*

He was fifty-one years old and had kept the Cape Corran light for twenty-two years, eleven months, and some days he did not count. The counting was the trouble. A man could keep a lighthouse by arithmetic — oil, wicks, rotations, hours — but a man could not keep a grief that way, though God knew he had tried.

Below him, on the cliff path, a figure came climbing against the wind. Small, dark-cloaked, bent into the gale like a nail driven sideways. Gideon set down his cup and watched her come. Nobody climbed to Cape Corran without reason. The fishermen knew better than to waste breath on the height; the revenue men came by boat when they came at all; and Edith had been dead six years come Candlemas, so there was no one left with cause to visit a keeper who spoke perhaps forty words a week and hoarded the rest.

By the time she reached the door he had descended the hundred and nine steps and unlatched it, and the wind shoved past him into the hall like an uninvited relative.

"Mrs. Quill," the woman said, holding out papers already curling at their edges. "Census. I'm told you're the last house on my list, and I begin to see why." She had a voice like dry gravel under wheels — not unkind, but not soft either — and eyes the colour of rainwater over slate, quick eyes, eyes that went round the room cataloguing it before Gideon had finished nodding.

"You'll have come up that path for nothing," he said. "I can write my own name."

"All the same." She stepped inside without quite being invited. Her boots left wet crescents on the flagstones. She unwound a scarf, produced a pencil from somewhere about her person as if drawing a weapon, and looked about the kitchen — the blackened range, the salt-whitened window, the single chair, the single everything. "You live alone?"

"I do."

"No family? No servant?"

"The gull on the chimney does the cooking."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An assessment. She sat where he indicated, spread her forms on the scrubbed table, and began in earnest. Name. Age. Occupation. He answered in the flat, economical way of a man who has given these answers to harbormasters and vicars and one coroner, years ago, and intends never to give more than required. *Yarrow. Fifty-one. Principal keeper, Trinity House.* His hand, when she asked him to make his mark, was steady as a rock in a wall. Twenty-two years of steady hands. It was the rest of him that moved underneath, like sand at half-tide.

She wrote swiftly, her pencil scratching, and outside the wind rose another note on its scale. The first spits of rain came against the window like thrown gravel. Gideon put the kettle on because there was nothing else to do with his hands, and because hospitality was a habit Edith had drilled into him so deep it outlived her, the way a bell keeps swinging after the ship has struck.

It was while the kettle sang that Mrs. Quill saw the prayer book.

It lay on the shelf above the range where it had lain for six years, between the tin of tea and the brass holder for the spare wicks. Black leather gone soft as cloth at the corners, gilt lettering worn to ghosts. Edith's. She had read it every night of her married life, aloud sometimes, her voice moving through the psalms like water through reeds. Gideon had not opened it since. He dusted the cover. That was all. Dusting was a kind of devotion too, or he had decided it must serve as one.

"That your wife's?" Mrs. Quill asked.

"It was."

"She'll not mind, then." And before he understood what she was about, the woman had reached up — small, quick, inevitable as weather — and taken it down.

Gideon felt something turn over in his chest, slow and cold, like a hull beginning to list.

"I only ask," Mrs. Quill said, "because we're told to record religion where we can. Saves the vicar writing to folk." She thumbed through the pages with the efficiency of a woman who handled other people's books daily and loved none of them. "Marriages, births, folk write such things in here. Half my records would be empty without—" 

She stopped.

The kettle screamed. Gideon took it off the heat and did not turn around. In the window glass, warped by old glass and older salt, he could see her reflection behind him, her head bent over the open book, her thumb resting on a page near the back — the pages where Edith had written things. Names. Dates. Little memorials of a life that had been, on the surface, so simple.

"There's a name crossed out here," Miriam Quill said.

"Is there."

"In ink. Not scratched through proper — just a line drawn through it, like someone couldn't bear to look at it but couldn't bear to cut it out either. *Elias Brandt.*" She read it carefully, sounding the foreignness of it, letting each syllable sit in her mouth a moment too long. "That's no Cornish name."

"No," Gideon said. "It isn't."

The rain arrived properly then, sheeting against the glass, and the light in the kitchen went the colour of pewter. Mrs. Quill turned in her chair, the prayer book still open in her hands, and looked at him with those slate-rain eyes, and Gideon understood that whatever he said next would be written down somewhere, if not on her form then in her memory, which he suspected was the better ledger of the two.

"My wife kept accounts," he said. "Of everything. Folk she'd fed, folk she'd prayed for. Sailors mostly. Half the coast passes this light, Mrs. Quill, and a good many of them aren't English, and Edith never met a drowning man whose language mattered." He let a silence fill with rain-sound. "She crossed it out herself, before she died. Said she was tidying."

"Tidying." Miriam Quill repeated the word as though tasting it for spoilage.

"People do odd things with grief."

"They do," she agreed, and closed the prayer book gently, and set it on the table instead of returning it to the shelf, and Gideon noted that too. Everything about her was notes. "And who was Elias Brandt, that a lighthouse keeper's wife should pray for a Prussian?"

"A sailor," Gideon said. His voice was level. He was rather proud of how level it was. "Sailors pass. You'd know that if you walked the shore."

"Oh, I walk the shore," she said. "Every day. It's remarkable what washes up."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Outside, the gale found its full voice; somewhere below the headland the sea detonated against the rocks with a sound like artillery. Then Mrs. Quill gathered her forms, tucked her pencil away, wound her scarf, and stood.

"I'll need you to confirm these particulars at the vicarage within the fortnight," she said. "Routine. Everyone does it." At the door she paused, half in the wind, cloak snapping, and looked back at him with something that was not quite suspicion and not quite sympathy — something stranger, that might have been recognition. "Your name will go in the book plain enough, Mr. Yarrow. It's the names other people leave in your house that worry me."

Then she was gone, down the cliff path, bent like a nail, and Gideon stood in his kitchen with Edith's prayer book on his table and his heart going hard against his ribs like surf.

* * *

He did not touch it for three hours. He trimmed the wicks. He polished the lens until the great drum of glass threw its wheeling spokes across the darkening water. He checked the oil reserves twice and logged the barometer falling, falling, falling, the needle sagging toward *storm* like a tired swimmer. There were things a keeper did, and he did them all, and every one of them was a way of not walking across that kitchen and opening the book.

But at half past nine, with the storm rattling the glazing of the lantern like a fist on a door, Gideon Yarrow sat down at his table with a candle lit and pulled Edith's prayer book toward him, and opened it where Miriam Quill had opened it.

There it was. *Elias Brandt* — written in Edith's careful hand, the letters upright and kind, and beneath them a date, 1873, and beneath the date a line of Prussian that Gideon could still read after twenty years the way you can still taste a childhood meal. And through it all, one stroke of ink, drawn hard, the pen pressed so fiercely it had nearly torn the page. Edith had done that. Two weeks before she died, when the fever had her and her mind wandered the old channels. He had been in the room. He had watched her draw the line. She had looked up at him with eyes gone bright and strange and said, *tidy it, Gid, tidy it before anyone sees,* and he had promised, and he had not, because cutting the name out would have been cutting out the man, and the man had saved his life, and there are debts even a tidy widow cannot cancel.

He set the book down. His hands were not steady now.

Across the kitchen, in the corner where the shadows pooled thickest, stood the sea chest. Iron-bound, salt-stained, locked these twenty years. He had told himself it held old charts and rotten rope and a dead man's spare clothes. He had told himself so long and so evenly that he had half made it true, the way you can half believe anything if the believing keeps the peace.

Gideon rose, fetched the key from its hiding place inside the hollow leg of Edith's worktable — a hiding place she had chosen, in a house she had ruled, guarding a secret she had never once asked him to explain — and knelt before the chest with the candle guttering and the storm shouting overhead.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, under oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay the past: a merchant seaman's discharge papers in the name of a younger man; a Prussian hymnal; a pipe carved with an anchor and an eagle; and folded flat, wrapped in waxed linen, two documents, each torn cleanly down the middle. One half he had carried home from the Baltic in 1873 and hidden ever since. The other half had gone away in Elias Brandt's breast pocket, over the horizon, into whatever life the sea had allowed him — with a promise stitched into the paper in two languages:

*If ever your people call mine enemy, come to me, and be sheltered.*

Gideon sat back on his heels. The candle flame leaned in the draught. Somewhere far off, out in all that violence of wind and water, the sea was doing what the sea always did — carrying things toward coasts, giving nothing warning.

Twenty years of silence, and tonight, for the first time, he heard it creak.

He took the oath-half out of the chest and held it to the light, and did not hear — could not have heard, over the storm — the far-off cry of gulls wheeling over black water, where something was already coming ashore.
