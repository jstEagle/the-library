## 2. What the Register Kept

The vestry of St. Enodoc's smelled of beeswax and cold stone, and Miriam Quill loved it the way some people loved libraries or bakeries — entirely, and without apology. Rain came off the Atlantic in grey curtains and rattled the small leaded window above the parish chest, but inside there was only the dry rustle of paper and the tick of her own pencil against her teeth.

She had been at the registers since seven that morning, wrapped in a shawl that smelled of woodsmoke from the inn, working through the census returns she'd collected in the week past. It was methodical work, and she was a methodical woman. Names into columns. Ages into columns. Occupations, birthplaces, infirmities — the word itself still made her flinch slightly, *infirmities*, as though being blind or lame were a moral failing to be recorded for posterity. She wrote *lunatic* beside old Tamsin Pascoe's entry because Tamsin had insisted upon it, cackling, saying she'd been mad since 1861 and saw no reason to lie to the Crown about it now.

It was good work. Honest work. Work that let her sit alone with other people's lives and not be asked any questions about her own.

The Yarrow entry sat third from the top of her loose-leaf pages, copied out in her small, upright hand:

*Yarrow, Gideon. Keeper of the Light. Age 44. Widower.*
*Occupation: Lighthouse Keeper.*
*Born: Falmouth, Cornwall.*

And beneath it, because she could not help herself, because curiosity was the one appetite she had never learned to starve: a note in the margin, smaller than the rest.

*Prayer book. Foreign name inked inside cover. He would not say whose.*

She had turned the matter over all night, the way one turns over a smooth stone in the pocket — not because it meant anything, but because it fit the hand so well. A foreign name. Inked, not written in pencil; someone had wanted it permanent. Inside a dead woman's prayer book, which meant the name had mattered to Edith Yarrow, or had mattered enough that Gideon had kept the book anyway, twenty years after burying her.

Miriam had buried a husband too. She knew what the dead left behind in books.

* * *

The parish chest stood against the north wall, iron-banded, older than the church around it in spirit if not in fact. Miriam knelt before it with the key the vicar had pressed on her — a distracted, consumptive man who seemed relieved beyond measure that someone else would handle the paperwork of souls — and lifted out the registers one by one, laying them on the vestry table like bodies prepared for examination.

Baptisms first, then burials, then marriages. The marriage register was the fattest, its leather spine cracked white with use, and she worked backward through it with growing absorption. Marriages told you everything, if you knew how to read them. Who stood up with whom. Which families joined and which stayed stubbornly apart. How many brides signed with an X and how many grooms couldn't hold a pen steady enough to make even that.

She reached the year 1873 and stopped.

There was nothing wrong with the page, precisely. Nothing wrong except its absence. Between the marriage of William Coad to Jane Trevanion in April and the marriage of John Bligh to Mary Ann Rundle in September, the numbering jumped. Folio eleven simply was not there. Someone had taken a blade to the binding — carefully, she thought, running her finger along the stub of the torn leaf, feeling where the paper ended in a clean vertical line rather than a ragged tear. Not vandalism. Surgery.

Someone had cut out a marriage record, and they had done it well.

Miriam sat back on the vestry stool and looked at the wound in the book for a long time while the rain wrote and rewrote itself against the glass.

Pages did not cut themselves. Registers did not bleed. In nineteen years of census work across three counties, she had found erasures before — an illegitimate child's entry scraped with a knife, a bigamous groom's name overwritten twice — and every one of them had been clumsy, panicked things, done by lamplight by hands that shook. This was different. This was patient. Whoever removed folio eleven had known exactly what they were removing and had taken their time about it.

And yet whoever did it had missed something, or had not thought it worth removing. For when Miriam turned to the index at the back of the volume — that humble alphabetical list that no one ever thought to censor — her pencil hovered over an entry that made the rain seem suddenly very loud.

*Brandt, Elias — m. Keverne, Ruth — see f. 11*

*See folio eleven.* The folio that no longer existed.

She read the index line four times. Then she read the witness column of the surrounding entries, hunting, and there — on the surviving half of the gathering, on the page facing the torn stub where the officiating minister had carried his entries across — a fragment of the missing record remained, caught in the gutter of the binding where the blade had not quite reached. Half a line. A few letters, ghosted in faded iron-gall ink.

*...witnessed by G. Yarrow, keeper of the...*

Miriam set down her pencil. Her hands were not shaking, but they wanted to.

Elias Brandt. A Prussian name if ever there was one — Brandt, like the fire that burns in a grate; she'd known a German family in Truro by the name. Married here, in this Cornish parish, in 1873, to a local woman, Ruth Keverne. Witnessed by Gideon Yarrow. And the record of it excised from the book so thoroughly that only an accident of binding and a clerk's thorough index preserved any memory of it at all.

Twenty years ago. The same year, near enough, that Gideon Yarrow had come to the light with a new wife and a closed face, and had said almost nothing to anyone for two decades except what the tide required of him.

* * *

She should have left it. That was the honest accounting, made later, in the dark: she should have copied the index line into her notes and then struck it through, the way she struck through anything that was not the Crown's business. Curiosity was her sin, but she had always kept it on a short leash. Other people's secrets were not hers. She of all people knew what it cost to have yours handled carelessly.

But the leash had slipped, and she went instead to the vicarage, and asked Mr. Tregenna, casually, over tea he was too ill to drink, whether the parish had ever kept a second copy of its registers.

"Duplicate books, you mean? Sent up to the bishop? Oh, yes. Every year." He sneezed. "Though I shouldn't wonder if half of them rotted in the diocesan cellar. Bishopston doesn't answer letters faster than once a quarter."

"And if a page were damaged locally — lost, defaced — would there be a record of the correction?"

He looked at her over his spectacles with mild surprise. "A restoration order, perhaps. Why? Has something happened to the books?"

"A question of completeness," she said smoothly, and hated how easily the lie came. "For the census verification."

That afternoon she walked to the harbour and found the oldest man in Porthquinn still capable of speech — Joseph Chenoweth, ninety-one, who sat wrapped in sacking outside the Ship Inn watching the sea as though it owed him money. She bought him a pint of stout he could barely lift and asked him, idly, about the year 1873.

He remembered it the way old men remember weather.

"Fog year," he said. "Bad spring. There was a wreck up toward Gull Rock in March — foreign brig, outward bound light. Two men drowned." His clouded eyes fixed on some middle distance. "One didn't. Big fellow, fair-haired. Spoke English like a hymn sung slow. Prussian, they said. Sailor off a Bremen ship, washed overboard weeks before, walking the coast trying to get home. Gideon Yarrow pulled him out of the surf himself, back when Gideon sailed merchant, before the light."

"What became of him?"

Chenoweth smiled, and there was something soft and sorrowful in it. "Married Ruth Keverne, didn't he. Pretty creature, always laughing. Her father nearly burst a blood vessel — a Prussian! In the year of our Lord!" The smile faded. "Then the orders come down. Government notice in the inn — aliens to be registered, certain nationals to be moved along. And one morning the pair of them were gone. Took ship from Falmouth, some said. Others said—" He shrugged under his sacking. "Others said men came for them in the night, and the register page went missing same season, and Reverend Pascoe preached a sermon about obedience he never finished writing. Ruth's mother wore black till she died and wouldn't say for whom."

Miriam's mouth was dry. "And Gideon Yarrow?"

"Witnessed the marriage. Never spoke of either of them again. Not once, not drunk, not dying." Chenoweth drained his stout with effort. "That kind of silence, miss, isn't grief. Grief talks. That kind is a debt."

* * *

The letter from the Home Office district office arrived on Thursday, forwarded through the census bureau with the district supervisor's compliments, and Miriam read it twice standing in the inn's narrow hallway with rain dripping off her hem onto the flagstones.

The language was civil. The language was always civil; that was what made it frightening. *In view of current concerns regarding persons of enemy origin residing within coastal districts,* it began, and Miriam heard, beneath the civility, the sound of something large turning over in its sleep — the sound the whole country was making that autumn, a low growl of suspicion rolling in from London like weather off the Atlantic. War talk. Fleet movements. Newspapers full of Prussian spies behind every hedge, though the nearest Prussian most Cornish folk would ever meet was a retired music teacher in Penzance.

*Enumerators are reminded that anomalies in parish records bearing upon alien registration may constitute matters of national importance, and should be reported without delay to the undersigned.*

*Without delay.* She carried the letter up to her room and set it on the table beside her census papers, and there it lay like a trap already sprung.

Because she had found an anomaly. She had found it within the hour of receiving the letter, in fact, sitting back down at the vestry table with her heart going hard — for the diocesan registry in Bodmin, queried by post a fortnight ago on an unrelated matter, had answered her with a clerk's terse efficiency, and among the enclosed extracts was a certified copy of a marriage solemnized at Porthquinn, the eleventh day of June, 1873: *Elias Brandt, mariner, of Bremen, Prussia, son of Heinrich Brandt, master mariner — and Ruth Keverne, spinster, of this parish.* Witnesses: *Gideon Yarrow* and *Margaret Keverne.*

So the record survived, safe in a cellar a hundred miles inland, and Gideon Yarrow's name stood in it in his own young handwriting, and somewhere in London a file might now exist with that same name in it, and Miriam Quill was the thread connecting the two.

She took out the reporting form. It was a dull buff-coloured thing, printed in a typeface designed to make urgency look like routine. *District. Parish. Nature of anomaly. Persons concerned. Recommended action.* She dipped her pen. She wrote *Porthquinn* in the first blank, and her hand stopped.

If she reported it — and the law, and the letter, and the rising temper of the times all said she must — then a man who had witnessed a friend's marriage twenty years ago would find navy men at his door asking questions he had spent half his life refusing to answer. A man who kept a light burning for strangers in the dark. A man whose wife's prayer book held a foreign name inked there like a scar he chose to keep.

And if she did not report it, and it surfaced later — records had a way of surfacing; she was living proof of that — then it would be her name in the wrong column, her career, her one clean refuge in the world, gone.

Miriam looked at the form for a long time. Outside, the wind had begun to rise, moaning in the inn's chimney, and far off she could hear the fog signal from the headland beginning its long, patient complaint — the voice of Gideon Yarrow's lighthouse, calling out over the water to people it would never see.

She thought of the torn page. Of the careful hand that had cut it. Of Joseph Chenoweth's cracked voice: *that kind of silence is a debt.*

She uncapped the ink bottle, and wrote — slowly, deliberately, in her small upright hand — a single line in the space marked *Nature of anomaly:* *Register deficient; page lost prior to present incumbent; no persons of interest identified.* She signed it. Sanded it. Folded it.

Then she set it unsealed on the corner of the table, weighted beneath the prayer book of her own that she carried everywhere, and told herself she would post it in the morning.

The wind rose all night. By dawn the gale had become a storm, and when Miriam went down to the harbour wall to watch the seas come ashore, the first thing she saw, far out through the driving grey, was the light on the headland — burning steady, burning faithful, sweeping the dark like a promise.

And the second thing she saw, being hauled dripping up the slipway by fishermen shouting themselves hoarse, was wreckage. Broken spars. A ship's boat stove in along one side.

And a woman's coat, sodden and salt-stiff, its collar embroidered with initials in a script no Cornish seamstress would use — *L.B.* — abandoned on the stones as though its owner had walked out of the sea and up toward the light.
