## 3. A Promise Made in Peacetime

The sea chest had not been opened in twenty years, and the key still hung where Edith had always kept it, on the nail behind the pantry door, because Gideon Yarrow had never been able to decide whether leaving it there was a kind of cowardice or a kind of faith.

He took it down now with hands that smelled of lamp oil and cold brass, and the little iron key was heavier than he remembered, or perhaps it was only that everything weighed more at night, when the light turned overhead and the gale leaned its shoulder against the tower and a woman's voice from the village asked questions too sharp to be answered with weather.

*Who is Elias Brandt?*

He sat on the edge of the bed Edith had died in and worked the key into the lock, and the chest exhaled a breath of old canvas and tar and something sweeter beneath it — dried lavender, Edith's doing, tucked in among his past like a blessing she'd known he would one day need. Inside lay the life of a man who did not exist: a merchant seaman's papers in a name that was almost his, letters salt-stiffened into bricks, a coil of hair ribboned with faded silk, and at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft as chamois, a folded page covered in a hand that was not English.

Gideon did not take it out. He only touched it, once, through the cloth, the way a man touches a scar to remind himself the wound was real.

*1873*, said the smell of the chest, and the lighthouse seemed to lean away around him, and the sound of the sea against the rocks became another sea entirely — warmer, stranger, speaking a language of shingle and shallow water under a sky the colour of pewter.

* * *

He had been twenty-two years old and already tired of the sea in the way young men are tired of things they have not yet learned to love honestly. The *Marguerite Ann* was a barque out of Falmouth carrying timber to Königsberg, and Gideon was the youngest hand aboard, all elbows and silence, good at knots and bad at conversation. He remembered the last night as a sequence of small wrongnesses: the barometer falling so fast the glass wept; the mate laughing too loudly at nothing; the water going that particular bruised green that Cornishmen are taught from birth to fear.

They never even got the name of the storm. It came off the Baltic like a thrown door, and by midnight the *Marguerite Ann* was making water faster than any pump could argue with her, and by dawn she was on her beam ends in the shallows off a coast none of them could read — low dunes, dark pines, a church spire thin as a needle — and the sea was taking men off her decks the way a child pulls legs off flies.

Gideon went into the water when the mainmast went. He remembered no decision in it, only the shock of cold closing over him like a book snapping shut, and then a long tumbling grey in which he lost all count of himself, and then — sand. Sand under his hands, coarse and pale, and his body dragged half out of the surf by hands gripping his collar, and a voice above him saying words he didn't understand, repeated patiently, as if language were a rope being thrown again and again until he caught hold of it.

When he could see straight, he saw a broad man kneeling over him in the rain, forty or so, built like a bollard, with a beard going grey at the chin and eyes the colour of the shallows themselves — pale, clear, unimpressed by anything. Behind him stood a girl of perhaps ten, holding a lantern against the wind with both arms, her hair plastered to her face.

That was Elias Brandt. That was Lena, though Gideon did not know her name then, and would not learn it for another twenty years.

Elias hauled him up the beach to a fisherman's cottage with walls a foot thick and a stove that breathed like a living thing, and for three days Gideon hovered between the world and somewhere else while a woman whose face he could barely recall now — Elias's wife, dead these many years, he would learn later, dead the same winter as his own Edith, though neither man knew it yet — fed him broth spoonful by spoonful and spoke to him in slow German he slowly began to answer in fragments. Yes. No. Cold. Thank you. The first words of every friendship, he thought later. Yes, no, thank you, and then one day you find you'd die for the person.

Of the *Marguerite Ann*'s crew of eleven, five came ashore alive. The other six were buried above the dune line in a row, and Elias Brandt stood bareheaded at their graves in the freezing rain although they were strangers and foreigners and Protestants of a strange island sort, and Gideon watched him do it and understood, in the wordless way of the young, that he was looking at a man who kept accounts with the sea and paid them in full.

The authorities wanted the survivors shipped home as soon as the ports cleared. But the storm had closed everything for nine days, and nine days is enough to change what a man is made of.

Gideon spent them working. It was the only gratitude he knew how to shape. He mended nets with Elias in the boat shed while sleet ticked on the roof, learning the German names for knots and finding, to his astonishment, that most of them were cousins of the Cornish ones — the same hands, the same sea, the same old knowledge wearing different clothes. He hauled the boat up the shingle. He split wood until his palms cracked. And in the evenings, when the girl — Lena — practiced her letters aloud by the stove, Elias talked, in a slow careful English learned from sailors, about the sea as a country that belonged to no flag.

"You wreck here," Elias said one night, tapping the floorboards, "you are my guest. I wreck there" — and he pointed west, out through the wall, across two hundred miles of grey water toward Cornwall — "you are mine to shelter. This is how it has always been between men who work the water. Before your Queen, before our Kaiser, before all of it."

"And after all of it?" Gideon asked, because he was twenty-two and the question was in the air that year, in every harbour tavern, in the way customs men looked at foreign hulls. Prussia was rising; everyone could feel the ground tilting toward some future reckoning.

Elias was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind worried the eaves like a dog with a bone.

"After," Elias said finally, "it may be that my country and yours decide they are enemies. Men in fine coats will decide it, in rooms we will never see." His pale eyes settled on Gideon with a weight the boy hadn't earned yet but would carry for the rest of his life. "If ever your people call mine enemy — then remember this kitchen. Remember that I pulled you from the water when your flag meant nothing to me but wet cloth. If that day comes, and you can give shelter without hanging for it, give it. And if I cannot, I will say so plainly, and we will part as friends. That is all an oath between honest men needs."

"I'll swear it," Gideon said. Too fast, the way the young swear.

"No." Elias raised one broad hand. "Not tonight. Tonight you are grateful, and gratitude is a tide — it goes out. Swear it when you are home, when it costs you nothing to remember me. Then it will mean something."

So Gideon waited. He sailed home on a Danish steamer in the spring, and the coast of Cornwall rose out of the mist to meet him, and he walked up from Penzance with salt still in his blood and wrote a letter in careful schoolboy German, and mailed it east with a pressed sprig of sea thrift inside. *I am home. I remember. If ever your people call mine enemy, come to me. I keep a light on the north Cornish coast. It burns for ships, but it will burn for you.*

The reply came four months later, in that same patient hand: a single sheet, and beneath Elias's signature a line copied out twice, once in German, once in English, in a fair round script that must have been the daughter's:

*If ever your people call mine enemy, I will come to you, and you will know me by my need and not my name.*

Two copies. Two halves of one promise. Gideon kept one folded in oilcloth at the bottom of the sea chest. The other Elias kept, and Gideon never doubted it traveled everywhere the sailor went, because that was the kind of man Elias Brandt had shown himself to be.

* * *

They wrote to each other for eleven years. Not often — the sea took up most of both their lives — but steadily, the way a light turns. Gideon told him of Edith, whom he married in 1878, and Elias sent back a carved wooden bird for the wedding, a cormorant with its wings spread, and Edith laughed until she cried at the ugliness of it and set it on the mantel where it sat for thirty years. When Gideon took the post at the light, Elias wrote: *A good trade. A man who keeps a light owes nothing to anyone's war.* When Gideon's daughter was born — lived nine days, nameless, buried beside the chapel — it was Elias's letter that said the only thing that helped: *The sea gives and takes. We are not owed explanations. We are owed each other.*

And then 1884, and the letters stopped. Not gradually. All at once, like a lamp going out. Gideon wrote twice more and received nothing, and pride kept him from writing a third time, and then shame, and then the long years in which a man folds his past smaller and smaller until it fits in a chest, and then a corner of a room, and then nowhere at all.

He learned later — much later, from a shipping notice in a two-day-old paper — that Elias Brandt had lost his boat with all hands in the North Sea in '84. Lost, the notice said. As if the sea were a pocket it fell through.

Now the chest lay open on the bed, and the oilcloth parcel sat in Gideon's lap, and outside the window the gale was building toward something the barometer had begun muttering about, and below in the dark the sea was working itself up into exactly the kind of violence that brought things ashore. Wrecks. Wood. Men. Names.

He unwrapped the page at last and read the fair round script by lamplight, the words he had written to himself a hundred times in the dark of twenty years:

*If ever your people call mine enemy, I will come to you, and you will know me by my need and not my name.*

His people were calling. The whole country was calling, louder every week, in the newspapers and the public houses and the census forms of a quiet-eyed woman with ink on her fingers. Enemy aliens. Detention orders. Names in registers, pages torn out.

And the wind, rising, rising, was coming off the sea from the east — from Prussia, from dunes and pines and a fisherman's kitchen where a girl with a lantern had once held the light steady against a storm.

Gideon sat very still in the turning glow, an old man holding a young man's oath, and listened to the sea come up the rocks below the tower like something answering a knock.
