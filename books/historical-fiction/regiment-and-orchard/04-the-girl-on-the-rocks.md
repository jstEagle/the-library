## 4. The Girl on the Rocks

The storm went out of the world the way a fever breaks — all at once, leaving behind that terrible washed silence in which every small sound seems an accusation.

Gideon stood at the gallery rail with his hands bare against the iron, letting the cold of it climb into his bones. The lamp turned above him, steady as a heartbeat he no longer owned. Below, the sea was still working through its tantrum, heaving long grey shoulders against the headland, but the shriek had gone out of the wind and what remained was only exhaustion — the sea's, the sky's, his own.

He had not slept. The light had demanded everything of him through the small hours: the wick trimmed to a whisper when the gusts came, the clockwork wound twice for fear the mechanism would falter under the strain, the glass wiped down three times where spray had found some seam in the mullions he'd never located in twenty years of looking. His arms ached from it. His eyes burned. And beneath all that ordinary fatigue lay something else, something he had carried up the stairs at midnight like a stone in his boot: Miriam Quill's voice, still asking her quiet questions. *Who taught your wife German hymns, Mr Yarrow?*

He had answered nothing well. He knew that now, in the flat honesty of morning, replaying it. He had given her the look of a man with nothing to hide, and she had looked back at him with the patience of a woman who read registers for a living and knew exactly what a crossed-out name meant.

Below the tower, the rocks were running white.

It took him a moment to understand what he was seeing — not foam, not the ordinary lace of retreating waves, but something caught. Something snagged fast in the black teeth of the ledge beneath the old landing stage, holding against the pull of each wave and then losing ground to it, inch by inch.

A spar, perhaps. Wreckage. The storm had been hungry; there would be flotsam scattered along the coast for days, and the village boys would come out at low tide with their sacks and their knives, cutting free whatever the sea surrendered.

Then the thing in the rocks moved, and Gideon's heart stopped being his own for a full five seconds.

* * *

The descent was worse than the storm had been. The steps cut into the cliff were slick with weed and running water, and twenty years of careful living argued with him at every turn — you are sixty-one years old, Gideon Yarrow, and whatever is down there is beyond your keeping, let the coastguard earn their pay. But his body was already going, boots finding the ledges they remembered from decades ago, one hand on the wet rock, the other carrying the coil of line he'd snatched from the store without deciding to.

The wind came off the sea in long shoves now rather than blows, knocking him sideways, filling his ears with its emptiness. Spray soaked him to the skin before he was halfway down. He did not feel the cold. That frightened him more than anything — the absence of feeling, the body's old sailor trick of spending itself without counting the cost.

She was wedged in a cleft between two boulders, face-down, one arm hooked over the stone above her as if she had been climbing even in unconsciousness. The sea came for her in slow green tongues, licked at her boots, withdrew. Her clothes told him nothing except that they had been fine once — good wool, ruined now, torn across the shoulder where the rocks had taken their toll. Dark hair plastered flat to her skull. Young. Younger than Edith had ever been allowed to be.

He got his arm around her chest beneath the arms and pulled, and the sea pulled back, and for a long ugly moment the two of them fought over her like dogs over a bone. Then a wave lifted them both instead of tearing them apart, and he used it — threw himself backward up the ledge with her dead weight dragging at his spine, and they came clear of the water together, sprawling on the shingle above the reach of the tide.

She was breathing. Barely. A flutter at her lips, shallow as a moth's wing. He pressed his ear to her chest and heard the sea inside her, gurgling and wrong, and he did what his first master had drilled into him forty years gone: rolled her onto her side, struck her back between the shoulder blades until his hand sang. Once. Twice. On the third blow she convulsed and brought up half the Channel, coughing, retching, alive — gloriously, rudely alive, gasping at the sky as though it owed her money.

"There," he heard himself say, uselessly, the way one speaks to anything suffering. "There, now."

Her eyes opened. They were the colour of deep water over sand, and they fixed on his face with a ferocity that had no weakness in it at all — terror, yes, but terror worn like armour, the kind that makes people dangerous rather than helpless. She said something in a language he had not heard spoken aloud in twenty years, and the sound of it went through him like a blade finding an old scar.

*Hilf mir.* Help me.

And then, watching his face, reading whatever passed across it, she said it again in English, each word placed with the care of someone stepping across ice: "Please. You are — the keeper. Please."

"Can you stand?"

"I can walk." She could not. She tried, and her legs folded, and he caught her, and this time she did not fight him. She weighed almost nothing. The sea had been drinking her for hours.

* * *

He got her up the cliff path by degrees, hauling her arm across his shoulders, stopping when her breath went ragged, starting again. Twice she looked back at the water. The third time he understood why: a piece of wreckage was rolling in the surf below, pale and rounded, and it took him too long to recognise it as the bottom of a ship's boat, stove in along one strake. She had come ashore in that. Or she had left a larger vessel in it, in a storm no sane man would launch a dinghy in, which amounted to the same arithmetic: whoever she was, staying aboard had been worse than drowning.

In the scullery he stripped her sodden coat — she clutched it to herself even half-conscious, and something about the fierceness of that grip made him handle it gently, laying it over the rail by the stove rather than dropping it in the copper with the rest — wrapped her in blankets, and set her in the chair closest to the fire. He forced warm milk laced with brandy between her lips while she shuddered and steamed like a kettle.

It was while she sat there, colour crawling back into her face degree by degree, that he saw her hands properly for the first time. Sailors' hands, cracked and salt-burned, but with ink stained into the creases of the fingers — the marks of someone who wrote, who wrote often, who had been writing even now, even fleeing. And around her neck, on a cord, a shape beneath her shirt.

When she saw where his eyes had gone, her whole body changed. The trembling stopped. She sat very straight in the blanket, a young woman suddenly become a courier, an ambassador, the bearer of something, and she reached down inside her collar and drew out the cord.

An oilcloth packet, small as a psalter, tied with tarred twine. It had been against her skin so long the cord had worn a groove in her neck.

"You are Gideon Yarrow," she said. Not a question.

He said nothing. Twenty years of saying nothing rose in him like a wall.

"My father is Elias Brandt." She pronounced the name slowly, watching him the way one watches a fuse burn. "Of Rostock. Master mariner." Her English was good but deliberate, each sentence assembled before it was spoken. "He died in February. In Stettin. Before he died, he gave me this, and told me — if I ever reached England, if I ever reached Cornwall—" Her voice failed on the geography, as if the distance itself were too much to carry. She steadied herself. "He told me there was one man in England bound to me by oath."

"The war," Gideon said. His own voice sounded strange, rusted. "They are detaining Germans. Your countrymen. Your countrywomen." He thought of Miriam Quill's reporting forms. He thought of the blacksmith Hobbs and his dead son and the mood in the harbour inn, thick enough to spread on bread. "You should not have come here."

"I had nowhere else." No self-pity in it. Simply the truth, laid on the table like a bill of lading.

Her fingers worked at the twine. He watched them, and some part of him already knew what was coming, had known since the moment he'd seen her hooked in the rocks like a barnacle, clinging to England with one arm. Some part of him had known for twenty years, and had built a life precisely so that it would never have to know.

She freed the knot and unfolded the oilcloth. Inside was paper, folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth, discoloured along the folds with two decades of handling — his handling first, hers after. On it, in a bold slanted hand he would have known anywhere, in any dark, in any language:

*To Gideon Yarrow, of Cornwall. If ever your people call mine enemy, then come to me, and I will keep you, as on this day you have kept me. My house is yours, my boat is yours, my life is yours. Sworn before God, off the Prussian coast, this 14th day of September, 1873.*

Beneath it, Elias Brandt's signature, and beneath that, younger and less certain, his own: *Gideon Yarrow.*

He had written his half of the oath on a matching page and given it to Elias, and Elias had kept it in his tobacco tin for thirty years, and had put it in his daughter's hands with his dying breath, and had sent her across a sea at war to collect on a promise made by a boy who no longer existed.

"She carries her father's half of the oath," Gideon said aloud, to no one, hearing how absurd it sounded — the ledger-language of it, as if honour were an account that could be settled.

Lena Brandt watched him read, and her eyes never left his face, and he understood that everything hung upon the next thirty seconds: whether the boy who signed that page still lived somewhere inside the keeper of this light, or whether twenty years of careful silence had buried him past all digging.

Gideon looked up at her. He thought of Edith singing hymns whose words she didn't know. He thought of a locked sea chest opened by candlelight only nights ago, and the smell of old rope and salt rising out of it like a voice.

"Lena," he said — her name, which she had not yet given him but which Elias must have written somewhere, because of course he had; Elias never trusted to memory alone. Her eyes went wide and bright.

"You know me."

"Elias spoke of a daughter born the winter after we parted. Lena, after his mother." He closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, the decision was already made, had been made perhaps since 1873, and everything since had only been waiting. "You will stay here. Not in the village — here. No one comes to the light in winter. You will be cold and bored and half-starved on my cooking, and you will hate it, and you will live."

Something broke open in her face — relief so violent it looked like grief, and probably was, grief for her father arriving all at once in a warm kitchen in Cornwall.

Then, through the window above the sink, Gideon saw it: a coastguard cutter standing off the headland, grey against the grey, its glass surely trained on the rocks below where a ship's boat lay stove in the shallows like a confession.

They would find the boat within the hour. They would ask questions by nightfall.

"Stay away from the windows," Gideon said quietly, folding the oilcloth shut. "And God forgive us both — we have work to do before they come."
