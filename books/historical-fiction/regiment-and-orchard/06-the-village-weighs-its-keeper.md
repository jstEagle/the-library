## 6. The Village Weighs Its Keeper

Rumour in Porthmarrin travelled the way the tide did — invisibly at first, then all at once, filling every crevice of the village before anyone could say where it had come from.

Miriam Quill heard it first at Mrs. Trevanion's bakery on Monday morning, delivered between loaves with the flour-dusted nonchalance of a woman who had been waiting all night to say it.

"They're saying there's been lights off the head," Mrs. Trevanion said, leaning across her counter as though the bread itself might listen. "Answered, mind. Yarrow's lamp shows once, and then out on the water — a flash back. Twice now that my brother's boy has seen it, walking home from the net sheds."

"Perhaps a fishing boat signalling its return," Miriam offered.

"At three in the morning? In weather no sane fisherman would put out in?" Mrs. Trevanion straightened, and her voice dropped into something harder. "There's them in this village think Gideon Yarrow's been alone too long up there. Alone men go strange. And strange men—" She did not finish the sentence. She didn't need to. *Strange men and foreigners* hung in the warm air of the bakery like smoke that would not clear.

The word *foreigner* had entered the village vocabulary sometime that autumn without anyone admitting to installing it. It arrived in sermons, half-swallowed; in letters from cousins in Falmouth; in the newspapers that came folded and late on the mail coach, their headlines growing bolder each week. Prussia was not yet an enemy in any way the law could name, but the law lagged behind the feeling, and the feeling moved fast along a coast where every family had a grave bought by the sea and needed someone new to blame for it.

On Wednesday, Mr. Pascoe's letter came down from the census office in Truro, and Miriam read it twice by her lodging-house window while rain stitched patterns on the glass.

*Reports received concerning irregular signals from the Porthmarrin headland,* it began, in Pascoe's careful clerk's hand. *Given your recent survey of the district and your familiarity with the keeper concerned, you are requested to return and make discreet enquiries. Note any anomalies. The Admiralty takes an interest.*

*Note any anomalies.* She thought of the reporting form folded in her satchel — the one she had carried for two weeks now, unsent, the spaces for *name*, *origin*, *nature of irregularity* still blank. She thought of the torn page in the parish register, and of the marriage record that should have sat upon it: *Elias Brandt of Stettin, mariner, to Edith Penhale of Porthmarrin, witnessed by G. Yarrow.* A Prussian sailor married into a Cornish family, twenty years ago, in peacetime, when such things raised eyebrows but not armies. And a witness named Yarrow who had stood up in God's house and put his name beneath a foreign man's happiness.

She had told herself the page meant nothing. She had told herself a great many things.

Now the office wanted her to be its eyes, and the village wanted her to be its ears, and somewhere between the two she was going to have to decide what she herself was.

* * *

She walked up to the lighthouse on Friday afternoon, under a sky the colour of pewter, the wind coming off the Atlantic in long cold shoves that made her lean into the path like a woman wading a river. The gorse bent flat. Far below, the sea was working itself into the mood it kept for November — not yet raging, but rehearsing.

Gideon Yarrow opened the door before she knocked. He had seen her coming, of course; from the gallery he could see every soul on the cliff path for a mile in either direction. That was the thing about lighthouse keepers, she was learning. They watched everything and explained nothing.

"Miss Quill." He stood aside to let her in. "Weather's turning. You'll take tea before the walk back?"

It was phrased as courtesy but shaped like shelter, and she accepted it as both.

The cottage was as she remembered — scrubbed, spare, smelling of lamp oil and peat smoke — but something in it had changed since her last visit, some small displacement of air, the way a room feels when a second heartbeat has recently occupied it. She noticed it before she could name it: a second cup washed and set upside-down on the dresser, still beaded with water. A blanket folded over the settle that had not been folded that way before — too carefully, corners squared by hands accustomed to rope-work rather than housework.

Gideon saw her seeing. His face did nothing at all, which was itself an answer.

"You've heard the talk in the village," he said, pouring tea.

"About the lights." She wrapped her hands around the cup. "I'm asked to enquire. Discreetly, they said, though I've never understood how discretion survives being written down afterward."

"And what will you write?"

"That I found a keeper tending his light and answering no one."

Something moved behind his eyes — not gratitude exactly; more like the flinch of a man handed something heavy and unsure whether to trust the hands offering it. "You'd do that?"

"I'd want to know why first." She set down the cup. "Mr. Yarrow, I found a page torn from the parish register. The marriage of Edith Penhale to Elias Brandt. You witnessed it. I know that much, and I've known it for weeks, and I haven't written that either. But the office is asking questions now, and the navy is asking questions behind the office, and this coast is in a mood to believe the worst about anything it can't explain. So I am asking you plainly: are you signalling someone from that tower?"

He was quiet a long time. The wind pressed at the windows. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and even, the voice of a man laying stones one at a time.

"I made a promise once. Twenty years past. To a man who pulled me out of the sea when my ship went down off the Prussian coast, and asked nothing for it but a word returned if ever our countries stood against each other. I gave him that word." He looked at her steadily. "That's what I can tell you today, Miss Quill. Not all of it. But true, every piece of it."

*Today.* The word rang in the room like a struck bell, and Miriam understood that he was telling her there were other pieces, held back, and that he was choosing — consciously, deliberately — how much of himself to place in her keeping. It should have offended her duty. Instead it settled something in her chest, because he was treating her as a person to be trusted gradually rather than a clerk to be managed.

"I see," she said softly.

"Do you?" Almost a smile. "I doubt I do myself, most days."

She stayed another hour. They spoke of the storm forecast in the shipping news; of the trawler fleet gone up to St. Ives; of his wife, a little — Edith, who had grown roses against the south wall and died too soon and left him a silence he had furnished with routine. He spoke of her the way men speak of amputated limbs, Miriam thought: easily enough, but always aware of the missing weight.

When she rose to leave, the light was failing, and the wind had begun to carry the taste of salt and violence that preceded real weather.

"Mind the path," Gideon said at the door. "And Miss Quill—" He hesitated. Whatever else he meant to say, he swallowed it. "Thank you for asking plainly. Most folk circle for years."

"I find circling wastes candle," she said, and he almost smiled again as she stepped out into the darkening.

* * *

She did not see the coat until she was nearly past the window.

She had come round the cottage on her way to the path — a detour of ten steps past the kitchen end, where lamplight spilled yellow across the cobbles — and there, hanging on the line stretched beneath the eave where the stove's warmth reached it, was a garment drying in the wind.

A coat. A young woman's coat, or a girl's — narrow through the shoulder, darned at the elbow with thread of a slightly wrong colour, the mending done by someone practical and poor. And across the collar, worked into the lining where it showed when the wind flipped the hem, a strip of tape embroidered with a name in a continental hand:

*L. Brandt.*

Miriam stopped so suddenly that her boot scraped stone, and for one absurd moment she ducked back against the wall as though the coat itself might cry alarm.

Lena Brandt. Elias Brandt's daughter, whose name had appeared in the shipping intelligence summaries that had begun arriving unasked on her lodging-house table — *daughter of the late E. Brandt, mariner, reported landed unknown circumstances, detention sought* — a girl hunted by the very machinery Miriam's own reports helped to turn.

The wind lifted the hem again. The embroidered name flashed white, then vanished.

She stood in the gathering dark with her heart going hard against her ribs and did the arithmetic she did not want to do. The second cup. The folded blanket. The promise of twenty years. *If ever your people call mine enemy.* Gideon had told her the truth today — most of it — and the rest had been drying on a washing line six feet from where they drank their tea, trusting the wind and the dusk and her own decency to keep it.

He hadn't lied to her. He had simply not finished. And now she held the ending in her hands like a live coal.

She walked away down the cliff path without looking back, though every nerve in her body wanted to, and the sea boomed somewhere below her like a held breath being slowly released.

* * *

The harbour inn on Saturday evening was hot with bodies and opinion.

Miriam had come to deliver her final census abstracts to the reeve and intended only to hand them over and slip out, but the room had other plans. Word of the navy's interest had leaked — these things always leaked, through coastguard wives and customs men who liked the taste of importance — and Porthmarrin had gathered to chew it.

At the centre of it, inevitable as a capstan, stood Tom Hosken the blacksmith.

Hosken was a big man gone grey at the temples, with forearms like mooring posts and a face that had forgotten how to be anything but grim. His son William had been lost with the *Providence* three winters past — swept from the nets in a squall the lighthouse had given fair warning of, though warning had not saved him — and grief had calcified in the man into something with edges. Lately the newspapers had given that grief a direction.

"Signals from the head," Hosken was saying as Miriam edged along the wall. "Answered from the water. My lad drowned within sight of that tower, and now we learn there's foreign lights being passed back and forth over his grave like—like a shop counter." His fist came down on a table, and the cups jumped. "Yarrow's either a fool or a traitor, and I've known him thirty years and can't tell which, and I'm sick of not knowing. There's a girl gone missing, they say — a German girl, slipped her keepers at Falmouth. Slipping about this coast somewhere. Well, I say find her, and find her fast, before whatever she's part of finds us."

A murmur ran the length of the room — not unanimous, Miriam noted, but heavy. Fear always out-shouted decency in a small space.

"The girl's half-drowned and fatherless," said old Mrs. Chegwin from her corner, mild as milk. "Half these families came over from Brittany themselves, Tom, and nobody hunted us."

"That was different."

"It never is."

But Hosken's voice rolled over hers. "The navy's coming to search the tower, I hear. Good. Let them search. And if there's a foreigner hidden up there, let them take her, and let Yarrow account for himself, because a man who keeps secrets with the sea keeps them against the rest of us." He looked round the room, daring anyone to meet his eye, and most did not — not out of agreement, Miriam thought, but out of the particular Cornish weariness of men who had learned that storms, like arguments, pass sooner if unattended.

Her duty sat in her like a swallowed stone. *Report anomalies.* She had an anomaly the size of a coat on a washing line. She had a form in her satchel and a name — two names — to fill it with, and a government behind her that would call her thorough and perhaps even promote her for it.

She also had Gideon Yarrow's voice in her ear: *a promise made in peacetime.* She had the torn register page, and the knowledge that he had stood witness for a foreign man's marriage when it cost him nothing but ink, and now stood witness for the man's daughter when it might cost him everything. She had the image of Lena Brandt's darned elbow, mended with the wrong-coloured thread by hands that had nothing else.

Duty said speak. Conscience, that older and quieter officer, said wait.

Outside, the wind had risen to a steady roar, and the first spit of true rain came flat against the inn windows. Down the harbour, the sea was already white-lipped. The barometer at the reeve's office had fallen all day like a stone dropped down a well.

Miriam buttoned her coat and stepped out into the dark. Above the headland, the lighthouse beam swung round, steady as a heartbeat, sweeping the boiling edge of the world — and she found herself counting the interval, willing it to be nothing more than what it seemed.

Behind her, through the closing door, she heard Tom Hosken call for another pint and announce to the room that he'd be watching the tower himself, come what may.

Above her, the light swept on. And far out in the black water, faint as a struck match, something answered.
