## 7. Interrogation at the Harbour Inn

Gideon saw her come up the harbour road before she reached the inn — a figure in a grey coat bent against the wind like a pen nib pressed too hard to paper. Miriam Quill. He had known, in the way a keeper knows weather before it arrives, that she would come. The village had been murmuring for three days: lights answered from the sea, a stranger's coat glimpsed where no stranger should be, and now the navy's launch anchored off the point, its grey hull sitting on the water like a verdict.

He had come to the Harbour Inn to be seen. That was the whole of his strategy, if he could call it one — to sit in the public bar with a pint of Shipwright's ale going flat before him, visible, unremarkable, a man doing what widowed keepers did on wet afternoons. Let them look at him. Let them find nothing.

But Miriam Quill was not looking for nothing. She came through the door shaking rain from her hat, and her eyes found him across the smoke-hazed room as surely as his beam found a hull in fog.

She did not approach at once. She ordered tea from Mrs. Tregose, who poured it with the elaborate deafness of an innkeeper's wife determined to hear everything and admit to nothing, and then she carried her cup across the flagstones and sat down opposite him without asking, the way people do when they have already rehearsed the conversation in their heads.

"Mr. Yarrow."

"Miss Quill." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the table. "Bad crossing?"

"I walked." She set down her cup without drinking. "I wanted time to think about how to ask you something, and I still haven't found the way, so I'll simply ask it. Who is staying at your lighthouse?"

The fire popped in the grate. Somewhere behind him a dominoes game clattered on, indifferent. Gideon kept his face still — twenty years of keeping a light had taught him stillness; the sea tested it nightly, and the sea was a harder examiner than any census woman.

"No one's staying at my lighthouse," he said. "It's not the sort of place folk stay. It's the sort of place folk leave."

"You know what I mean."

"I know what you've been told, maybe. Lights answered from the water. I'd ask who told you, but half this village would swear they saw Moses parting Mount's Bay last week if it meant a bit of excitement." He drank. The ale was indeed flat. "My light burns every night it's needed and answers nothing but God's weather. You can write that down if you like. I expect you're good at writing things down."

Something flickered across her face — not anger. Hurt, perhaps, or the ghost of it. She folded her hands on the table, and he noticed they were ink-stained at the nails, the hands of someone who wrote past the end of the working day.

"That's fair," she said quietly. "I deserve that, maybe. But I'm not here as the census woman, Mr. Yarrow. I'm here because in four days — three, now — a naval party is coming ashore to search the headland. Every outbuilding, every store room, the tower itself. There's a list of names they're checking against. Brandt is on it."

The name went through him like cold water through a boot seam. He did not move. He willed himself not to move, and knew even as he did it that the not-moving was itself an answer, and that she saw it, and that she had known it would be.

"Elias Brandt is dead," Gideon said. "Drowned off Heligoland two years since. Whatever list they've got is chasing a dead man's shadow."

"The list has more than one name on it." Her voice dropped lower, beneath the dominoes and the fire. "A daughter. Lena Brandt. Twenty-three years old. Detained at Falmouth in the spring under the order, escaped custody six weeks ago. They think she made for the coast. They think—" she hesitated, and then finished it anyway, gently, the way you break bad news to a family, "—they think someone on this coast is helping her."

Gideon looked into his glass. In its amber depths he saw, absurdly, the lamp room: Lena asleep in the keeper's chair with her father's oilcloth packet clutched to her chest like a child with a doll, her hair drying in wild salt-stiffened curls, her wrists still ringed with the marks of the manacles she had somehow slipped. *You promised him,* she had said, or rather the packet had said, Elias's own hand on the oilcloth, the ink faded to the brown of old blood: *if ever your people call mine enemy, shelter him as I sheltered you.* And beneath it, in a younger, shakier script: *He kept his half. Papa said you were a man who keeps his.*

"I can't help you," Gideon said. "And I'll tell you why, Miss Quill, and then you can decide what kind of person I am. I don't know where this girl is. But I know that if she came to my door half-drowned, with soldiers hunting her, I'd have taken her in. Any man who'd ever been wrecked on a foreign shore and lived would. So if your naval gentlemen want to search my lighthouse, let them search. They'll find what I've got: Edith's chair, Edith's roses gone woody for the winter, forty years of logbooks, and dust enough to embarrass a museum."

Miriam studied him for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the inn's sign, and the harbour water slapped the quay stones in the rhythm of a thing counting.

"You're lying to me," she said at last. Not cruelly. Almost wonderingly. "And you're very good at it, and that frightens me more than the truth would."

* * *

Miriam walked back along the cliff path with her collar up and her mind running ahead of her feet like a dog that won't heel.

She had lied too, of course. That was the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had told him the search was in four days — three — because she wanted to see his face when she said it, and his face had told her everything: not guilt exactly, but *weight*. A man bracing under a load. And she had not told him the other thing, the thing that had made her walk two hours in the rain rather than send a letter or stay away altogether.

Her report was written. It sat in her satchel right now, against her hip, in her neatest clerk's hand: *anomalies noted, register page mutilated, witness 'G. Yarrow' of record 1873, recommend inquiry.* All true. All verifiable. All of it the honest work of an honest officer of the census, done at the request of superiors who had begun using words like *enemy* the way other men used *hello.*

And beneath it, folded twice, was the page itself — or a tracing of it, made by candlelight in the vestry while the vicar's back was turned. *Elias Brandt, mariner, of Stettin, Prussia,* and beside the names, in the witness column, *Gideon Yarrow.* A marriage. Twenty years ago. To a woman whose name had been inked over so thoroughly that only the first letters survived — E., and then a scrape, and then nothing.

Edith. His wife had been the record. His wife had been Prussian-born, or German-born, whatever the difference amounted to when the newspapers wanted a word for it — and he had scratched her out of the book himself, or paid someone to, and built a quiet life on top of the erasure the way you build a cottage on a filled-in mine shaft.

She had known it for five days now and had written nothing of it in her report, and every morning she woke and the report was still there and the omission was still there, growing heavier, like snow loading a roof.

At the bend above the cove she stopped. Below her, the lighthouse stood grey and patient against the bruising sky, and she thought: *he trusts me with the oath but not with the girl. He gave me the story because he judged I'd already found it, and he kept back the one thing that would hang him, and he thinks I don't know that too.*

She should feel deceived. Instead she felt — included. Admitted, grudgingly, into a conspiracy of silence, which was the strangest invitation she had ever received and the only one lately that hadn't made her skin crawl.

"They'll hang him for it," she said aloud to the wind. "Or worse. Deportation orders, the assizes, a man of sixty transported like cargo because he married a girl from Stettin and kept his word to her brother-in-arms." The wind took the words and shredded them. She thought of the blacksmith Penhale at the public meeting, his voice cracking on his drowned son's name, and understood with terrible clarity that reason would not save Gideon Yarrow. Grief would be the judge, and grief does not read registers.

She thought of her own form. One line, one truthful line, and the machinery would start turning, and it would not stop for oaths or orchards or twenty years of clean light. Machinery never did. She had watched it turn in the office at Truro, great paper wheels grinding ordinary men into categories.

Miriam Quill had spent her whole life believing that records were sacred — that a thing written down became a small immortal truth. Now she held the power to write a truth that would kill a good man, and she understood, finally, what the torn page had cost him: that sometimes the holiest act is a hole in the paper.

She opened her satchel. Rain spattered the leather. For a long moment she stood there with the report in her hands, the wind trying to take it, and below her the lighthouse lamp lit for the evening watch — one white flash, steady, faithful, sweeping the dark water for anyone the storm might deliver.

Then she struck a match, shielded it with her body, and touched the flame to the corner of the page.

The paper caught reluctantly, damp and unwilling, and she held it until the fire reached her fingers, until the last legible word — *Yarrow* — curled and went to ash, and she scattered what remained over the cliff edge into the sea.

When she raised her head, she saw, impossibly far below and impossibly clearly, a light in the keeper's cottage window that was not the lamp — a small yellow rectangle, a second presence moving behind the glass. Then it was gone, snuffed quick, the way you smother a candle when a knock comes.

Miriam stood in the rain with burnt fingers and a heart going like a drum, and made her choice complete and irrevocable, though she said it only to herself:

Three days. Whatever was going to happen at that lighthouse would happen in three days — and when it did, she intended to be standing on Gideon Yarrow's side of the door.
