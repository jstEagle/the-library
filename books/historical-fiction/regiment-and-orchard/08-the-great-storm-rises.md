## 8. The Great Storm Rises

The barometer had been falling for two days, and by the morning of the ninth of October it had fallen so far that the needle lay against the pin at the bottom of its arc like a bird that had given up beating against glass. Gideon Yarrow stood in the kitchen with his coffee going cold and watched the needle and thought, with a strange calm, that the sea had finally decided to come collect.

He had lived on this headland twenty-two years. He had seen storms peel slates off the cottage roof and throw them into the orchard like playing cards. He had seen the spray go over the lamp room — a hundred and forty feet of climb for the water, and it went anyway, salt freezing on the glazing until he'd had to chip it clear with a chisel. But he had never seen the barometer touch that pin. No one living had.

Behind him, Lena Brandt was grinding coffee beans because she could not bear to be idle, and the sound of her doing something so ordinary — something Edith used to do, in this same kitchen, at this same window — made his chest ache in a way he did not examine.

"The glass says we should go to the mainland," she said.

"The glass says a great many things." He drank the cold coffee anyway. "The navy's launch won't cross today, whatever the glass says. The search is delayed."

"Delayed is not cancelled."

"No," he agreed. "Delayed is not cancelled."

She set down the grinder and looked at him across the kitchen, this girl with her father's jaw and her father's stubbornness worn thinner by what she'd carried to get here. The oilcloth packet sat in her coat pocket where it always sat; he had seen her touch it a dozen times a day, the way a man touches a compass he no longer trusts but cannot put down.

"If they come," she said, "and find me here—"

"They won't find you here."

"Gideon." She almost never used his name. When she did, it landed like a stone dropped down a well. "I have run from them in Bremen, and from them in Hull, and from them in a ship's hold between those two places. I know how it ends when they come. What I do not know is how it ends for *you*."

He looked out the window instead of answering. Below the headland, the sea had turned the colour of gunmetal and was already working itself into white horses a mile offshore, though the wind was still coming out of the southeast and had not yet found its true voice. By nightfall it would swing round to southwest and come at them with everything the Atlantic had been saving up since September.

"There's a place," he said at last. "Under the oil store, there's an old powder magazine from before my time. Brick walls three feet thick, door of iron. It smells of lamp oil and rust, and you'll hate it, and you'll stay in it if I tell you to stay, and that is not a request."

"I am not your daughter to order about."

"You are Elias Brandt's daughter," Gideon said, "and I gave my word to Elias Brandt, and while my word holds, you will do as I say. That is how promises work. They are not comfortable."

Something moved in her face — not quite a smile, but the shadow one leaves behind. She picked up the grinder again.

By noon the wind had arrived in earnest. By two o'clock the first gusts came over the headland flat and hard, without warning, like fists through a doorway, and Gideon walked the gallery rail with a rope around his waist checking every fastening twice. The gulls had gone inland hours ago, all at once, the whole sky emptying of them, which was the oldest and worst kind of weather-wisdom. Even the cormorants had abandoned the rocks below the light.

At half past three, the *Providence* came apart on the Gull Rocks.

He saw it happen from the lamp room. She was a small steam trawler, Fleetwood-registered by her funnel markings, wallowing south with the storm already behind her and her steering evidently gone — he could see her circling, wide drunken circles, before the swell took her broadside and drove her in. The Gull Rocks stood six feet above low water a quarter mile east of the light, black teeth in the white. The trawler struck them bow-first with a sound he felt through the tower's masonry rather than heard, and then the sea folded over her, and when it drew back she was broken amidships and listing, and men were moving on her deck like ants on a drowning log.

Gideon counted. Six figures. Maybe seven. One was already gone — swept off before the stranding, or lost in the break. The others clung to the after section, which was the higher, and which would hold perhaps another hour against the sea that was marching toward it.

His hands were moving before his mind caught up. Rocket apparatus — breeches buoy, line, faking box, the whole drill he had rehearsed in fair weather on the grass inside the compound until the villagers laughed about the mad keeper firing lines at seagulls. He got the Lyle gun to the cliff top above the wreck, the best firing position, and found the wind so violent he had to kneel and shield the rocket with his own body to keep it dry enough to fire.

The line went out and fell short, dragged sideways by a gust that nearly took him with it. He reloaded. His fingers were cut and bleeding and he could not feel most of them. The second shot carried true and dropped across the trawler's stern, and a man — young, bearded, half-naked with cold — hauled it aboard and bent it to the strongest point he could find, and Gideon began to haul the tail block out with the whip line, and for four minutes the world narrowed to rope and water and the count of waves.

Then he saw the face.

The man at the buoy harness, being swung off the wreck hand over hand through the surf, was looking up at him as he came across. And even at that distance, even through sheets of spray, even with twenty years of weather between then and now, Gideon knew him. Tom Pellowe. Harbour boy, once. Apprentice to old Treloar the fisherman. A boy who had sat on Gideon's own doorstep eating bread and honey while Edith told him stories. A boy who had gone to sea at fifteen and come back a man, and who had — Gideon remembered it with a coldness worse than the storm — been drinking in the Harbour Inn on the night in September when talk of enemy aliens ran hot, and whose brother-in-law worked the coastguard patrol.

Tom Pellowe reached the cliff top. Gideon cut him out of the harness, wrapped him in oilskins, and Tom looked up at him with blue lips and streaming eyes and said, thickly:

"Keeper. There's five more. Four more." He coughed seawater. "Captain's still aboard. And—" his eyes went past Gideon, up the path toward the lighthouse compound, and back again, and something flickered in his face that might have been nothing at all, "—you're alone up here, are you?"

"Alone as ever," Gideon said, and hauled the next rocket forward.

* * *

They came across one at a time. A big man called Curnow who wept openly with relief. Two brothers named Tregaskis, grey-faced and silent, holding each other upright. The youngest, a deckhand of maybe seventeen, who had to be tied into the harness because his hands were frozen shut. And last the captain, Harleigh, who refused to leave until the ship's dog was sent first, and who crossed last of all with the animal under one arm like a sack of coal.

Six men alive. One lost, and Gideon said the words over the sea where the man had been, quietly, the old words, because someone should.

The survivors were crowded into the lamp room's lower store, wrapped in every blanket the station owned, fed on bread and brandy and the stew Lena had made that morning from nothing much. Lena had cooked it herself and left it in the passage, refusing to show herself, and Gideon had served it as his own work, and no one questioned it — why would they? A lighthouse keeper lived alone; everyone knew a keeper's cooking was a sin against food.

But Tom Pellowe kept watching the door.

It was small things. The way his eyes tracked to the passage when the stew smell came. The way he noted the second cup on the shelf — Gideon had missed it, one cup too many washed and drying, and Tom's gaze had rested on it a half-second longer than a half-second ought to last. The way he asked, casually, into the general noise: "Heard tell there's been lights answered from the sea off this headland. Navy's keen on it. You hear anything, Keeper?"

"I hear the fog signal and the wind," Gideon said. "Nothing else answers me out here."

"Mm," said Tom Pellowe.

Outside, the storm reached its full voice. The wind came round southwest and hit the tower like a solid thing, and the whole structure leaned and groaned, and the light above them turned and turned and turned, sweeping the blackness, and every time the beam passed the windows the survivors' faces went white and gold, white and gold, like figures in a magic lantern show.

Gideon stepped out onto the gallery once, roped, just to see. The sea had come right up the headland. Waves were striking the base of the tower a hundred feet below him and throwing spray *over* him, over the lamp room, over everything — the air itself was half seawater, and the sound was beyond sound, a continuous concussion like the world's heart failing. Somewhere out in that chaos other ships were fighting for their lives, and the light burned, and would burn, and that was the whole of his religion.

When he came back in, soaked through, Tom Pellowe was waiting by the stove. Alone. The others slept.

"Keeper," Tom said, low. "I'm going to say a thing, and you're going to listen, because I owed your missus bread-and-honey and I've never forgotten it." His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "There was a girl's coat hanging in your passage. Good wool coat. Not Cornish cut. And there's a name been passed round the harbour these three weeks — a foreign woman, landed off a wreck up-coast, wanted by the patrol. Captain Harleigh saw the coat too. He's a careful man, but he's a tired man, and tired men talk when they get home."

Gideon said nothing. There was nothing left to say that wasn't surrender.

"My brother-in-law's patrol comes tomorrow, storm or no storm, if the navy doesn't beat them to it." Tom stood, joints cracking, an old man's sounds in a young man's body. "I haven't said a word. Yet. I don't know yet what I'll say tomorrow. But you should know, Keeper, that the sea took my cousin tonight — took him off that wreck before we ever touched the rocks — and grief makes a man want someone to blame, and there's folk ashore who'll feed him a name to blame." He paused at the door to the lower store. "You've been good to us. I remember who's been good to me. But I can't speak for Harleigh, and I can't speak for the brothers Tregaskis, and God help me, Keeper, I can't speak for myself tomorrow either."

He went down the stairs. Gideon stood alone by the stove, listening to the storm try to take the tower apart stone by stone, and understood that the silence he had built his life upon had finally, completely, run out of rooms to hide in.

Above him the light turned. Below the headland the sea roared. And somewhere in the iron-door dark of the powder magazine, a Prussian sailor's daughter waited, trusting a promise made fifty years' worth of miles and a whole lifetime ago — while under his own roof slept six witnesses, one of whom would betray her by breakfast.

Gideon banked the fire, checked the clockwork of the light, and began, in the howling dark, to plan how to save her from the very men he had just saved.
