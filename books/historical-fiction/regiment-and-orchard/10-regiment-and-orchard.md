## 10. Regiment and Orchard

The inquiry was held in the harbourmaster's office on the last Tuesday of March, in a room that smelled of tar, cold tea, and official displeasure. Three men sat behind a trestle table borrowed from the inn: a lieutenant of the Royal Navy with a face like a shut ledger, a magistrate from Truro whose collar had been starched into a permanent expression of judgment, and a clerk who wrote everything down twice, once in ink and once, it seemed to Gideon, in disapproval.

Gideon stood before them with his cap in his hands and told the truth.

He told it plainly, in the order it had happened, because he had learned — twenty years ago, and again that winter — that truth told crooked will always find a way to straighten itself at your expense. He told them about Elias Brandt, and the wreck of the *Katharina* off the Prussian coast in '73, and the oath sworn between two young men with salt still drying on their skins. He told them about Lena, pulled half-drowned from the rocks below the light, and about the storm, and the trawler, and the line run out through seas that should have killed him three times over. He did not tell them what Miriam Quill had done, because that was hers to tell or not tell, and she had chosen silence, and he would honor it as she had honored him.

The lieutenant asked most of the questions. They came in a flat rhythm, like waves against a seawall.

"You knowingly harboured an enemy alien."

"I knowingly kept a promise," Gideon said. "She was also a survivor of a shipwreck, which the law says is to be sheltered first and questioned after. I did both. In that order."

"She was German."

"Her father saved my life when England and Prussia were friends. Nations change their minds more often than men do."

The magistrate's pen stopped. The clerk looked up. For a moment the only sound was the wind worrying at the window latch, and somewhere below, the gulls arguing over the harbour refuse.

"You understand," the lieutenant said at last, "that we cannot retain a keeper who has demonstrated — however honorably — a capacity for disobedience."

"I understand it perfectly," Gideon said. "I counted the cost before I paid it. That's what an oath is."

* * *

They stripped him of the post by letter, which arrived eleven days later, written in the same clerk's careful hand. Keeper of the light at Carn Du, discharged for cause, gratuity forfeited, thanks expressed for services rendered during the rescue of the trawler *Providence*, four lives saved. The sentence and the gratitude lived together in the same paragraph, comfortable as old enemies sharing a bench.

Gideon read it standing in the lamp room, then folded it and put it in Edith's prayer book, where the crossed-out name lived. It seemed the right shelf for it.

What surprised him — what surprised everyone — was the blacksmith.

Trembath had come down to the lighthouse in February with frost in his beard and grief in his fists, demanding to know why foreigners were being hidden while English boys drowned. He had stood in Gideon's kitchen with the storm still howling outside and called him a traitor to his face. Now, three weeks later, he climbed the headland path again, slower, hat in hand, and stood in the doorway of the cottage without coming in.

"They're saying you'll be turned out," he said.

"They're right. Turned out in June, once they've trained the new man."

Trembath worked his jaw the way he worked iron, slowly and with great heat building behind it. "My boy went down off the Lizard in '88," he said. "Eleven years I've hated the sea for it. And then I watched you go out into that storm with a line round your waist like a man walking into his own funeral, and I watched you bring four of ours home." He stopped. Started again. "I said things in this kitchen I'd take back if the words were nails and I had the pulling power."

"The words were true enough," Gideon said. "I did hide her."

"Aye. And the sea threw her at your door half-dead, and you didn't let her drown because a paper told you to." Trembath put his hat on, which seemed to settle something in him. "There's a meeting at the inn Thursday. The inquiry men will read their findings. There'll be some there to speak for you, if speaking does any good."

"It won't."

"No. But it'll do some good anyway." He turned to go, then paused on the threshold. "Your roses are dead, keeper. The ones along the south wall. Storm took them."

"They were Edith's," Gideon said. "I know. I've been meaning to do something about that."

* * *

Thursday's meeting filled the inn to its beams. The lieutenant read the findings in a voice pitched for parade grounds, and when he reached the phrase *discharged for cause*, a chair scraped, and old Trembath rose to his feet like something being forged upright.

He did not make a speech. He was not a speech-making man. He simply stood there, huge and grey-bearded, and said: "Four of our men are alive because this keeper kept his word to a dead foreigner. I'd sooner be kept alive by such a man than governed by ones who keep nothing but papers." Then he sat down, and the room made a noise that was not quite applause but was louder, in its way — the low rumble of a village deciding something it had already decided.

It changed nothing officially. The discharge stood. But the innkeeper refused to charge the navy men for their rooms, the fisherfolk declined, with elaborate courtesy, to sell them fresh herring, and by Friday the lieutenant had developed an urgent need to be back in Falmouth.

* * *

Lena stayed.

There had never been a decision about it, exactly. The spring came on mild and silvered, and the new keeper — a brisk young man from Porthleven named Curnow — arrived to learn the light, and found that the cottage behind it was still occupied by the discharged keeper, and that the discharged keeper's garden had acquired a second gardener, a young woman who spoke with an accent like water over stones and who could prune an apple tree better than anyone he had ever seen.

Curnow, to his lasting credit, asked no questions. He had heard about the storm. Everyone had heard about the storm.

Lena worked the orchard behind the cottage — an abandoned thing, half-wild, planted by some keeper's wife fifty years gone and left to the gulls and the canker since. Through April and May she cleared it, cutting out the dead wood and opening the crowns to the light, and the trees, given one season of attention after decades of neglect, repaid her extravagantly. Blossom came late that year but thick, the whole orchard going white and pink at once as if the headland had caught a cloud and decided to keep it.

Gideon watched her from the kitchen window more mornings than he admitted even to himself. She moved among the trees the way Edith used to move among her roses — talking to them, low and constant, in whatever language plants prefer. Some days she spoke German under her breath, and the blossoms did not seem to mind.

In the evenings they sat either side of the stove, and she taught him words, and he taught her knots, and between them they rebuilt the small grammar of a household. She called him *Onkel Gideon* sometimes, testing the word, and he let it stand, though it opened something in his chest that ached in the particular way of things healing.

"You'll sail in the autumn," he said one evening. Not a question. Her cousins were in Baltimore; the passage was booked through a shipping agent in Penzance who owed Miriam Quill a favor nobody discussed.

"In September. When the apples come." She looked into the fire. "You could come too. There is room in the world, Onkel. America is very large and very bad at asking questions."

Gideon smiled and shook his head slowly. "This is my ground. I buried two people here — the one in the churchyard and the one I pretended to be. A man only gets so much burying in a lifetime. I mean to stay and let both of them rest."

* * *

In June, when the discharge took effect and Curnow lit his first lamp alone, Gideon took the spade down to the south wall of the cottage where Edith's roses had died in the storm. He dug out the blackened roots carefully, the way you dig around anything that has been loved, and laid them to rest beneath the wall.

Then he walked down to the orchard, where Lena had left a space open — he had noticed it weeks ago, a gap in her planting plan she had never explained — and there he set out six rose bushes bought from a nursery in Falmouth with the last of his gratuity money. Damasks, the old kind, the sort Edith had grown, heavy-headed and scented like honey and memory.

"What are those?" Lena asked, coming up the path with dirt on her hands and blossom petals caught in her hair.

"Roses. My wife grew them along the south wall. I thought they'd rather live here, among your father's daughter's trees." He tamped the earth firm around the roots. "Regiment and orchard, eh? The whole coast used to be regimented — forts, patrols, lights ordered about by the Admiralty. And underneath all of it, this. Trees. Roses. People keeping promises." He sat back on his heels. "I used to think identity was the thing you hide. Twenty years I hid mine so well I nearly lost it. Turns out it's the thing you honor. Everything else is just weather."

Lena knelt beside him in the warm dirt and helped him plant, and neither of them spoke for a long while, because some silences protect people, and some simply hold them gently until they're ready.

The summer came on. The roses took. And in September, when the apple harvest hung heavy and gold in the orchard behind the keeper's cottage, a ship would sail from Falmouth carrying a young woman named Brandt toward a new life — and would leave behind an old keeper who had finally, at sixty-one, stopped hiding, and begun to grow.
