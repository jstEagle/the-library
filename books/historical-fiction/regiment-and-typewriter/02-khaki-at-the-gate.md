## 2. Khaki at the Gate

They came in the night, and Mara knew it first by the sound of the sea changing.

She had lived her whole life within earshot of Harrow Point's harbor, and she could tell you what the water meant in its sleep — the soft slap of a calm tide against the pilings, the long drag of a swell after a storm. But past two o'clock on the fourth of October, the sound was wrong. Engines. Dozens of them, idling low out beyond the point, where no engines had any business idling.

She sat up in bed with the ledger already in her hands before she understood why. She had taken to keeping it under her pillow like other girls kept letters from sweethearts. The pages were cool and familiar under her thumb. *Harrow Point, requisition* — that word again, marching through her mind in a colonel's voice.

By the time she reached the window, the first landing boats were grinding up onto the shingle below the seawall, and men were pouring out of them in lines so straight they looked poured rather than born. Khaki in the dark, hundreds deep, silent as a tide coming in. Nobody shouted. That was the worst part. Armies in stories announced themselves with drums. This one simply arrived, the way weather arrives, the way grief arrives.

Down the hall, her mother's door opened. Edith Whitlock did not run to windows. Mara heard her light the lamp in the study, heard the particular firmness of her mother's footsteps on the stairs, and by the time Mara came down, Edith was standing at the front door in her dressing gown with her arms folded, watching the green fill up with men and crates as though she were taking attendance.

"Well," she said. "They've come for our town."

"They can't just take it," Nell said from the top of the stairs, fourteen years old and clutching the banister. "There's laws."

"There are laws," Edith said, "and there are wars, and only one of them is being well managed at present. Go back to bed, Nell. Both of you." But she didn't move from the doorway, and neither did Mara, and together they stood watch like a pair of lighthouse keepers while the sun came up gray over a green that had stopped belonging to anyone.

* * *

By seven the churchyard had barbed wire around it.

Mara drove out at half past eight to see for herself, because seeing was her trade now, whether she'd chosen it or not. The motorcar coughed along the coast road, past cottages whose curtains twitched as she passed — everyone awake, everyone watching, nobody speaking. At St. Brendan's, the church where Mara had been christened, where Tom had once been sick into his hat during the Christmas service and never entirely lived it down, soldiers were driving iron stakes into the grass between the graves. The wire went up spool by spool, glinting, cheerful almost, the way terrible things often look in morning light.

An old man stood at the gate. Mr. Penhale, the verger, seventy if he was a day, holding his ring of keys against his chest like a breastplate.

"They've locked us out of God's house," he said when Mara stopped the car. His voice was very small. "Says this sign here." He pointed to a board staked into the verge: **RESTRICTED AREA — MILITARY GOVERNANCE IN EFFECT**. The ink was still wet enough to smudge.

"Have they said how long?" Mara asked, though she knew the answer would be either nothing or a lie.

"Nobody's said anything, miss. They don't talk *to* you, see. They talk *near* you." He turned the keys over in his hands. "Your mother will be wanting to know about this."

"My mother," Mara said, "will want to know about everything," and she wrote the time, the regimental number off the gatepost, and Penhale's name in her ledger right there in the motorcar, engine ticking, because names were the only currency that couldn't be requisitioned. Yet.

The green itself had transformed overnight into something between a market and a wound. Crates stacked in pyramids. Mules tethered along the war memorial, which someone had draped with a tarpaulin — whether out of respect or embarrassment, Mara couldn't decide. A cook wagon. A field telephone line strung from the lighthouse road to a tent that hadn't existed yesterday. Men moved everywhere with the unhurried urgency of people who intended to stay.

And the harbor: closed. A chain now ran across the mouth of it, low in the water, with a patrol boat riding at anchor beside it like a dog tied to a post. Two fishing crews stood on the quay looking at their own boats the way men look at a locked door to a house they still hold the mortgage on. The *Ellen May*, the *Providence*, the *Sweet Alice* — bobbing uselessly behind the wire, full of fish nobody would be allowed to catch.

Mara sat in the car with her notebook open and did the arithmetic she was becoming known for. Four hundred souls in Harrow Point. One church, wired shut. One harbor, chained. And arriving even now, a second column of lorries on the north road, each one stamped with markings she copied carefully, because a name was a name whether it belonged to a person or a regiment.

Information, her mother liked to say, is the one weapon they cannot confiscate, because they never believe a woman is carrying it.

Mara believed they'd learn otherwise.

* * *

Edith did not wait for the afternoon meeting. She held it on the steps of the requisitioned schoolhouse at noon, with the troops' cook smoke drifting across the green and the whole town gathering the way water gathers toward a drain.

She wore her good coat, the navy one with the buttons she'd had recast from suffragette metal, and she stood on the third step so that every head in the crowd had to tilt slightly upward to hear her. She'd been doing this for nine years; Mara could have conducted the speech herself from muscle memory. But today her mother's voice had an edge on it that Mara hadn't heard since the ugly spring in London, and the crowd felt it too. They went quiet faster than usual.

"They have taken our harbor without asking our leave," Edith said. "They have padlocked our church and strung wire over our dead. I ask you — is this what we are fighting Germany for? So that Englishmen may do to English towns what we are told the enemy does to Belgium?"

A murmur went around like wind through barley.

"I am told there is a war on," Edith continued, "and I say, yes — I have given my son to the idea of service, my daughter to the work of organizing, and my health to the platform, and I do not begrudge one hour of it. But I will not stand quietly in my own village while the government decides that the people of Harrow Point are furniture — to be shifted, stored, or sat upon as convenient!" She raised one finger. "We have petitioned. We have marched. We have been jailed and starved and force-fed, and we are *still here*, and I tell you the army will learn what the Cabinet has learned: the women of this coast do not evaporate because a man with a clipboard wishes it!"

The cheer that went up startled gulls off the schoolhouse roof. Old Mrs. Tregowan was crying. The fisherman Cobbett lifted his cap and put it back on again twice, unable to settle on a gesture adequate to the moment. Even Nell, who pretended to be bored by all of it, was on her toes.

Mara stood at the edge of the crowd and did not cheer, because she was doing what she always did: counting. Forty-three faces she could name outright. Another dozen she knew by trade if not by Christian name. She marked who stood nearest her mother — those would be questioned first. She marked the three shopkeepers conspicuously absent. She marked, near the back, a face she did not know at all: a young officer in a immaculate uniform, cap squared, gloved hands clasped behind him, watching the speech the way an auditor watches a ledger.

He wasn't listening. That was what unsettled her. Everyone else in the square was hanging on Edith's words; this man was reading the crowd itself, moving his gaze from face to face in slow, methodical sweeps, and more than once the sweep paused on Mara.

She looked away first. Then, furious at having looked away first, she looked back.

He saw her do it. Something at the corner of his mouth moved — not quite a smile, more like an entry being made in a book.

* * *

He was still there at four o'clock.

Mara noticed it from the upstairs window, having gone up to fetch Nell's escaped cat, and she might have missed him altogether except that everything else on the lane had changed and he had not. All day the khaki had flowed and eddied around the village, but this officer stood motionless at the corner where the coast road met the lane to their gate, apparently studying the sea, and had been studying it, Mara would have sworn, for the better part of an hour in precisely the direction of their house.

She watched him from behind the curtain until the watching began to feel like a kind of surrender. Then she went downstairs, put on her coat, and walked out to meet him, because Edith Whitlock's daughter did not peer through curtains; she presented herself.

Up close he was younger than the uniform suggested — twenty-eight perhaps, maybe thirty, with the kind of neat, unremarkable face that would make him excellent at his work whatever that work was. His boots were polished to a shine that spoke either of vanity or discipline, and she suspected both.

"Afternoon," she said.

"Miss Whitlock." He touched his cap. "Captain Renshaw. Fourth Army Liaison." Liaison, Mara noted, to what, he did not say. "Fine view from your gate."

"It's a fine view from most gates in Harrow Point."

"Yes." He considered the sea with what appeared to be genuine pleasure. "I imagine you know everyone in it. The town, I mean. Everyone's business, everyone's comings and goings." His eyes came back to hers, mild and patient. "A driver for the suffrage movement, I'm told. You must cover a great many miles."

"A great many miles," Mara agreed. "Mostly to meetings. We're a tedious lot — resolutions, minutes, subscriptions. You'd find nothing in our papers but quorum counts."

"I shouldn't dream of looking at your papers, Miss Whitlock." He smiled then, fully, and it was somehow worse than not smiling. "I only wanted to introduce myself. In an occupation, one likes to know one's neighbors." He touched his cap again. "Good afternoon."

He walked away up the coast road without hurrying, and Mara stood at her own gate and watched him go, and the feeling in her chest was cold and specific, the feeling of a page turning somewhere she couldn't see.

That night she took out the ledger and read through it slowly, all of it, the way a gambler counts his chips before the game goes sour. Every secret in Harrow Point lay in her hand: the affair, the debts, the poaching, the forged medical certificates, the pacifist letters, the drunk constable — and there, in Tom's careful disguised handwriting from last March, the entry she had promised herself she'd tear out and never did:

*T. W. — returned home 14th, no leave papers. Says ship lost at Q.? Verify.*

Her brother, the deserter, alive and hiding ten miles up the coast in a lighthouse the navy had forgotten. Written in her own hand. Filed under W.

Outside, a searchlight swept the harbor for the first time, dragging its white finger across the water, across the chained boats, across the dark, and Mara understood with perfect clarity that the town had died in its sleep and woken up occupied — and that Captain Renshaw had not come to watch her mother's speech.

He had come to take attendance.

And somewhere in his pocket, or his tent, or his orderly mind, there was very likely a list — and the question that kept Mara awake, pencil hovering over the ledger, was how soon his list and hers would be compared, entry by entry, name by name.
