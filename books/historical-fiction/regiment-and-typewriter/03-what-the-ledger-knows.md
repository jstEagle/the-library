## 3. What the Ledger Knows

He came on a Tuesday, which felt deliberate. Armies, Mara had learned in the past week, did nothing without deliberation. The soldiers who had arrived overnight like a tide of grey-green water had spent seven days converting Harrow Point into something unrecognizable — the green a supply depot stacked with crated rifles, the harbor closed with a boom of chained barrels, the churchyard ringed in barbed wire so that the dead could no longer be visited without a pass. Seven days of watching. And now, on the eighth day, a motorcar with an army plate pulled up outside Whitlock House at ten in the morning precisely, and Captain Renshaw stepped out of it as though he had been invited.

Mara saw him from the window of her mother's study, where she had been transcribing the morning's letters into the ledger. She closed the book before she finished the sentence. It was becoming instinct, this — the closing of the book, the flattening of her hand over the cover, as if paper could be quieted the way one quieted a startled bird.

"Mother," she said. "The army's at the gate."

Edith didn't look up from the pamphlet proofs spread across her desk. "Then they can wait to be received."

"He'll not wait. He's the sort who was never once made to wait as a boy and has decided the world owes him the habit."

"Then let us find out what he wants." Edith removed her spectacles and folded them with the unhurried precision she brought to everything — arguments, arrests, the raising of difficult children. "Mara. Whatever he asks for, we are a private household conducting private correspondence. Say less than that. Say nothing cleverer than that."

It was, Mara thought later, the only piece of advice her mother ever gave her that Edith herself failed to follow within the hour.

* * *

Renshaw filled the parlour the way a filed report fills a folder: completely, efficiently, leaving no room for anything else. He was younger than his captaincy suggested, fair-haired, with the kind of face that would go soft at forty and was making the most of its remaining years of sharpness. He accepted tea he did not drink. He sat with his knees together and his cap on his knee like a schoolboy at assembly, and when he spoke it was in the voice of a man reading terms aloud before asking for a signature.

"Mrs. Whitlock. Miss Whitlock. Thank you for receiving me. I'll be brief, as I know your time is valuable —" a small smile, acknowledging without naming her reputation for having none of it to waste — "and I'll be equally frank, because I've found frankness saves everyone embarrassment."

"How modern," said Edith.

"The War Office has requisitioned Harrow Point for the staging of troops. You know this. What you may not know is that the requisition includes the security of the port itself, and the security of the port includes —" he produced a folded paper, unfolded it with two crisp movements — "the identification of persons whose activities might impede military operations. Agitators. Disaffected elements. Persons circulating correspondence of a seditious or morale-damaging character." He laid the paper on the table between them. "I'm instructed to collect all such correspondence held by known agitators. Voluntarily, wherever possible. I should very much prefer voluntarily."

"And who has supplied you with this list of known agitators?" Edith asked.

Renshaw looked at her with mild, terrible patience. "Your name heads it, Mrs. Whitlock. I'd have thought you'd take some pride in that."

Mara kept her eyes on the tea things. Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like a clock winding down. Because she understood now — not all at once but in pieces, the way ice goes out of a harbour — exactly what he was describing. Correspondence. Circulating. Held by agitators. Every word he used described her ledger. Not the pamphlets on her mother's desk, not the speeches, not the banners sewn by ladies in drawing rooms. The ledger. Three years of delivered and intercepted and quietly read letters, recorded in her own small hand: who owed whom money, whose son had enlisted under false age, which fisherman's wife wrote to a sister in a German-name town upcountry, which magistrate drank, which alderman beat his wife, which curate had doubts. And one page near the back, written in a hand shakier than her own because she had been crying when she inked it:

*Thomas Whitlock, b. 1899. Did not report to depot, 14 March. Last seen Harrow Point quay. Whereabouts unknown.*

She had written it because the ledger did not distinguish between secrets worth keeping and secrets that were simply true. That had always been its virtue. It had never once occurred to her, until this man set his cap on his knee in her mother's parlour, that its virtue was also its catastrophe.

"We hold no correspondence beyond family letters," Edith was saying, in her rally voice, the one that carried to the back of halls without effort. "And our family letters concern ourselves. If the army wishes to read my sister's recipe for seed cake, Captain, it is welcome to apply in writing."

"I admire that," Renshaw said. "Truly. But I want to be clear about what refusal costs." He turned then — slowly, deliberately — and looked at Mara for the first time since sitting down. "Miss Whitlock. You drive your mother. A woman who drives must handle the motorcar's affairs. Tyres, repairs, storage. Tell me — do you keep papers in the car? Lists? Notes?"

"No," Mara said.

"You're certain."

"Certainly," she said, and heard her own voice come out level, and marveled at it, and hated herself for the marveling, because it meant lying had become a skill rather than an event.

He nodded as though she had confirmed something. He rose, replaced his cap, thanked Edith for the tea in terms that acknowledged neither its temperature nor its existence, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth, Miss Whitlock — and I say this professionally, not kindly — the families who cooperate leave on the first ships, with their names intact and their houses locked behind them. The families who make me search don't get searched gently, and they don't get remembered fondly when the lists are drawn up for passage." He put his hat brim briefly to his forehead, a gesture so perfectly calibrated it could not be sincere. "Good day, Mrs. Whitlock. Good day, Miss Whitlock. Do think about seed cake."

* * *

The motorcar stood in the coach house where it had stood since the harbour closed, its tyres deflated to half-pressure against the day the petrol ran out entirely. Mara went to it within minutes of Renshaw's car disappearing down the lane — not running, because running was information too, and the lane had eyes in every upstairs window now.

Her hands shook only once she was inside, alone, with the door shut and the light coming through the slats in dusty bars. She knelt on the coach-house floor with the ledger open across her knees and read it the way Renshaw would read it if he ever held it: not as a record but as an instrument. Page after page of leverage. She had believed, three years and four hundred entries ago, that information was power, and that she was its honest keeper, and that honesty consisted of writing things down truly. She saw now that she had confused custody with innocence. Nothing in these pages was neutral. Every name was a handhold for someone climbing toward something.

And Tom's page — God, Tom's page. If Renshaw found the ledger, he did not need to search the lighthouse at all. The ledger would tell him where to look, or at least that there was somewhere to look, and Tom — who had run from the depot white-faced and silent, who had not been able to explain even to her why, only that his body had refused it the way a horse refuses a jump — Tom would be found, or starved, or shot against a wall with the sea behind him, and the last entry in his sister's careful hand would be the reason.

She did not burn it. That surprised her. She had matches in her pocket — she always had matches; drivers did — and she got as far as striking one, cupping the flame over the page with the magistrate's drinking, the alderman's fists, the curate's doubt, all of it trembling in the small heat.

But the whole book was the town. Burning it was burning the map of everyone she had ever driven past, and the army wanted exactly that — wanted her to destroy it or surrender it, either way to erase the evidence that Harrow Point had ever been a place full of people rather than a port full of cargo. Renshaw's list of agitators was a list of names. Hers was a list of neighbours. There was a difference, and the difference was the only thing she owned that mattered.

So she blew the match out, and thought instead about tyres.

The spare rode beneath the rear seat, and the well beneath the spare was lined with oiled canvas against rust, and the canvas lining left a cavity the depth of two fingers' width and the width of a hymnal. She wrapped the ledger in oilcloth from the tool drawer, tied it with the cord she used for the bonnet stays, and lowered it into the well, and set the spare tyre above it, and sat back on her heels in the dust and listened to the coach house tick and settle around her secret.

It would not survive a thorough search. She knew that. Nothing survived a thorough search; that was the whole meaning of a thorough search. But Renshaw had told her, in his tidy professional way, exactly how searches worked here: voluntarily, or not gently. Which meant there would be warning. Which meant the ledger's safety depended not on hiding it better but on moving it sooner than they came.

Which meant driving. Which meant the coast road, tonight, in the dark, without lamps, to the lighthouse.

Where Tom would ask her, as he asked her every week, whether she had burned his page yet.

She wiped her hands on her driving coat and stood, and the fear that had sat in her chest all morning shifted shape — not gone, but sharpened, whetted, given a job to do. They wanted her book. Let them come for it. She had been keeping other people's secrets for three years, and she was done pretending that keeping them meant surrendering them to whoever arrived first with a requisition order and good manners.

From the house came the sound of her mother's typewriter beginning up again, patient and relentless, hammering out another leaflet the army would want next.

Mara latched the coach house door and went in to pack for the dark.
