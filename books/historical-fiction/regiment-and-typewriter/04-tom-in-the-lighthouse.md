## 4. Tom in the Lighthouse

The lighthouse had been dark for six years, but Tom Whitlock still climbed her stairs every morning as if the lamp were lit and he were the one expected to keep it.

He counted the steps out of habit — ninety-three, iron, each one ringing faintly under his boots no matter how carefully he placed them. At the top, the lantern room smelled of rust and gull droppings and old machine oil, and through the salt-hazed glass he could see the whole sweep of Harrow Point: the harbor with its new boom of gray-painted barges, the green where the supply tents had gone up like mushrooms after rain, and beyond the headland, the sea itself, flat and pewter-colored in the early light.

That was where he looked. Not at the town. The town was full of things he couldn't bear to count.

The sea gave him ships.

Three days ago there'd been a convoy — eleven hulls, low in the water, riding out past the point in a line so long their wakes stitched the horizon together like a seam. Troopships. He'd known them by the shape, by the lifeboats stacked along their sides like eggs in a carton, by the way they sat heavy and patient, waiting on the tide. Eleven ships. Even if you guessed conservatively, that was thousands of men going out to France, and every one of them would stand where Tom had stood, in the mud at Passchendaele's edge, and hear the guns open up, and learn what a man learned there.

*And I ran from it,* he told himself, the way he told himself every morning, because he had decided that lying to yourself in the dark of a lighthouse was the one luxury left to him. *I stood my watch and then I turned around and walked away from my own company, and now I live above the sea like a wick without a flame.*

He had not run during the fighting. That was the thing he clung to and the thing that shamed him most, because clinging to it felt like cheating. He had run afterward — after the barrage, after the counterattack, after he'd found young Prewitt's helmet half-buried and understood, in some animal place below thought, that he would be ordered back into the line tomorrow and the day after and the day after that until his number came up. He had simply walked. Out of the communication trench, down a lane, onto a lorry headed for the coast, wearing the face of a man with orders. Nobody had stopped him. That was the unbearable part — how easy it was. The army was so vast that one man walking away made no ripple at all.

Except here. Except in this room, where the ripple filled the whole lantern chamber and pressed against the glass.

He heard the motorcar before he saw it — the particular stutter of the Whitlock Hupmobile's second gear, a sound he'd known since childhood — and he went down the stairs two at a time, ninety-three, ninety-two, ninety-one, because Mara always knocked twice on the oil-store door and never waited for a third.

* * *

She came in smelling of petrol and cold air, her cheeks wind-scoured, a basket over her arm. She was nineteen and she moved like their mother, which was to say like weather coming toward you.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You're kind."

"I'm honest. It's different." She set the basket on an upturned crate: bread, a heel of cheese wrapped in cloth, three apples, and — he saw her hand hesitate over it before she surrendered it — a small jar of their mother's plum preserve. "Nell sends her love. She wanted to come herself. I told her the path's being watched."

"Is it?"

"Everything is watched now. There's a corporal who walks the cliff road at dusk. He whistles 'Pack Up Your Troubles' very badly." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "There's news."

"There's always news. It's why you keep me fed."

"Tom." She sat on the crate across from him, knees nearly touching his, and lowered her voice though there was no one within a mile. "They've posted evacuation notices. One ship. Civilians only, berths limited, sailing on the morning tide — Thursday next. The army wants the town empty before the next convoy loads."

He stared at her. Outside, a gull screamed once and fell silent, as if it too were waiting.

"One ship," he said. "For the whole town?"

"For whoever can get aboard. Ration cards and papers checked at the quay. Which means—" she stopped.

"Which means checking for deserters."

"Which means checking for deserters."

He got up and went to the glass, because standing still felt suddenly like sinking. Below, the harbor lay bright and indifferent, the boom barges strung across it like beads on a wire. Somewhere beyond the headland, the sea road home to France waited, patient as arithmetic.

"So that's it," he said. "I stay. I hide in the hills until it blows over, or—"

"Or nothing. Listen to me." She crossed to him and took his sleeve, and her grip was stronger than her size suggested; she hauled motors out of ditches, their mother said, and she hauled brothers too. "There are three berths with our name on them. Mother has been promised passage — Renshaw himself offered it, which frightens me more than if he'd refused. You, me, Nell. Four souls, three places. But we'll manage the arithmetic later. First there's something else, and I want you to hear all of it before you speak."

He turned. Her face had gone careful, the way it did when she drove on ice.

"It's about your pages," she said. "In the ledger."

The word landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He'd known it was coming. He'd known since the night he'd arrived, soaked and shaking, and seen the black book in her lap by lamplight, and understood that everything he was — the desertion, the false papers he'd traveled under, the name he'd given the ferryman — lived now in his sister's small square handwriting, catalogued between the grocer's affair and the vicar's debts.

"Burn them," he said. "Mara. Burn them tonight."

"And lose the only record of how you got here? Of who helped you on the road? If anything happens to you, those names are how we'd ever trace—"

"If anything happens to *you*, those names are how they hang four other people alongside me." His voice cracked and he hated it, hated the way the lantern room threw echoes, so that his fear sounded like a crowd. "You don't understand what a court-martial does with paper. Paper is all they need. A page in a book — dates, names, a route — that's not protection, Mara. That's a confession with witnesses built in. You think you're keeping me safe in ink. You're keeping me *prosecutable*."

She didn't let go of his sleeve. "I have kept every name honestly. Every one. That's the whole point of the ledger — nobody in this town is invisible while I'm—"

"While you're what? Its little archangel?" He gentled immediately, because her face flickered, and because he loved her, and because love in wartime meant striking someone and then bandaging them in the same breath. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. But listen to what you're saying. You believe information is power. I believed duty was a coat you put on, and look where the believing got me. Things change when the army comes, Mara. Paper stops being history and starts being ammunition."

"Then give me a reason it's different for your pages."

"They're not mine alone!" He pulled free and paced the narrow ring of the lantern room, gesturing at the town below as if he could show her the danger spread across it like a chart. "Mrs. Cardew hid me a night and fed me — her name's in your book, isn't it? Don't answer, I know it is, I watched you write it. The fisherman who carried me past the checkpoint — he has sons in uniform. If the ledger falls into their hands, everyone who touched me becomes an accessory. You wouldn't just be burning *my* secret. You'd be burning theirs free."

For a long moment she said nothing. He watched her do what she always did — turn the problem over, weigh it, treat truth as a thing that could be balanced like ledgers, like accounts. He wanted to shake her and embrace her in equal measure.

"Honestly kept," she said finally, quietly. "That's what I promised myself. That the book would tell the truth even when the truth cost something. If I start tearing pages out for the people I love best, then it was never a record. It was just a weapon pointed whichever way I pleased."

"And when they come for the book — and they will, Mara, Renshaw has your mother's measure already — whose weapon will it be then?"

She looked at him then with an expression he couldn't read, something older than nineteen, something almost frightened, and he realized with a cold drop in his stomach that she had already asked herself that question, and that she did not like any of her answers.

"The tide's turning," she said at last, repacking the basket with hands that moved too smoothly, too busily. "I'll think about it. That's not a promise, Tom. It's the only thing I have that's bigger than a promise."

"Mara—"

"Thursday," she said, at the door, wind pushing her hair sideways. "One ship. Morning tide. Keep off the stairs at dusk — the corporal whistles badly, but he counts windows." Then she was gone, down the iron stairs, and the motorcar stuttered away along the cliff road, and he was alone again with ninety-three steps and the sea.

That night he did not sleep. He sat in the lantern room watching the troopship lights far out past the boom, riding at anchor like a string of fallen stars, and he thought about ink. About how his sister believed the truth was a thing you could hold in trust for people. And about how, somewhere below him in the dark, wrapped in oilcloth inside a spare tire, the truth about him lay sleeping — patient, exact, complete — waiting for whoever came to claim it.

Two mornings later, before dawn, he heard boots on the cliff path. Two men, unhurried, military. They did not climb to the lighthouse.

They stopped at the bottom, struck a match, and began reading something aloud to each other in the wind.

Tom stood at the dark window, heart slamming, and knew with absolute certainty that whatever they held in their hands — it had started as a page in his sister's book.
