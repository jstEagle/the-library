## 5. Edith's Empty Chair

They took me at the corner of Chapel Street, where the cobbles gave way to the army's new gravel, and they did it politely, which was the worst of it.

"Mrs. Whitlock." A corporal, not much older than Tom. He touched his cap as though I were a widow he'd come to collect rent from. "You'll need to come along."

"Along where?" I asked, though I knew. Half the town stood watching from doorways and shop windows — Mrs. Penhale with her washing basket frozen mid-lift, the Tregenna boys on the wall like gulls. I made sure my hat was straight before I let him take my elbow.

"The schoolhouse, ma'am."

Our schoolhouse. Where Mara had learned her letters. Where she'd written her first essay, *On the Fairness of Laws*, aged eleven, and been made to copy it out again because the master said a girl should argue more gently. I had gone in myself and told the master that gentleness was not the same as silence, and that if he made her copy it out, he should copy it out a hundred times so he could learn it by heart.

Now there was barbed wire where the bicycle racks had been, and a soldier at the door where the headmistress used to stand ringing the bell, and inside, the smell of boot polish laid over chalk dust like paint over a wound.

* * *

They put me in the old Standard Four room, alone, and left me for three hours while the light moved across the floor and found nothing to land on but desks stacked against the wall. I sat in a child's chair because it was the only chair, and I thought: this is how they will do it. Not with violence. With waiting. With making you small enough to fit their furniture.

At four o'clock a private brought me tea in a tin mug, weak, no milk. At six, Captain Renshaw came.

He was younger than his rank suggested, and he had the particular stillness of men who have learned that listening is a weapon cheaper than speaking. He pulled out a desk — one of the little ones, absurd beneath him — turned it to face mine, and sat down like a schoolmaster about to discuss a boy's conduct.

"Mrs. Whitlock. You spoke today to roughly two hundred people, without a permit, in a designated military zone."

"I spoke," I said, "about citizenship. Which your own posters claim we are fighting for."

"And you distributed literature bearing names of persons who have written to this command requesting their names be kept private."

I looked at him. "You've read my pamphlets."

"We read everything now, ma'am. That's rather the point of us." He folded his hands. "You believe in records. I've seen the suffrage press; you people keep better files than the War Office. Membership rolls, subscription lists, correspondence indexes. It's admirable, honestly. Discipline is discipline." He smiled, thin and courteous. "So you'll understand when I say that what concerns us is not your speeches. Speeches end. Files don't."

The light was going orange behind him through the tall windows. Somewhere down the corridor a telephone rang twice and stopped, and I understood that even the school bell had been rewired into something else.

"What is it you want, Captain?"

"Nothing tonight. Tonight you're our guest until the transport comes at nine. Tomorrow you'll be released, because arresting you makes you a martyr and martyrs are inefficient." He stood, straightening his jacket. "But I want you to think, overnight, about what happens to a town's secrets when a town is emptied. Names go somewhere, Mrs. Whitlock. The only question is who holds them when the tide goes out."

Then he left, and the lock turned, and I sat in the dark of Standard Four with my tea going cold, doing exactly what he'd told me to do.

That was the humiliation of it. He hadn't threatened me. He'd simply handed me my own arithmetic and let me work it.

* * *

I did not sleep. I inventoried instead.

Through the wall I could hear the machine running — that was the word for it, machine, though I hated to grant them the dignity. Boots on gravel at fixed intervals. A voice calling numbers. The clatter of a typewriter somewhere below, tireless as rain. Once, two officers passed my door arguing quietly about tonnage, and I caught the phrase *embarkation schedule*, and then *berths*, and my heart did something ugly and hopeful that I was ashamed of before I'd finished feeling it.

Berths. There would be a ship. There was always, eventually, a ship.

I thought about the women whose names I had carried for twenty years. Mrs. Curnow, who signed the first petition with a hand ruined by fish-gutting and asked me afterward never to tell her husband. Dr. Lanyon, who prescribed "hysteria" to wives so they could legally leave violent houses, and whose ledger of those prescriptions would ruin him if the army ever saw it. The girl in Porthmear — I would not write her name even in my own head — who had come to Edith Whitlock at seventeen, pregnant by a man whose family owned half the harbor, and whom I had helped onto a train to Bristol with money collected under the table from six respectable subscribers.

Twenty years of names. Twenty years of women handing me their most flammable truths because I promised to keep them safe, and I had believed keeping meant hiding.

Renshaw wanted files. The movement needed leverage. And between those two appetites sat a leather ledger in my daughter's keeping that I had never fully read, only trusted.

I had spent my life telling women: *your name is power, give it to us.* Standing in that schoolroom in the dark, I heard my own voice come back to me changed — *your name is power, spend it.*

I told myself it would be different this time. That I would be careful. That I would use it surgically, name by name, only what was needed, only to save the cause and the people worth saving. Every person who ever wielded information has told themselves some version of this sentence. We are all honest at the moment we decide to be dishonest.

By dawn I had stopped pretending it was anything but a bargain. The question was only the price.

* * *

They released me at nine o'clock sharp, as promised, walking me to the wire gate past the assembly yard where soldiers were loading crates marked with stencils I couldn't read. A sergeant saluted me — actually saluted — and I nearly laughed. Courtesy as containment. They'd learned it from us, I suppose. We had spent fifty years being polite in order to be heard.

Mara was waiting outside the gate with the motorcar, and the sight of her face undid me more than the night had. She had that look she gets when she has been awake deciding things — jaw set, eyes too bright, hands very still on the wheel. She had Tom's look. She had my look, God help her.

She opened her mouth to speak and I got in first, because I am her mother and I know the cost of letting her ask questions I'm not ready to answer.

"Drive," I said. "Not home. The coast road. I want to think."

She drove. The sea came and went beyond the hedge gaps, grey and glittering and indifferent. After two miles she said, carefully, "They searched the house while you were gone. Renshaw's men. They didn't find anything."

"No," I said. "Because you're cleverer than they are."

"Mama—"

"There's a ship, Mara. On the morning tide, soon — days, not weeks. Limited berths. I heard them discussing it." I watched her knuckles whiten on the wheel. "Which means every family in Harrow Point is about to discover what they're worth to the army, and the army is about to discover what it's willing to trade. Information is going to become currency in this town, and we hold more of it than anyone."

She pulled over. Actually stopped the car on the coast road and turned to face me, and I saw that she had already guessed, because she is me, and I hated her a little for it.

"You want my ledger."

"I want what's *in* it. Selectively. Strategically." I heard my own voice, level, reasonable, the voice from the platforms. "There are names in that book that could buy us passage, protection, time. There are names in it that could destroy people if the wrong side holds them — which means we cannot let the wrong side take it whole. If we must spend, better we choose what to spend than let Renshaw requisition it like everything else."

"That book isn't mine to spend." Her voice cracked on it. "Those are *people*. Mrs. Curnow. Dr. Lanyon. Mama, you told me — you *taught* me —"

"I taught you that information is power."

"You taught me that information is *trust*. That's why they give it to us. Because we keep it." She was crying now, furious about it, wiping her eyes with the back of one hand like a child. "If we start trading it, we're just another army."

I looked at my daughter and felt the whole weight of the night settle into my chest — the little chair, the typewriter clattering in the dark, Renshaw's courteous arithmetic. He had known, sitting there in Standard Four. He had known that a woman who believes in files can be beaten with her own filing system. All he'd had to do was wait for me to do the math.

And I had done the math. I was doing it still.

"The alternative," I said quietly, "is that everyone in that book drowns or starves with their secrets intact. Is that your trust? Trust that keeps nobody alive?"

She didn't answer. She put the car in gear and drove us home in silence, and the silence between us was worse than the arrest, because the arrest had an ending and this does not.

When we reached the house I saw, before we'd even stopped, that the front door stood open, and Nell was on the step, white-faced, holding a note in one hand.

"Mara," she said. "It's Tom. Someone's been asking after the lighthouse."
