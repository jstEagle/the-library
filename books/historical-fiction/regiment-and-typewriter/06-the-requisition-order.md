## 6. The Requisition Order

The notices went up at dawn, pasted to the door of the post office, the fishmonger's, the churchyard gate — everywhere a man might pause long enough to read. Mara saw them first from the motorcar, idling outside the bakery while Mrs. Trenoweth wrapped yesterday's loaves in newspaper that was suddenly more valuable than bread.

*By order of the Military Authority: The civilian population of Harrow Point will evacuate aboard the S.S. *Corvus*, departing on the morning tide, Friday next. One vessel only will be provided. Berths are limited. Each person may carry one bag.*

One bag.

Mara read it three times while the engine ticked and the gulls argued over the harbor water. One bag. Not one trunk, not one household. One bag per soul, as though a life could be folded down to its essentials like a spare shirt.

Mrs. Trenoweth came out wiping her hands on her apron. "You've seen it, then." Her voice was flat in the way voices go when feeling has been beaten out of them overnight. "Forty years in that shop. My mother's china came round Cape Horn in 1861. What am I meant to do with Cape Horn china?"

"You'll take what matters," Mara said, and heard how hollow it sounded even before it finished leaving her mouth.

"Take what matters," Mrs. Trenoweth repeated, tasting it. "And who's decided what matters? That captain of yours?" She looked out toward the green, where the supply depot had grown overnight another row of tents and another stack of crates stenciled with numbers no one in Harrow Point could read. "He'll be deciding for us all soon enough."

She pressed the loaves into Mara's hands and refused payment. "For your mother. Tell her—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell her I said nothing. Tell her I never said anything at all."

The bell above the door jingled shut behind her, and Mara sat in the car understanding exactly what she'd just witnessed: a woman erasing herself, preemptively, before anyone could do it for her. It was what the whole town would be doing by Friday. Unwriting itself.

* * *

Edith read the notice at their own gate without touching it, hands clasped behind her back like a woman inspecting a rival's handbill.

"One ship," she said. "How convenient. They empty the town of witnesses and call it mercy."

"They're calling it protection," Mara said. "Enemy raiders off the coast. That's the line."

"And you believe the line?"

"I believe there are raiders off the coast. I also believe they've wanted this harbor empty since April." Mara handed her the loaves. "There's a list at the harbor office. Berth allocation. Renshaw's office is compiling it personally."

Edith's chin lifted, which in her language meant *war*. "Compiling. As though we were cargo."

"We are cargo, Mother. That's rather the point of a requisition."

It was the wrong thing to say and she knew it even as she said it, but three weeks of barbed wire around the churchyard had worn her patience to wire as well. Edith turned on the path, and for a moment Mara saw something she almost never saw in her mother's face: uncertainty. It passed quickly — it always did — but it had been there.

"How many berths," Edith said quietly, "on this Corvus?"

"Three hundred. There are eleven hundred of us."

The arithmetic hung between them like weather. Neither of them said what both were thinking, because saying it aloud made it real: lists meant triage, and triage meant someone deciding whose grandmother counted and whose did not.

Inside, the house felt different already, the way a body feels different once a diagnosis is spoken. Rooms that had held forty years of living now held furniture awaiting valuation. Nell was upstairs packing and repacking her single permitted bag, and the sound of her — the zipper, the unzipper, the small despairing thump of rejected things — carried through the ceiling like a heartbeat too fast to be healthy.

* * *

They came for the documents on Tuesday.

Not soldiers, at first — clerks, four of them, moving door to door with clipboards and the terrible politeness of men doing lawful work. Private correspondence, printed materials, photographic plates, maps. *All documents of a seditious or strategic nature.* Edith met them on the doorstep with her suffrage literature arranged openly on the hall table, daring them, and the clerks took two pamphlets and a photograph of a rally and left a receipt, as though the Whitlock house were a library and history could be checked out and returned.

At number fourteen, old Mr. Pascoe's letters from his son at the Front were confiscated unopened. At the corner house, the Fenwicks' German prayer book — three generations English, and still — went into a canvas sack. Mara drove past these small plunderings all morning and understood that the search was not really about sedition. It was about inventory. They were cataloguing the town before dissolving it, the way an executor catalogs an estate.

Which meant they would come for the ledger. Not today. But inevitably, and with a receipt.

She stopped the car on the cliff road where no one could see and opened the spare tire well with cold fingers. The ledger lay where she had buried it among the tools, wrapped in oilcloth, and she did not open it — she had learned that much discipline — but she rested her hand on its cover the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you might have to drown.

Eleven hundred people. Every debt, every affair, every quiet kindness, every shameful secret she had collected for two years because information was power and she was its honest keeper. And now power had arrived at her door wearing khaki, offering to trade.

She hadn't told Edith where it was. She hadn't told anyone. That fact, she was beginning to understand, was the only currency she had left that couldn't be inventoried by a clerk.

* * *

Renshaw was waiting when she got home. Not at the door — he was too careful for doorsteps now — but standing in the garden looking at Edith's roses with his hands behind his back, hatless, so that the afternoon light showed her how tired he actually was. She hadn't expected that. It unsettled her more than menace would have.

"Miss Whitlock." He didn't turn. "Do you know what a manifest is?"

"A shipping list."

"A list of everything aboard, so that if the ship goes down, someone knows what was lost." He turned then. "I have been making manifests my entire career. Men, munitions, grain. This week it is a town." He paused. "Your name is not yet on any list I control. That is becoming an administrative difficulty."

"Then put me on the Corvus and solve it."

"It isn't that simple, and you know it's not." He walked toward her across the grass, unhurried, a man crossing his own property. "Berth allocation requires vetting. Vetting requires records. Your mother has records. Your brother—" He let the word sit there, gentle as a laid egg. "Your brother has a file that began before I ever reached this coast. Desertion travels, Miss Whitlock. Paper travels faster."

Her heart was doing something slow and enormous behind her ribs. She kept her face still. Two years of reading other people's letters had taught her that.

"What do you want, Captain?"

"The ledger." He said it plainly, no theater, and somehow that was worse than a demand. "Every name in it. Who owes whom, who fears whom, who meets whom after dark. You've built the most complete civil record of this community in existence — don't look surprised, we've watched you long enough to know its shape if not its contents. I can use it to sort eleven hundred people into three hundred berths fairly. Or I can sort them without it, unfairly, by rumor, by spite, by whoever shouts loudest at my office door." He took his hat from under his arm and turned it once in his hands. "You fancy yourself an honest keeper of secrets. Here is your honesty tested. Three days. Surrender it, and you, your mother, and guaranteed passage — all four of you, and I will lose the deserter's file somewhere in a drawer. Keep it, and I allocate berths blind, and I promise you the blind do not see widows or children first."

He put on his hat. At the gate he stopped, not turning around.

"For what it's worth, Miss Whitlock — I read your mother's file last night. All two hundred pages of it. Do you know what struck me?" He glanced back. "Half of it was written by you. Letters copied, meetings noted, names recorded. Someone taught the army how to watch this town." He tipped the brim. "You did. With the best intentions, I imagine. They usually are."

Then he was gone up the lane, and Mara stood in her mother's ruined rose bed holding a trade offer in each hand: her family's passage, or her neighbors' names.

* * *

That night she took stock, because taking stock was the only ritual she trusted.

In the kitchen, by lamplight, she laid things out on the table like evidence: her one bag, half packed. Nell's rag doll, sitting on the dresser where Nell had left it, having decided — Mara could tell by the angle, the deliberate facing-away — that dolls were among the things that did not matter. Edith's movement papers, already tied in bundles for surrender or smuggling, whichever the hour required. Tom's share of everything, absent, weighing on every object anyway.

Everything must now be measured by what it weighed. A coat or a photograph. A kettle or a letter from a dead husband. Eleven hundred lives against three hundred berths, and every soul in town tonight kneeling on a floor somewhere, holding two objects in their hands and learning, finally, brutally, what they valued.

And beneath the floorboards of the cliff road, in a tire well smelling of rubber and rust, lay the one thing in Harrow Point heavier than all of it: a book of names.

Renshaw thought he'd offered her a bargain. He hadn't. He'd offered her a mirror, and shown her that the army had simply done, at scale, with rifles and receipts, what she had spent two years doing alone with a pen — turning people into entries, entries into leverage, leverage into survival.

The lamp guttered. Upstairs, Nell had finally stopped packing; the silence said she'd chosen, at nine years old, what mattered.

Mara blew out the flame and sat in the dark with her hands flat on the table, and made herself ask the question she'd been avoiding since dawn:

If she surrendered the ledger, whose secrets would buy the berths? And if she burned it instead — if she chose that —

Down the lane, faint but unmistakable in the night air, came the sound she'd been dreading all week: boots on gravel, many of them, stopping not at her gate but going on, toward the cliffs.

Toward the lighthouse.
