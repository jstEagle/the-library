## 7. Names We Owe

The notices went up on a Tuesday, pasted to the post office door with Army paste, and by Wednesday the whole of Harrow Point had learned the arithmetic. One ship. The *Corvid*, a coastal steamer requisitioned from the fruit run out of Falmouth, would take on civilians at dawn on Saturday's morning tide. Berths for two hundred souls. Harrow Point held better than six hundred.

Mara read the notice twice, then a third time, standing in the queue for ration bread while women around her did the same sum she was doing and arrived at the same terrible remainder.

By Thursday, they had begun to come to the house.

* * *

Mrs. Penhale came first, before the dew was off the grass. Mara found her on the doorstep in her good black coat, hands folded like a woman at a funeral, which in a way she was — attending the burial of her own certainty that the world was fair.

"Your mother said—" Mrs. Penhale began, and stopped, and started again. "I know you keep the letters, girl."

Mara said nothing. In the hall behind her, Edith's typewriter sat silent under its dust sheet; Edith herself was still at the schoolhouse under arrest, due back today or tomorrow depending on Renshaw's mood, and the house felt like a body holding its breath.

"I'm not ashamed of it," Mrs. Penhale went on, chin up. "My sister's boy is at the Front and I wrote him every week what I heard, that's all, gossip mostly, but the Army calls it something worse when it crosses the water. They've a list. I've seen the list. My name's on it and names on that list don't get berths." Her voice cracked on *berths* like a plate dropped on stone. "But you know everyone, Mara Whitlock. You know who's clean and who isn't, better than any captain with his little book. Tell me what to say. Tell me how to stand. One word from you—"

"One word from me is worth nothing," Mara said. "I'm not on the committee that decides."

"But you *know* things." Mrs. Penhale leaned close, and her breath smelled of chamomile and desperation. "You know things that could buy a berth for anyone. Everyone says so. Everyone says the Whitlock girl keeps a book."

The words went through Mara like cold water finding a seam. *Everyone says.* She had believed herself a secret keeper. It appeared she was a bank, and the town had come to make withdrawals.

She sent Mrs. Penhale away with tea and no promises, and stood at the window watching the woman walk down the lane with her shoulders set like a soldier's, already rehearsing whatever story Mara's silence had left her to invent.

* * *

They came all day after that.

The Tregose brothers, whose smuggling during the herring shortage was known to precisely three people, one of whom was Mara's ledger. Old Mr. Curnow, who wanted to know if the Army's medical exemption lists could be influenced — his son had flat feet and a bad heart and a conscription notice, and a berth on the *Corvid* would solve both problems at once. Young Ruth Vosper, seventeen, who came not to beg but to trade: her uncle worked the harbor master's office, and he'd seen the berth allocation draft, and she'd tell Mara where her name fell on it if Mara told her whether her father's letters to the Irish relatives were dangerous enough to burn.

"You're keeping accounts," Ruth said, with the terrible clarity of the young. "Everyone knows it. So make them balance for us."

By evening Mara had turned away eleven households and helped three, and she could no longer say which act had cost more. For the Vospers she had gone into the ledger — hidden now inside the spare tire well of the motorcar, wrapped in oilcloth — and read aloud what she knew of their correspondence, judging the danger line by line like a woman weighing fish. For the others she had given nothing, and watched hope drain out of their faces, and understood that she was now a power in Harrow Point, and that power was a thing you could not pick up without getting some of it on your hands.

Her mother would have called it leverage. Edith had come home from the schoolhouse that morning, released without charge but not without message, and she had looked at Mara across the kitchen table with new eyes — calculating eyes — and said, "That book of yours is worth more than my speeches now. Worth more than all of us, perhaps."

"It isn't for sale."

"Everything is for sale, love. The only question is the currency." Edith had smiled, tired and hard at once. "Berths. Freedom. Tom's neck. Think about the currency."

Mara thought about nothing else.

* * *

It was Nell who told her, on Friday afternoon, in the voice of someone reporting weather.

"There's a boat at the lighthouse," her cousin said. She was packing her one permitted bag on the kitchen floor, folding and refolding the same two dresses, and she didn't look up. "Soldiers. Two lorries' worth. They went along the cliff path an hour ago."

Mara was moving before the sentence finished. Through the village, past the barbed wire strung along the churchyard wall, up the coast road at a pace that would have scandalized the driving instructor who'd taught her — and all the while the thought turning over and over like an engine that wouldn't catch: *they can't have found him, they can't have—*

But they had. She saw it from the headland: the decommissioned lighthouse with soldiers at its door, and Tom being walked down the cliff path between two of them, not struggling, his hands loose at his sides, his face turned once toward the sea and once toward her motorcar as it slewed to a stop on the gravel.

"Mara." He said it through the gap in the escorting soldiers' backs, and it wasn't a plea. It was almost relief. Three weeks in a lighthouse will do that to a man. "Don't. Don't do anything stupid."

"They'll court-martial you—"

"Better than the Front," he said, and grinned, and broke her heart with it. "Go home, Mar. Mind the book."

They took him toward the schoolhouse, and Mara sat in the motorcar with her hands locked on the wheel and let the engine idle and tried to think the question through: *who knew?*

Tom's hiding place was known to her, to Edith, to Nell. To no one else alive.

Unless.

Unless someone had followed her on one of her food runs. Unless someone had watched the cliff path. Unless someone desperate enough, frightened enough, had traded the one thing they possessed for the only currency being spent this week in Harrow Point.

A berth.

* * *

She found out at dusk, because Harrow Point was a small town and guilt traveled faster than shame.

It was the Tregoses. Not both brothers — just the younger one, Jory, who had come to her door on Thursday begging for his family and been refused. He had gone from her door to Captain Renshaw's office with the only intelligence he owned: the deserter in the lighthouse. And Renshaw, true to the Army's grim bookkeeping, had entered a name against a berth, and Jory Tregose would sail on the morning tide with his conscience stowed below decks where he wouldn't have to look at it.

Mara stood outside the Tregose cottage in the dark and did not knock. Through the window she could see Jory at his supper, eating steadily, mechanically, like a man feeding a furnace that consumed everything. His wife sat opposite him, and neither spoke.

She thought about the ledger in the tire well. About how many pages had a Tregose in them. About how easily she could open that book tonight and find a price for what he'd done — his smuggling, his brother's, the lot of it — and hand it to Renshaw and watch the scales tip the other way. An eye. A page for a page.

Her hand was on the gate latch before she understood what she was doing.

She stood there in the dark with the latch cold under her fingers and saw, with terrible clarity, the shape of the road ahead. It led to Renshaw's office. It led to her mother's arithmetic — *everything is currency* — and it ended with Mara Whitlock keeping a ledger of grievances to match her ledger of secrets, trading names like the soldiers traded berths, until there was nothing left of the honest keeper she'd believed herself to be but the book itself, blank-faced and hungry, buying and selling the town she loved one betrayal at a time.

Jory Tregose had sold a man for a berth. She would be selling a family for a principle, and calling it justice.

She took her hand off the latch.

The walk home was long, and cold, and instructive. Small powers, she thought, were the most corrupting kind — big ones you could see coming, could brace against, but a ledger, a secret, a single name whispered in the right ear: those got inside you and did their work quietly, like damp in a wall.

At the house, a light burned in the front room. Edith was awake, waiting, and on the table before her lay a telegram, and her face when she looked up was the face of a woman who had finished a calculation she did not like.

"Mara," she said. "Sit down. There's news about the berths — and about Tom. We need to decide. Tonight. All of it, tonight."

Mara sat. Outside, far off, she could hear the sea working at the harbor mouth, patient as a clerk, counting down the hours until morning tide.
