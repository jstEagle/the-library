## 8. Three Berths, Four Souls

Edith Whitlock had spent thirty years teaching women to count themselves. One woman, one vote. She had said it at a thousand kitchen tables and a thousand rallies, had watched faces change as the arithmetic landed — *you are one, you are whole, you count* — and she had believed every word of it.

Now the arithmetic was four into three, and it would not be taught away.

The requisition notice was nailed to their own front door, which she supposed was efficiency of a kind. *One civilian vessel. Berths limited. Priority to women, children, and persons of established residence. Departure on morning tide.* Someone in the military governor's office had typed it on the same machine that typed the evacuation orders, the curfew orders, the list of prohibited assemblies. Edith had read it twice, standing on her own porch with the wind coming off the water, and felt something in her chest go very quiet, the way it did before a fight.

Three berths. Four souls.

She laid the notice on the parlor table that evening, weighted with the salt cellar, and called them in the way she once called a committee to order. Mara first, always Mara, who came in wiping motor grease from her hands and sat down without asking what for. Then Nell, fifteen years old and already folding herself smaller in doorways, a habit Edith had been meaning to break for two years and hadn't. Tom came last, down from the lighthouse after dark, hood up, moving along the fence line like a man crossing his own grave.

He stood by the window rather than sit. He had not sat in this house since April.

"You've seen it," Edith said, touching the paper.

Nobody answered. Nobody had to.

"The ship sails on the morning tide," she went on, because someone had to say the numbers aloud or they would only grow in the dark. "There are three berths under our name. There are four of us." She paused, and made herself say the rest plainly, because plainness was the discipline she had built her life on. "One of us does not sail."

The clock on the mantel ticked through five full seconds. Outside, a patrol went past on the road, boots keeping time, and everyone in the room listened to it the way sailors listen to weather.

"I'll stay."

It was Nell who said it. Fifteen years old, voice steady, hands folded on the table like a woman at a meeting. Edith stared at her and felt something crack sideways in her chest.

"Nell—"

"Nobody will miss me. I'm nobody's cause." A ghost of a smile. "I'm quick and I'm quiet and I can keep out of the way better than any of you. Aunt Edith, please. You're needed. Everyone says so. Even the soldiers say so — that's why they watch you."

"You are fifteen," Edith said, "and you will do as you are told."

Nell looked at her then, straight on, and there was something in the girl's face that Edith recognized with a jolt: her own stubbornness, arrived by some side door of blood, wearing a younger face. *I am nobody's cause,* the girl had said, and meant it as a boast, and Edith heard it as an indictment. This was what the movement had cost without ever putting it in a ledger. Not the arrests, not the hunger strikes, not Edith's name in the papers. This child at the table, volunteering herself out of existence because she had learned, watching this family, that love was measured in usefulness.

"No," Edith said. "That is not how we decide."

"Then how?"

Edith opened her mouth and found nothing there. That was the truth of it, the shameful truth: she had speeches for everything except this.

* * *

Tom broke the silence. He had come away from the window at last, drawn by Nell's offer like a man seeing his own thought spoken in another's mouth.

"It should be me," he said. "You all know it. Say it and have done."

"Thomas—" Edith began.

"A deserter deserves nothing." He said it fast, flat, like reciting a sentence he'd been sentenced to. "That's the law and it's also just arithmetic, Mother, you taught us arithmetic. Three berths, four souls. Strike the one whose country has already struck him off. I'm not on any manifest. I'm not anything. Put Mara and Nell and you on that boat and let me walk back down to the light."

"And do what?" Mara asked quietly.

Tom's jaw worked. "Wait."

"For what? For Renshaw's men to come up the lighthouse stairs?" Mara leaned forward. "They'll find you, Tom. The neighbor who reported the Halletts' cellar will report your light. It's only a matter of weeks. You'd be trading a berth for a cell, and not even a clean trade — they'd still take three of us, and you'd hang or worse, and for nothing."

"For my soul, then," Tom said, and there was such raw bitterness in it that the room flinched. "Since apparently that's the only thing left I own."

Edith watched her son stand there in his stolen civilian coat with the lighthouse smell of oil and cold stone on him, and she understood suddenly that he had already decided this — had decided it weeks ago, maybe, in the dark at the top of the tower, counting ships going out and none coming back. He wasn't arguing. He was informing them.

And God help her, part of her — the part that had marched past jeering crowds for twenty years, the part that knew causes eat their children — part of her wanted to let him.

She hated that part. She turned her face from it.

"This family does not spend its people," she said. "Not for the movement, not for shame, not for arithmetic."

"Then what does it spend?" Tom asked. "Because it's spent plenty. Look around, Mother. Look what's left."

What was left: a parlor with the good rug rolled up against requisitions. A house with a notice nailed to its door. A daughter with grease on her hands who hadn't spoken since Tom came in, which for Mara was its own kind of alarm bell. A niece packing — Edith realized with a start that Nell had slipped out of the room, and could hear, faintly, drawers opening upstairs. One bag allowed per person. The girl was obeying rules that hadn't been set yet.

* * *

Later — after Tom had gone back up the coast road, refusing escort, refusing argument; after Nell had been found sitting on her packed bag in the dark like a small patient parcel and ordered, fruitlessly, to unpack it — Edith sat alone with the requisition notice and did what she had done at every hard crossroads of her life: she counted.

Mara. Twenty-six. Driver, keeper, spine of the household. The army wanted her ledger more than it wanted any single person in Harrow Point; Renshaw had offered passage for surrender, and Mara had come home and told her so with her chin up and her eyes flat, waiting to see what her mother would do with that information. Edith had not yet answered her, and the not-answering sat between them now like furniture.

Tom. Twenty-three. A deserter by the letter of the law and by his own confession of it, hourly, to anyone who would listen. But she had read his letters from France before the censors got sharp — she was reading letters before reading letters was dangerous — and she knew what he had seen at the Somme, knew why a boy who could not kill again might choose the smaller death of disgrace. He deserved nothing, he said. He was wrong. He deserved exactly what she had failed to give him: permission to come home without earning it.

Nell. Fifteen. Nobody's cause. The phrase rang in Edith's head like a struck bell and would not stop.

And herself. Fifty-four years old, president of the county union, name known from here to London, face on the recruiting posters of the movement's enemies and its friends alike. *The movement needs me.* She had said it at the table tonight, and Mara had looked at her — that look, cool and level, the auditor's look — and Edith had heard her own words come back across the table smelling slightly of rot. Because the truth underneath the sentence was this: if Edith Whitlock took the third berth, the cause got a speaker, and her daughters got an absence, and Nell got a lesson about what women were for. If she gave it up, the movement lost a voice in a season when voices were being jailed faster than they could be raised.

Both things were true. Both things were unbearable. That, she thought, was the actual definition of a hard choice: not choosing between right and wrong, but between two rights that had stopped speaking to each other.

She thought of Alice Paul in the workhouse, force-fed through a tube. She thought of the women who had chained themselves to railings so that other women could someday sit in rooms where decisions were made. She had always believed that sacrifice was a currency, that it bought something, that suffering in a good cause was an investment. Standing at her own parlor window with a troop ship's lights strung along the black harbor like a carnival that had come to the wrong town, Edith Whitlock admitted, alone, to no one, that she had never once considered what the currency cost the people who were spent rather than spending.

Nell's bag sat packed by the door. Edith went and knelt beside it and put her hand flat on the canvas, the way you'd check a fever.

"You are somebody's cause," she whispered to the empty hallway. "You are mine."

* * *

Morning brought Renshaw.

She saw the staff car from the upstairs window — Mara's old route, the coast road, coming too fast — and by the time she reached the hall Mara was already there, keys in hand, coat half on, positioned between the door and the stairs where Tom's spare blanket still smelled of oil if you knew to smell for it.

"He's early," Mara said. "Passage-for-the-ledger was supposed to be three days."

"Perhaps he's impatient." Or perhaps, Edith thought, watching the car stop at their gate, he knows something we don't. Perhaps the tide schedule had moved. Perhaps the berths had shrunk. Perhaps a neighbor's report about a light in a lighthouse had already traveled the short distance from cowardice to headquarters.

Renshaw came up the path with two soldiers and stopped at the bottom step, and there was something different in his face this morning — not triumph, she noted, and filed that away. Something more like reluctance, or its well-trained cousin.

"Mrs. Whitlock." He touched his cap. "I've come about the berths."

"We haven't asked for your help allocating them, Captain."

"No, ma'am." He glanced once at Mara, then back. "But I've come to tell you the sailing has been advanced. Not tomorrow's tide. Tonight's." He paused, and delivered the rest like a man laying cards face down, one at a time. "And there's a complication. One of the berths under your name has been reassigned. Requisition priorities." His eyes flicked toward the stairs, toward the lighthouse beyond the headland, and Edith understood in one cold drop that the ledger was no longer the only thing being traded in this town. "You'll want to decide quickly, Mrs. Whitlock. The tender leaves at dusk."

Behind her, on the stairs, she heard Nell come down with her one bag over her shoulder, ready — the girl had slept dressed, Edith realized, ready to be sent anywhere, ready to be nobody's cause — and heard Tom's absence in the house like a missing tooth.

Two berths now. Four souls. And dusk coming on.

Edith turned to Mara and saw her daughter already looking at the beach road, already calculating, already reaching for the car keys — and knew, with the certainty of thirty years of losing fights worth winning, that whatever Mara was planning, it would break all their hearts and might, just possibly, save them.
