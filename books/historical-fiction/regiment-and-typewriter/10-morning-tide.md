## 10. Morning Tide

The tide came in gray and patient, the way it always had, as if it hadn't noticed the barbed wire or the supply crates or the three years of khaki that had swallowed Harrow Point whole. Mara stood on the quay with her hands empty for the first time in months. No ledger. No letters to read in secret by lamplight. No pages to burn. Just her hands, cold at the wrists, and the smell of coal smoke and salt off the water.

The *Corvid* sat low in the harbor, a civilian steamer pressed into service, her rail already crowded with faces. Somewhere behind Mara a soldier shouted numbers — berths, not names, though it amounted to the same arithmetic now. One hundred and twelve civilians. One ship. The rest of Harrow Point would scatter inland on army lorries like seed grain, or stay, conscripted into the machinery of the staging ground.

Edith Whitlock stood at the gangway with her spine straight as a flagpole, and even now — especially now — she looked like a woman about to address a crowd.

"You can still change your mind," Edith said. "There's room if we make it room. I've made less from nothing."

"There isn't," Mara said gently. "And you know there isn't. You taught me to count."

"I taught you to count votes," Edith said, "not to give yours away."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. "You taught me that everything is measured by what can be carried. I'm carrying this." She touched her own chest, once, lightly, as if indicating a pocket. "It doesn't weigh anything. It weighs everything. It stays here."

For a moment her mother's composure cracked — not into grief but into something fiercer, the look she wore when a meeting turned against her and she decided to win anyway. She gripped Mara's shoulders hard enough to bruise through the coat.

"Write to me," Edith said. "They can't stop ink."

"They can stop most things," Mara said. "But I'll find ways. I always did."

That landed. Her mother knew exactly what she meant — every letter read, every name recorded, every secret kept honest in its small black book. Edith's eyes went bright and wet, and she blinked it away because there were people watching, because there were always people watching, and a suffragist learned early that tears were ammunition better saved.

Nell came last down the quay, one bag in her hand, the single allowed bag she'd packed in silence days ago without ever asking whose berth she'd take. Fourteen years old and she'd understood the arithmetic before any of them said it aloud. She stopped in front of Mara and stared at the cobblestones.

"You're staying because of Tom," Nell said.

"I'm staying because of everyone," Mara said. "Tom's just the one I can reach."

"He'll be court-martialed." Nell's voice was flat, rehearsed; she'd heard the soldiers talk. "Deserters get shot sometimes. That's what they said."

"That's why someone has to be here," Mara said. "To write things down. To say them out loud. To make sure the record of him isn't just theirs."

Nell looked up then, and her face was so much like Tom's that Mara had to breathe through it. Then Nell threw her arms around her and held on the way children hold on — entirely, without strategy — and Mara held back and thought: *this* is the thing no berth carries, no ledger records, no army requisitions.

Edith touched both their heads once, a benediction, and walked up the gangway without looking back. Mara knew she wouldn't. Looking back was not in her mother's grammar. But halfway up, Edith paused — one heartbeat, two — her hand white on the rail, and then climbed on.

The whistle blew. The mooring lines came in heavy and wet. Mara stood on the quay and watched the *Corvid* turn her blunt bow toward the open channel, watched her mother and her cousin shrink into the crowd at the rail, watched until the ship was a smudge between the gray water and the grayer sky, and then not even that.

The harbor was closed again the moment she passed. A soldier rolled the gate shut behind her as if the sea itself were being requisitioned.

* * *

They kept Tom in the old coastguard boathouse, converted overnight into a holding cell with a sentry at the door and a stove that smoked. Mara found him sitting on a cot with his hands laced over his knees, looking older than twenty-three, looking like a man who had spent months in a lighthouse talking to gulls and had finally run out of arguments with himself.

"You should have gone," he said when she came in under the escort's bored gaze.

"And leave you to their mercy? Their mercy is a filing system, Tom. Someone has to be the file clerk who remembers you're a person."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound, half sob. "You always were insufferable with your books and your lists."

"It's how I love," Mara said, sitting beside him on the cot. "Badly organized, obsessively documented."

He leaned his shoulder into hers. For a while neither of them spoke, and the tide worked at the pilings below, patient, indifferent, going on.

"They'll shoot me," he said finally. Not a question. An inventory item.

"Not if there's a record worth more than an example," Mara said. "I burned the ledger so no one could use those names. That doesn't mean I stop knowing them. Renshaw thinks he took something from me when I fed those pages to the fire. He didn't. I gave them away — to the people they belonged to. Now I'm going to spend what's left on you."

"Mara—"

"Don't argue with me. I'm much better at it than you are. I've been arguing with Mother since I could talk."

He put his arm around her, and she let him, and outside the sentry shifted his weight and pretended not to see.

* * *

Renshaw came at dusk, alone, which was itself a message. He stood in the doorway of the boathouse with his cap in his hand — a small courtesy, or a performance of one — and looked at Mara sitting beside her brother like a woman keeping vigil, which was precisely what she was.

"The transport for prisoners leaves Thursday," he said. "Court-martial board sits at Falmouth."

"Then I'll be in Falmouth," Mara said.

"That's not how this works, Miss Whitlock."

"How does it work, Captain? You requisitioned a town. You closed a harbor. You searched my house twice and found nothing, because I'm better at hiding things than your men are at finding them. Tell me how it works, and I'll tell you whether I intend to fit inside it."

Something moved behind his eyes — irritation, yes, but underneath it something wearier, almost admiring, the look of a man who had expected resistance and gotten something stranger instead: a civilian who refused to behave like cargo.

He was silent long enough that the stove ticked and the tide filled the pause.

"The fire on the beach," he said at last. "You burned it in front of my men. Every page. Do you know what you cost me? I had orders to retrieve that ledger. I reported it destroyed rather than report that a girl made a bonfire of military intelligence."

"Why?" Mara asked. "Why lie?"

"Because some part of me—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I've been reading requisition reports for eleven months, and every one of them has a name in it that used to belong to someone. And you were the only person in this town who seemed to think those names mattered. I couldn't bring myself to write down that I'd taken them from you too."

Mara studied him — the tired line of his mouth, the cap turning slowly in his hands — and understood that she was looking at a man caught inside his own machine, and that machines, however large, are operated by men, and men can hesitate.

"Then let me visit the prisoners," she said. "Not just Tom. All of them. There are four others locked up since the searches — men who objected, men who hid, men who simply refused. Let me sit with them. Let me write to their families. Give me that, Captain, and I will be so quiet and so lawful that your reports will bore you."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will stand on that quay every morning and tell every soldier and every remaining civilian exactly what happened to each man in that cell, in detail, until your superiors decide a noisy woman is worse than a quiet one. You've met my mother. You know we don't tire."

Renshaw almost smiled. It was a terrible thing, his smile — reluctant, crooked, human.

"One hour a day," he said. "Supervised. And Miss Whitlock — if your brother's board goes badly, don't expect me to be able to stop it."

"No," Mara said. "Expect me to be able to make it expensive."

He put his cap back on and left, and the door closed behind him, and Tom exhaled like a man surfacing.

"What did you do?" Tom whispered.

"I opened an account," Mara said. "With a man who owes the town more than he can admit. Now we collect."

* * *

She went at seven the next morning, with bread and paper and a pencil stub hidden in her sleeve, past the sentry who had decided, somewhere in the night, to look at the horizon instead of at her. The five prisoners sat in the gray light of the boathouse — Tom among them now, moved in with the others, and four strangers who were not strangers at all: Mr. Pellowe from the chandlery, who had refused to sign over his boats; young Aldous Renfree, who had run rather than register; the fisherman Tregose and his son, locked up together for pulling a net through a closed channel out of simple hunger.

They looked up when she came in, five faces with the particular hunger of men who have become paperwork.

"My name is Mara Whitlock," she said, sitting down on an upturned crate, taking out the pencil. "Some of you know me. Some of you know what I used to keep. Either way — I'm here now, and I'm not leaving, and I need to know everything. Your names, spelled properly. Your families, and where they've gone. What was taken from you, and what you're owed. We're going to build a record, gentlemen, and this time it won't be mine to burn. It will be ours."

Mr. Pellowe cleared his throat. "And when they come for that one too?"

Mara clicked the pencil stub and set the first blank page on her knee.

"Then they'll have to requisition it out of my hands," she said. "And I promise you, I've gotten very good at making that expensive."

Outside, the morning tide turned, and began, patient and gray, to come in.
