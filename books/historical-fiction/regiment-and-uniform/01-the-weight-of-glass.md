## 1. The Weight of Glass

There is a particular way the light comes off a first-order Fresnel lens at dawn — not thrown, exactly, but poured, as though the glass were holding back a tide of it and letting it leak through in rings. I have spent nine years learning that light. Nine years of standing inside the lantern room with a chamois cloth and a bottle of spirits, wiping salt from ninety-two prism faces while the sea does its best below me to shake the rock out from under my feet.

Ninety-two faces. You count them. That is the first thing they don't tell you about keeping a light: it is not a job for someone who thinks. It is a job for someone who needs the thinking taken away from her.

The season turned on Tuesday. I knew before the barometer told me; my hands knew first, the old ache settling into the knuckles like tenants coming home. The gulls went quiet on the south stack, which is their way of saying what they always say this time of year: *we are leaving, and you should consider it.* The water off the point had gone that bruised grey-green that means the Atlantic has remembered its temper. And the wind came around to the southwest in the afternoon with a sound I can only describe as a door opening somewhere very large.

Storm season at Widow's Rock. My ninth.

I had spent Monday preparing for it, because preparation is the other thing I do instead of feeling. The oil store: full, one hundred and forty gallons, drums stenciled and stacked two deep against the north wall where the damp can't reach them. Wicks: eleven dozen, wrapped in oilcloth. The spare panes for the lantern: six, each one crated in straw and each one inspected twice, because a cracked pane discovered in January is not an inconvenience, it is a death sentence with paperwork attached. Fresh water in the cistern. Flour, tea, salt pork, tinned milk, a sack of onions going soft at the bottom of the basket because I keep forgetting onions are a food and not a decoration.

I counted everything twice. I always count twice. Once for the Board's ledger and once for myself, and the numbers never disagree, which is more than I can say for any person I have ever known.

* * *

The lens gets polished every day whether it needs it or not. It needed it Tuesday morning. Three days of fog before the front came through lays a skin of salt on the glass so fine you can't see it but the light can feel it, and a light that has to push through salt haze is a light losing an argument with the ocean. So up the hundred and twelve steps I went — I have never counted the steps aloud, but I know the number the way you know your own pulse — with the chamois over my shoulder and the spirits in a stoppered bottle, and I worked my way around the lamp clockwise, face by face, the way Kenji taught me.

Not that Kenji taught me lighthouse work. The Board's inspector taught me lighthouse work, a red-faced man named Culverhouse who pronounced my husband's name three different ways in the course of one interview and settled, eventually, on calling him "the Jap" when he thought I was out of earshot. I was within earshot. I am always within earshot. It is one of the few advantages of being a widow nobody looks at.

No — what Kenji taught me was the polishing. He had been a keeper's assistant in his first years here, before me, before the name Nakamura became a thing whispered about rather than said, and he used to say the lens was the only honest thing on the coast. "Glass doesn't lie," he'd tell me, turning a prism toward the window. "A man will tell you the channel is safe when he's been paid to say it. Glass shows you exactly what it sees." Then he would catch himself, the way he always did, as if honesty were a coin he'd accidentally spent too much of, and go quiet, and polish.

He died four years ago. The sea took him off the low rocks in March, on a night calm enough that the inquiry called it an accident without troubling itself to look hard, and the town called it an accident because the town had already decided everything there was to decide about Kenji Nakamura years before. A foreigner on the rocks. A foreigner who kept to himself. A foreigner whose wife now kept the light alone, which was irregular, and tolerated, and spoken of in the shops in the tone people use for weather they disapprove of.

I applied for the post myself. That surprised them, I think. A woman, applying. But the pay was small and the applicant pool smaller, and Widow's Rock is not a prize anyone fights over — it is a sentence with a view. Culverhouse came out, looked down his nose at my references, looked harder at my face looking for whatever men look for in a widow's face, found nothing he could name, and signed the paper. *Mrs. S. Nakamura, Keeper.* They never asked what the S stood for. Sable is not a name you put on documents if you can help it. It invites questions, and questions are a currency I stopped carrying some years ago.

So: the lens. Face by face, clockwise, breath held near the glass so the fog of it shows me the smears. The light itself sat cold in its mercury bath, waiting for dusk, and outside the storm-glass panes the sea was beginning to stand up in long green shoulders and throw itself flat again.

I talked to the lens while I worked. Not words, mostly. Sounds. A hum, sometimes, an old song Kenji used to hum with a tune that wandered off wherever it pleased and came back when it felt like it. I don't hum the words. I'm not sure there were words. But the humming keeps the silence at a manageable size, and silence on a rock forty fathoms from the mainland is a thing that grows teeth if you feed it.

Nine years alone, and people ask — well. Nobody asks, actually. Nobody comes. But if they did ask how I bear it, I would tell them the truth, which is that I don't bear it, I *schedule* it. Grief gets its hour after the lamp is lit and the log is written and the supper things are washed: one hour, sitting by the stove, hands empty, letting it come through me like weather. Then I bank the fire and go to bed, and in the morning the routine is waiting for me like a good dog, and I hold onto its collar and we get through the day together.

That is the whole secret of Widow's Rock. Routine, and a stove, and ninety-two pieces of honest glass.

* * *

Wednesday the wind arrived properly.

It came in the night with a long shove against the tower that set the whole structure humming — not the lamp, the *tower*, a note low in the masonry like a struck bell heard through a wall. By dawn the spray was going over the lantern, which happens perhaps ten times a year, and the sound of it on the glazing is like fists made of gravel. I went up anyway, of course. The light burned all night and would burn all night again, and the storm panels were latched, and there was nothing in the world that needed doing except everything, constantly, forever.

I spent the daylight hours on the lower gallery with a boathook, retrieving what the sea offered. The sea gives gifts in storm season. Mostly it gives rope, weed, the occasional dead gull, once a ship's door with brass hinges that I still have. Wednesday it gave me half a crate of somebody's oranges, bobbing in the surge like a rumor, and a length of good pine planking I broke down for kindling. I stood at the rail with the wind trying to peel me off the gallery and watched the horizon do its trick of erasing itself, and I thought, as I think every year at this season, that the sea and grief operate on the same principle: they return nothing you ask for, and everything you don't.

Thursday was the supply boat.

The *Maggie Dune* runs the Rock every second Thursday, April through February, weather permitting, and in nine years she has missed — let me see — twice. Once for the great blow of '93 that took the roof off the harbor chapel, and once when old Tam Brelade, her master, had a tooth out and couldn't be persuaded to sail with his jaw wired shut, a decision I respected then and respect more now. Otherwise: Thursday, the *Maggie Dune*, the thump of fenders against my landing stage, Tam's voice shouting over the engine about the price of coal and the morals of the Board, an hour of crates and conversation conducted mostly in gestures, and then the thump of the engine fading south and the silence closing behind it like water.

Tam is the closest thing I have to a colleague. We are not friends. Friendship requires being seen, and Tam sees approximately none of me — he sees a widow in black who pays in exact coin and wants no gossip, and I have carefully cultivated being that person for nine years, and Tam has obligingly never once wondered about me. But he brings news folded inside the newspapers, and he brings seed catalogues in spring, and last Christmas he brought a tin of candied ginger that I ate one piece at a time across three months like a woman rationing her own heart, and when he goes I stand on the gallery until the smoke of his funnel goes down, and then I go inside and write the log and the hour is manageable.

Thursday the wind was still up — a full gale, white water over the outer shoal, the sort of day the *Maggie Dune* has pushed through before with Tam swearing cheerfully at the sky. I watched the southern horizon from first light. I watched it through the middle of the day, from the gallery, from the kitchen window while I baked bread I didn't want, from the lantern room while I trimmed the wick against evening. Grey, grey, and grey.

By four o'clock I had run out of horizons to watch and excuses to invent, and I made myself say it plainly, in the empty kitchen, the way you say things to make them real:

"He's not coming."

And the kitchen said nothing back, and the stove ticked, and outside the wind leaned on the tower and the tower hummed its low note, and I understood that I had just been handed the first missed delivery in seven years — not weather, not teeth, just a gap, a blank square in the calendar where a man and a boat and an hour of human noise were supposed to be.

I did what any sensible keeper does with a problem. I wrote it down. *Supply boat not seen,* I entered in the log, in my best round hand. *Wind SW gale. Suspect delay; expect run Friday or Saturday if sea moderates.*

Suspect delay. Expect a run. As though the world were a machine with parts, and every part would show up if you logged it properly.

Then I ate the bread, fed the cat — I should mention the cat, a grey brute named Buoy who arrived on Tam's boat six years ago and decided I was furniture worth sleeping on — banked the fire, and climbed the hundred and twelve steps to light the lamp against the dark.

* * *

Here is the thing about lighting the lamp at dusk in storm season, alone, knowing the boat isn't coming.

You light it anyway.

You trim the wick, you check the mercury, you give the clockwork a quarter turn and listen to it purr, and at the minute the sun goes down you open the fuel valve and touch the match, and the flame stands up inside all that glass and the lens takes it and pours it out over the water in rings, and somewhere beyond the erased horizon there may be a ship, and that ship may see you, and that is the entire transaction. Nobody sees the keeper. The keeper is not part of the transaction. The keeper is the hand that strikes the match and then withdraws, and the light goes on being a fact about the coast, and the coast does not care whose hand it was.

I stood in the lantern room that night with the storm hammering the panes and the beam swinging out over the black water, and I let myself have the hour early. I sat on the service ladder with my hands around a mug of tea gone cold, and I thought about Tam, and whether the *Maggie Dune* was hove-to somewhere under double canvas or lying in the lee of the point with Tam's tooth — no. His tooth came out years ago. Something else, then. Weather, or coal, or money, or a Board order I hadn't received because orders travel by the same boat that didn't come.

Or something else entirely. The mind in its ninth year of solitude learns to walk out to the end of every bad thought and look over the edge, and I walked out to the edge of mine: the boat lost with all hands, and me on my rock with my counted supplies, discovering exactly how long one hundred and forty gallons of oil and one sack of softening onions will hold a woman against the winter.

But that was the fear talking, and I have learned to recognize its voice the way I recognize the tower's hum — constant, structural, not necessarily meaning anything. Tam Brelade has been sailing this coast since before I had a surname anyone approved of. The sea is rough, but the sea is always rough, and Tam treats it the way I treat grief: scheduled, managed, insulted daily.

"Friday," I said to the lens. The lens, being glass, showed me exactly what it saw: a woman in black, lit from behind by her own fire, talking to no one. "Or Saturday. And if you're not here Saturday, Tam Brelade, I am putting it in the log in language the Board will have to read twice."

The lens declined to answer. Honest glass.

I finished my cold tea. Below me the sea worked at the rock with its long patience, and above me the light turned, and turned, and turned, pouring itself out over a darkness with nothing in it — no sail, no smoke, no answering flicker from the headland light twelve miles south, which I could usually find on clear nights and which tonight was smothered in the same murk that had eaten my horizon.

Just the light, and the dark, and me between them, keeping the arithmetic.

I went down the steps to bed, leaving the lamp to its work. Buoy followed me down the stairs with the air of one accompanying an inferior to her quarters, and the wind sang in the ventilators its one long note, and somewhere out in all that noise the sea was doing something I would not learn about until morning.

I did not know yet — how could I? — that by Friday dawn the sea would be done delivering weather and start delivering something else entirely. Something tangled in my rocks below the tower, with hands, and a heartbeat, and a packet clutched against its chest like a letter it had crossed an ocean to post.

But that was Friday. This was Thursday night, and I slept the sleep of a woman whose worst problem is a missing boat, and if the wind moaned at the windows I did not hear it, and if the tower hummed its note I did not dream of it, and the light turned overhead all night like a patient wheel, sweeping its honest circles over water that had already begun, quietly, without telling anyone, to gather what it meant to give me next.
