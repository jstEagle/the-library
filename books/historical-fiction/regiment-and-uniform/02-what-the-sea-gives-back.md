## 2. What the Sea Gives Back

The storm came in off the north water around midnight, the way the bad ones always did — not with any theatrical announcement, but with a change in the sound of the surf that I felt through the stone floor before I heard it. Forty years of keepers had stood on that floor, and every one of them had learned to read the sea through their boot soles. It's a kind of literacy they don't put in the instruction manuals.

I was up anyway. I was always up. Sleep and I had reached an arrangement over the two years since Kenji died: it could have the hours between three and six if it didn't ask for more, and in exchange I would stop lying awake listening to it fail to come.

By one o'clock the wind had worked itself into one of its moods, the kind where it stopped being weather and became a presence — something with intentions, something you could imagine leaning its shoulder against the tower just to see what would happen. The glass in the lantern room shuddered in its frame. Below, the surf on Widow's Rock went from a rumble to a continuous detonation, wave after wave throwing itself at the shoal like men at a locked door.

I did what a keeper does. I checked the lamp, trimmed the wick, wiped the salt haze from the storm panes even though the spray would undo the work inside the hour. I wound the clockwork that turned the lens. I made tea I didn't drink. And I watched the water, because watching the water is the whole job, distilled: everything else is maintenance.

There is a particular misery in standing a light over a sea that no sane captain should be out in. You can't help doing the arithmetic. Somewhere south of me, some skipper who left harbor an hour too confident was now looking at his barometer and doing sums of his own, and my lamp was the only fixed thing in his world. That's the burden of the work — you are never allowed to be merely a woman in a room; you are a point on other people's charts.

Around four, the storm peaked. Around five, it began to spend itself, which storms do all at once, like a man weeping after holding it in all evening. By first grey light the wind had backed off to a sulk, and the surf, though still enormous, had lost its anger and kept only its habit.

That was when I saw the gulls.

Not the ordinary wheeling crowd that hangs about the rock hoping I'll be careless with my fish scraps. These were working birds — dropping, lifting, dropping again, concentrated on the seaward shelf below the tower where the tide leaves its leavings. Gulls at a fixed point mean one of two things this time of year: a dead seal, or worse.

I took the telescope up to the gallery. The rail was cold enough to bite through wool. I found the spot between the breakers' surges and held the glass steady against the residual shake of my own hands.

At first I thought: timber. A plank, maybe part of a boat's gunwale, snagged in the rocks where the gulls were quarreling. Then the plank moved, and the movement had nothing to do with wood.

A person. On the ledge below the old landing stairs, half in the wash, tangled — I could see it even through the glass — in kelp and in the iron ring of the old mooring bollard, rusted loose years ago, its chain snaking down among the stones. One arm hooked through that chain like someone who had decided, somewhere in the dark water, that she was not finished yet.

*She.* Even at that distance I could see it was a woman, small, dark-haired, wearing what was left of a man's oilskin coat two sizes too big for her.

Here is the thing I will confess only once, and only to this page: my first feeling was not compassion. My first feeling was arithmetic. One person on my rock meant questions, meant a name written in my logbook, meant the Board asking why the widow Nakamura was entertaining castaways. Two years of careful invisibility, and the sea had gone and delivered a complication to my front door.

My second feeling arrived about four seconds later and overruled the first, because that is how the heart works when it's paying attention: *she is going to die while you stand here deciding whether to be inconvenienced.*

I got the rope, the boathook, the blanket from my own bed, and the brandy bottle I'd been saving against my own death and had privately begun to suspect would never come. I did not stop to lock the tower door. There was no one to lock it against.

* * *

The descent to the landing shelf is not something I do often anymore. The stairs are cut into the rock and the sea keeps them honest by trying to kill anyone who uses them. This morning they ran with weed and streaming water, and the swell was still coming through in sets of three — long ones that filled the channel below the shelf and then withdrew with a sucking noise like the sea clearing its throat.

I went down sideways, the way Kenji taught me the first winter, when he caught me taking the steps like a lady instead of like a keeper. *"The rock doesn't care about your dignity,"* he said. *"Give it your attention instead."* His English was accented then, each word set down carefully as a stone in a wall, but he was never wrong about the sea. Not once in eleven years. It is a strange thing to be angry at a dead man for having been right about everything.

I got to the shelf and waited for the set to pass, then crossed to the ledge where she lay.

Up close she looked worse than through the glass, which is saying something. Young — younger than I'd guessed, twenty perhaps, twenty-two. Lips blue-grey. Skin the color of the underside of a fish. The kelp had whipped a raw stripe across one cheekbone, and there was blood in her hair, diluted to pink by the spray. Her hands — and this is what stayed with me, what I keep returning to — her hands were ruined. Torn, abraded, the nails split, one finger bent at an angle fingers don't care for. She had held on. Whatever else was true about this girl, she had fought the entire North Sea for her own life and had declined, at every stage, to lose.

Her right fist was clenched shut against her chest, knuckles white even in that grey face, and when I tried to loosen the arm hooked through the mooring chain, her grip on that fist did not so much as flicker.

"Let go," I said. "I've got you."

She didn't let go of either thing. But her eyes opened — brown, enormous, focused somewhere past my shoulder at the middle distance of the dying — and she said, in a voice scraped down to gravel:

"The packet."

Of all the sentences a half-drowned woman might choose. Not *help me*, not *where am I*. *The packet.*

"There's no packet," I said, because there wasn't, visibly, and because I have learned that the fastest way to quiet drowning people is agreement.

"In the coat." Her hand spasmed against her chest. "Inside. Don't—" A wave came through and I spent the next ten seconds keeping us both from joining the scenery. When I turned back she had used the interruption to pass fully into unconsciousness, which struck me as unsporting, since it left me with the entire remainder of the rescue to conduct alone.

I'll spare the ledger of it: the cutting of kelp with my knife, the unhooking of the arm from the chain, the haul up the stairs in stages with the swell counting out our tempo, the indignity of dragging a sodden stranger across my kitchen floor and leaving a tide-line of seawater and weed behind her that I would later mop without complaint, which tells you something about how far my standards had fallen.

I got her by the stove. I stripped off the oilskin — a man's coat, good quality, salt-stiffened, with a name scratched from the inner collar so thoroughly it had left a pale patch — and got her out of the worst of the wet and into blankets. Her right fist came away from her chest exactly once during all of it, and only far enough to reveal the corner of an oilskin packet, wrapped and tied, which she then clamped back over like a limpet defending its rock.

I warmed brandy and got a measure of it into her, which produced a coughing fit that seemed, perversely, to improve her color. I rubbed warmth back into her hands and did what I could for the torn ones — clean linen, salve, splinting the crooked finger with a lollipop stick I'd been using to mark my place in the supply book.

And the whole time, the packet sat there under her fists, tied with waxed cord, and I sat there not looking at it, which — ask any keeper — is a skill.

* * *

She woke properly near noon.

I knew it before I turned from the stove because the quality of the silence changed. Unconscious silence and awake silence are different animals; the second one watches you.

"You're on Widow's Rock," I said, without turning. "Lighthouse. My lighthouse. You came ashore on the morning tide, tangled in the mooring chain on the lower shelf, and you've been asleep six hours. The sea gave you back. Whether it meant to is between you and the sea."

Silence. Then, hoarse: "You pulled me out."

"I pulled you out."

"Why?"

It was such a naked question — no gratitude in it, just genuine bafflement — that I did turn around. She was propped on one elbow, grey-faced, hair drying into a dark riot, watching me with eyes that had a survivor's arithmetic running behind them. Taking my measure. Deciding something.

"Because you were dying on my rock," I said, "and the alternative was writing you up in the logbook as a corpse, and corpses require paperwork."

That earned me nothing. No smile. If anything her expression closed another degree, and I recognized the look, because I wore it in mirrors for two years: the look of someone calculating how much truth a stranger can safely be told, and arriving at a low number.

"What's your name?" I asked.

A pause, measured out like medicine. "Ruth."

Just that. No surname offered, and the omission so deliberate it functioned almost as one. Ruth. I thought: *that's a lie, or half of one, or a fragment salvaged from a wreck along with the rest of you.* I thought: *you're in no position to throw stones at borrowed names, Sable.*

"And yours?" she said.

"Nakamura. Mrs. Nakamura. I keep the light."

Something crossed her face at that — quickly, badly hidden, the way a door closes when someone inside hears a footstep. I logged it. I've had a lot of practice logging things and saying nothing.

"I'm going to make soup," I said, "and you're going to eat it, and then you're going to sleep again, and tomorrow, when you can stand, we're going to discuss what a young woman in a dead man's coat was doing swimming off my rock at four in the morning in the first storm of the season. Because I've kept this station nine years, and in nine years the sea has given me driftwood, herring barrels, one drowned sheep, and a great many fish. It has never once given me a person carrying a parcel she wouldn't surrender to save her own life."

Her hand moved toward her chest. Stopped. Deliberately flattened against the blanket instead, the way you'd smooth down a telltale hem.

"I don't know what you mean," said Ruth, who lied the way most people whistle in the dark — loudly, and in hopes of company.

I ladled the soup. I handed it over. Our fingers did not touch, because she managed it so that they didn't, and I noted that too: a girl who had practiced not touching strangers.

"Eat," I said.

She ate. Halfway down the bowl, exhaustion ambushed her mid-spoonful — I watched it happen, the body simply presenting its invoice — and she slept again sitting up, bowl tilting, until I eased it from her hands and laid her down.

Then I stood at my window a long while, looking at the rocks below where the tide was tidying away the last evidence of the night, and I thought about the packet.

An oilskin packet, wrapped against water, tied with waxed cord, guarded by a drowning woman past the boundary of consciousness. People wrap documents that way. Ledgers, letters, things that must survive the sea even if their carrier doesn't. I know because Kenji wrapped his letters home that way, every month for eleven years, to a sister in Yokohama whose replies took four months and were read aloud to me twice, slowly, so I could follow.

Whatever was in that packet, it mattered more to that girl than breathing. Which meant it was dangerous enough that someone, somewhere, wanted it lost.

I banked the stove. I checked the lamp early, though it wasn't due. And I wrote the day's log entry in my neat keeper's hand — *Observed: heavy surf, moderating by dawn. Recovered: one adult female, alive, from lower shelf* — and I sat looking at the word *female*, because I already knew I was going to have decisions to make about what went in that book and what stayed out of it.

In the kitchen, the stranger slept with her fist curled shut even in dreams, guarding whatever the sea had been paid to deliver to my door.
