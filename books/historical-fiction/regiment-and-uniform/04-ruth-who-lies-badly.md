## 4. Ruth, Who Lies Badly

I have always been a poor liar. My father used to say so at the breakfast table, folding his letters into thirds with that precise clerk's motion of his, saying, *Ruth, you have your mother's face and your mother's honesty, and between the two of them you will never survive a card game.* He said it fondly. He said most things fondly, even the ones that turned out to be warnings.

He was right about the cards. He was wrong — God, how I hope he was wrong — about the surviving.

The lighthouse keeper's kitchen smelled of paraffin and salt and something else, something I couldn't name at first because my teeth were chattering too hard to think. It was a clean smell underneath the others, like iron left out in the sun. Later I would learn it was simply the smell of her: Sable Nakamura kept herself and everything she owned the way other people kept ledgers, every line ruled straight.

I was wrapped in a blanket that smelled of lamp oil, sitting on a chair drawn up close to a black iron stove, holding a mug of tea I had no intention of drinking because my hands were shaking too badly to lift it without spilling. The keeper stood across the room with her arms folded, watching me the way one watches weather come in off the water — not with fear exactly, but with the professional assessment of someone who has learned that all trouble arrives eventually, and it is best to note its speed and bearing early.

"You came off the rocks," she said.

"Yes."

"Nobody comes off those rocks alive."

"I did."

"So you did." She let that sit between us like a stone dropped into a well. "In what?"

"In what?"

"What were you in? A boat? A hull? People don't fall out of the sky onto Widow's Rock in October."

"A fishing boat," I said, which was true in the sense that any boat that fishes for anything could be called a fishing boat, and the *Merry Alice* had fished me out of the estuary two nights ago before the sea decided it wanted me back. "We were caught in the squall."

"The squall." She said it flatly, tasting it. "The squall that put down three feet of foam over the breakwater at Gray Mouth. That squall."

"Yes."

"And where is this boat now?"

"Gone." True again. I was accumulating truths the way a drowning man accumulates water — each one individually harmless, together quite sufficient to kill me. "I don't know where. I was washed overboard near the point."

She crossed to the window. Through the streaming glass I could see the tower behind us, white paint gone gray with salt, and beyond it the sea working itself into that long gray fury it keeps up all winter, as though it holds a grudge against the land specifically.

"Washed overboard," she repeated. "Near the point. And the sea carried you — what, half a mile through breakers, in the dark, and set you down gently among my rocks with an oilskin packet under your arm?"

I looked down at the packet. It lay on the table between us, dripping onto the boards in a small dark puddle, having refused every attempt she'd made to take it from me. I had woken on her cot clutching it like a child clutches a doll, and when she'd tried to pry it loose I had apparently made a sound that frightened both of us.

"I need to deliver it," I said. "That's all. It's papers. Family papers."

"Family papers."

"My father's."

"Your father's."

I was beginning to understand how interrogation worked from the inside: not by force but by repetition, each question landing on the same spot until the bone showed through. I drank some of the tea to buy time and burned my tongue, which at least gave me something honest to do.

"He's dead," I said.

Something moved in her face then — not softening exactly, more like a door opening a crack to check whether the knocking was real. "Mine too," she said, and then, quickly, as if she hadn't meant to say it: "Two years ago."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Tell me about yours."

And there it was, the trap laid out plain on the scrubbed table between the teapot and the oilskin packet. Because I could lie about boats and squalls and points of land, but if she asked me directly about him — his name, his work, the shape of his life — I would either have to invent a whole man out of nothing, which I could not do, or tell her the truth, which I must not do. Not yet. Not to a stranger whose loyalties I couldn't weigh, whose island I couldn't leave, whose stove I was currently warming my corpse-to-be at.

"He was a government man," I said. "A clerk. Customs, mostly. He wrote things down for a living."

"That's not unusual on this coast."

"No."

"And he left you papers."

"He wanted them delivered. To people in the south. Family business." I heard myself say *family business* and winced internally; it sounded like something from a grocer's advertisement. "It's private."

"Everything private washes up on my rock eventually," she said. "Driftwood, dead gulls, drowned men, secrets. The tide delivers. I log them all."

"You log secrets?"

"I log what the sea gives back." Her eyes went to the packet again, and I pulled it toward me, and she watched me do it, and I saw her decide — visibly, the way a helmsman decides to come about — not to press. Yet. "You'll tell me eventually," she said. "People always do. There's nothing else on this rock to talk to."

* * *

She put me to work, which I later understood was another kind of questioning. A person's hands will confess what their mouth won't. She gave me a cloth and pointed me at the brass fittings in the kitchen while she went up to tend the lamp, and I polished a kettle and a door handle and a rail until my arms burned, thinking: *she knows. Of course she knows. She has known since she pulled me off the rocks, and she is waiting to see what I am.*

Because here is what I knew that she didn't, here is the weight I had carried across three counties and one very nearly fatal stretch of water: my father did not die of the fever they printed in the gazette. My father was hanged. Two years ago, in the yard of the county gaol, before a crowd that had come for a spectacle and got, instead, a thin man in borrowed clothes who walked up the steps without shaking and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that anyone would believe.

Elias Vane. Convicted of treason — of passing shipping schedules to foreign agents. Convicted on evidence assembled by Magistrate Corliss Hobb, testimony given by his own trusted clerk, sentenced by Hobb himself, hanged by Hobb's arrangement, buried quick and quiet. And innocent. Innocent, and I was the only living soul who knew it for certain, because six weeks before the end, when he finally understood the shape of the net around him, my father had done the one thing left to a clerk: he had written everything down. Dates. Payments. Signatures. Names. He had copied it fair, sealed it, and smuggled it out of the gaol inside a consignment of condemned men's effects — a Bible, would you believe it, a Bible sent to his daughter, with the pages hollowed and the truth sewn into the binding.

And then he had been taken before he could tell me what to do with it, and I had spent two years being afraid of it — afraid of the Bible on my shelf, afraid of opening it, afraid of what opening it would make me. Because a confession is only paper until someone carries it, and whoever carries it becomes the traitor in the new story. That is how these things work. I had watched it work once already, from the front row, aged nineteen, wearing mourning.

What had changed — what had finally driven me north with a false name and my mother's good boots and a terror of deep water — was a letter. An anonymous letter, slipped under my landlady's door: *They are searching for the Vane girl. Burn what you have or be burned with it.* Someone knew. Someone in the ring had done arithmetic and concluded that the daughter might have inherited more than her mother's face. So I stopped being Ruth Vane in a boarding house and became Ruth — just Ruth — on a coastal steamer, carrying the ledger sewn into an oilskin packet against my ribs, planning to reach the assizes and put it into Justice Merrow's own hand.

The sea had other plans. The sea, as it turns out, is not on anyone's side.

* * *

Evening came down gray and early. The keeper climbed down from the lamp room smelling of cold air and oil, cooked fish and potatoes without asking if I ate fish, and we sat across from each other at the table while the storm outside threw handfuls of shingle at the windows.

"Sable," she said abruptly. "My name. Since you'll be needing it to scream if you fall down the stairs."

"Ruth," I said. Just Ruth. The lie of omission, the only kind I'm any good at.

"Well, Ruth-just-Ruth." She ladled more potatoes onto my plate without ceremony. "Tomorrow the supply boat comes. First run of the season. You can go back on it."

My stomach dropped. "Back where?"

"Wherever you came from. Wherever your family business is." She wasn't looking at me; she was looking at the fire, at the coals shifting. "This isn't a place people stay who have somewhere to be."

And there it was again, the fork in the road, except both tines led off a cliff. If I went back on the supply boat, I'd arrive at the mainland landing with no money worth mentioning, a description of myself circulating among Hobb's men, and eleven days — eleven days, I'd counted and recounted them on my fingers until my fingers blurred — until Justice Merrow boarded the mail packet and was gone south for another year. Eleven days to cross three checkpoints, any one of which might hold a man who'd been shown my likeness.

If I stayed, I was asking a stranger to shelter a fugitive without telling her why she should, which was not merely dangerous but — and this is the part that sat worst in me — *ungrateful*. She had dragged me off her rocks. She had heated her blanket stones. She had given me fish and a stove and a name, her actual name, unprompted, while I sat there hoarding mine like a miser.

Guilt, I have found, is not the sharp thing people describe. It's a slow rot. It works at you in warm rooms, by lamplight, over food someone else cooked.

"The boat skips runs sometimes," I said. "Doesn't it? In bad weather."

"It hasn't skipped a run in nine years," she said. "I told you that."

"Suppose it skips tomorrow."

"Then suppose I ask you again what's in the packet."

I looked at her. She looked back. Outside, the wind took the chimney and shook it like a rat.

And I want to record honestly, because this account will otherwise flatter me: I almost told her then. The words were actually in my mouth — *my father was Elias Vane, they hanged him, the magistrate murdered him, and I am carrying the proof against my ribs and I am so tired, God, I am so tired of carrying it alone* — and what stopped me was not caution. Caution would have been wise. What stopped me was the older fear, the deeper one: that she would look at me the way the crowd looked at my father on the steps. Not with belief. With appetite. Everyone wants a piece of a scandal; I had learned that much standing in that yard at nineteen. Give a stranger the truth and you find out what they're made of, and I could not bear, I literally could not physically endure, being looked at like that by the one person in the world who had been kind to me without knowing what I was.

So instead I said, "If the boat doesn't come, I can pay for my keep. I have a little money." Which was a third-rate lie, because what I had was four shillings and my mother's ring, and we both probably knew it.

She studied me for a long moment, and then she did something worse than pressing me further. She nodded, once, and began stacking dishes, and said, "The lamp needs trimming at eight. You can hold the ladder steady, if your hands have stopped shaking."

That was all. No agreement, no refusal, just conscription into the ordinary machinery of her days, as though my future were a fixture to be polished. And I understood, climbing the spiral stair behind her that evening with my palms flat against the cold painted wall, that I had passed some kind of test without knowing what it was — or rather, that she had decided the test could wait.

At the top, in the lamp room, the great lens turned above us, throwing its beam out over the black water, sweeping, sweeping. She checked the wick, adjusted the clockwork, wrote something in the logbook with a pencil stub. I held the ladder, as instructed, and watched her, and thought about how easy it would be to trust someone whose entire profession consisted of putting a light in a window for strangers.

"Keeper," I said, before I could stop myself. "If a person had something — something important, something dangerous — and nowhere safe to carry it, and no one they could give it to—"

"Then they'd better find someone," she said, not turning from the lens, "before the sea decides for them. It's not sentimental. It takes what it likes."

The beam swung round and lit her face, once each revolution: light, dark, light, dark. And in one of those flashes I saw her glance sideways at the oilskin packet, still strapped beneath my coat where I hadn't let it out of my reach since the rocks — and I saw, in that single strobe-lit instant, something in her expression that was not idle curiosity at all.

It was recognition.

She knew that handwriting. I would swear it on anything: in that one white flash she had seen enough of the letter's exposed corner to know it, the way you know a voice through a wall. And I stood gripping the ladder in the turning dark, cold all over despite the stove-warmth trapped in the glass room, understanding that the questions had never ended — they had merely changed hands.

Because I knew where my father's trial had been held. I knew who had attended it, day after day, taking notes nobody ever read. Kenji Nakamura, customs surveyor, the dead keeper's husband, the name nobody on this coast spoke aloud.

And my father had written one letter besides the confession. A short one. To the only man at that trial who had looked at him — really looked at him — with doubt in his eyes.

I had never understood why Father had addressed it to a Japanese customs surveyor he barely knew.

Standing in the light and the dark and the light, I was suddenly, terribly afraid that the keeper below me knew exactly why.
