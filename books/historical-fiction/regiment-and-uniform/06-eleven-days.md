## 6. Eleven Days

I learned the arithmetic of my father's last morning the way you learn anything that matters: by heart, without wanting to.

Eleven days. That was what Sable said, standing at her kitchen window with the rain running down the glass like it was trying to get in and read over our shoulders. Eleven days until Justice Merrow's circuit opened at the county seat of Harrowgate, eleven days until he heard petitions and writs and the ordinary grinding business of other people's crimes, and eleven days after that — no, less, nine — until he boarded the mail packet at Gray Mouth and sailed out of Hobb's reach for another year.

"Nine days of him being reachable," I said. "Two days of him being here."

"And a storm season between us and him." Sable turned from the window. She had that way of speaking as though she were reading off a chart — flat, exact, no wasted syllables. "Three checkpoints on the coast road. The toll bridge at Marlow Bend. The ferry at Gray Mouth. The customs house at the Pike Gate. And every one of them staffed by men who take Hobb's shilling."

"You know this how?"

"I've kept a light on this coast for six years. Boats tell keepers things they don't tell their wives." She sat down across from me at the deal table, and for a moment I saw past the widow's black and the keeper's calm to something older and more tired underneath. "So we don't have eleven days. We have whatever's left after you're well enough to walk, minus the weather."

I looked down at my own hands wrapped around the tin mug of tea she'd pressed on me. They were still mottled from the sea, the nails blue-rimmed, and when I flexed them they ached in a way that felt like an accusation. *You let go of the rail. You let the water take you because holding on hurt more.*

"I'm well enough now," I lied.

Sable looked at me for a long moment. She had gray eyes, the color of the sea before it decides to be cruel, and I understood in that moment that she could see through lies the way her lens cut through fog. She said nothing about the lie. That was worse than calling it.

* * *

Here is what I had not told her, lying in her bed under blankets that smelled of lamp oil and salt while she sat up all night watching me breathe:

My name is not Ruth.

Well — it is Ruth. Ruth Vane. But the girl who washed ashore under Widow's Rock was supposed to be nobody, a milliner's assistant traveling to an aunt, a person so unremarkable that the sea itself would apologize for bothering with her. I had rehearsed it on the coach from London, in the third-class carriage, watching the fields go by and mouthing my false biography to the window glass. *Ruth Marsh. Seamstress. Going to stay with family near Tarn Head.* A biography with no edges, nothing to catch on.

And then the storm had caught the packet boat anyway, and the sea had taken my hat and my ticket and my carefully edgeless story, and thrown me onto rocks below a lighthouse kept by the one woman on this coast who might actually help me — or might, with a word to the magistrate, hang me beside my father.

Because that is what the warrant would say, if anyone looked closely. *Accessory.* The word still made my skin crawl. My father had been dead two years, and they had found a crime for him to share with his only daughter. It was Hobb's little joke, I think. Or Pike's. Keep the family quiet by making silence itself criminal.

I had come north to retrieve the confession. That was all. Papa had smuggled it out of the prison in the weeks before the end — I never learned how; bribed a turnkey, probably, or charmed some clerk who didn't know what he was carrying — and sent it to a shipping agent in Tarn Head with instructions that if a young woman ever came asking after the *Merrow*, a parcel should change hands. The agent had died in the spring. His widow had held the parcel, frightened of it, and had put it on a boat rather than keep it in her house one more night. And the boat had met the storm, and the parcel had met me, and now the whole rotten weight of it sat in an oilskin wrapper on the dresser of a stranger's kitchen while its rightful courier lay half-drowned in a chair.

I should have gone straight inland. I knew that. Every hour at the lighthouse was an hour Hobb's men might spend looking for wreckage, survivors, a girl described by a boatman who'd seen me board. But when Sable had pulled me out of the rocks — I remembered fragments of it: hands on my collar, the scrape of stone, a voice saying *hold on, hold on, I have you* with the flat certainty of someone reporting the tide table — when she'd carried me up the stairs and stripped off my soaked coat and put tea in my hands without asking a single question, something in me had simply... stopped. Stopped running. Stopped lying, mostly. Stopped being alone, which was the dangerous thing, because alone was safe and safe was what I'd been for two years and it hadn't saved Papa.

* * *

On the second evening she asked, not unkindly, "Who taught you to lie?"

We were washing dishes. It seemed to be the hour for confessions at Widow's Rock; the light above us turned its slow wheel, and the wind worried at the tower like a dog at a bone, and there was nowhere either of us could go.

"I don't lie," I said.

"The packet boat. You told me you were a seamstress going to your aunt's. Then you talked in your sleep, and you said a name — Merrow. And when I asked how you came to be swimming in a gale off Tarn Head, you said you'd been swept off the pier at Whitby. There is no pier at Whitby a swell could take you from in October, and no aunt within forty miles of here, and your hands are a clerk's hands, not a seamstress's. Ink stains." She set a plate in the rack. "You lie badly, Ruth. Which tells me two things. One, you're not a liar by trade. Two, whoever you're hiding from frightens you more than drowning did."

The water in the basin had gone cold. I watched a thread of grease spiral on its surface and thought: *tell her nothing, tell her nothing,* and then thought: *she pulled you out of the sea and burned her own logbook draft for you, and you owe her the truth or at least a down payment on it.*

"My father was hanged two winters ago," I said. "At Kirkby Gaol. For treason." I made myself look up. "His name was Elias Vane."

I expected the mug to drop, or the color to leave her face, or something dramatic. What happened instead was smaller and stranger. Sable went very still — the stillness of the tower itself, of things built to stand through weather — and then she said, quietly, "I know. My husband attended the trial. He came home and didn't speak for three days."

"Your husband—"

"Is dead too. Since spring. An accident on the rocks." She dried her hands on her apron with great care, each finger separately, a ritual, an armor. "So. Elias Vane's daughter. In my kitchen. Carrying—" she nodded at the oilskin packet on the dresser "—something worth drowning for."

"A confession. His. Written before—" My voice did something humiliating in the middle of the sentence, cracked like a girl's, and I hated it. "He wrote out everything. Who paid him to look away, and then who framed him when he stopped looking away, and why. And there's a ledger. Payments. Dates. Signatures."

"Whose signature?"

I took a breath. "The magistrate's. Corliss Hobb. The same magistrate who tried him."

For a long time the only sound was the wind and the wheel of the light overhead, that patient mechanical heartbeat. Then Sable said, "Sit down, Ruth Vane," and it was not a request, and I sat.

* * *

She read the letter first, alone, at the table while I pretended to mend a tear in my sleeve and watched her face instead. I will say this for her: she gave nothing away. Her eyes moved down the page and her expression stayed level as ballast, and only her thumb, pressing whiter and whiter against the paper, betrayed that anything inside her was moving at all.

When she finished, she laid the pages down square, aligned their edges precisely, and said, "Say the dates again. Slowly."

I said them. Circuit opens at Harrowgate in eleven days. Merrow sits nine days. Mail packet sails on the twelfth day, tide permitting, which in October meant tide *not* permitting, which meant it might sail early to beat the weather, which meant our margin was thinner than the paper the confession was written on.

"And the checkpoints," she said. "You mean to carry this through three customs posts on an open road, in storm season, with Hobb's own men at all three, and a description of you already circulating from the wreck site."

"Do you have a better idea?"

"No." She almost smiled; it was a grim, small thing, gone as fast as it came. "I have a worse one and fewer choices. Sit properly. We're going to plan this like a watch rotation, because a plan that can't survive a bad night isn't a plan, it's a wish."

So we planned it like a watch rotation. She fetched a slate and chalked it up the way, I gathered, she chalked the light's consumption of oil — everything measured, everything counted.

"One," she wrote. "The toll bridge at Marlow Bend. Customs officer named Brack. Bored, fat, takes his dinner at the inn on the far side. He'll search a cart and wave through mourners." She glanced at my black dress — my father's mourning, worn so long it had become simply my skin. "We travel as sisters going to bury an aunt. Grief is better than a pass. No one searches grief thoroughly; it embarrasses them."

"You've done this before," I said.

"I've watched boats run contraband past this light for six years and wondered who let them." Her jaw tightened. "Now I know who. Two. The ferry at Gray Mouth. Ferryman named Cobb — no relation to our problem — honest but poor, and the crossing costs more than it should. If the weather holds, fine. If it doesn't, we'll need coin I don't have."

"I have four pounds and eleven shillings," I said, "and my mother's ring, which is paste, and my father's watch, which is not."

"Noted. Three. The Pike Gate." She wrote the words and looked at them for a moment the way you'd look at a reef marked on a chart. "Fortified customs house. Garrisoned. Commanded by Sergeant Aldous Pike."

"Pike." The name tasted like copper. "Hobb's chief man. He was my father's clerk, once. Before he sold him."

"Then he knows your face."

"He knew it when I was fifteen. People forget faces in five years." Even as I said it, I heard how thin it was. Sable heard it too, and had the grace not to say so.

"Three posts," she said, chalking a line under the list. "One bored, one bought, one personal. And behind all of them, riders. Once Hobb hears a survivor came ashore at Widow's Rock — and he will hear it, because wreckage washes south along this coast into every fishing village and fishermen talk — he'll send men up the road asking after two women. So we are not two women traveling together where it can be avoided. We split at Gray Mouth if we must. One carries the ledger. One carries the letter. Never both together, never both on the same road."

"Split up?" My voice climbed before I could stop it. "After — you pulled me out of the sea, and now you want to—"

"I want the evidence to reach Harrowgate," she said, flat and hard and not unkind, the way you'd speak to someone standing too close to a cliff edge. "Not us. Understand the difference, Ruth. We are couriers. The package matters; the courier is replaceable." She stopped. Something moved behind her eyes — some memory, some grave only she attended. "That is what a keeper learns. The light must burn whether the keeper sleeps or drowns. You arrange things so that the work survives the worker."

"That's a terrible philosophy."

"It's a working one." She wiped the slate clean except for the single figure at the top: **11**. "Eleven days. Tomorrow we mend what needs mending, and I teach you the road. Day after, we walk."

* * *

I couldn't sleep that night. The tower hummed above me, the lamp's machinery turning in its endless patient circle, and I lay in Sable's narrow bed listening to the wind and doing sums that wouldn't close.

Four pounds eleven. Three checkpoints. Nine usable days. One judge. One chance.

And underneath the sums, the other arithmetic, the one I couldn't chalk anywhere: *if we fail, Papa stays hanged forever.* Not just dead — condemned. History would keep him a traitor because history is written by magistrates with ledgers. Every year the ink would set harder. And Ruth Vane, accessory, would live out her edgeless life under an edgeless name in some southern town, flinching at uniforms, and the truth would sit at the bottom of the sea where the storm tried to put it.

Unless.

I got up, barefoot on cold boards, and went down to the kitchen. Sable was there — of course she was; I think she slept the way the light burns, in short measured intervals, always half-awake — sitting with the ledger open under the lamp, reading it again, page by page, lips slightly moving.

"You should rest," I said.

"So should you." But she didn't look up, and after a moment she turned the ledger toward me and tapped one entry with a blunt fingernail. "This date. The fourteenth of March, two years ago. A payment recorded the week before the arrest. Do you know what happened that week?"

"They arrested him."

"They arrested him *after* the payment cleared," Sable said. "Which means the frame wasn't improvised. It was scheduled. Booked like cargo." She closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a coffin. "Your father didn't die of treachery in the abstract, Ruth. He died of an entry in a book. And books can be read aloud."

I stood there in the lamplight with my arms around myself and felt something shift in my chest — fear turning over, slowly, like a keel coming about, and becoming something with more iron in it. Not courage exactly. Courage sounded calm in stories. This was closer to anger wearing courage's coat.

"Eleven days," I said.

"Ten, now." Sable banked the lamp. "Get your sleep, Ruth Vane. Tomorrow I show you the coast road, and you learn where every ditch and hedge stands between here and Harrowgate — because if the plan goes wrong, and plans do, you'll need to know the country well enough to disappear into it."

"And what will you be learning?"

She paused at the door, silhouetted against the dark stairwell, the light-wheel turning faintly above her head like a slow silver crown.

"Me?" she said. "Tomorrow I start practicing how to bury an aunt."

* * *

But sleep wouldn't come even then, and near dawn I heard her boots on the stair, and through the shutter-crack I watched her go out into the gray blowing morning and stand at the head of the rocks, looking south — down the coast road, toward Marlow Bend, toward Gray Mouth, toward the Pike Gate, toward everything waiting for us there like stations of a cross we hadn't earned. She stood a long time. Then she went to the supply shed, and I heard her counting under her breath — oil, bread, matches, cordage — the keeper's litany, everything measured, everything counted, nothing wasted.

Including, I understood then, me.

I lay back and did the sum once more, and this time it closed. Ten days. Three gates. One book.

It wasn't hope, quite. Hope was a luxury with a tariff we couldn't pay. But it was a heading, and headings were enough to sail by, and when I finally slept I dreamed not of the rope or the water but of a courtroom in Harrowgate, and a small dry voice reading numbers aloud while a magistrate's face came apart like wet paper — and somewhere behind it, steady as a lamp, a woman in widow's black who had decided, for reasons she hadn't fully explained even to herself, that my father's name was worth the crossing.

We would leave on the morning tide.

God help whoever stood at the first gate.
