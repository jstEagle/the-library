## 7. The Logbook Habit

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a lighthouse kitchen at four in the morning, and it is not the peaceful sort they put on biscuit tins. It is the silence of two people who have each decided not to say something, sitting across from one another like uninvited guests.

I had made tea because tea was what I knew how to do with my hands when my hands were not to be trusted. The girl — Ruth, she'd said, though I already had my doubts about that name, delivered as it had been a half-second too late, like an actor reaching for a prop — sat wrapped in Kenji's old boat cloak, holding her cup in both fists as if it might bolt. She had drunk three cups without tasting any of them, which I know because I refilled them and watched her face do nothing whatsoever.

"You should sleep," I said.

"Later." A word she said the way some people say *never*.

I let it go. There would be time for interrogation by daylight, or there wouldn't. Meanwhile there was a thing I did every morning of my adult life, storm or calm, sick or well, grieving or not, and that thing did not care about my circumstances. The log waits for no man. Or woman. The log is very democratic that way.

* * *

The keeper's logbook is a peculiar document when you think about it, which mostly keepers don't, because thinking about it too hard makes you feel like a monk copying scripture you only half believe. Date, hour, wind, sea state, barometer. Lamp trimmed at such-and-such an hour. Oil consumed. Fog signal run from whenever to whenever. And then, in the space marked *Remarks*, whatever the night gave you.

Most nights the remarks are dull as ditchwater. *Steamer *Corinthian* passed south, showing proper lights. Fishing fleet out at dawn, eleven sail.* Some nights the remarks are the last honest record of something terrible. A light seen where no light should be. A flare. A name written down so that somebody, somewhere ashore, will eventually bother to count boats.

Kenji used to say the logbook was the truest diary ever kept, because nobody writes a diary expecting strangers to read it, but a keeper writes his log knowing the Board will inspect it, and still — still — we write the truth, because the truth about the sea has saved more lives than any lie ever told. He said this with his particular smile, the one that meant he had thought about it longer than he wanted me to know. He wrote beautiful entries. Precise, dry, and underneath it all, if you knew him, funny. Once, after the district inspector spent an entire visit lecturing him on the correct angle of the lamp-room brass, Kenji logged: *Inspector's visit concluded. Brass inspected. Weather fair. Inspector fair to middling.*

They made him take it out. He kept the draft page in a drawer for years and took it out on bad days like other men take out whiskey.

I sat down at the table with the logbook open to the fresh day and dipped the pen, and I found my hand was steady, which surprised me, given everything.

*October 3rd. Wind SW gale veering WNW by midnight, moderating toward dawn. Sea very heavy. Barometer 28.90 rising. Lamp lit 6:40 p.m., extinguished 5:55 a.m. Oil consumed, 2 gal. 3 pt. Fog signal not required. During the night, heavy seas over the lower rocks. At approx. 4 a.m. observed wreckage below tower. On inspection at first light, recovered from the rocks one person, female, young, alive but exhausted and half-drowned. Recovered also one oilskin packet, contents private. Survivor recovering by the stove. No vessel visible. No distress rockets observed during the night.*

I read it back. Every word of it true. Every word of it a problem.

Because here is what the log does not say, and here is what the log cannot say, and here is the trouble with honesty, which is that it is never merely honest. The moment you write a survivor's name in a government book, that girl stops being a girl and becomes a line item. The moment you note "oilskin packet, contents private," you have told every inspector, every coastguard, every bored clerk in the county office that something came ashore worth noting. And the moment — this is the part I understood while my pen was still wet, the ink shining on the page like something freshly wounded — the moment anyone official reads *female, young, alive*, someone will come to Widow's Rock to ask her questions, and she will answer them badly, because I had already watched her fail to answer three simple ones over tea.

She hadn't known what day it was. Not the date — the *year*. She'd corrected herself fast, said the storm had knocked her senseless, and that was plausible enough; the sea does scramble heads. But she'd flinched at the name Vane the way you flinch at a slammed door, and she looked at the packet the way you look at a sleeping dog you're afraid of waking.

A body washed up on Widow's Rock carrying a sealed packet is news. News travels to the magistrate's office faster than gulls follow a fish cart. And I don't know why that thought made my stomach drop the way it did. I had no reason to distrust Magistrate Hobb beyond the ordinary reasons anyone distrusts a magistrate, which is to say, none at all, really, just the general human suspicion that men who wear chains of office have usually bought the chain.

But there was something else, older and less reasonable. Two winters ago, Kenji had gone into town for the hanging.

I want to be clear about what that means, because it sounds strange even now. Keepers don't go to hangings. Keepers are half-monks; their parish is the tide table. But Elias Vane had been a fixture on that coast before his fall — customs surveyor, they called him, though everyone knew he was really the man who counted what the smugglers didn't want counted — and when he was convicted, half the county turned out as though for a fair. Kenji went, and I asked him why, and he said, "Somebody should watch for the family." There wasn't any family present, as it happened. Or if there was, they stood at the back where nobody looked. Kenji came home quiet in a way I had learned to recognize as the quiet of a man who has seen something done in his name, in all our names, that did not sit right in his stomach. He said only: "He protested innocence to the last. They always say that, of course." A pause. "But he said it *well*."

Then he never spoke of it again, and neither did I, because that is marriage.

Now a drowned bird of a girl was shivering at my stove clutching a packet, and the handwriting inside it — I had seen only a corner of one page, an accident of oilskin come loose — was a cramped, slanted, urgent hand, and something in the back of my skull whispered *you have seen that hand before*, and I could not for the life of me think where, and I hated myself for wanting to look again.

* * *

Ruth slept at last around seven, folded into the settle like a letter into an envelope, the oilskin packet tucked under her cheek. I had offered to hold it for her and watched her whole body close like a fist, so I let it be. Wise, probably. Trust is a door you open from your own side.

I climbed the tower and put the lamp to bed — curtain drawn, lens covered, the great glass eyes blindfolded until dusk. Then I went back down, and I sat with the logbook, and I did the arithmetic of my life.

Here is what a falsified entry actually costs. People imagine it's like theft, a clean transaction: you gain safety, you pay truth. It isn't. It's more like letting a little water into the hull. One entry, nothing. You'd never notice. But you *know*. You know the water is in there, sloshing about below the floorboards, and every entry after that one, you'll hear it. A keeper's log is the one place in her life where a keeper stands up straight. Corrupt it, and every future entry leans a little.

And yet.

If I wrote the truth, the machinery would start. Coastguard notified. Wreck report filed. Questions asked by men whose questions are never idle. And a frightened girl with a packet full of — of something, of a dead surveyor's secrets — would be handed over to the very offices she'd apparently risked the sea to avoid. Because she *had* risked the sea. Nobody comes through those rocks by accident. Those rocks have eaten better sailors than whoever steered her toward them; the fact that she arrived alive at all meant she'd been aiming, desperate, for the one light on this coast, God help her, mine.

Or — crueler thought, and it came anyway — she'd been aiming elsewhere entirely and the sea had simply been indifferent, in its usual way, and I had caught what the tide threw me, like a piece of driftwood with a story attached.

Either way, the choice was the same. Write her down, and give her to the county. Leave her out, and make her mine.

I thought of Kenji watching that hanging. Somebody should watch for the family. Well. Here, possibly, was family, washed up on my doorstep like a parcel with the address soaked off. And here was I, with a pen in my hand, deciding whether to sign for it.

* * *

I wrote the draft on scrap paper first, which was itself a confession of intent. Honest keepers write the log straight through. Only a keeper planning to lie drafts first.

*October 3rd.* Wind, barometer, lamp hours, oil — all of that stayed, because that part was innocent and verifiable and lying about oil consumption is the sort of small treason that gets a keeper dismissed without ceremony. The remarks were the question. I wrote three versions.

Version one, the truth. I have set it out above. I burned it in the stove and watched the words curl, *female, young, alive* going last, stubbornly legible almost to the end, as if arguing.

Version two, the coward's version: *Nothing unusual to report.* This is the entry of a keeper who slept soundly, and since I had not slept soundly, and since any coastguard man walking my beach at low water would find timber and torn canvas scattered among the rocks like the aftermath of a party, this version would not survive its first inspection. A lie must at least be *plausible*, or it is worse than the truth, because the truth merely informs people and a bad lie arouses them. I burned that too.

Version three took me the longest, and it cost me the most, and I want to record exactly what it cost, because that is rather the point of this exercise.

Version three: *During the night, heavy seas over the lower rocks. At dawn observed considerable wreckage below the tower — planking, canvas, one ship's chest stove open and empty. No bodies recovered. No survivors observed. Wreckage consistent with a small coaster; name unknown. Remains gathered and burned above high-water mark per standing orders.*

Do you see what it does? It tells the truth about the wreck — the wreckage was real, anyone can verify the wreckage — and it lies only about the one thing that matters, which is her. *No survivors observed.* Four words. Four small words, and under them, a living girl breathing at my stove.

It even explains itself. In a storm like last night's, a survivor thrown onto those rocks would more likely than not be smashed or swept away before dawn; no one ashore would marvel that I found none. The empty chest suggests salvage — and salvage, unfortunately, suggests scavengers, and scavengers suggest that anything of value was already carried off by persons unknown, which gives any future searcher a lovely wild goose to chase down the coast while she sits safe in my kitchen drinking her fourth cup of tea without tasting it.

Kenji would have seen through it in a heartbeat. That was the worst part. Not the Board's inspectors, not the coastguard — Kenji, reading over my shoulder with his particular smile, saying nothing, just looking at me with those patient dark eyes that missed nothing, while the little water sloshed in below my floorboards forever.

I copied version three into the log in my best round hand. My hand shook on *No survivors observed*, and the shake shows, a tiny tremor in the downstroke of the *v*, and I sat there considering whether to rule a fresh page and copy it again, and then I laughed — alone in my kitchen at eight in the morning, laughing like a madwoman — because the tremor was the most honest thing on the page, and I decided to leave it. Let some inspector a hundred years from now squint at that little wobble and wonder what the widow of Widow's Rock was feeling that October morning. He'll never know. That is the luxury of being dead when your lies are read.

I fed the remaining drafts to the stove. The paper caught quickly, being good rag stock, and the ink flashed green-blue as it burned, which I had never noticed ink doing before, and I watched it the way you watch anything vanish.

* * *

By noon the weather had settled into the long grey grumble that follows a gale — not calm, exactly, but tired, like a dog after a fight. I worked the routine: polished what wanted polishing, checked the fog signal's steam valve, inventoried the oil store against the delivery that had not come.

That was the other thing gnawing at me, under everything else. The supply boat had skipped its run. Nine years on this rock and it had never once missed — not in the March gales, not the year the pilot broke his arm, never. Missed runs mean weather, or breakdown, or a changed schedule, and none of those explanations had arrived with a note, because no explanation had arrived at all. A supply boat that misses its run without sending word is either careless or forbidden, and I did not care for either possibility.

Add it to the pile, then: a stranger from the sea, a packet of dead-man's letters, a missed boat, a girl who lied badly and flinched at names. I stood at the kitchen window with my tea going cold and looked out at the rocks where the tide was uncovering the wreckage again, brown ribs of some poor coaster showing and hiding, showing and hiding, like the truth about anything.

Ruth woke around two, grey-faced and steadier, and ate the bread and cheese I put in front of her like a person remembering that bodies require maintenance. She looked at the stove, at the ashes, and something crossed her face — she'd seen the burnt scraps, perhaps, or smelled the paper — and she said nothing, and I said nothing, and the two silences sat down at the table with us like old friends of the family.

"I need to go to town," she said finally.

"No."

"It's not a request—"

"It wasn't an answer." I set down my cup. "Girl, you cannot walk into any town on this coast for a fortnight. The wreck will be reported — I've reported it, in a manner of speaking, which was the least dishonest way to protect you, and you're welcome, by the way — and there will be men looking along the shore for whatever the sea gave up. You go to town now, you may as well deliver yourself."

"Deliver myself to *whom*?" And there it was again, that flinch, that slammed-door jumpiness. She knew whom. She knew precisely whom, and she wasn't telling.

"To whoever is looking," I said, "which you know better than I do, and which you'll tell me when you're ready, or you won't, and I'll decide how much longer you stay under my roof regardless, because that is apparently the kind of fool I am now." I heard my own voice sharpen and reined it. "You nearly died on my rocks last night. Whatever you're carrying, it nearly killed you getting it here. So we are going to do this properly: you rest today. Tomorrow, we talk plainly — plainly, mind, none of your Ruth-and-a-storm-did-it nonsense — and *then* we decide what happens next. Together. Because whether you like it or not, my logbook says there was no survivor on these rocks, and that makes you my lie now, and I keep what's mine."

She stared at me. Something moved behind her eyes — relief, I think, arriving sideways, dressed as wariness, the way relief does in people who've been hunted.

"And the packet," I said. "Before tomorrow. You'll show me what's in it. All of it." I held up a hand before she could close like a fist again. "Not because I'm curious. Because I burned my first lie for you this morning, and I don't burn things for strangers. Whoever you are — whoever sent you — I'll need to know what I'm keeping safe, and why, and from whom. That's not curiosity. That's the price of the stove."

The fire ticked. Out past the point, the sea went on rearranging itself, indifferent, eternal, dropping things on rocks and taking them away again.

Ruth looked down at her hands for a long moment. When she looked up, the fear in her face had changed shape — less like a rabbit's fear and more like a person standing at a door she'd been leaning against for years, finally trying the handle.

"Tonight," she said. "After dark. And Mrs.—" She stopped. Blinked. "I don't actually know your name."

"Sable Nakamura," I said, and heard myself say it aloud for the first time in six years, and the sound of it filled the kitchen like something returning on the tide.
