## 8. Checkpoint One: The Toll Bridge

I had never considered, before that morning, how much of grief is costume.

We spent an hour in the loft above Marta Quill's cousin's stable — no, that comes later; forgive me, the days have since knotted themselves together like wet rope — we spent an hour in the back room of a lodging house in Fennwick, and Sable turned me into a mourner the way other women turn flour into bread: without ceremony, without wasted motion, with the grim competence of someone who has done her grieving already and knows exactly what it weighs.

"Arms up," she said.

I raised them. She wound black crepe around my bodice with quick turns of her wrist, pinning as she went, and I stood there like a maypole for mourning, watching her face in the spotted mirror. It was a face doing arithmetic. Not the sums of cloth and pin, either. Sums of risk, of distance, of how long a customs officer's attention lasts before boredom wins.

"You'll be the younger sister," she said. "Grief sits strangely on the young. People expect it to look wrong on you, so whatever you do will look right."

"And what do I do?"

"Nothing. That's the whole art of it. You are too sad to speak. You stare at the middle distance and think about your father."

"I don't have to pretend to think about my father," I said, and heard my own voice go flat as a dropped plate.

Sable's hands paused at the last pin. In the mirror our eyes met, hers dark and level over my shoulder.

"No," she said quietly. "You don't. Good. Then it will be easy."

It was not easy. But I'm ahead of myself.

* * *

The ledger rode against Sable's body. That was her decision and I argued it and lost, which should have been my first lesson in who commanded this expedition, because I had thought — foolishly, being twenty-three and having read novels — that the person carrying the evidence would be the person with the most to lose, and that was me. My father's confession. My father's hand. My inheritance, if you could call eleven pages of damning figures an inheritance, which by then I had learned you could.

"It can't be you," Sable said, sewing by lamplight with a sailmaker's needle she'd bought from a man who didn't ask questions because she paid him not to. "If they search you, they search the Vane girl. If they search me, they search a widow going to bury her brother. A widow is the most boring creature God ever made. I intend to be magnificent at it."

She'd unpicked the seam of her mourning skirt — the heavy black one, the one she told me she'd worn every day for two years until the crepe had gone grey as ash — and set the oilskin packet flat into the hem band, between two layers of wool, then stitched it closed with stitches so fine I couldn't find them when she handed it to me to check. The ledger lay against her hip like a second bone.

"Won't it show when you walk?"

"A widow walks stiff anyway," she said. "Grief sets in the joints."

* * *

The toll bridge at Marle Cross spanned the River Orr where the coast road squeezed down to a single lane, and it was exactly what its name promised and nothing more: stone piers, timber deck, a toll house squatting at the far end like a toad, and beside the toll house a customs post flying the Crown's flag at half-mast for some royal death or other, which Sable said was the luckiest thing to happen all week, because flags at half-mast put officers in mind of funerals and funerals made them sentimental instead of thorough.

We came down to the bridge on foot, having paid a carter to drop us at the crossroads two miles back. He'd been talkative. Sable had been perfect — a widow answering questions about weather and roads in a voice like a closed door — while I sat with my hands folded and my eyes lowered and practiced being nobody.

The rain had stopped before dawn but everything it touched still dripped. The river ran high and brown under the bridge, carrying branches and one indignant sheep, and the mist came off the water in slow coils, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt almost obscene, *my father crossed bridges like this. My father was walked across bridges like this, in irons, to his trial,* and then I stopped thinking it because my knees informed me that thinking it was a luxury we couldn't afford.

Two horses tied outside the customs post. Two riders' wet capes hanging on pegs. Magistrate's livery, dark blue with the silver Orr-and-Crown badge.

Sable saw them the same moment I did. Her step didn't change. Mine did — a hitch, half a heartbeat — and her hand came to my elbow, light as a moth landing, and steered me onward as though the hitch were simply grief, as though sisters steadied each other over rivers every day of their lives. Which, I suppose, they do.

"Mrs. Nakamura," I murmured through my teeth. "Riders."

"Hush. You're too sad to know their names."

"That's your name you just—"

"Grieve more quietly, Ruth."

* * *

The customs officer was young, which was the first mercy, and bored, which was the second, and cold, which nearly undid both.

He sat inside the toll house behind a slat desk with a stove that smoked, stamping papers with the enthusiasm of a man stamping his own death warrant, and he looked up at our approach with the expression of a man who has asked four hundred travelers the same four hundred questions and expects nothing from the rest of eternity but more of the same.

"Names and business." He didn't stand. His tea steamed at his elbow and he guarded it with his forearm the way a man guards a drink in rough company.

"Nakamura," Sable said. "Widow. This is my sister. We're traveling to Tarnmouth for my brother-in-law's funeral. Thursday last he took ill; Sunday he was buried in name only, the ground being frozen, and the stone-laying is Friday. We're late because the roads." She let the sentence die, which was masterly. Roads need no explanation in November. Roads explain themselves.

The officer wrote *Nakamura* with a small frown, the letters giving him trouble, and something moved through me like ice water finding a seam. The name. The name was real — Sable had insisted on it, over my objection, over my outright protest — *"a false name is a rope they can hang you with twice, but my name has hung on a doorplate for nine years without anyone reading it"* — and here was a frown bending over it, here were the letters K-N-A-K being sounded out by a bored young man with a stamp.

"Nakamura," he said. "Foreign."

"My husband was Japanese," Sable said. "I am not. My sister is not. Our brother-in-law was Mr. Edwin Pryce of Tarnmouth, born and bred, and if you require his death certificate I regret it is with the vicar, along with our letter of introduction, because we left in haste and sorrow and I have been organizing papers since we left with less success than I'd like."

It was beautiful. It was genuinely beautiful — the way she offered him the missing documents herself, the way she made the absence of proof sound like the natural consequence of death rather than its purpose. I watched the officer's suspicion rise like a fish toward bait and then sink again, uninterested, because suspicion is work and his tea was getting cold.

"Purpose of crossing?"

"I believe I've said. A funeral."

"Anything to declare? Goods, correspondence, parcels for persons beyond the river?"

"Only mourning," Sable said, "and not much of that. Grief doesn't pack well."

And the young man — I could have kissed him — actually smiled, a tired twitch, the smile of every clerk everywhere confronted by a woman older than himself who declined to be frightened of him, and he waved us toward the door with his stamp.

"Quarter penny each, toll. Move along, then. Mind the deck boards, third one from the pier is rotten."

That was the moment I understood we might live. And that was the moment, naturally, that the universe — which had been eavesdropping, and resents human hope the way cats resent closed doors — sent in the sergeant.

* * *

I heard him before I saw him: boots on the boardwalk, unhurried, and a voice saying to someone inside, "...no, leave the riders where they are, Hobb wants them rested before the afternoon—" and then he filled the doorway.

He was fifty maybe, built like a bollard, with a beard going iron-grey and small eyes that had done a great deal of looking at things people wished he wouldn't look at. The silver badge on his cape was brighter than the young officer's. Seniority, worn like the livery itself: polished daily.

"Afternoon, ma'am." To Sable, but the small eyes went to me first, catalogued me — height, age, travel-worn boots, red eyes, which were genuine, thank God; I hadn't needed to fake those since the second day — and dismissed me, and came back to Sable with more interest than I liked.

"Good afternoon, Sergeant," Sable said, and gave him the widow's nod, three degrees of the head, no more.

"Crossing for the funeral at Tarnmouth?" He'd heard. Of course he'd heard; he'd been standing within earshot. "Pryce, was it? Know the family. Sad business."

My heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as falling downstairs in reverse.

"Did you," Sable said, in a voice of mild, weary courtesy that contained, somewhere far beneath it, a blade sliding free. "Then you'll know better than I whether the stone-mason's work is done. The vicar writes that the ground was too hard for the marker until spring thaw."

"Ah." A pause. The small eyes narrowed a fraction. "Well. I knew a Pryce family, years back. Fish merchants. May be thinking of another lot." He smiled, and the smile showed strong teeth. "Lots of Pryces on this coast. Lots of grief too, this time of year. Where was it you said you'd come from, ma'am? North road, was it?"

"The coast road," Sable said. "From Widow's Rock direction, if the driver took the turn I paid him for. He talked the whole way and I confess I listened to perhaps a tenth of it."

"Widow's Rock." He said it the way a man says a word he's been given on a list. "Lighthouse out there. Keeper's a widow too, I'm told."

"Is she," said Sable. "Then she and I have that in common, and little else worth comparing."

Silence. The stove ticked. The river muttered under the boards. Somewhere behind me the young officer stamped a paper with a sound like a small gunshot, and I flinched — flinched visibly, shoulders jumping — and the sergeant's eyes cut to me and stayed there.

"And the sister," he said. "Crying fit to break. Very natural. Very fitting." He stepped closer to the desk, close enough that I could smell wet wool and pipe smoke on him. "Only it strikes me, ma'am — and correct me if I overstep, I'm only a plain man doing plain work — that a lady traveling with papers left behind, foreign name, sister who jumps at sounds, heading for Tarnmouth the very week the assize judges are expected..." He let it hang. Men like him love letting things hang. It saves them the labor of accusation; they let you accuse yourself. "Strikes me it might be worth a look in the luggage. If there is luggage."

"There is no luggage," Sable said. "We carry what we wear. Search my pockets if it pleases you, Sergeant, and you'll find a railway timetable, a prayer book, and forty-one shillings I will count aloud before and after, because I've heard what happens to money in customs houses."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Recalculation. Bored men can be managed, but this one wasn't bored anymore, and Sable's boldness, which would have finished the young officer entirely, was landing on this one like blows on a locked door. He wasn't retreating. He was deciding.

And then he did the worst thing. He reached out — casually, the gesture of a man adjusting his cape, or so it was meant to look — and took hold of a fold of Sable's skirt where it swung near his hand, weighing the wool between finger and thumb the way a fishmonger weighs a fish.

"Heavy cloth," he said. "For mourning."

"Ground's frozen," Sable said. "Graves are dug deep. Widows dress for the weather, Sergeant, not for fashion. If you'll excuse us — the tide won't wait for Tarnmouth and neither will the mason."

He didn't let go.

I want to be honest in this account, because honesty is the entire point of it, my father died for the lack of it — I want to be honest and say that in that moment I did nothing. I stood with my hands folded and my heart hammering and my mind running uselessly through prayers I didn't believe in, addressed to a God I'd stopped billing with requests sometime during the trial, and I did nothing, because I was too sad to speak, which had been the plan, and the plan was now a cage.

It was the young officer who saved us. Providence wears many uniforms; that day it wore a bad one and wanted its tea.

"Sergeant." A touch of apology in it. "Begging pardon — those riders, sir, the magistrate's men? They're asking after you. Something about the afternoon patrol and the quarry road."

The sergeant's hand opened. The fold of wool dropped. For one moment his small eyes went from the officer to Sable to me and back, filing us away in whatever cabinet such men keep, and I understood that we were not safe, merely postponed; that we had been weighed and found interesting, and interesting was a debt that collected interest.

"Mind how you go, ladies," he said. "Roads are bad past the ferry. Full of folk walking who ought to be riding." And he touched his hat brim — insolently, precisely, the way a cat touches a bird it has decided not to eat yet — and turned back into the customs house.

* * *

We crossed the bridge. Third board from the pier, rotten, as promised; Sable stepped over it without looking and I nearly went through it, because my eyes were full of the toll house shrinking behind us.

Neither of us spoke until the road bent and the bridge was lost in the river mist. Then Sable laughed — a short, low, terrible laugh with nothing in it of humor.

"Forty-one shillings," she said. "I said forty-one because I counted them this morning and there were thirty-eight, and I thought, if I invent a number he'll want to check it, but if I state a true number wrongly he'll assume I miscounted, and no one arrests a woman for miscounting."

"That's insane."

"That's customs work," she said. "Kenji taught me. He said every official is a lock, and locks are beaten by knowing what the locksmith was afraid of. That one fears his superiors. So I gave him a superior to fear." Her arm came through mine, formal as church, two mourning sisters walking. "Now walk slower. We were too fast coming off that bridge. Grieving women don't hurry."

"And what," I said, when my voice would obey me, "do we do about the fact that he knows the word *Nakamura*, and knows there's a keeper at Widow's Rock, and will spend his evening putting those two facts together like a man doing a child's puzzle?"

Sable was quiet for several steps. The mist thickened ahead of us, and somewhere in it a cart creaked, and a dog barked twice at something unseen.

"We walk faster," she said.

But that was not the worst of what the day held, though I didn't learn it until dusk, when we reached the market town and its lamplit square and the fishmongers packing up their stalls — because it was there, among the crates and the ice and the smell of salt, that an old man with a gaff over his shoulder looked hard at Sable's face, frowned, and said a name I had never heard her speak.

A name that made her stop as though shot.
