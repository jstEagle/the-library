## 9. The Man Who Knew Her Husband

The market town of Grayling's Cross smelled of wet wool and herring, which is to say it smelled like every market town on this coast, which is to say it smelled like home, or at least like the memory of home that had been left out in the rain too long.

I had not been into Grayling's Cross in four years. There had been no reason. The supply boat came to me; the world, such as I required of it, came by water. A town is a thing you can avoid if you are willing to pay for the avoidance in small comforts — fresh bread, thread, the sound of more than one voice at a time — and I had paid gladly. Widow's Rock did not want company. I told myself that so often I almost believed it.

Now I walked its high street with a dead man's confession sewn into my mourning skirt and a girl on my arm who was lying her way through grief with the skill of a first-year actress, and I found I remembered every stone.

* * *

Ruth — Ruth, who was not Ruth, who was as much a Ruth as I am a plain widow with nothing in my past but salt — clung to my elbow in the manner we had rehearsed on the coast road. Grieving sisters, traveling to a funeral. It was a good cover because it asked for no performance beyond what we could both supply honestly. She *was* grieving. I *was* in mourning. The rest was costume.

"Keep your chin down," I murmured. "Grief looks modest."

"And what does fear look like?" she whispered back.

"Fear looks exactly like grief. That's why the disguise works."

We had errands, real ones, because a pair of sisters traveling to a funeral who buy nothing will be remembered for buying nothing. We bought black ribbon. We bought bread, cheese, a twist of tea. We bought a candle for the funeral, beeswax, because Ruth insisted her father would have wanted beeswax and then went quiet when she realized she'd said it aloud. I paid for all of it with coins counted out slow, a widow careful with her purse. Every transaction was a small play, and I had learned long ago how to stand on a stage without moving my face.

It was at the fishmonger's stall — the third one down, where the ice glittered under a canvas awning and the smell hit you like an open door — that everything came apart.

Not loudly. That's the thing about the moments that break you. They rarely announce themselves. They arrive quietly, wearing an ordinary face.

An old man sat on a stool behind the stall, gutting mackerel with a knife worn thin as a communion wafer. He looked up as I passed, and his eyes went from my face to my hands to the hem of my skirt, and he said, in the mild voice of a man asking about weather:

"Nakamura?"

I stopped. You understand, I had to make myself stop, because every muscle I owned had already voted for walking on.

He said it again, softer, testing it: "Nakamura. Kenji Nakamura's wife."

The market noise went on around us — gulls, bargaining, a cart wheel complaining — but for me the whole street dropped away like a curtain falling. Four years. Four years since anyone had spoken that name aloud to me. Four years of signing *Sable Nakamura* only in the logbook, where no one read past the surname, and even there I wrote it small.

"I'm sorry," I said. "You've mistaken me."

But he was already rising off the stool, wiping his hands on his apron, and there was no mistaking in his face at all. He was perhaps seventy, bent like a mast in a permanent blow, with a beard gone the color of sea-foam and hands like knotted rope. And his face — God help me — his face was *kind*.

"You're her," he said. "You're the keeper's wife. You used to come down with him on the mail boat, summers, before—" He stopped himself. "I'm Tam Pellowe. I fished the grounds east of the Rock thirty years. Your husband saved my brother's life once. Did he ever tell you that?"

"No," I said, and my voice came out strange, thin, like wire under tension.

"He wouldn't have." Tam nodded as if this confirmed something. "That was Kenji. He pulled my brother Dossie out of the water after the *Merry Alice* went down — '89, that was — swam out twice through the breakers when every other man on that beach stood there calculating odds. And then he went back up to his light and trimmed his wick and never mentioned it to a living soul. I heard about it a week later from Dossie, who couldn't stop talking about it, and I thought, now there's a man who does a thing for its own sake and not for the telling of it."

Ruth's hand tightened on my arm. I felt her watching me, felt her understanding shift and settle into something new. Until that moment I think she had seen me as terrain — difficult, useful terrain. Now she was seeing the woman inside the armor, and I hated it, and some starved part of me was grateful, and I didn't have the strength to sort out which feeling was louder.

"I think you have me confused," I said again, weaker this time, because the lie was dying in my mouth and we both knew it.

"Aye," said Tam Pellowe, mildly, and picked up his knife, and went back to his mackerel. "Confused. Of course. Terrible thing, forgetting faces. Happens to us all."

And he gutted his fish and let me go, and that was its own kind of mercy, and I nearly wept over it right there between the herring and the ice.

* * *

Ruth steered me into the lane behind the chandlery before I entirely trusted my legs.

"You knew him," she said. Not a question. An accusation, almost — though aimed less at me than at the size of what she'd just witnessed. "You said— on the boat, you said your husband was a keeper. You never said—"

"I never say anything about him," I said. "That is the entire point of how I live."

"Why?" It burst out of her, raw. "Why bury him? Why hide his name? He sounds like—" Her voice caught. She was thinking of her own father, of course, another good man whose name had been buried, though in her father's case they'd buried it under a hangman's rope rather than silence. "He sounds like someone who should be *remembered*."

I should have given her the practical answer. The safe answer. The answer about magistrates and suspicion and how a foreign name on a lonely light is a lamp that draws the wrong moths, and I had learned to keep mine dark. Instead, standing in that reeking lane with the gulls screaming overhead, I gave her the true one.

"Because remembering him hurt," I said, "and I had work to do, and work doesn't wait for grief to finish."

She stared at me.

"You want the longer version," I said. "Fine. Come along, then, and mind the puddles. If I'm going to crack open like an egg, I'd rather do it walking."

* * *

We walked out along the harbor wall, past the drying nets and the tarred boats turned belly-up like sleeping animals, until we reached the end where the stone drops away and there's nothing but gray water working at the rocks below. The wind was picking up — it was always picking up, that year; the whole autumn felt like a held breath — and I stood with my skirt snapping and told a stranger the story I hadn't told anyone.

"Kenji came here in '74," I said. "Out of Nagasaki, by way of every port between. He signed onto a whaler to get passage and jumped ship at Grayling's Cross because — his words — *the coast reminded him of home, if home were colder and drank more*. He worked the fishing fleets. He was the best navigator most of these men ever saw, and half of them resented it, because how dare a foreigner read water better than men born to it."

I watched the water while I talked. It's easier.

"We married in '81. The Trinity House men gave him the Widow's Rock appointment in '86 — best light on the coast, they said, and they weren't wrong, and there were those who said they only gave it to him to get him *off* the land, out where his face wouldn't trouble anyone. Maybe both things were true. They often are, here."

"And then?" Ruth asked. Quietly. Like approaching a wild animal.

"And then he died." I heard my own voice go flat and was glad of it. Flat was safe. "Eleven years ago. A fall, they called it. From the cliff path below the tower, at night, in fog. They found him at the bottom two days later." I turned to look at her. "He had climbed that path in fog three hundred times. He knew every stone the way you know your own teeth. And they found him at the bottom of it, and the magistrate who certified the accident was a rising man named Corliss Hobb, and everyone said how sad, how terrible, these coastal paths, and nobody — *nobody* — asked why a man who knew those stones like his own teeth would miss his footing."

Ruth had gone very still.

"You think he was murdered."

"I think," I said, "that for eleven years I have kept that thought locked in a box and sat on the lid, because opening it meant admitting my husband might have been killed by the same kind of men who kill witnesses and forge ledgers, and I had a light to keep and no army to fight them with. So I sat on the lid. I polished glass instead. I counted tins. I made routine into a religion and called the religion peace." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "And then a half-drowned girl washed up under my tower carrying a letter written by a hanged man, and now the box is open, and everything in it smells the same as what's in your oilskin packet, and I would give anything — *anything* — to sit on that lid again and not know. But I know. So."

The wind took my words and scattered them seaward, and for a while neither of us spoke. Down in the harbor a bell buoy clanked, patient as a metronome. Ruth wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, quick and angry, the way people cry when they've decided crying is inefficient.

"The fisherman," she said finally. "Tam. He said Kenji's name like it was — like it was a *good* name."

"It is a good name."

"But you flinched. When he said it, you flinched like he'd struck you."

"Because no one has said it aloud in four years," I said. "Since the funeral. Do you know what happens to a name when no one speaks it? It goes hollow. You carry it around and it weighs nothing and weighs everything, and you begin to wonder whether you dreamed the whole man. Eleven years a widow, four years a ghost's wife. And then an old fisherman gutting mackerel says *Nakamura* across a fish stall like it's nothing, like it's a neighbor's name, like the man *existed* —" I stopped. Pressed my lips together. Started again. "For one moment in that market I wasn't a widow keeping a light. I was a wife on a mail boat in summer, with my hand on his sleeve, and he was pointing out porpoises. And then the moment closed and I had to be the armor again, because the armor is what keeps us alive this week."

Ruth looked out at the water for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, steadier, the voice she must have inherited from the hanged man.

"My father used to say that a lie repeated isn't a lie anymore, it's a load-bearing wall," she said. "You've built your whole house on one."

"Yes."

"And now we're pulling it out."

"Now," I agreed, "we're pulling it out."

* * *

We bought what we needed and made our farewells to Grayling's Cross with no further incidents, or so I believed at the time, and I record here for the benefit of any future reader of my logbook that belief is a luxury item and I have rarely been able to afford it.

At the mouth of the high street, Tam Pellowe was waiting. He fell into step beside me without asking, which is a liberty, but old fishermen extend liberties the way lighthouses extend beams — indiscriminately, and take them personally if refused.

"Dossie died in '94," he said. "My brother. The one Kenji pulled out. Bad chest. But he never forgot, and neither did I, and there's others besides. Half this harbor owes your husband. More than half. Men don't forget who pulled them from deep water, or whose husband did." He glanced sideways at me, sharp as a gull. "You're in some kind of trouble. Don't deny it, I've got eyes, and your sister there has a murderer's face, poor lamb. I won't ask what. But if you need a boat, or a message carried quiet, or a man looked at and described wrong afterward — you come to the third stall, evening tide, ask for Tam."

"Why?" I said. It came out harsher than I intended. "Why would you risk it? You don't know me. You knew a dead man."

He stopped walking. He looked at me with an expression I can only call disappointed, the way you'd look at a lighthouse that had failed to show its character.

"Lass," he said, "I've fished this coast fifty years. I know exactly what a debt in salt is worth, and I know what happens to folk who try to pay theirs late." He touched his cap. "*Nakamura.* Good name. Keep it lit."

And he walked off toward his stall, leaving me standing in the street with my basket and my ledger against my ribs and my eyes stinging in the wind like a green girl's.

Ruth caught up to me. She didn't say anything. She just took my arm, properly this time, not as disguise but as the real article, and we walked out of Grayling's Cross together, two women in black carrying a funeral candle neither of us would burn.

We made maybe half a mile along the coast road before we saw them: two riders, far behind us, in magistrate's livery, moving at an easy walk. Watching. Not hurrying.

A hunt doesn't rush. A hunt knows the quarry is tired.

Ruth's fingers dug into my arm. "Sable," she breathed. "Don't turn around."

I didn't turn around. I kept my chin down and my pace even, grief walking in its modest way, and I thought about Tam Pellowe's voice saying *keep it lit*, and about the box that would not close now, and about how eleven days had just become ten, and how the road ahead ran straight through the country of the men who had already killed once for less than what we carried.

Behind us, unhurried, patient as tide, the riders came on.
