## 10. Hobb's Men

The trouble with lying, I had learned by my ninth year, is not the invention of the lie. Anyone can invent a lie; children do it before they can tie their shoes. The trouble is the maintenance of it — the way a single falsehood, once set loose in the world, demands feeding, grooming, a whole architecture of supporting falsehoods, until you are less a person than a housekeeper for your own deceit.

I was thinking about this in the market town of Pellow's Cross, standing at a fish stall with herring scales drying on my cuffs, while Sable argued gently with a woman about the price of salt pork. I was supposed to be buying bread. I had been buying bread for some minutes now and had somehow failed to buy any, because the baker's boy kept looking at me.

Not rudely. Curiously. The way one looks at something slightly out of place — a gull inland after a storm, a hat on the wrong head.

"You're not from here," he said finally, with the blunt courage of the very young.

"Nobody's from anywhere," I said, which was true and useless.

"My mum says there's been strangers on the coast road." He said it the way boys say things they have overheard and half understood, testing the weight of them. "Two ladies. Riding men come asking after them."

My hands went still on the bread. "Riding men?"

"Magistrate's men. In the grey coats." He shrugged, bored already, attention sliding toward the tray of buns. "They gave tuppence to our Will for describing folk that passed through. He said one of the ladies was tall and walked like she owned the road. That'll be sixpence for the loaf, miss."

I paid him. My fingers were steady, which surprised me; inside, everything had gone cold and quick, like the moment on the wreck rocks when the sea lets go of you all at once and you understand, with total clarity, how deep the water is.

* * *

Sable heard me out in the lee of the chandler's wall, where the wind couldn't carry us to anyone's ears, though of course wind carries nothing but weather, and what I feared carried itself.

"Grey coats," she repeated. She said it without alarm, the way she said most things, her voice flat and even as a spirit level. It was one of the first things I'd noticed about her, back when I was too half-drowned to notice much: she did not waste motion or tone. Everything about her was trimmed for economy, like a wick cut to burn exactly so.

"They asked after two women," I said. "One tall. Walking like—" I stopped.

"Like you own the road," Sable finished. A ghost of dryness crossed her face. "The baker's boy has a poet's ear."

"It's me they're describing. They've been to the wreck site. Someone found my boot prints, or the boat, or—" My throat tightened around the rest. *Or they found my father's letter and know exactly what it is.* "They're hunting me by description, Mrs. Nakamura. Not by name. Which means whoever sent them doesn't yet know my name. That's the only mercy in it, and mercies run out."

She was quiet a moment. Below us the harbor clinked and knocked, boats worrying their moorings, the water green-grey and restless under a sky the color of unwashed wool.

"How long since we left the toll bridge?" she asked.

"Four days."

"And how far does word travel in four days?"

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. "If Hobb's riders ride hard and change horses — the coast road, the market towns, every inn between here and Gray Mouth. Fast."

"Then we assume they know two women passed the bridge. Two women traveling together, dressed for mourning, asking after assizes." Her eyes came up to mine, grey as the harbor. "Which means, Ruth, that we stop being those women."

* * *

We could not stop being those women, of course, not entirely. Grief is the one disguise that never needs adjusting; nobody looks twice at mourning, they look away from it, and we had built our whole passage on that principle. But Sable, being Sable, set about the problem the way she set about everything: practically, thoroughly, and without a single wasted gesture.

She bought us each a shawl in a color that would not have been permitted near a funeral — mine a faded rust, hers a brown gone almost yellow with wear — and made me pin my hair differently, coiled low instead of braided crown. Small changes. She explained them while we walked out of Pellow's Cross along the cliff path, the town shrinking behind us into a smudge of smoke and gulls.

"A man remembers a silhouette before he remembers a face," she said. "Change the silhouette and you buy an hour of his doubt. An hour is often enough."

"Enough for what?"

"Enough to be somewhere else when his doubt finishes."

I wanted to laugh. There was something absurd in it — this spare, competent woman lecturing me on evasion as if she were discussing lamp oil — and something terrible, because she spoke from knowledge. I had begun, in the days since the kitchen and the letter and the ledger laid open between us, to collect these small evidences of who Sable Nakamura was beneath the widow's black. She had a husband once, whose name she did not say. She had a life before Widow's Rock that she handled the way she handled the lens: carefully, with gloves, in the dark. And she knew things about being hunted that no honest woman learns honestly.

"Mrs. Nakamura," I said, "why do you know how to do this?"

The wind took a moment answering for her. Then: "Because I have spent eleven years making sure no one looked at me twice. You learn what looking costs, and therefore what not-looking buys." She adjusted the bundle on her shoulder and did not look at me. "Ask me again sometime when we're not walking toward a magistrate's men."

"I wasn't walking toward them."

"Everyone on this road is walking toward someone. The question is whether you arrive first."

* * *

We saw them that evening, and I will record it plainly because memory makes everything cinematic afterward and the truth was smaller and worse than cinema.

We had taken shelter in the porch of a drovers' chapel — not the smugglers' church, another one, meaner, a stone box with a leaking roof and a bench worn smooth by generations of tired backsides — and we were sharing the heel of the Pellow's Cross loaf and arguing quietly about whether the assize judge could be reached by letter instead of by our own risky persons, when hoofbeats came up the coast road.

Two horses. Grey coats. Lanterns unlit in the last light, because men who want to see without being seen don't show a flame.

They drew rein perhaps forty yards below the chapel gate, at the fork where the coast road met the drove track, and one of them dismounted and crouched over something — a scrap of paper, a boot print in the mud, I couldn't tell — while the other sat his horse and turned slowly, scanning the hedgerows, the chapel, the long grass. His gaze crossed our hiding place and moved on, and in that moment I learned something about fear that I have never forgotten: it is not loud. It is a great silence. My heartbeat, my breath, the blood in my ears, all of it simply stopped, the body going dark like a doused lamp, waiting.

"Description matches," the mounted man said. His voice carried on the evening air with insulting clarity. "Tall lass, twenty-odd, dark hair. Travels with an older woman, keeps herself close. Seen heading east from Pellow's Cross this morning."

"That's yesterday's news," the dismounted man said. "Pike wants them before Gray Mouth. If they cross the ferry—"

"They won't cross the ferry. Ferry's shut with the storm coming. Nobody crosses till it clears." He laughed, short and unpleasant. "Storm's doing half our work. Sea's got no doors for them to slip out of."

Then the dismounted man stood, brushed his knees, and said the thing that has lived behind my eyes ever since:

"Pike says check the hands. The girl'll have burned fingertips — paper burns. She was burning papers at the wreck site, papers washed ashore off the packet boat. Check the right hand, thumb and first two fingers."

I looked down at my own right hand in the failing light, at the fingertips I had singed the night I burned the false papers I'd carried as decoys, before the storm took the boat and threw me onto Sable's rocks with the true ones sewn into my coat. Three small healed burns. Evidence. Written on my body in my father's handwriting, so to speak, and readable by anyone who knew to read it.

The riders mounted up and moved on east, lanterns still dark, hooves soft in the mud, and the sound of them faded and faded until it was only the sea again, and the wind, and my heart starting up again all at once, painfully, like a lamp turned too fast.

* * *

"He knows," I said, when I could speak. "This Pike — he was there, or someone told him exactly. Burned fingertips. Who else would know that? Only someone who saw the shore after I came ashore. Only—"

"The wreck site was searched," Sable said. She had not relaxed when the riders left; she had simply transferred her stillness from hiding to planning, and I was beginning to understand that the two states were nearly identical in her. "By whom?"

"Anyone Hobb sent. Or—" I stopped. The thought arrived cold. "Or the smugglers themselves. The packet boat that went down — it was carrying contraband too, wasn't it? That's why it ran the channel in a storm. If Hobb's ring lost a cargo, they'd send men to the wreck site to salvage. And those men would find signs someone survived. Boot prints. A burnt fire. Paper ash."

"And Pike collects what they find," Sable said, "because Pike is the man who collects things." She said it with a certainty that startled me. "You know this sergeant."

"I know the name. Aldous Pike." My mouth was dry. "He was my father's clerk. Before. He sat at our table. He—" I had not spoken of it aloud since the night in Sable's kitchen, and saying it now, on a cold hillside with the hunt's hoofbeats still ringing somewhere east of us, made it real in a way the telling hadn't managed before. "My father trusted him with everything. Every document, every case, every private grief. And then my father went to the scaffold, and Pike went on Hobb's payroll, and I have spent two years telling myself he must have been coerced, threatened, that there was some explanation—"

"There was an explanation," Sable said. "It's called thirty pieces of silver, and it is the oldest explanation there is."

I wanted to cry. I wanted, more than I wanted safety or success or the judge's signature, to sit down on that chapel bench and weep for the girl I had been, who had set a place for Sergeant Pike at Christmas and believed, absolutely, in the decency of the men in her father's office. But crying takes time, and time was a purse with a hole in it, so I did what Sable would have done. I filed the grief where she filed hers — somewhere deep and load-bearing — and I got practical.

"Check the hands," I said. "So every man between here and Gray Mouth will be looking at hands. What do we do — gloves? Mitten weather is coming anyway."

"Gloves help." Sable considered. "But a glove is also a thing that gets asked to be removed. Better: we give them hands worth checking. Yours get wrapped — a scald, a burn, a keeper's wife's bandaging. Women fuss over injuries; it's expected. Nobody removes a bandage from a scald. They pity it."

"And yours?"

Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Mine are a keeper's hands. Rope-burned, oil-stained, cracked from salt. Half the women on this coast have hands like mine. I am invisible everywhere except the one place I can never go back to." She said the last lightly, but I heard the weight under it, the same way you hear the stone under shallow water.

We sat in the chapel while full dark came down, and I worked through what the riders had said, pulling threads apart the way my father taught me to pull apart a legal argument — find the load-bearing claim and test it.

"They think we'll try for the ferry," I said finally. "That's why they're riding east. 'Sea's got no doors.' They think the storm traps us on the coast road, that we have to pass Gray Mouth like everyone else, through the checkpoint like good little travelers."

"Do you disagree?"

"I disagree that we're predictable anymore. They're hunting two mourners. We're not those women. They're watching the ferry and the roads. So maybe—" and here the plan assembled itself in my mouth before I fully understood I was building it, "maybe we don't behave like quarry. Quarry runs. Maybe we walk straight past them, slow, wrapped hands and changed silhouettes, and let them look at us and see nothing worth tuppence."

Sable was silent for so long I began to fear I'd offended her. Then she said, in the tone of a woman confirming a wick trim, "Your father taught you law?"

"He tried. I mostly learned argument by losing gracefully."

"Hm." In the dark I heard rather than saw the almost-smile. "Losing gracefully is the whole art. Very well. We go east, past them, toward Gray Mouth — but we go tomorrow, early, before the roads fill, and we go as what we now appear to be: a keeper's widow and her sister, traveling to nurse a sick aunt before the storms close the passes. Sick-aunt stories bore people. Bored people don't ask questions."

"And if they stop us anyway? If someone checks the bandage and finds three neat burns underneath?"

Sable rose, and in the doorway of the chapel her silhouette was black against the last iron gleam of the sea, straight-backed and unhurried, a woman who had stood watch over dangerous water for eleven years and intended to stand watch over this too.

"Then we learn," she said, "whether a lie holds better than a truth. Come. We sleep in shifts tonight. And Ruth—" she paused, and for a moment her voice dropped its level trim and showed the grain of the wood beneath, "—from tonight on, you burn nothing, you write nothing, and you touch no paper bare-handed. From tonight, your hands tell whatever story we decide they tell. Understood?"

Understood. I lay on the cold chapel bench with my shawl over us both and listened to the sea working the rocks below, patient as a magistrate, and I understood something else entirely, something I didn't say out loud because it would have sounded like despair:

They were hunting a description of me. And descriptions, unlike names, cannot be changed by crossing a river or sewing a new hem. Somewhere behind us rode two men in grey coats carrying my outline folded in their saddlebags like a pattern for a dress — tall, dark-haired, twenty-odd, burned fingertips — and every mile east we traveled, we traveled deeper into the fit of it.

Seven days, now, until the judge sailed. Seven days, and the whole coast learning my shape by heart.
