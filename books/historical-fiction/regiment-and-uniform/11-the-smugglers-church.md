## 11. The Smugglers' Church

The storm found us on the headland road with the particular enthusiasm of a creditor who has been kept waiting. One moment we were walking, heads down, coats plastered to us like second skins; the next the sky simply gave up all pretense and emptied itself onto the coast as though it held a personal grudge against the two of us specifically.

"There," Ruth shouted, pointing through the gray curtain. "Chapel. There's a chapel."

I saw it a heartbeat later — a low stone shape crouched at the cliff's edge like an old animal that had lain down one day and decided not to get up again. I knew of it, distantly. The fishermen called it the Old Hope. It had been deconsecrated before my time on this coast, abandoned when the new parish was built in the town, and left to the gulls and the weather and whatever else cared to use it.

Whatever else, as it turned out.

We ran for it, and the door — thank God and whoever had last oiled it — swung open at a touch. We tumbled inside into sudden blessed quiet, the storm cut off as neatly as if a hand had drawn a curtain across it.

I stood dripping on the flagstones and caught my breath, and that was when I noticed the floor was too clean.

Now, you must understand what I mean by clean. A ruined chapel on a drowned coast collects its filth honestly — gull droppings, blown sand, the droppings of sheep sheltering from weather exactly as we were, leaves and salt-scum and bird bones in every corner. This floor had some of that at the edges, yes. But the center of the nave was swept. Recently. The stone flags showed a pale track worn down the middle aisle, wide enough for a man carrying something heavy on his shoulder, walked so many times the wear had polished it.

"Ruth," I said quietly.

She was wringing out her hair by the door, shivering. "What?"

"Come look at this floor."

She came. She looked. She was cleverer than her thin lies had suggested when first I met her, and I watched her arrive at the same conclusion I did: nobody sweeps a ruin for piety.

"Somebody uses this place," she said.

"Somebody uses this place *often*."

* * *

We did what sensible people do in a storm, which is nothing quickly. We got a fire going in the old vestry hearth — there was half a rick of peat stacked there, cut and dried, another courtesy of our absent sweeper — and stripped our outer layers to steam them, and ate cold bread and harder cheese while the wind threw itself against the east wall hard enough to make the stones hum.

It was Ruth, drying her stockings and pretending she wasn't, who said: "Sable. If this is a drop point—"

"I know."

"—and someone is expected tonight—"

"The storm will delay them," I said. "Or it won't. Either way we should know everything we can know before we decide anything."

"Decide what? We could just leave."

"Out into *that*?" I nodded at the window, where the world had become a rumor of water. "On a road that floods at the dip past the point? We'd be found face-down in a ditch by morning, or worse, found alive by whoever comes here tonight." I fed another block of peat to the fire. "No. If they come, they'll come expecting an empty church. We'll be quiet, we'll watch, and when they're gone we'll go."

"And if they're not alone? If they stay?"

I didn't answer that, because I hadn't an answer worth giving. Instead I said, "Help me look around properly. Quietly. If this is the heart of it, I want to see its face."

* * *

A smugglers' church. There's a phrase I'd have laughed at, two weeks ago. Now I moved through it with a lamp turned so low the wick was barely alive, and every object seemed to be lying to me.

The pews were genuine enough — old, wormed, most of them broken-backed. But three of them near the back wall were newer than the rest, and their seat boards lifted. Beneath each was a cavity, and in the cavities, rope. Coils of good tarred line, blocks and tackle, a boat compass in a brass case, a signal lamp with its lens cracked and replaced. Working gear, stored dry and ready.

The font had been filled with cement. I knocked on it and it rang dead and solid, and I thought: that's not reverence, that's a strongbox lid poured permanent. Whatever sat inside that stone had been sealed away from even its own owners' temptation.

Behind the altar, where a cross would hang in a living church, there was instead a board, and on the board, chalk marks. Columns of them. Tally strokes grouped in fives, dates beside them in a cramped hand, and single letters I couldn't read — initials, perhaps, or code. A schedule of arrivals written where only the faithful would think to look.

Ruth stood beside me copying the dates into the back of her father's letter — the letter itself she kept sewn into her bodice now, but she'd copied it twice, and one copy lived folded flat in her boot. She wrote fast, tongue between her teeth.

"This date," she said, tapping the chalk. "Three days ago. And this one—" her finger moved down, stopped. Her voice went carefully level, which I had learned was how she sounded when she was most frightened. "This one is tomorrow. Or tonight. Depending how you count after midnight."

"So someone is expected."

"Someone is always expected. The question is whether they expect *us*."

* * *

We settled in the gallery — the rotted remnants of one at the west end, up a stair that groaned but held. From there we could see the whole nave below us, the swept aisle, the altar, the door. I put out the lamp entirely. The fire in the vestry we let burn low but not die; anyone coming would see its glow and think, perhaps, of sheep or vagrants, and vagrants are invisible on this coast. Everyone knows the poor sleep in ruins. Nobody looks at them.

We waited. The storm worked at the building like a patient locksmith. Hours went by. Ruth slept against my shoulder for a while and I let her, because fear spends the body faster than walking does, and I remembered being young and terrified and pretending otherwise.

I thought about Kenji. I don't know why I tell you that, except that in dark places waiting for danger, he is always what arrives first. He used to say that a lighthouse keeper's real work was not the light but the waiting — that any fool can trim a wick, but it takes a certain character to sit night after night with the weather coming and do nothing but be ready. *Be ready*, he'd say, tapping his own chest. *Not afraid. Ready. They live in different rooms.*

I sat in the dark gallery of a smugglers' church and tried to live in the right room.

* * *

They came a little after eleven.

I heard them before I saw them — boots on the path outside, several pairs, and under the wind a snatch of voices, men talking the easy talk of men doing familiar work. Not skulkers. Routine men. That frightened me more than skulking would have; routine means practice means years.

The door opened and lamplight spilled across the swept aisle, and four men came in out of the rain shaking themselves like dogs. I pressed my eye to the gap in the gallery rail and counted: four. Three I didn't know. The fourth—

The fourth was the customs sergeant from the toll bridge. I'd have known him anywhere; he'd looked straight into my mourning veiled face not ten days past and waved us through with bored eyes. He stood now in a dead church with rain running off him and began, unhurriedly, to give orders.

"Two kegs short of the full lot — the *Merrow's Pride* lost a crate overboard off the point, so don't ask. Get the boards up. Marta's boy isn't riding tonight in this, so it sits till the next dry spell."

Marta's boy. I filed that away with hands that had gone suddenly cold, and hated myself a little for filing it, because a name heard once is a thread you cannot unhear.

The other men pried up floorboards along the south wall — I'd noticed those boards earlier, newer wood, fresher nails, and told myself it was repair work. Underneath lay a cavity lined with oilcloth, and in it, contraband. Brandy in straw-packed kegs, silk bolts wrapped in sacking, a small chest that clinked when it shifted. They passed things up, restacked things, made entries in a ledger — a *ledger*, God help me, they kept books — and the sergeant checked each entry against the chalk board behind the altar with the diligence of any honest clerk.

And then, as the others carried kegs toward some inner door I hadn't known existed — a door behind the vestry, low, leading down toward the cliffs and, I'd wager, a landing stage in a cave below — the sergeant stayed a moment alone at the altar. He took out a paper, read it, read it again, and then he laughed. Short, ugly, satisfied.

He said to himself, or to the empty church, or to God's abandoned house: "Widow's Rock. Clever girl." And he put the paper in his breast pocket and followed the others down.

* * *

They were gone twenty minutes, maybe less. Long enough for my heart to try to climb out through my throat and be reasoned back down. When they returned, empty-handed, they barred nothing — why bar a ruin? — collected their lamps, and went out into the rain, and their voices faded, and the storm closed over the sound of them like water over a dropped stone.

For a long while neither of us moved.

Then Ruth said, from the dark beside me, very quietly: "You heard what he said. About Widow's Rock."

"I heard."

"They know. Someone told them — the wreck, the survivor, *something*. Sable, they said 'clever girl,' that's not suspicion, that's—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. "That's a hunt."

Below us the nave lay open and honest in the way robbed houses are honest. The floorboards stood loose where the men had left them. The chalk board waited behind the altar with its dates and tallies, keeping faith with no one.

"We go down," I said, "we look at what they've built down there, and then we leave before first light, and we never speak of this chapel to anyone who doesn't already know about it."

"And what we heard? The sergeant? Marta's son?"

"Marta's son rides for them unknowing or knowing, and either way it isn't ours to carry tonight. But the sergeant—" I felt something settle in me, cold and level, the feeling I get when a storm has finally committed to a course. "The sergeant is the toll bridge, Ruth. Checkpoint one. We walked our ledger through his hands once already by luck. We now know we will never do it twice."

We climbed down. Against my own better judgment — against every instinct the sea ever taught me — I knelt at the loose boards and lifted one, and looked into the oilcloth-lined dark where the brandy and silk had sat, and understood at last whose church this was. Not the gulls'. Not God's.

Magistrate Corliss Hobb's. His parish. His congregation. His tithe.

And somewhere out in the rain, riding home warm and dry, a customs sergeant with a paper in his pocket that mentioned my lighthouse by name, and the words *clever girl* attached to it — which meant that by morning, checkpoints two and three would be looking for two women, and one of them a widow who walked with a keeper's stride and could not, for the life of her, disguise it.

"First light," I said to Ruth. "We move at first light. And we take the quarry road, not the coast path — because after tonight, the coast path belongs to them."

Ruth looked at the chalk board one more time, at the fresh date that meant *tonight*, already crossed out by men who considered this church theirs beyond question.

"They'll come back," she said.

"Yes," I said. "So we'd best be a memory."
