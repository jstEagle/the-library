## 12. The Night Watcher

There is a particular kind of misery reserved for hiding in a church that isn't yours, in a storm that isn't stopping, waiting for criminals who have an appointment and you don't. I had been raised to believe churches offered sanctuary. Nobody told me they also offer a stone floor, a draft that seems personally offended by you, and the distinct sensation of your own heartbeat being loud enough to give away the entire operation.

Sable had no such complaints. She crouched behind the vestry partition with the stillness of furniture — good furniture, the sort that has outlived three generations of clumsy children — and watched the chapel door through a gap in the wood as if she'd been doing surveillance since birth. For all I knew, she had. Lighthouse keeping is mostly watching, I was learning. Watching water, watching weather, watching for the thing that finally comes.

We'd found the drop point by accident, which is how I suspect most important things are found. The storm had driven us off the cliff path two hours after dark, into the little chapel of St. Brendan-the-Less — a name that struck me as honest, a saint ranked below his ambitions — and Sable, hunting dry kindling, had pried up a floorboard that came away too easily. Beneath it: oilcloth bundles, tins without labels, a coil of new rope, and a crate stenciled with a shipping line's mark that meant nothing to me and clearly something to her.

"Contraband," she'd said, not touching any of it. "Tobacco, most like. Maybe silk." Then she'd crouched lower, tilting her head at the floor around the cache. "Fresh prints."

And so we were still there, because leaving felt more dangerous than staying, and because — though neither of us said it — whoever came to collect that cargo might be worth seeing.

I want to be clear that this was not my idea of a good evening. My father was hanged for crimes he didn't commit, I am carrying his confession sewn into another woman's skirt, and I am spending the night on a church floor watching smugglers' boots. If my mother could see me, she would say I never did have the sense God gives a goose. She would be right. But sense and survival are different trades, and I was learning the second one late.

* * *

They came at half past ten, by the chapel's cracked clock, which I could read only because lightning kept lending me light.

Three men, then four. They didn't knock; they had keys or knew the latch. They spoke low, but storms flatten sound strangely — sometimes swallowing a shout, sometimes delivering a whisper from across the room — and I caught fragments. *Tide's wrong. Take the west lot tonight, the rest Friday.* Boots on stone. The scrape of floorboards being lifted and replaced by hands far more practiced than mine.

Sable's hand found my wrist in the dark. Not a squeeze. Just contact — *stay*, without a word spent on it. I stayed.

Then a fifth man arrived, and everything changed temperature.

He came in shaking off rain, and even by lightning-strobe I knew him before my mind finished assembling him from parts. Broad shoulders under a customs coat. The heavy, unhurried way he moved, like a man who has never once been made to wait. Sergeant Bracken. The toll bridge. Checkpoint one. The bored officer who had run his fingers along the hem of Sable's mourning skirt while I stood there smiling a funeral smile with my heart trying to climb out through my teeth.

He had looked at us for perhaps ninety seconds total. I would have sworn he'd forgotten us before we were out of sight.

He hadn't. He'd filed us.

"Evening, all," Bracken said, and the room rearranged itself around him. He wasn't in charge — I could see that much — but he was the one whose arrival mattered. A younger man handed him a lantern and he set it down without looking, the way a man handles objects in his own kitchen.

"Roads are swamped past the quarry," someone said. "Hobb wants the west lot moved before it floods the pit."

"Hobb wants a great many things." Bracken crouched at the open cache, lifted a tin, weighed it in one hand like a housewife judging a potato. "He'll get what keeps. Who's on the ferry tomorrow?"

"Nobody yet. Gray Mouth won't cross in this weather."

"It'll cross Thursday. It always crosses Thursday; the ferryman likes eating." Bracken laughed — a short, ugly bark — and several men laughed with him, the way men do when the joke is employment. "Thursday, then. And Pike's gate stays shut to everyone until Hobb says otherwise. There's two women on the coast road he wants stopped. One of them's the Vane girl."

The floor did not open and swallow me, though I waited a moment to see if it might. It occurred to me, distantly, that I was lying on top of a smuggling cache in a church while men discussed capturing me, and that some part of me was taking notes. That part of me has been a problem my whole life. It's the part that wrote letters instead of crying at funerals.

"Vane girl," one of them repeated. "Thought the old man's lot died with him."

"The old man's lot didn't die. That's the trouble." Bracken stood, dusted his knees. "Somebody's been passing his papers about. Magistrate's had word from London. There's a judge coming on the circuit who asks questions." He said the last flatly, and the room went quiet in a way that told me everything: these men were afraid of Justice Merrow too. Fear runs downhill in organizations like this. Hobb feared the judge, so the sergeant feared Hobb, so the men feared the sergeant, and somewhere at the bottom of that slope were two women on a cold church floor, holding all the evidence and none of the power.

"Which roads?" someone asked.

"All of them. But listen—" Bracken's voice dropped, and I had to strain through the rain against the roof to catch it. "—the widow keeper's station gets searched same as anywhere. Hobb's got a notion the girl went to ground along the light. So when we do Widow's Rock, we do it proper."

Beside me, in the dark, Sable Nakamura became very, very still. I felt it more than saw it — the quality of stillness changing, the way water changes just before it freezes. Her hand left my wrist.

They talked another twenty minutes. I heard schedules, signals, a code phrase involving a red lantern at Gray Mouth, and the name Aldous Pike spoken twice with the casual dread men reserve for weather and taxes. I committed what I could to memory — I have a decent memory; it's the one inheritance my father left me that nobody could confiscate — and then the boards went back down, the lanterns went out one by one, and the chapel emptied into the storm.

Bracken left last. At the door he paused, turned back, and looked directly at the vestry partition where we hid.

For three full seconds — I counted them; counting is what I do instead of screaming — he looked straight at us. Rain blew in past his shoulders. Lightning filled the windows white.

Then he pulled his collar up and walked out into the night, and I understood that he hadn't seen us at all. He'd simply been checking his storehouse the way a farmer checks his field. We were beneath his notice. Two drowned rats in a corner.

Being beneath a man's notice has never felt so much like a gift.

* * *

We didn't move for a long time after the door closed. Sable counted, I think — she had that air — and when she finally rose, she rose without a single joint protesting audibly, which struck me as almost supernatural in a woman who climbed lighthouse stairs for a living.

"He's paid," she said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Bracken. Yes. Did you hear the schedule? Thursday at Gray Mouth, the ferry—"

"I heard." She lit a match and cupped it, and in that small trembling light her face was grim and calm at once, which seemed to be her factory setting. "You understand what you've learned tonight?"

"That our customs officer at the toll bridge takes smuggler's coin, and the magistrate knows exactly who I am, and they mean to search your home."

"No." The match went out between her fingers. In the dark again, her voice came level. "What you've learned is which checkpoints are rotten and which are merely stupid. It's not the same thing, and the difference will keep you alive. Bracken's bought. Pike's gate — that man is Hobb's own creature, worse than bought, loyal. But the toll bridge sergeant being corrupt means the bridge can be *managed*. Corrupt men take instruction, if you know their price. Honest ones can't be managed at all, only endured."

"And Gray Mouth?"

"The ferryman crosses Thursdays because he likes eating. That's not corruption. That's hunger. Hunger's negotiable." Something in her voice shifted — I thought of a wedding ring later, though I didn't know why yet — and she turned toward the door. "Come. We've a walk ahead and less night than I'd like."

We stepped out into the storm and I nearly lost my breath to it — wind off the sea like a wall that kept relocating, rain coming sideways with opinions. Sable took the cliff path without hesitation, one hand on my sleeve, shouting instructions close to my ear: *wall side, wall side, don't look at the water, look at your feet.*

It was somewhere in the second mile, bent double against the gale, that the full weight of the evening landed on me.

Knowledge, my father used to say, is a tool that cuts the hand that holds it. He said it about printing presses and pamphlets and other things that got him hanged, and I'd nodded along as a girl without understanding. Now I understood. Twenty-four hours ago I knew nothing except fear. Now I knew the ring's whole shape — its schedules, its signals, its sergeant, its magistrate — and every piece of that knowledge was a weight I now carried alongside the ledger, and every mile of road ahead had men on it who wanted both the ledger and me, and knowing where they'd be didn't make the road safer.

It just made it legible.

That's the bargain nobody warns you about. Ignorance is terror, but knowledge is responsibility, and I couldn't decide which sat heavier in the chest. Knowing Bracken was bought meant we could plan around the toll bridge — but it also meant that every traveler who crossed that bridge trusting his badge was walking into a lie, and I knew it now, and knowing is a thing you can't unknow. Somewhere south of here, honest merchants were paying honest duties to a dishonest man, and I was going to walk past him and keep his secret for eleven more days because my father's name depended on my silence.

My father would have understood that arithmetic. He'd have hated it and understood it in the same breath. That, I think, is what it means to be his daughter.

"Sable!" I shouted into the wind, because there was one more thing gnawing at me. "They said they're searching Widow's Rock!"

"I heard."

"Aren't you—"

"They'll search it when they search it." Her grip tightened on my sleeve, steadying me over loose rock. "A station's just stone and glass and oil. What matters there can't be confiscated." She paused, and the wind ate whatever she said next, and I spent the rest of the walk wondering whether she'd said *the lamp* or something else entirely.

* * *

We reached shelter near dawn — a fisherman's drying shed above the cove, salt-crusted nets hanging like the ghosts of enormous spiders, and Sable negotiated our presence with the owner via a look and a coin, no words required. Apparently that's a language she speaks fluently.

I sat against a post with my father's letter-weight pressing its familiar ache against my ribs — the ledger rode inside my bodice now; we'd re-sewn it after the bridge — and I tried to write down what I'd heard before sleep took it from me. Schedules. Thursday. Red lantern. Pike. *Widow's Rock, done proper.*

My pencil stopped at that last line.

"You're frowning at your own handwriting," Sable observed. She was mending something — she is always mending something; I believe if you handed her the end of the world she'd ask for thread — and didn't look up.

"When they come to your lighthouse," I said. "When they search it 'proper.' What happens to you?"

She considered the question longer than it deserved, which meant it deserved exactly that length.

"I've been alone on that rock for six years," she said finally. "Men coming up my stairs with lanterns and bad intentions is not the worst weather I've survived." Then she looked at me, and her eyes in the grey dawn light were the exact color of the sea before it decides what it's going to do to you. "But Ruth. Tonight you heard them say my station by name. Tomorrow they may ride there. Which means the place I was safest is now the place they're looking — and I find I'm not frightened of that." She returned to her mending. "I'm *interested*. That's usually worse."

I didn't know what she meant then.

I know now. But that understanding cost us the flooded quarry, and a night on a ledge, and nearly cost me the sea itself — so let me leave it where she left it, hanging in the air of a drying shed while the storm wore itself out against the coast:

She was interested. And when Sable Nakamura became interested in something, the thing should have started praying.

Outside, the rain thinned towards morning, and eight days remained, and somewhere east of us a ferryman was deciding whether Thursday was worth the crossing.
