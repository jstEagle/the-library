## 13. A Debt in Salt

Marta Quill lived four miles up the coast road in a cottage that looked like it had been arguing with the wind for sixty years and losing on points, though never conceding the fight. The chimney leaned east, the fence leaned west, and the door had given up entirely and hung at an angle that suggested a house shrugging. I had not knocked on that door since before Kenji died.

I stood outside it now with Ruth shivering under my oilskin and a satchel full of treason pressed against my ribs, and I found that my hand would not rise to knock.

"You knew her," Ruth said. It wasn't a question. She'd spent two days listening to me talk myself into this errand, and she'd learned to read me the way sailors read weather — by what I didn't say.

"I knew her husband first. Tom Quill, keeper at Gray Head before they automated the light. Marta kept his logs for thirty years because his handwriting was a scandal and hers was beautiful." My hand still wouldn't move. "When I married Kenji, she was one of perhaps three people on this coast who didn't look through him as if he were glass."

"And she owes him?"

"She owes him his life, which is a different currency altogether." The wind came off the water and tried its usual trick of making everything sound like an accusation. "Nineteen years ago, before I ever saw this coast, Kenji was second mate on a coaster out of the north. She was aboard too — going to visit her sister, I think, or running from Tom Quill, depending on which version you get. The ship went down off the Teeth in November fog. Kenji got eleven people into a boat when there was room and sense for six. Marta was the eleventh."

Ruth was quiet a moment. "And he told you this?"

"He told me almost nothing. Marta told me the rest, once, over brandy, and then we agreed never to speak of it again, because gratitude is a heavy thing to carry between neighbors and lighter as a debt." I finally made my fist behave and rapped on the leaning door. "Today the debt gets called in. Try not to look like a fugitive."

"I've been practicing," Ruth said, with the particular flatness she used when a joke was the only structural support available. "Apparently I'm bad at it."

* * *

Marta Quill opened the door before the third knock finished — keepers' wives develop an ear for knocking that isn't routine — and looked at us the way she'd once looked at barometers: taking the reading without comment.

"Well," she said. "Sable Nakamura. And someone's trouble."

"Marta—"

"Don't 'Marta' me on my own doorstep. Come in before God and the customs men see you standing there like a pair of drowned cats with a secret." She stepped back to let us pass, and I caught it — just a flicker — the way her eyes went past me to Ruth, cataloguing her: young, frightened, salt-stiff hair, a bruise going yellow along the jaw from the rocks. Cataloguing and, worse, *recognizing something*. Marta Quill had read coast gossip for fifty years. There had been a wreck below Widow's Rock, and there had been a survivor, and survivors got logged.

The cottage inside smelled of peat smoke and lamp oil and the particular staleness of a house kept by one stubborn woman. A cat regarded us from the dresser with the contempt of senior management.

"Sit," Marta said, pointing at chairs as if seating unruly children. "Tea. And then you'll tell me why a lighthouse widow is walking the coast road in a rising gale with a girl who looks like she's been pulled out of the sea twice — once by the water and once by whatever's chasing her."

I sat. The tea appeared with the efficiency of long habit. And I looked at this old woman — seventy if she was a day, hands like knotted rope, eyes that missed nothing — and understood that I could not lie to her, not because she'd catch me, but because the whole architecture of what I'd come to ask was built on truth.

"Do you remember Elias Vane?" I said.

Her cup paused halfway to her mouth. "The clerk they hanged. Smuggling, wasn't it? Corrupt ledgers." Her eyes narrowed. "That was an ugly business. Hobb pushed it through fast. Too fast, some said, and then stopped saying it."

"The ledger that convicted him was false. The real ledger exists. It names every man in the ring — the bribes, the dates, the signatures. Magistrate Hobb's signature among them." I set the oilskin packet on the table between us. "This girl is his daughter. The assize judge lands at the county seat in nine days. We have to get this to Justice Merrow before his circuit sails, and between here and there sit three checkpoints and every man Hobb can buy."

The fire ticked. The cat yawned. Marta looked at the packet for a long time without touching it, the way you look at a thing that might be unexploded ordnance.

"Say the rest," she said quietly. "There's more. You didn't walk four miles in weather like this to hide a document. Documents can be posted."

"The ring killed Kenji." My voice came out flatter than I intended, which was maybe better. "Not directly. But he knew something, or they thought he might know something, and they wrote his name into their book as a bribe he never took — a payment to bind a man in silence whether he spoke or not. Three months later he went out to check the moorings in weather no sane man would go out in. They call it an accident. I've called it an accident for six years because calling it anything else meant there was nowhere to put the anger." I turned the packet slightly on the table. "Now there's somewhere to put it."

Marta set down her cup. She looked at Ruth — really looked, and Ruth held the look, which took more spine than most people manage across a stranger's kitchen table.

"Vane's daughter," Marta said. Not a question either.

"Yes," Ruth said. First time she'd said it aloud to anyone but me. Her voice cracked on the *yes*, and she steadied it. "They think I'm dead, probably. Or they think I'm worth fifty pounds. Either way."

"Ah," said Marta. And then, to me, with the weariness of a woman who has seen exactly this shape of trouble before: "You want me to hide her. Hide you both. Hide the book."

"I have no one else," I said. "Everyone kind enough to help is too frightened, everyone brave enough is too far, and everyone close enough is bought. That leaves you."

"Flattering." But something moved behind the weathered face — and I knew what it was before she said it, because I'd watched her say it once before, over brandy, about a night of fog and eleven people in a boat built for six. "Kenji Nakamura pulled me out of black water when every instinct said leave me, because there wasn't room for mercy and he made room anyway. I have owed a dead man for nineteen years and interest accrues." She pushed back her chair. "You'll stay. Both of you. The loft above the washhouse — no one sees it but the gulls. I'll dry your clothes, feed you, and lie to any official face that darkens that door, which will be a novel experience for me and I intend to enjoy it."

The relief hit me so hard I had to grip the table. I hadn't known how much of me was braced against refusal until refusal stopped being possible.

But Marta wasn't finished. She stood at the window, looking out at the gray churn of the sea, and when she turned around her face had gone careful.

"There's a thing you need to hear before you thank me properly," she said. "A poster's been up in the Gray Mouth post office a week now. I don't hold with gossip, so I walked in myself and read it twice, because I have a memory for faces and it bothered me."

"What poster?"

"'Wanted,'" she said. "'Ruth Vane. Accessory to treason. Fifty pounds reward.' With a likeness." Her eyes rested on my companion. "It's a poor likeness. Hair wrong, chin wrong. But the jaw—" she tapped her own jaw with one rope-knuckled finger, mirroring the bruise "—the jaw they got right. Somebody described her from that wreck site, Sable. Somebody who saw her face before the sea did."

The warmth drained out of the kitchen. Ruth had gone very still, the stillness of a creature that has heard the hawk.

"Fifty pounds," Ruth said at last, and laughed — a small, awful sound. "My father was worth less than that alive. He used to joke that his salary wouldn't cover his funeral."

"They're not hunting you for what you're worth," Marta said. "They're hunting you for what you're carrying. The poster's bait, girl. Every soul on this coast knows the number, and every soul on this coast has a lean winter coming. You are fifty pounds of temptation walking around in boots." She came back to the table and sat, and her voice dropped to the register keepers use for weather warnings. "Which brings me to the hard part of the arithmetic, since you've come to me for arithmetic."

"Go on."

"My son." She said it simply, and it landed like ballast. "Joseph. He rides customs for the crown — patrols the coast road out of Gray Mouth. A good boy. An honest boy, as honest as the service allows, which I grant is a sliding scale." She looked at her own hands. "If I harbor fugitives and it comes out — and things come out, Sable, they always come out, that's the one law on this coast that never fails — then Joseph isn't a customs rider anymore. He's the son of a woman who aided treason. His career dies, and depending on Hobb's temper, worse might follow. Fifty pounds buys a lot of testimony against a family."

Nobody spoke. The cat, sensing the drop in temperature, left the room.

"So," Marta continued, "here's what I'll do and won't do, so there's no misunderstanding and no resentment later. I'll hide you. I'll feed you. I'll swear blind to any magistrate's man who knocks that I haven't seen a living soul but gulls since Tuesday. What I won't do is carry messages, contact anyone, or let you use my house to plan anything that requires leaving it — beyond the leaving itself. You stay warm and invisible until your road is ready, and then you take that road yourselves. If Joseph comes to this door, he comes as a son visiting his mother, and by God he'll find nothing here but tea and his mother's complaints about her knees."

"That's fair," Ruth said quickly. "More than fair—"

"It's not fair," Marta said. "Fairness would be me doing all of it, and fairness is how families hang. This is arithmetic." She looked at me, and for one moment the keeper's wife of thirty years showed through the old woman: the tallying, the watchfulness, the understanding that a light kept burning is a series of small compromises with the dark. "Your husband made room in that boat when there wasn't room. I make room in this house when there isn't safety. Neither of those debts gets paid clean. You pay them bent, and you hope the bend holds."

I reached across the table and took her hand. It was like gripping a mooring post.

"The bend holds," I said.

* * *

We settled into the washhouse loft as the gale arrived in earnest, the wind arriving like a creditor with a list. The space was low and smelled of soap-lye and dried lavender, and through the one small window you could see nothing but weather. Ruth spread the ledger pages out to check them yet again — her ritual, her rosary — while I sat on the edge of the cot and listened to the storm interrogate the roof.

"Sable." She didn't look up from the pages. "Why didn't you tell me about the poster before? Marta says a week."

Because I hadn't known, and because I'd been carrying enough dread without adding hers. But that wasn't the true answer, and lying badly to Ruth Vane seemed suddenly like a luxury I couldn't afford. "Because a week ago I didn't know what we were carrying. Because I thought we'd be through a checkpoint or two and onto a mail packet before the hunt found its legs. Because fear shared is fear doubled, and I wanted to spare you one night of it." I met her eyes. "None of those reasons survive daylight. I'm sorry."

She considered that with the seriousness she brought to everything, this girl who had crossed half the country alone on a dead man's instructions. "Don't do it again," she said. "Whatever it costs me to know, it costs me more not to. That's the lesson of my whole life lately." She went back to the pages, and then, quieter: "Fifty pounds. Do you know what I could have done with fifty pounds last year? Fed myself for a winter. Instead I spent it on trains and disguises to come dig up my father's ghost, and now the ghost's price tag is on my head." Her laugh again, that terrible small one. "He'd have hated it. He hated owing anyone anything."

"He owed the truth," I said. "And it turns out the truth collects."

Outside, the gale climbed another register, and somewhere down the coast road I imagined riders moving through it — Joseph Quill among them, perhaps, patrolling honestly through a dishonest country, riding unknowing past his mother's roof where his mother was breaking the law by lamplight for the sake of a debt in salt.

Marta brought us supper on a tray and stood in the doorway a moment longer than the tray required.

"One more thing," she said, "and then I'll leave you to your ghosts. That poster in the post office — I noticed the date on it. Posted eight days gone." Her eyes went from Ruth to me and settled, grim, on mine. "Eight days, and it's only now reaching the villages this far up. Which means it started at the county seat and worked outward, slow as molasses, along every road Hobb controls." She handed me the tray. "Which means, my dears, that by tomorrow it'll be on every wall from here to the ferry, and every eye on this coast will be a paid informer. Whatever you're planning next —"

She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. The wind filled the silence for her, rattling the little window like a hand demanding entry, and down in the dark toward Gray Mouth the road waited, posted and watched, with nine days left on the clock.
