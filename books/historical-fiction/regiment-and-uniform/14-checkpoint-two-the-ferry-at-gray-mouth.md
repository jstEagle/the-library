## 14. Checkpoint Two: The Ferry at Gray Mouth

I had learned three things about Sable Nakamura in the days since the sea gave her to me, and none of them were on any poster.

First: she counted everything. Steps, coins, hours, the turns of a conversation. I once watched her tally a fisherman's change twice before he'd finished pocketing it, and when he shorted himself she corrected him with the gentle authority of a woman who had spent years being the only arithmetic in a hundred miles.

Second: she lied beautifully — but only with her face. Her words stayed honest as a ledger line. It was her expression that did the work, the widow's calm laid over whatever moved beneath it like oilskin over cargo.

Third: she never spoke of her husband. Not his name, not his hands, not the ring she still wore though mourning permitted no ornament and she wore nothing else. I noticed the ring the way you notice a nail that holds up a whole wall. Gold, plain, worn thin on one side from turning.

I thought about that ring all the way down to Gray Mouth, because I was doing sums of my own now. She had taught me without meaning to. Eleven days had become eight. The storm had eaten two whole days at Marta Quill's cottage while we sat drying ledger pages by peat smoke and listening to the wind try to take the roof off like a lid. Eight days until Justice Merrow's mail packet sailed, and between us and the county seat lay one river wide enough to swallow a coach, one ferry, and every road Hobb's riders could ride.

"They'll have watchers at the crossing," Sable said. We stood on the last rise above Gray Mouth, the river spread below us brown and swollen, carrying whole trees sideways like matchwood. "If they're watching anywhere, it's there."

"You could go around. Upstream."

"Two days' detour through country where every farmer knows every farmer's dog." She shook her head. "And the ford at Marlow's Deep is drowned by now. It's the ferry or nothing."

The village of Gray Mouth huddled at the river's edge as if it had been trying to climb away from the water for a hundred years and given up halfway. Slate roofs, a chapel, an inn with a sign swinging so hard in the gale it seemed determined to leave. And at the foot of the lane, the ferry house — a long low building with a slipway, a winch, and a chain running out into the churning gray where, somewhere, a flat-bottomed boat strained against its mooring.

A notice board stood beside the slipway, and the notice on it was fresh enough that the rain hadn't yet blurred the ink:

**FERRY SUSPENDED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. BY ORDER OF THE CUSTOMS COMMISSIONER.**

"Further notice," I said. "Meaning when?"

"Meaning whenever it suits them." Sable's eyes went along the waterfront, unhurried, cataloguing. A customs man under the inn's eaves, sheltering a pipe, watching the road more than the river. A boy hauling crates. An old woman who watched us back with the frank interest of someone with nothing better to do. "One officer. That's fewer than I expected."

"That's what worries me."

"Yes," she said. "Me too."

* * *

We took a room at the inn because standing in the rain made us memorable and sitting indoors made us furniture. Sable ordered tea and bread and cheese with the flat competence of a woman traveling to her sister's funeral, which was the story we'd settled on somewhere past the toll bridge and never improved upon because it needed no improvement. Grief was the best disguise I ever wore. Nobody presses a mourning woman for details; they press her for nothing at all, they just lower their voices around her and look sorry, and you want to scream.

The customs officer came in an hour later, stamped the rain off his cape, and drank one porter at the bar while reading the shipping news. I kept my head down over my tea and let my hood do its work, but I felt him glance at us the way a man glances at two horses in a field — noting color, noting condition, moving on.

"He didn't look at your face," Sable murmured.

"No."

"He looked at your boots."

I looked down. My boots were ruined, salt-crusted, torn at one seam from the quarry flood — from the night the water tried to keep Ruth Vane for itself, and Sable Nakamura went in after her without a second's hesitation, which is how I came to owe her a life I hadn't asked her to spend. "They're only boots."

"They're boots that have walked a coast nobody walks. When he goes out, we buy new ones. There was a cobbler two doors down."

"You count everything," I said.

"I count what keeps us alive." She turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer, a small ritual I'd seen a dozen times, and I understood suddenly that it wasn't nerves. It was her version of polishing the lens. Something for the hands while the mind worked. "The ferryman. Did you see him?"

"At the slipway? Big man, gray beard."

"He was looking at the water, not the road. The customs man watches the road because that's where trouble comes from. A ferryman watches the water because that's where his living drowns." Another quarter turn. "He'll cross tonight if the price is right. Men like that don't stop for weather; they stop for permission."

"And permission costs?"

"It always costs. The question is whether we pay in coin or something else."

* * *

We found him at dusk in the ferry house, mending net by lamplight, and he was exactly what Sable had predicted: a man built by forty years of river work into something low and dense as a bollard, with hands like knotted rope and a face that had stopped expecting good news sometime in the previous century. His name, painted on the boat and half-scraped by storms, was TREGEAR.

Sable didn't approach him directly. That was the artistry of it. She sent me to ask — the daughter, the grieving one, the soft touch — and stood back by the door with her hands folded like a servant waiting on her mistress, playing the part so completely I nearly forgot it was a part.

"The crossing's suspended," Tregear said, not looking up. "You can read."

"We can read," I said. "We can also pay."

That got his eyes up. They were pale as river fog and about as easy to see into. "Pay who? The notice says suspended. Money doesn't unsuspend things."

"Money doesn't," I said, "but it can be very persuasive company."

He set down his netting needle then, slowly, and looked from me to Sable and back. "What are you carrying?"

"Nothing. We're sisters. Our aunt died at Penzance way and we're wanted at the funeral."

"Funny kind of funeral, crossing a flooded river at night in a storm season."

"Grief doesn't check the weather," I said, and heard my father in it — Elias Vane could put a moral in a sentence the way other men put a hook in bait — and hated myself a little for using his voice as cover, and used it anyway.

Tregear grunted. He stood, joints cracking like the winch, and walked to the door and looked out at the dark and the rain and the river eating its banks. Then he named a price that would have bought his boat outright, plus the winch, plus probably the inn.

I had the money. We had money — carefully hoarded, carefully rationed, the whole journey budgeted like a voyage. But I hesitated, and here is the thing I will tell the truth about even when I lie about everything else: I hesitated because I was afraid. Not of the price. Of the transaction. Money leaves a shape. A big payment to a ferryman on a night crossing is a story a witness tells a magistrate, and I could see the shape of that story already: *the Vane girl paid her way across the Gray Mouth, gold in her glove.*

Sable must have seen the same shape, or a different one, because she stepped forward then, out of her servant's stillness, and said quietly:

"Will you take it in kind?"

Tregear looked at her. "In what kind?"

She pulled her left hand from her muff. The firelight caught the gold. "This."

* * *

I want to write that I objected. I want to write that I said, *no, not that, anything but that,* and that she smiled sadly and insisted, and that there was a moment of drama worthy of the ballads. None of that happened. That's not how sacrifice works, I've learned. The ballads get it wrong. Sacrifice made properly looks almost like nothing at all — a hand extended, a weight considered, a nod.

"Wedding ring," Tregear said. "Gold's gold. But a ring's worth less than the coin, madam. It's a hard market for sentiment."

"I'm not selling sentiment," Sable said. "I'm selling weight. Weigh it if you doubt me. And add this." She drew from her sleeve a small purse and poured a modest stream of sovereigns onto his table — perhaps a third of his asking price, perhaps less. "The rest in the ring. If the gold comes up light, we settle the difference in Penzance way, after the funeral."

He weighed it in his palm, that practiced river-man's weighing that is worth more than any scale, and looked at her hand, bare now, the pale band of skin where the ring had sat twenty years like a scar in reverse.

"Your husband dead?" he asked. It wasn't sentiment. It was appraisal — widows sell rings, everyone knew that, it was practically a trade.

"Two years," Sable said. Which was true. Which was also the most complicated true thing she had ever said, because the husband dead two years was Kenji Nakamura, keeper of Widow's Rock, drowned in an accident arranged by men whose names were written in the ledger sewn against my ribs — and the woman saying *two years* had carried that ring through every checkpoint, every lie, every mile, and set it down now on a ferryman's table between a mending needle and a cold lamp, without ceremony, without trembling, without one word of explanation to me.

"Storm took him?"

"The river did," Sable said. "Rivers always do."

Tregear grunted again, and something in the grunt was almost gentleness. "My brother went at the Mouth. Thirty-one years back." He pushed the ring into his pocket with the tenderness men reserve for objects they intend to resell. "Be at the slip at moonset. Two hours. Bring nothing that floats and wear nothing that shines. And if the customs man asks, I never saw you, because I didn't."

* * *

We had two hours to fill, and Sable filled them the way she filled everything: with counting. She counted our remaining coin aloud in a whisper, re-sewed the ledger's wrapping tighter against my body, and sat by the inn window with her hands folded, watching the rain.

"Say it," I finally said. "Whatever you're not saying."

"There's nothing not being said."

"Sable. You just handed a stranger the only thing you owned that mattered."

She was quiet a moment. Rain ran down the glass like the river running sideways. "It mattered," she agreed. "That's why it worked. A bribe in coin is a crime someone remembers. A wedding ring is a sad story someone forgets." She turned toward me, and in the lamplight her face was the widow's calm again, seamless — except that I had seen her dive into flooded quarry water for a girl she'd known five days, and I knew now that the calm was not emptiness. It was armor, and armor gets heavy. "Kenji gave it to me on the supply boat, coming out to the Rock the first time. He was seasick the entire way and pretended he wasn't. He said—" and here her voice did something I had never heard it do, a small fracture, quickly mortared over, "—he said a keeper's wife should have something steady to hold when the ground moves."

I reached across and put my hand over hers. "You didn't have to—"

"Yes, I did." Flat. Final. Keeper's arithmetic. "You carry the ledger. I carry the cost. That's the division of labor, and I chose it." She looked back at the window. "Besides. The ground has been moving for two years, Ruth. I've been holding a circle of gold and calling it steady. Tonight I found out what it actually buys."

"What does it buy?"

"A crossing." Almost a smile. "Which is what it always bought. I just never priced it honestly before."

* * *

Moonset came late and sullen, the moon going down behind cloud like a coin dropped into mud. At the slip, Tregear waited with the boat straining at its lines, the river roaring past gray-black and full of the storm's debris. The customs man, God bless his porter and his boredom, was nowhere in sight.

"In," Tregear said. "Sit low. Touch nothing."

We sat on the bilge boards with our backs against the gunwale while he cast off and took to the sweeps, and the river took the boat the way a cat takes a mouse — playfully at first, then seriously. Gray Mouth lived up to its name and worse; the current ran like a train, and trees came down on it whole, and once something pale and long slid past that Tregear looked at steadily and I made myself not look at at all.

Halfway across, the boat swung broadside to the current, and for ten seconds — I counted them, Sable's habit infecting me — for ten full seconds we were neither going nor arriving, just hanging on the water while the far bank refused to come closer. My hand found Sable's in the dark. Hers was steady. Mine was not.

"Tregear," she said calmly, over the roar, "how deep is it here?"

"Deep enough," he called back, "that what sinks stays sunk. So don't sink." And he bent to the sweeps, and the far bank came, and came, and the keel ground gravel at last in the lee of the opposite slip, and we climbed out into a different county, which felt absurdly anticlimactic — all that terror, and the border was just wet shingle.

Tregear held the boat against the bank, breathing hard. "Roads ahead," he said. "Customs post at the top of the hill, but it's a lazy one — they only stop carts. Walk apart, walk slow, look bored." He paused, then dug in his pocket and held out his hand. On his palm lay the ring.

"Change of heart?" Sable asked.

"Change of arithmetic." He wouldn't meet her eye. "My brother went at the Mouth, I told you. Drowned keeping a fool's promise to a man he shouldn't have trusted. Somebody paid his widow afterward. Quietly. Never knew who." He closed her fingers over the ring with his great rope-hand. "River gives back what it likes. Go on, before I come to my senses. Gold's gold, but some rings are bad luck to hold."

Sable stood a moment with the ring in her fist, and the rain came down, and the river roared, and I saw her throat move once.

"Thank you," she said, and put it on, and turned her hand over in the dark to watch the gold catch what little light there was.

Then Tregear pushed off, and we turned uphill toward the lazy customs post and the long road to the county seat — and that was when we saw it: far behind us, back along the road we'd come, two points of lantern light swinging fast through the rain, closing on the slipway we had just left.

Hobb's riders had reached Gray Mouth.

They were four minutes too late. I counted.
