## 15. The Middle of the Water

The ferry at Gray Mouth was not a ferry so much as a large, bad-tempered rowboat with pretensions. It was flat-bottomed and tarred black, and its master — a man named Cobb, which struck me as the sea's idea of a joke — had a face like a dropped pie and the conversational range of one.

"Crossing's suspended," he said when we reached the slip, arms crossed over an oilskin that had seen more weather than most churches. "River's in spate. Patrol'll have my head if I put out."

Sable didn't argue. Sable never argued. She simply stood there in her widow's black with the rain coming off her hood like a curtain, and she let the silence do the work that other people needed words for. I have watched her do it perhaps forty times since we left Widow's Rock, and I have never once grown used to it. Silence, in her hands, is a loaded weapon. She simply holds it out, and men find themselves explaining themselves to fill it.

"Storm's coming down from the north," she said at last, mildly, as though remarking on the price of herring. "Roads'll flood by morning. Anyone wanting to cross will want to cross tonight."

Cobb looked at her. He looked at me — Ruth, who lies badly, doing my level best to look like a niece attending a funeral rather than the most wanted woman on this coast. Then he looked back at Sable, and something moved behind his eyes that I have learned to recognize: the arithmetic of a man deciding how much trouble he can afford.

"Ferry's four shillings," he said.

"Six," said Sable, "if you don't light your lamp past mid-channel."

* * *

I want it recorded somewhere — even if only in the privacy of my own skull, which is the only ledger anyone is likely to keep of these days — that I objected. Quietly, but I objected. The ring she held out wasn't gold worth six shillings; it was worn thin at the band, warm from her hand, and there was an inscription inside that I caught only a flash of before her fist closed around it. Kenji. A K and a name in small letters, like a secret kept against the skin for years.

"You could sell your coat first," I said. "Sell mine. Sell me, possibly. There's a market for quiet women who can row."

"It's only metal," she said, and gave it to Cobb, and Cobb bit it out of habit, then seemed ashamed of himself for biting something that plainly mattered more than gold, and stowed it in his breast pocket with unusual gentleness.

That was the thing about Sable Nakamura I was only beginning to understand, in those days: grief did not make her brittle. It made her precise. She spent what she had exactly where it needed spending, without ceremony, because ceremony was a luxury and luxuries got people hanged. My father would have liked her enormously. That thought arrived like a stone dropping into still water, and I made myself watch the river instead.

We boarded. Cobb took the oars. His boy — a nephew, maybe, all elbows and fourteen years — took the steering sweep, and Sable sat amidships with her hands folded in her lap like a woman going to church, except that under her skirt, sewn into the mourning braid above the hem, lay the ledger that would hang a magistrate or hang us all.

The Gray Mouth at night, in spate, is not a crossing so much as an argument between the river and the sea, with the boat as the disputed territory. The current came down brown and fast, the tide pushed back up salt and stubborn, and where they met the water stood up in short, vicious seas that smacked the hull and threw spray across us in cold handfuls. Cobb rowed with the economy of a man who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more, and his nephew steered by some private conversation he appeared to be having with the water itself.

Halfway across, the middle of the river, the true middle of the dark — that was when the lantern happened.

It came off our stern quarter, maybe two hundred yards, a white riding light swinging gently, patient as a fisherman's float. A patrol boat. Of course a patrol boat. Hobb owned the checkpoints, but a river in flood is a checkpoint too, and the crown does love its paperwork.

"Down," Cobb said, without turning his head, and his voice was so flat and so ordinary that we obeyed before understanding, both of us folding down into the bilge water and tarpaulin like sacks of meal.

Then he did the thing I will remember until I die. He reached up — never missing a stroke — and killed his own lamp.

Not hid it. Killed it. Pinched the wick with wet fingers, quick as snuffing a candle at bedtime, and the world went black. Total, absolute, river-black, the kind of dark where you cannot see your own hand and must take the existence of everything else entirely on faith.

And then he shipped the oars.

"Muffled," he breathed, barely a sound at all. "Now. Nobody breathes loud."

His nephew wrapped the oar-locks in sacking, and the boat stopped fighting the current and started drifting with it, sideways, downstream, a dead leaf among dead leaves. We were no longer a ferry carrying fugitives. We were flotsam. We were nothing. The whole trick of it, I realized, lying with my cheek against wet canvas and Sable's shoulder hard against mine, was to become so uninteresting that the night itself forgot us.

The white light grew.

* * *

There is a particular quality to fear in complete darkness: it has texture. It has weight. It sits on the chest like a cat that has decided you are furniture. I lay in the bilge with the ledger's presence somehow audible — I could feel it against Sable's side, or imagined I could, a small stiff heartbeat sewn into black cloth — and I listened to the patrol boat come on.

Voices carried over the water. Two men, maybe three. The creak of a tiller. The slap of water against a hull bigger and better-built than ours. And underneath it all, the river itself, talking constantly, saying nothing, covering every small sound we couldn't help making — the drip off the steering sweep, Cobb's controlled breathing, the tiny knock of the muffled oars against the thole pins as the drift rolled us.

The light swung closer. Closer. It passed abeam — I knew it not because I could see anything, but because the voices were suddenly on our left instead of ahead, near enough that I could smell pipe smoke off the water, and hear one of them say:

"—nothing out tonight. Ferryman's no fool. Not in this."

"Not paid to be a fool either," said the second voice. "Hobb wants eyes on every crossing. If two women come through—"

"Two women." The first voice laughed, short and ugly. "Half the widows on this coast are two women. What's she done, then, this Vane girl? Fly?"

"Fly or drown. Either way, fifty pounds says someone collects."

Fifty pounds. My father's life, priced at less than a good fishing boat. I felt fury rise in me like the flood water, hot and stupid and wanting to shout — and then Sable's hand found mine in the dark.

She didn't squeeze it. That's the remarkable part. She simply covered it, the way you'd cover a candle flame against wind, and held it there, and I understood without a word being spoken: *not now, not here, not ever, if we do this right.* Her palm was dry and steady and slightly calloused from the lamp-room work, and it was the calmest thing in that entire river, calmer than the water, calmer than Cobb, possibly calmer than God.

I held still. We held still. The boat drifted.

The patrol light slid past our stern by what I judged — with nothing to judge by but terror — to be thirty feet. Maybe less. Close enough that when one of them coughed, I heard him swallow after. Close enough that if any man aboard had thought to sweep a lantern low along the black water instead of trusting the dark to be empty, we'd have been lit up like a stage, three bodies and a boy in a tarred tub, and fifty pounds would have changed hands before the current finished with us.

But they trusted the dark. Men always trust the dark to be empty, because mostly it is, and because looking properly into it is work. Our whole survival hung on their laziness, and I resolved, lying there in the bilge, that if I lived I would never again be lazy in the dark as long as I drew breath.

"Mid-channel's clear," said the first voice, receding now. "Come about. Storm's building; I'm not drowning for Hobb's nerves."

The light dwindled. The voices went with it, arguing comfortably about tea or brandy or whose turn it was to bail, and then there was only the river again, vast and indifferent, carrying us sideways toward a bank none of us could see.

* * *

Nobody moved for a long time. I counted — it's what I do, counting, my father taught me to count when frightened, "numbers are honest, Ruthie, numbers can't be bribed" — and I counted four hundred of Cobb's slow breaths before he finally whispered, "Lamp stays dead till the far shore. Nephew, ease her round. Oars soft."

The boy unwound the sacking just enough, and the oars went in without a splash, and we began to move again, slowly, silently, a ghost ferry poling through the flooded dark. Sable's hand stayed on mine the whole while. Neither of us mentioned it then. Neither of us ever mentioned it after. Some things are truer for going unsaid.

It was Cobb who broke the silence eventually, in his way — which is to say, grudgingly, facing forward, addressing the river rather than us.

"That ring," he said. "Weren't pawn-shop metal. I've handled enough of it to know."

"No," Sable said.

"He give it to you?"

"A jeweler in the county town. He chose the inscription himself. Said a ring should say what the mouth forgets."

Cobb chewed on that for half the channel. "My missus has one like that," he said finally. "Never takes hers off neither. Not for washing, not for burying." A pause. "You'll have it back, come morning. No charge for the crossing."

"You took the ring."

"I took the ring so you'd have a reason to live long enough to collect it." He rowed. "A woman who pays six shillings for a boat ride wants something badly enough to survive getting it. A woman who gives away a wedding ring free — that one's halfway to letting the river have her. I've ferried that kind too. Once."

The rain thickened. Somewhere behind us the storm we'd outrun began announcing itself against the hills, low thunder rolling down the valley like furniture being moved in heaven. And Sable, in the dark beside me, said nothing at all — but her hand, still resting near mine on the wet thwart, had closed into a fist, and stayed that way the rest of the crossing.

The far shore came up out of the blackness all at once, the way shores do — reeds first, then mud-smell, then the loom of the customs house on the bluff with its one mean window burning. Checkpoint Two, waiting for us on the far side, though we meant to go around it, wide through the marsh, walking the long way in the dark rather than showing ourselves at the gate.

Cobb ran the bow into the reeds soft as a kiss. His nephew held the boat steady while we stepped out into shin-deep water and sucking mud, and then Cobb leaned over the gunwale, pressed something into Sable's palm, and closed her fingers over it.

Her ring. Warm from his pocket, as promised.

"Go on," he said. "And missus — whatever's sewn into that skirt of yours? I ferried smugglers twenty years. I know a cargo hold when I sit next to one." He spat over the side, philosophically. "Hope it's worth the river."

Then he pushed off, lamp still dead, and melted back into the dark he'd come from, and we stood alone in the reeds with the storm coming down off the hills toward us like a debt collector.

Sable opened her fist. Looked at the ring a moment. Put it back on her finger, where it belonged, where it had always belonged.

"The marsh path," she said quietly, "runs three miles to the drover's road. After that—" She stopped. In the lightning's brief glare I saw her face turned south, toward Marta Quill's cottage, toward shelter, toward the next impossible thing. But what she actually said was: "After that, Ruth, we should talk about my husband. You've been carrying my secrets. It seems only fair I carry yours."

And the rain came down, and we walked.
