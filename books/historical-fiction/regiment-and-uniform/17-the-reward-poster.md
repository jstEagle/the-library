## 17. The Reward Poster

I had been dead for two years before I was ever born.

That is the sort of sentence you can only write about yourself when your life has become a legal document with a clerical error in it, which mine had. Elias Vane, customs clerk of the port town of Harrowgate, convicted of treason against the Crown, hanged on a grey morning in the autumn of '97 while I watched from a crowd that would not look at me — that man had been dead two years. His daughter Ruth Vane, who had spent those two years being nobody in three different towns under three different dull names, had never legally existed at all until last week, when she washed out of the sea at Widow's Rock and started, apparently, existing very loudly.

The poster in the stationer's window made it official.

*WANTED — RUTH VANE, ACCESSORY TO TREASON. FIFTY POUNDS REWARD. ANSWERS TO NO KNOWN ALIAS. LAST SEEN ON THE NORTH COAST.*

Fifty pounds. I stood in the drizzle outside Mr. Tredwell's shop in the village of Marrow St. Anne — a village whose entire commercial life appeared to consist of one stationer's, one butcher's, one chapel, and an enormous quantity of mud — and I read my own name four times, waiting for it to stop looking like mine.

It did not stop.

*Answers to no known alias.* Well, that was flattering. It meant Hobb's men had given up trying to guess what I called myself, which meant someone had described me accurately, which meant the wreck site had been picked over by more than gulls. Someone had found my boot prints in the shingle, or a scrap of my petticoat, or — worse — had seen us, two women walking the coast road, and put the description together with the reward arithmetic and come up with a sum worth riding inland for.

Sable stood beside me under the shop's dripping awning, her basket over her arm, wearing the particular stillness of a woman doing sums of her own. She had sent me ahead to buy tea and lamp oil while she spoke to the fishmonger about mackerel, because we had agreed — without ever saying it aloud — that we should not be seen standing together too long in any one place. Two women traveling alone invite comment. Two women traveling together invite *description*. And descriptions were now, evidently, currency.

"Fifty pounds," I said quietly.

"I see it."

"That's a year's wages for a good clerk. My father was a good clerk."

"I know what he was." She said it flatly, not unkindly, the way you'd correct a child who'd said something obvious. "Look at the printing, not the number."

So I looked at the printing. The notice bore the county crest and the words *By Order of the Magistrate*, and beneath them a signature I recognized from my father's papers: *Corliss Hobb, J.P.* The same hand that had signed the death warrant. Now signing me. There was a neatness to it that made my teeth ache — the same pen, the same ink, the same man converting a living woman into a fugitive as easily as he'd converted an honest clerk into a traitor. He didn't even have to invent anything new. He just took his old lie and gave it a daughter.

"He didn't even bother to make it interesting," I said. "*Accessory to treason.* Not smuggling, not conspiracy, not misprision. Treason. He wants me hanged for the same crime, so no one asks why there are two warrants with the same handwriting."

"Or so that anyone who finds you feels justified," Sable said. "Treason frightens people. Smuggling only interests them." She shifted the basket. "We should go."

"In a moment." I couldn't help myself. I read it once more, all of it, every word, committing it to memory the way I'd once memorized tariff schedules at my father's elbow. *Last seen on the North Coast.* They knew the coast. They didn't yet know the rock, or they'd have sealed Widow's Rock already — unless they were saving it, letting us run home like rabbits to a warren they intended to dig out at leisure. That thought arrived cold and stayed cold.

A woman came out of the stationer's, bell jangling, and paused beside us to shake rain from her umbrella. Her eyes went to the window. To the poster. To me. A beat — half a second, no longer — in which the whole machinery of the world seemed to hold its breath: fifty pounds, a young woman, dark hair, wrong clothes for this weather.

"She'll have gone far by now," the woman said comfortably, to no one in particular. "They say down the coast. My sister's boy saw the wreck at Widow's Rock — nothing left but timber." She tucked her umbrella under her arm and trudged off through the mud toward the chapel, and I discovered I had been holding a breath long enough to feel faint.

"Nothing left but timber," I repeated.

"People see what a notice tells them to see," Sable said. "She looked straight past you because you're standing in a doorway buying tea, not drowning. Come along, Ruth. Grieve somewhere indoors."

*Grieve.* I nearly laughed. But she was right, in her granite way. What I felt wasn't fear, or not mostly. It was something with more iron in it.

* * *

The inn at Marrow St. Anne had a fire, watered beer, and a landlady who asked no questions because she charged enough that questions would have seemed like ingratitude. We took the back room, the one with the low ceiling and the window onto the yard, and Sable counted our remaining coin on the table with the grim attention of a quartermaster, and I sat by the fire and let myself be angry properly, in private, the way you take a bandage off a wound you've been carrying all day.

Because here is the thing I could not say in the street: the poster was not frightening. It was *insulting*. It was a libel written by a coward with a magistrate's seal, and everyone who read it would believe it, because paper believes itself. Somewhere in London or in the assize records there was a true account of my father — the man who worked Sundays without pay during the harvest crisis; who reported the short-weighted grain shipments in '94 and was thanked with a demotion; who taught me to add columns of figures in my head because "a girl who can total faster than a man will always know when she's being cheated." That man had been rewritten, two years ago, into a traitor. And now the same pen was redrafting me in his image, as though treachery were an inheritance, as though I'd been born owing the rope a debt.

*Accessory.* As if I'd carried his supposed treason in my pocket like a housewife carries keys. As if I had stood beside him at that trial — I hadn't; they wouldn't allow family near him after the verdict — and nodded along.

My father had smuggled one thing out before they took him, and it was the truth, sewn into an oilskin packet and given to a fisherman who owed him his son's liberty, and it had waited two years in the dark of Widow's Rock for the sea to give it back. Everything else — the name, the honor, the quiet decent life — Hobb had burned. And now Hobb wanted the packet's courier hanged too, tidily, cheaply, for the original sin of existing.

"You're doing it again," Sable said.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting like a kettle about to whistle." She set the last coin down. "Say it instead. It travels better."

So I said it. "He signed my death warrant with the same hand that signed my father's. He's using the same lie twice because he knows it works. And fifty pounds means every bored, broke, ambitious man between here and the county seat is going to be looking at young women's faces for the next eleven days and imagining themselves heroes." I heard my own voice climb and made myself stop. "There. Kettle whistled."

"Good." She poured the watered beer into two cups, which told you everything about our finances. "Now think like your father taught you. You said you can total faster than a man."

"What has that to do with—"

"Everything. Fifty pounds buys pursuit, but it also advertises. Every man hunting you for that money now knows the ring exists — knows there's a girl, a coast, a deadline. You cannot keep a secret and post it in windows at the same time. Hobb has chosen breadth over depth. He's searching everywhere, which means he's searching nowhere well." She drank, watching me over the rim. "Your father understood ledgers. So does Hobb. He has just entered a very large expense against himself, and large expenses make men nervous, and nervous men make mistakes."

I stared at her. Two years I had run on fear — small rooms, false names, trains taken at dawn, the constant animal arithmetic of *am I safe, am I safe, am I safe* — and in five minutes this woman had reframed my own manhunt as a line item in somebody else's accounts.

"You talk about my father's murder like a balance book," I said, and meant it as almost a compliment.

"It's the only language that thing understands." She tipped her chin at my satchel, where the ledger rode against my spare shift, wrapped in oilcloth. "And it's the language we'll beat him in. Not by outrunning fifty pounds' worth of strangers. By making sure that when Justice Merrow opens that ledger, there isn't a soul in the county who hasn't already half-guessed the truth. Fear keeps secrets. Shame publishes them. Hobb's whole power is that decent people can't imagine a magistrate selling his country's coastline. So we make them imagine it."

"How?"

"We get to Gray Mouth first," she said, "and we let the road do its work. One checkpoint at a time." She rose to bank the fire, and paused with her hand on the poker. "Also, we burn that poster out of your head by morning. Fury is fuel, Ruth, but it burns fast and leaves ash. Resolve is coal. It burns slow."

"And how do I make that trade, exactly?"

"You write your father's name on a piece of paper," she said, "and then you write what you intend to do about it underneath. Then you fold it up small and carry it where the poster can't reach." She held out a stub of pencil and the back of the tea wrapper. "Keeper's habit. We log everything. Even ourselves."

I took the pencil. My hand shook — not with fear, for once, but with something that had been waiting two years for permission.

I wrote: *Elias Vane, customs clerk, honest.* And beneath it: *His daughter is going to make Corliss Hobb read his own signature aloud to a judge.*

I folded it small and put it in my breast pocket, over my heart, where the ledger's weight usually lay. It fit there as though it had been cut to measure.

* * *

We left Marrow St. Anne before first light, in rain fine as needlework, and walked the coast road south with our collars up and our faces down, two mourners, one funeral, no story worth a stranger's second glance. At the top of the lane above the village I permitted myself one look back. The stationer's window was a pale rectangle in the blue-dark, and behind the glass, faint as a ghost in its own house, the poster hung with my name on it.

Let it hang, I thought. Let fifty pounds hang in every window from here to the county seat. My father used to say that a false entry in a ledger doesn't weaken the true ones — it just sits among them, getting wronger every day, waiting for the audit.

The audit was coming. Eleven days, one judge, one packet of oilskin truth, and two women the Crown had finally, officially, admitted existed.

Behind us, the rain erased the road. Ahead of us, at Gray Mouth, a ferry waited on a river the color of pewter — and somewhere on that road, though we did not know it yet, a man in magistrate's livery was already asking questions about two women, one of whom answered to no known alias, and both of whom were about to learn exactly how expensive fifty pounds can become.
