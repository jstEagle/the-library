## 18. The Turncoat's Daughter

Marta Quill kept her kettle on the hob the way other women kept a dog — close, warm, and always within reach of her hand. By the second morning at her cottage above Gray Mouth, I had learned that the kettle meant *sit*, the pouring meant *talk is coming*, and the silence afterward meant *I am deciding how much to tell you*.

She was deciding now.

Ruth — still Ruth, still wearing my dead sister-in-law's name like a borrowed coat two sizes too small — sat across from me at the scrubbed pine table, mending a tear in her sleeve with stitches so fine they were nearly invisible. She had been a clerk's daughter before she was anything else, and it showed in her hands. Everything she did was precise, deliberate, and slightly too careful, as though the whole world might be an examination she hadn't studied for.

I had spent three days watching this girl lie badly and mend beautifully, and I had come to a conclusion I did not share with either of them: Ruth Vane was not her father's daughter in temperament. Elias Vane, from everything the letter and the ledger and Marta's careful gossip had assembled, had been a man who trusted too easily and wrote too much. His daughter trusted no one and wrote nothing down. She kept everything in her head, where it could not be seized at a checkpoint or read over her shoulder by candlelight.

Except, of course, she had already trusted me. That was the strange arithmetic of our journey. She had handed a stranger a packet worth her life because the stranger had pulled her out of the surf, and now we were bound together by the weight of that transaction. You do not betray the person who dragged you breathing from the rocks. It would be like drowning them twice.

The kettle began its low complaint. Marta poured three cups without asking, which meant talk was indeed coming, and set the pot back on the hob.

"Now," she said, sitting down heavily in her chair by the fire, "you'll want to know about Pike."

I looked up. Ruth's needle stopped mid-stitch.

"Aldous Pike," Marta said, and the way she said the surname made it sound like something you'd scrape off your boot. "Sergeant Aldous Pike, chief man to Magistrate Hobb these six years, and stationed where d'you think?"

"Checkpoint three," Ruth said quietly. "The customs house at the headland."

"That's right." Marta wrapped both hands around her cup, though I noticed she didn't drink. "And do you know what Pike was, before he was a sergeant? Before he wore the magistrate's livery and collected his wages from men better born than him?"

"No," Ruth said.

"He was your father's clerk."

I heard the words land. Ruth did not move, did not flinch, but her face went very still — that particular stillness I had learned to recognize in myself when grief arrived dressed as news. The needle stayed exactly where it was, piercing the cloth, and for perhaps four seconds she did not breathe at all.

"His clerk," Ruth repeated.

"Elias Vane took him on when Pike was nineteen," Marta said. "Nineteen! A nobody's son from up the valley, no trade, no letters, and Vane — who was soft as butter where hard cases were concerned, God forgive him — gave him a desk, taught him the law, paid him out of his own pocket when the fees ran short. My Kenji—" she stopped herself, glancing at me, and I gave her the smallest nod; she had earned the husband's name, if anyone had, "—my late friend used to say Vane kept Pike the way a man keeps a stray dog, out of habit and kindness and no very good sense."

"And Pike betrayed him." Ruth's voice came out flat as slate.

"Pike informed on him," Marta corrected, with the precision of a woman who had spent forty years married to a customs man and believed words should be weighed like contraband. "There's a difference, though I grant you it's a difference without much mercy in it. He went to Hobb — some say before the smuggling was ever discovered, some say after — and he told Hobb where Vane kept his papers, which nights he worked late, which witnesses could be bought and which would need to be frightened instead. He drew maps of the office. He copied pages. A clerk knows where everything is kept, girl. A clerk is the lock's own key turned against it."

Ruth set down the mending. Very carefully, the way one sets down something fragile, or something explosive.

"I remember him," she said.

Marta and I both looked at her.

"I was twelve." She was staring into the middle distance, at some year and some room only she could see. "He used to bring his supper to the office and eat at the corner of my father's desk. He'd let me wind his watch. He had a watch with a cracked crystal and he'd say, *you've the steadiest fingers in the house, Miss Ruth, mind you don't drop it.*" Her mouth did something terrible — half smile, half wound. "I dropped it once. The glass cracked worse. He said it wasn't my fault. He said the watch had always been unlucky."

Nobody spoke. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the wind worried at the eaves with the persistence of a creditor.

"The watch was unlucky," I said finally, "or the man was."

"The man," Ruth said. "The man was unlucky for everyone who trusted him." She looked down at her hands, those precise, careful hands. "I let a murderer wind a watch in my memory all these years and called it a kindness."

"You were twelve," I said. "You can't try a child for the crimes of her elders. Heaven knows someone ought to have told the law that, since it seems to have forgotten."

That earned the ghost of a laugh, which had been my intention. But underneath the small joke, something colder was settling into me, the way silt settles in still water. Because I knew what it was to discover that a person you had lived beside was not the person you had thought. I had spent seven years learning it. The town had decided what Kenji Nakamura was before he ever opened his mouth, and no amount of lamp-oil or honest logbook entries could burn that verdict away. The town had needed a foreigner to blame for the wrecks and the shortages and the bad luck of a hard coast, and Kenji had stood in the doorway of their need until the day the sea took him, and even then they'd said *perhaps*, *perhaps it wasn't accident*, in the way people say such things when they want the comfort of suspicion without the labor of proof.

Pike had done the same thing to Vane, only with paperwork. There was something almost respectable in it. Almost English.

"How do you know all this?" I asked Marta. "Truly. Gossip is one thing, but you speak as though you'd read the man's diary."

Marta drank at last, setting the cup down with a click. "Because my Tom rode customs dispatches for eleven years before his chest gave out, and dispatch riders hear things. And because Pike himself passed through here twice — once on his way up, once last spring, riding escort to Hobb. He took tea in this very room." She nodded at the table, and Ruth drew her hands into her lap as though the wood itself had grown teeth. "A smooth man. Complimentary about my baking. Asked clever questions about the coast road and never once looked at your face while doing it — watched your hands instead. That's how you know a born informer. Honest men look at faces. Informers watch hands, because hands hold things."

I thought of my own hands, folded on the table, three feet from where the ledger lay sewn into the false bottom of Ruth's traveling basket under a layer of darning wool. I moved them anyway. Habit.

"So this is the man at checkpoint three," I said. "This is the gate we must pass."

"Not pass," Ruth said. Something had changed in her voice since Marta had spoken the name. The fear was still there — I would not insult her by pretending otherwise — but fear had been joined by something harder. "Gotten around. Or gotten through. Marta — does he know me? Would he know my face?"

Marta was quiet a moment, and I watched her weigh the truth against kindness and find the scale tipping, as it always tipped with her, toward truth. "He knew you at twelve, love. Twelve-year-olds grow. And grief changes a face more than years do — you've got your mother's look now where you had your father's then, if I recall. But a man like Pike doesn't rely on memory. He relies on paper. Descriptions. He'll have a sheet with your age, your height, the color of your eyes, and he'll have men at every gate comparing the sheet to every young woman that passes." She paused. "And there's the reward."

"The reward," Ruth repeated. Fifty pounds. It had been posted in three villages between here and the headland, and we had passed two of them with our collars up. Fifty pounds was more than a fisherman earned in two years. Fifty pounds had turned neighbors into informers along this coast for generations, and it would again.

"Fifty pounds makes fathers thoughtful about daughters," Marta said bluntly. "It makes sisters thoughtful about sisters. It made *me* thoughtful, and I owe this house's very roof-beams to a debt I'll tell you about someday, girl, and not today. So understand what you're carrying. You're not carrying a ledger. You're carrying fifty pounds walking around in a skirt, and every soul from here to the assize town will be weighing whether to sell you."

"And yet here you sit, telling us everything," I said, "instead of riding to the magistrate."

Marta looked at me across the table, and her old eyes had the gray patience of weather. "Because fifty pounds spends," she said. "And debts don't. Your husband carried me out of the surf at Widow's Rock when I was a fool girl in a leaking boat, Astrid, and I have been paying interest on that rescue for thirty years. Pike sold a man who fed him off his own table. I know which of the two of us I'd rather be. It's the only wealth I've got left, but it's mine."

Ruth had gone white. I reached across and put my hand over hers, and she gripped back hard enough to hurt, which was, I think, the point.

* * *

We walked out that afternoon, the two of us, because walls get small when dread lives in them. The storm had blown itself east overnight and left the coast washed and glittering, gulls wheeling and complaining over the tideline like unpaid clerks. Below us, Gray Mouth's river ran brown with the rain, and the ferry landing looked small and abandoned, the crossing chain hanging slack.

"Tell me about him," Ruth said. "About my father. Not the trial. Everyone tells the trial. Tell me the man."

I hesitated. "I met him once."

Her head came around sharply. In eleven days — no, nine now, nine days until Justice Merrow's circuit left on the mail packet — she had never once asked me directly, though I had seen the question living behind her eyes like a tenant behind curtains.

"At the trial?" she whispered.

"Before it." I kept my eyes on the water. "My husband attended every day of the proceedings. He said a man deserved witnesses, even condemned ones. Especially condemned ones. But I'd seen Vane earlier — two summers before, when he came to the light station on circuit business. There had been a dispute about a wrecked cargo and whose jurisdiction covered the salvage, and the magistrate's office sent someone to take depositions from the keepers." I smiled despite myself. "It was supposed to be Hobb himself. Hobb sent his clerk instead. Too much trouble to ride out for a widow and a foreigner, I imagine."

"What was he like?"

"Tired," I said. "Kind. He took off his hat to me, which no official man had ever done at that door. And when Kenji brought him tea, he drank it and complimented the brew, and I saw Kenji decide in that moment that the man was good. Kenji judged people by how they treated keepers and servants and dogs. He said the rest was performance." I paused. "Vane asked me questions about the lens. Real questions. He wanted to understand how the light turned, why the glass was ground in that particular curve, what kept it turning through the storm. Most men ask to be polite. He asked because he genuinely wanted to know. And then he wrote everything down in a little book he kept in his breast pocket, and he thanked me as though I'd done him a service instead of answering questions."

Ruth was crying silently. She did not wipe her cheeks or apologize for it, which I respected enormously.

"The little book," she said. "He always had the little book. Mother hated it. She said he trusted ink more than flesh."

"He did," I said gently. "Ink was kinder to him than flesh turned out to be. Until it wasn't."

We stood a while in the wind.

"My father trusted Pike with everything," Ruth said at last. "The letter says so — you've read it. *I cannot believe Aldous would.* Those are nearly his last written words about the man. He suspected everyone else. He'd begun to suspect Hobb. But Pike —" She shook her head. "He couldn't see it. The one blind spot. And the blind spot hanged him."

"Then we won't have blind spots," I said. "That's the lesson, isn't it? We assume Pike already knows a woman matching your description is on the coast road. We assume he knows the wreck site where you came ashore. We assume the toll bridge sergeant reported two travelers, one in deep mourning, and that report is already on Pike's desk. We stop hoping we're invisible. Invisible is a luxury we lost the night the sea gave you back to me."

"Then what do we have?"

"We have the fact that Pike is a clerk," I said, "and clerks believe in paper. He believes the world can be filed and described and caught in a written net. So we become something his net has no mesh wide enough for." I turned to face her. "Nine days. One checkpoint left between us and the assize town, and it belongs to the man who knows your face best of anyone alive. We're going to need help that isn't mine or Marta's."

"Whose, then?"

I looked north along the coast, toward the dark smudge on the horizon where Widow's Rock stood wearing its cloud, and felt the tower pull at me like a tide.

"There's an answer to that," I said. "But it's going to cost more than fifty pounds, and I don't yet know what the coin will be."

Behind us, up the lane, a horse's hooves rang on wet stone — unhurried, official, wrong. Marta appeared in her doorway and did not call out. She simply raised one hand, palm flat, the old keeper's signal every coastal wife knew: *stranger coming, quiet now.*

The rider wore magistrate's livery.

Ruth stepped back into the shadow of the gorse without being told, and I stayed where I was in plain view, an honest widow watching the tide go out, while Sergeant Aldous Pike's dispatch rider slowed his horse and looked at me with the appraising, hand-watching eyes of a man who had been given a description — and was deciding, right there in the lane, how closely I fit it.
