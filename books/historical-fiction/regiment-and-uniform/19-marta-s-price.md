## 19. Marta's Price

Marta Quill kept her cottage the way she kept her promises: sparsely, and with nothing wasted. I had known her for two days by then — long enough to learn that she salted her porridge like a woman settling a grudge against blandness, and long enough to understand that every kindness she'd extended to us had been weighed on scales older and more accurate than any customs officer's.

She weighed things now, standing at her window with her arms folded, watching the road.

"He'll come tomorrow," she said. "Maybe tonight, if the weather turns them back early. Tam rides the Gray Mouth circuit Thursdays. He's ridden it eleven years."

I sat at her table with a cup of tea going cold in front of me, and I did the arithmetic before I could stop myself. A customs rider's mother, sheltering two fugitives — one of them the most wanted woman on the coast, worth fifty pounds to anyone with loose morals and a fast horse. "Then we should go tonight," I said.

"We should have gone yesterday," Marta said, without turning around. "But the ford was up, and Sable said drowning my guests would be poor thanks." She glanced back at me, and there was something in her face that wasn't quite humor. "So. Tonight, then. Before first light if you must, but before Tam comes home."

* * *

I want to be honest about what those last hours in Marta's cottage were, because it would be easy — now, looking back — to dress them up as noble. They weren't noble. They were cramped and damp and full of small dishonesties, and I spent most of them lying badly to a woman who could smell a lie the way gulls smell fish offal.

"You could come with us," I said, somewhere past midnight, when the peat fire had burned down to a red eye and the rain had settled into that steady, patient drumming that means it intends to stay all night and well into next week. "There's nothing for you here but trouble once Hobb learns you hid us."

"There was nothing for me here before you came either," Marta said. She was re-packing our provisions with the brisk economy of a woman who had provisioned ships, houses, funerals, and at least one elopement in her time. "That's not the same as leaving."

"Your son—"

"Is a grown man who chose his uniform," she said, "the same way Kenji Nakamura chose his lamp, the same way your father chose his ledgers, the same way I chose to owe a debt I can't pay except like this." She tied the sack with a jerk. "Tam doesn't know we're here. He doesn't know about the debt. And he will never know, because dead men can't be asked questions, and I intend for both of us to go on being alive men who don't get asked."

It was the longest speech I'd heard from her. I understood perhaps three-quarters of it, and the quarter I didn't understand was the part about the debt itself. Sable knew. Sable had called it in with four sentences and a look, whatever it was — some night years ago when a keeper named Kenji had pulled a drowning woman out of the sea, or stood between her and something worse; Sable never told me the whole of it, and Marta never offered, and I learned eventually that some debts are like the ledger sewn into my skirt: you carry them close, and you show them to almost no one.

"I'm sorry," I said, which was inadequate, and she knew it was inadequate, and she let me say it anyway because that too is a kind of hospitality.

"Don't be sorry," she said. "Be gone by dawn."

* * *

Sable slept, or appeared to. That was her talent, then and always: she could fold herself into stillness the way other people folded laundry, and you couldn't tell rest from vigil until it mattered. I lay awake beside the banked fire listening to the rain rehearse its storm, and I listened to Marta moving through the dark rooms of her cottage doing small necessary things — banking the fire, mending a shutter, pausing once outside the room where Sable slept, for a long moment, without opening the door.

At some hour I gave up pretending and went to her.

She was at the table again, and this time she had a candle lit, and she was writing. Not much. A few lines on a scrap of paper no bigger than a prayer card, her hand slow and deliberate, the way people write when they're writing something they may not live to explain.

"What's that?" I asked.

"Instructions," she said, "for my son. If it ever comes to him that his mother harbored the Vane girl." She blew on the ink, folded the scrap twice, and slid it into the tin where she kept her tea — which I understood, even then, was the one place in a customs household no one would search, because searching the tea was an accusation, and accusations among neighbors are expensive. "It says the debt was mine alone, incurred before he was born, and that if he loves me he'll burn it unread."

"And will he?"

Marta looked at me across the candle flame, and for a moment she looked very old and very tired and absolutely immovable, like a seam of rock the sea has been arguing with for ten thousand years and losing to. "He'll burn it," she said. "He reads slow and he obeys fast. It's why he survives the job." Then, softer, almost to herself: "And it's why I'm sending you away, girl. Not because I'm afraid of Hobb. Because I'm not afraid of anything that happens to *me*, and that's a luxury a mother can't afford twice."

I should tell you what I felt, because this chapter deserves honesty even if I didn't manage it at the time. I felt ashamed. Not the useful kind of shame that makes you careful — the useless kind, the kind that sits in your chest like swallowed river water. Here was a woman giving us her bread, her roof, her silence, and her son's unknowing neck, and asking in return only that we be gone before the man who loved her walked through the door. And I had offered to fix it with *come with us*, as if a fifty-pound warrant were a traveling companion you could tuck into a cart.

"Marta," I started, "if there's ever—"

"There won't be," she said. "Don't make promises you'd have to break to keep me safe. That's the worst kind." She pinched out the candle with wet fingers. "Sleep. Dawn's coming whether you sleep or not, and it's bringing worse weather with it. I can feel it in my knee."

* * *

We left in the blue-black hour before light, when the world is all rumor and no testimony. Marta fed us first — porridge, heavily salted, and bread with butter that tasted of the good days — and she packed the sack while we ate, narrating the route in the flat voice of a woman reading a shipping notice.

"The coast road's flooded past the mill, so you'll take the sheep track over the shoulder of the hill. It's steep and it's boggy and in this rain it'll try to kill you, but nobody rides it, which is the point. Come down at the old kiln and pick the road back up beyond the toll. From there—" she paused, and her mouth did something complicated, "—from there it's Sable's country again, isn't it? You'll manage."

Sable took the sack and Marta's hands together, and held them, and said nothing, and Marta nodded once, and that was the entire transaction. I've seen lawyers take an hour to accomplish less.

At the door, Marta caught my sleeve. Just mine. Sable was already out in the rain, becoming a shape, becoming a shadow, already halfway to being the lighthouse woman again instead of the guest.

"Fifty pounds," Marta said quietly. "That's what your life's worth to Hobb's paper. But hear me — it's worth more than that to the wrong sort of man alive than to the right sort of man paid. Don't let anyone collect you cheap." She pushed a coin into my palm — a shilling, worn smooth. "For luck. It's not a debt. It's not a promise. It's a shilling."

I carried it the rest of the way to Widow's Rock and I have it still. Some prices you pay. Some you're given. I've stopped trying to sort them into tidy columns; the ledger taught me better.

* * *

The sheep track was exactly as advertised, which is to say it hated us.

The rain came sideways off the sea, hard enough to sting, and the track itself was less a path than a suggestion the hill occasionally honored. We climbed with our heads down and our hands full of wet grass and each other's coats, and the wind found every gap in my oilskins and wrote its opinions there. Below us and behind, the cottage shrank to a smudge of dark with one grey thread of smoke — Marta up early, keeping her fire ordinary, keeping her morning innocent. I watched that smoke until the hill took it away, and I made myself a vow I hadn't asked her leave to make: that when this was finished, when Merrow had the ledger and Hobb had his chains, I would find some way to stand in front of Tam Quill and tell him his mother was the bravest person I'd met on this whole drowned coast. Whether he thanked me for it was another matter. Truths are like that. You can owe someone a thing they'd rather never receive.

Sable climbed like the weather was a personal acquaintance she'd stopped bothering to greet. Twice she reached back and hauled me over rocks I'd have negotiated alone for a quarter hour, and neither time did she say anything, and neither time did I thank her, because between us gratitude had gone past words into something more like ballast — unglamorous, essential, distributed evenly so the boat didn't tip.

Halfway along the shoulder, the storm announced its intentions properly. The rain thickened to a roaring curtain, the wind stood up and shoved, and somewhere ahead the sea began making sounds I didn't care for — deep, structural sounds, the sound of water rearranging coastline.

"We won't make the kiln before full light," Sable shouted, close against my ear.

"Then we'll be visible," I shouted back. "Two women on a hillside in broad daylight."

"One widow and her sister," she corrected. "Traveling to a funeral. We've practiced."

"We've practiced in fair weather!"

"Funerals don't wait for fair weather," she said, and there was something in her voice — dry as tinder under all that rain — that made me laugh, or make the sound that passes for laughter when your face is full of sea. That was Sable's gift, though I didn't name it as a gift for weeks yet: she could put irony around fear the way you put a hoop around a barrel, and somehow the fear held its shape instead of spilling everywhere.

* * *

We came down off the hill an hour after light, soaked to the architecture, and found the old kiln standing in its hollow like a broken tooth. The road beyond was a brown river with pretensions of being a road. And on the road — ahead of us, maybe half a mile, blurred by rain — movement. Two riders. Dark coats. Moving slowly, stopping, bending to look at the ground at the verge.

Horsemen who read mud.

Sable pulled me back into the kiln's shadow and stood very still, watching, and I watched her watch, and I thought about Marta's shilling in my pocket and Marta's son riding his circuit somewhere on roads like these, and about how the whole coast had become a hand closing, finger by finger, and how the only direction left open was the one pointing home — back toward Widow's Rock, back toward the tower where Hobb would surely think to look, back toward the one place we might turn from prey into something with teeth.

One of the riders straightened in his saddle and turned his horse. Toward the hill. Toward the sheep track. Toward the kiln.

"They've found the track," Sable murmured. Her hand found mine and gripped it, cold and certain. "Ruth. There's one way through from here, and it's not the road."

I followed her eyes — past the riders, past the flooded road, down the hollow to where the land fell away toward the sea — and what I saw there made Marta's warnings about the ford sound like a lullaby.

"It's the quarry," she said. "And it's flooded."
