## 20. The Storm Season Turns

The barometer began to lie to me on Tuesday morning.

I say *lie*, but that is unkind to a fine instrument that has never told me anything but the truth. It was the weather that lied — the sky wore a pleasant grey, the wind came soft off the water, and the glass sat at 29.4 inches and falling like a stone down a well. Any keeper knows what that means. The sea had made its decision hours before it bothered to inform the land.

"Storm," I said, over Marta Quill's breakfast table, with the particular flatness of a woman delivering bad news she'd already finished grieving about.

Marta looked up from her tea. "It's not due till Friday."

"The season doesn't consult the almanac." I tapped the glass case with one finger, the way Kenji used to, as if the instrument might be shamed into reconsidering. It never worked for him either. "This is the big one. The one I'd have been battening down for at the Rock since dawn."

Ruth — who was learning, day by day, to eat like someone who expected to keep her meals down — set her spoon down carefully. "How bad?"

"How bad do you want it to be?"

"I don't want it to be at all."

"Well, there's your answer. It'll be worse than that."

I will give Ruth this: two years of hiding from magistrates and smugglers had taught her not to waste time on the stages of grief. She went straight from denial to planning without pausing at bargaining, which put her ahead of most men I've known, including several judges. "The roads," she said. "If the roads flood—"

"They'll flood. The coast road always goes under at Gray Mouth, and the low stretch past the quarry turns into a river you could float a punt on. Which means—" I held up a finger, "—no riders. Hobb's men can't move either."

"—and no ferry," she went on, counting on her fingers, "and the mail packet—"

"The mail packet may not sail at all." I said it plainly because there was no point sugar-coating it; sugar doesn't dissolve in weather like what was coming. Justice Merrow was meant to leave on that packet in eight days. If the packet didn't sail, the judge stayed. And if the judge stayed—

"That could be good," Ruth said slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond. "More time."

"Or it could be very bad indeed. A cornered magistrate with eight extra days and no judge watching him is a magistrate with time to tidy his ledger problems." I looked at her until she understood what I wasn't saying. *Problems* meaning witnesses. Meaning two women, one of them worth fifty pounds, loose somewhere along a drowned coast with every road closed and nowhere to run.

Marta, who had been following this the way one follows a ball at a tennis match, finally spoke. "You're saying the storm cuts both ways."

"I'm saying the storm doesn't care which way it cuts. That's the thing about weather, Marta. It's the only honest player at this table. It plays for nobody."

* * *

By noon the wind had come around to the northeast and begun to make its arguments in earnest. By two o'clock the rain arrived — not falling so much as arriving sideways, in freight quantities, as if some celestial warehouse had tipped over. Marta's cottage, built by a man who clearly distrusted the elements on a personal level, creaked and shrugged and held.

We spent the afternoon doing what coastal people have always done before storms: work. Marta brought in shutters and stacked peat by the fire until the hearth looked like a small black fortress. I checked our stores — we were living, at present, on Marta's charity and my dwindling purse, minus one wedding ring traded at Gray Mouth, an expenditure I still declined to discuss — and Ruth copied pages.

She'd taken to copying the ledger in her own hand, entry by entry, in case the original was lost. Two copies of the truth, she reasoned, were harder to drown than one. It was good thinking and I told her so, and she looked at me with such startled gratitude that I realized nobody had praised her in a long while, possibly ever. There is a particular kind of loneliness that comes from being hunted, but there is another kind that comes before it, and I recognized both.

"Your father kept accounts?" I asked, nodding at the neat columns she produced.

"He was a customs clerk before—" She stopped. Before the frame, before the rope, before everything. "He believed in ledgers. He said a page doesn't know how to lie. People lie, ink just records them."

"Your father would have gotten along with my husband. Kenji trusted instruments over people his whole life. Said a compass never flattered anyone."

"What did he think of people?"

I turned that over while the rain hammered the roof like a creditor at the door. "He thought they were like weather," I said at last. "Worth studying. Not worth trusting to hold a course."

* * *

The storm hit full force after dark, and I will admit something here that I admitted to no one at the time: for about an hour, I was happy.

Not glad — happy is a different animal entirely, and rarer. But there is a thing that happens when a great storm comes against a solid building and you are inside it, dry, with work to do and hands busy, and the whole shrieking world outside can do nothing to you. Every lighthouse keeper knows it. It is the closest thing to safety the sea ever offers, and it is entirely borrowed, and you know it is borrowed, and you love it anyway.

I stood at Marta's window with a candle cupped in my hand and watched the dark rage, and I thought about Widow's Rock standing out there in it, alone, lamp cold, tower empty, taking the sea's worst blows on its granite shoulders and giving nothing back. My tower. My post, though the Commission would dispute the tense. Somewhere out in all that violence the gulls were huddled wherever gulls go, and the wreck-stones off the point were doing what they always do, waiting with the patience of stone for something foolish and wooden to come along.

And then I thought about the fact that I was not there, that I was forty miles down a flooded coast harboring stolen evidence and a fugitive, and that my absence from that tower was itself a kind of signal any competent enemy could read. A missing keeper is a loud silence.

"Sable." Ruth's voice behind me. "You've gone somewhere."

"Just visiting." I set the candle down. "Get your copywork near the fire. Damp is the enemy of ink."

"You sound like my father again."

"Your father sounds like he was right about things. It's a family trait, apparently. You have it too, when you stop lying long enough to use it."

That earned me a look, but a fair one. We had passed beyond offense weeks ago, Ruth and I. Fear burns off fast when it's constant; what's left underneath is something sturdier.

* * *

Wednesday the storm settled in like a relative with no return ticket.

Word came to us in fragments, the way news travels in weather — the ostler's boy from the village, half-soaked, paid a penny to shout through Marta's door and gone again before the rain finished with him. The coast road was under three feet of water at Gray Mouth. Two cottages lost their roofs at Harrow Point. The ferry was tied double and the ferryman, I noted with some private satisfaction, was telling everyone who'd listen about the two ladies whose ring had bought them the last crossing before the suspension — though he described us, generously, as "quality folk, poor souls, caught out by the weather," which is the best fifty-pounds-worth of misdirection a wedding band ever purchased.

"And the packet?" Ruth asked, when the boy had gone.

"Gone to anchor in the harbor roads, if it has any sense. No captain puts to sea in this." I fed the fire another brick of peat. "Which means Merrow stays ashore. Which means, for now, he's reachable — and so are we."

"You keep going back and forth. Is the storm helping us or hurting us?"

"Ruth. How many times must I say it? The storm isn't *doing* anything. It doesn't know we exist. That flood at Gray Mouth isn't blocking Hobb's riders as a favor to us, and it isn't trapping us as a favor to him. It's just water finding its level." I heard the sharpness in my own voice and softened it, because none of this was her fault and some of it was mine. "But yes. To answer what you're actually asking. Right now, today, it helps more than it hurts. Every day the judge sits in that county seat unable to leave is a day the ledger stays valuable. And every day the roads are impassable is a day Pike can't consolidate."

"Pike." She said the name the way one touches a bruise to check it's still there.

"Sergeant Aldous Pike. Checkpoint three. Your father's clerk." I had said it before; I would say it again; repetition, in my experience, is how dread becomes resolve instead of rot. "Who will be sitting behind his fortified door with orders to search every soul moving south, and who now knows — if Pike's letter reached Hobb, and letters reach Hobb the way water reaches bilges — that the Vane girl was seen making for the coast."

"So we wait out the storm here, and then—"

"And then we walk into the teeth of it. Yes."

Marta, from her chair by the fire, made a noise I had learned to interpret as Quill-family commentary. "You two plan like generals," she said, "and travel like beggars, and between you you've got one oilskin coat and no ring. Generals indeed."

"Marta," I said, "in thirty years of keeping lights I have learned that the equipment never matters as much as whether the person holding it refuses to quit. I once ran a fog signal for nine days on a hand pump and spite."

"Whose spite?"

"Mine. Mostly at the Commission. Partly at the fog."

Ruth laughed — actually laughed, a short startled bark, the first I'd heard from her — and then pressed her hand over her mouth as though laughter were also evidence.

"Don't apologize for it," I told her. "Laughing in a storm is a professional skill. My husband used to say the sea respects nothing except a person who finds it funny."

* * *

Thursday night the storm peaked, and I want to set down what it was like, because I have kept logs for twenty years and a log is where I put things so I can stop carrying them.

The wind reached, by my estimate, seventy miles an hour with gusts past ninety. The rain turned to spume and then to salt spray driven inland from a sea I couldn't see but could hear — a continuous deep percussion, like the world's largest drum struck by the world's angriest hand. The chimney drew badly and the fire smoked and Marta cursed it in three dialects. The window glass flexed in its frames. At the height of it, something large struck the side of the cottage — a shutter torn loose from the byre, most likely — and Ruth grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise, and I gripped back, and we sat like that for a while, two women holding onto each other in the dark while the sky tried to take the roof.

"My husband died in a storm," I found myself saying. Not a plan to say it. It simply came up, the way wreckage comes up.

Ruth said nothing. She had learned that from me, I suspect. Silence at the right moment.

"Not this kind. A quiet one — a summer squall, the treacherous kind, the ones that look small and kill boats anyway. They told me he was swept from the harbor rocks checking his lines." I watched the fire. "For years I believed it the way you believe a logbook entry. Because it was written down. Because someone official had signed it."

"And now?"

"Now I know that the same office that signed his death certificate signed away Elias Vane's life two winters before, and took payments in margins, and I find I believe nothing with a signature on it anymore except signatures against Magistrate Corliss Hobb, which I believe completely."

The wind screamed. Somewhere outside, a tree gave up — a long groan and then a crack like the world's knuckle — and was gone.

"When we read those pages by the fire at the quarry ledge," Ruth said quietly, "you saw the name. Nakamura. In the margin."

"I saw it."

"You never said what it meant."

"No," I agreed. "I didn't."

She waited. The storm waited with her, gathering breath for its next assault. And I understood, sitting there, that the deadline I'd been counting — eleven days, ten, nine, eight — had quietly stopped being about the judge. Somewhere between the quarry flood and the peat fire it had become about a margin note in a dead man's handwriting, and a question I had carried for six years wearing mourning clothes: *why Kenji? why that rock, that night, that accident?*

I knew now. I had known since the quarry ledge, and I had not said it aloud because saying it would make it permanent, the way entries become permanent.

"The payment to K. Nakamura, keeper," I said at last. "Recorded as taken. Never taken. Do you understand what a bribe recorded but refused means, Ruth?"

"It means—" Her voice changed as she worked it through. "It means whoever refused it knew something. Or was positioned to know something. A keeper sees every boat working the coast."

"It means my husband looked at the smuggling traffic through Widow's Rock channel and did what a keeper does. He logged it. Dates, hulls, lights shown and lights hidden. He wrote it in his book and sent it up to the customs house — to men who were already bought." The words came out steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to cost more. "And then one calm summer evening my husband went down to the rocks to check his lines and never came home, and the magistrate's coroner called it drowning, and I signed the paper because I had no reason not to, because I was a widow with a light to tend and grief enough without inventing conspiracies."

"Oh," Ruth said. Small, and terrible, and sufficient.

"So you ask whether the storm helps us or hurts us." I stood, and took the poker, and split a peat block with more force than the fire required. "Neither. The storm owes us nothing. But I owe Kenji a truth, and I mean to pay it before that mail packet's stern disappears over the horizon, with or without fair weather, with or without roads, with or against Sergeant Aldous Pike and every rider Corliss Hobb can mount."

* * *

Friday morning the storm broke the way they do — all at once, petulant, as if embarrassed by its own display. The sun came up on a drowned world: fields turned to lakes, the lane in front of Marta's cottage running like a millrace, a fence post standing in six feet of water wearing a gull like a hat.

But the glass was rising. Steadily. The honest instrument, recovering its manners.

"Two days," I said, reading it on the doorstep while steam rolled off the saturated ground. "Maybe three before the roads firm up. After that, everyone moves — us, Hobb's men, the mail, all of it, at once."

Ruth came out beside me, wrapped in Marta's spare shawl, squinting at a sky scrubbed clean and blue and innocent as a witness with a rehearsed story. "The packet sails as soon as it can."

"If it can get out of the harbor. And Merrow sails with it, and our eight days become four." I shaded my eyes eastward, toward the county seat, toward the third checkpoint and the man stationed behind it. "Or the harbor silt will have shoaled and the packet waits another tide, and we gain a little. Either way, Ruth — we don't wait here to find out which. We move tomorrow at first light."

"Through what? Half the coast road is underwater."

"Half of it is. Which tells you exactly which half everyone else will be watching." I turned to her, and felt the old keeper's arithmetic settle into place — the same arithmetic that had lit lamps through hurricanes, the kind that weighs risk against necessity and always, always comes out *light the lamp*. "There's a way south that doesn't use the coast road at all. It goes over the headland. Through the old quarry."

Ruth's face did something complicated. "The flooded quarry."

"The same."

"The one you just described as a river you could float a punt on."

"At the bottom, yes. But quarries have rims, and rims have paths, and I have walked worse in worse weather for less reason." I looked north, toward the drowned road, toward the wall of water and mud and armed men that lay between us and the judge — and past all of it, invisible over the horizon, the gray shape of Widow's Rock standing its watch with a cold lamp. "Besides. Whatever's waiting for us at that customs house, I'd rather arrive wet and unexpected than dry and expected. Wouldn't you?"

Ruth looked at the flooded lane, at the ruined fields, at the vast indifferent blue overhead, and I watched her make the only choice that was ever really available to either of us.

"Tomorrow at first light," she said. "Before the whole world starts moving."

"Before the whole world starts moving," I agreed. "Because when it does, my girl, it won't be moving for us. It never is. We just have to be faster than it looks."

Behind us, inside the cottage, Marta began packing bread into an oilskin bag without being asked, which is the truest form of blessing I know.
