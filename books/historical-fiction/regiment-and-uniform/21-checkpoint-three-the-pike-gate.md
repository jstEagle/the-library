## 21. Checkpoint Three: The Pike Gate

There is a particular quality to a wall that has been a wall for a long time. It stops being stone and becomes a kind of weather — something you simply move through your life under, the way farmers under a bad sky stop remarking on it. The customs house at Harrow Gate had been a wall in that sense since before I was born: squat, granite-faced, two stories of official disapproval planted square across the only road south of Gray Mouth, with a flagstaff out front flying the Crown's colors and a stable block behind it housing six horses and, as we were soon to learn, one very specific devil.

We saw it first from the ridge above the coast road, Sable's field glass pressed to her eye, her body held so still she might have been part of the drystone wall herself.

"Well," she said at last, and handed me the glass without ceremony, the way you hand someone news you'd rather not have carried yourself.

I looked. The glass was old and the lenses had gone soft with salt fog, but I could read the essentials plainly enough. A timber barrier across the road. Lanterns already lit though it was barely mid-afternoon, because the storm coming up from the southwest was eating the light an hour early. Men in customs blue moving about with the unhurried purpose of men who are being paid to wait. And above the gate itself, nailed to a board fresh enough to show white wood where the nails had bitten:

**ALL TRAVELERS TO BE EXAMINED. NO EXCEPTIONS. BY ORDER OF SERGT. A. PIKE, HM CUSTOMS.**

"Pike," I said.

"Pike," Sable agreed.

* * *

You should understand something about Aldous Pike before I go any further, because everything that happened at Harrow Gate happened in the long shadow he cast. Pike had been my father's clerk. Not his subordinate exactly — clerks are never quite subordinates, they're the people who hold all the small sharp instruments of an office while their masters hold the large blunt ones — but a man who sat three feet from my father's desk for eleven years. Who sharpened his pens. Who knew his shorthand. Who, when the time came and thirty pieces of silver were offered by Magistrate Corliss Hobb, sold every secret my father had ever confided in him, wrapped it up neatly with a bow of forged evidence, and delivered Elias Vane to the gallows.

Marta Quill had told us this in her kitchen over peat smoke and barley tea, in the voice of a woman reporting the price of fish. "The turncoat's daughter" is what she'd called him — meaning not that he had a daughter who turned coats, but that betrayal ran in him like bloodline. He had been rewarded for it, of course. They always are. Sergeant's stripes. A posting of his own choosing. Command of the last checkpoint between us and the county seat, which is the sort of coincidence that isn't one.

So when I took the glass back from Sable and looked at the customs house again, I wasn't looking at a building anymore. I was looking at the man who had killed my father, or at least the house he lived in, and I felt something in my chest that I recognized, with some embarrassment, as hatred — clean, simple, almost restful after weeks of fear. Fear scatters you. Hatred focuses you like a lens. It was, I understood even then, a dangerous comfort, and I put it away carefully, the way Sable put away her wedding ring, to be spent later at need.

"We can't go through," I said.

"No."

"And we can't go around. The road's the only crossing over the Torr, and the Torr's in spate."

"Mm." Sable took the glass back and swept it slowly along the headland east of the gate, where the land climbed into black gorse and broken rock and then fell away toward the sea. Her glass stopped. "But," she said, in the tone of a woman who has found a supply crate mislabeled and is deciding whether to report it or quietly keep its contents, "there's the quarry."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the quarry at Carn Ddu, which is to say here is what everyone within forty miles knew and no one said aloud, because saying it aloud would have meant admitting the thing had been allowed to happen.

It had been a slate quarry once, worked for sixty years, and then it hadn't, and then the pumps that kept it dry had been sold off piece by piece by whatever committee inherits such things, and then the rains had come, year after year, until the bottom hundred feet of it were a cold green lake lying in the earth like a coin dropped in grass. The cart road that had once spiraled down through it to the working faces now entered the water on one side and emerged on the other, running along a ledge cut into the far cliff, and surfaced beyond the quarry's north lip half a mile from where it dove in. In summer, boys swam there and occasionally drowned, and their mothers cursed the quarry, and nothing changed.

In November, with the storm season upon us and the ledge slicked black with spray, nobody crossed it at all.

"It can be done," Sable said. We were crouched behind a wind-bent hawthorn, studying the quarry's eastern rim through breaks in the gale. Far below, the flooded pit lay flat and colorless, and the ledge — I could just make it out — ran along the opposite wall like a scar. "Kenji walked it twice. Said the footing's honest if you keep your shoulder to the rock and don't look down."

"When did he walk it?"

"The year we married." She said it evenly, but I had learned to hear the seams in her evenness. "He was proving a point to me about fear. I told him fear was sensible. He said sensible fear and cowardice wear the same coat and you can't always tell them apart until the buttons come off." She paused. "He never told me which he thought I was."

"You know which."

"I know which," she agreed. Then, briskly, before the feeling could get its boots on: "The problem is the ledger."

The problem was the ledger. Of course it was. Everything, that week, was the ledger. Eleven days had become eight, and eight days had become five, and Justice Merrow's departure on the mail packet hung over every calculation like a blade by a thread. The ledger — my father's confession bound to Hobb's own signatures in bribes and dates — rode against Sable's ribs inside her bodice, wrapped in oilcloth, and every plan we made had to be measured against one question: *if the worst happens, does the paper survive?*

"If you go into that water," Sable said, "the ledger goes into that water. And wet ink forgives nothing."

"So we leave it with you and I cross alone—"

"No." Flat. Final. The keeper's voice, the one that ended discussions the way the fog signal ended conversation. "You don't cross that ledge alone in a rising gale, and I don't stay behind on this side of the wall with the only copy of the truth sewn into my skirt while you're fifty feet up a wet cliff where anyone could be waiting. If we go, we go together, and the ledger comes with us, because—" and here she hesitated, and I watched her decide to say the true thing instead of the safe one, "—because I've learned this month that the worst thing isn't dying. It's surviving with the proof destroyed."

* * *

We went down off the ridge to think, because thinking requires shelter and shelter requires distance from the eyes at Harrow Gate. There was a shepherd's bothy a mile inland, roofless on one side, and we sat in its lee sharing hard bread and harder cheese while the storm arrived properly overhead and began doing its work on the world.

And it was there, chewing bread that could have resoled a boot, that I had the thought I want to tell you about, because it changed everything, and because it came to me not from courage or cleverness but from spite, which I have come to regard as the most underrated of the virtues.

"His own gate," I said.

Sable looked up.

"Pike's own gate. You said it yourself — direct passage is impossible. But walls aren't only things that keep people out, are they? A wall keeps people *in*. And a man who commands a checkpoint doesn't just have to stop travelers. He has to let through anyone he *chooses* to let through." My heart had started going, that quick traitor drum. "Sable. What opens a fortified gate faster than anything in the world?"

"A superior officer."

"A summons." I was talking too fast and didn't care. "Pike answers to Hobb, yes, but Hobb answers to London, and Pike knows it. What if word came to Harrow Gate — tonight, tomorrow — that a crown courier was on the road? Carrying dispatches for the assize court. Riding ahead of Justice Merrow himself. Would Pike open his gate for a crown courier?"

"He would open it," Sable said slowly, "at bayonet point if he had to. No sergeant in the service refuses a courier's seal — it's a hanging offense and they all know it." She was staring past me now, at the rain, doing arithmetic. "But we haven't got a courier's seal. We haven't got dispatches. We haven't got anything but a smuggled ledger and my mother's mourning dress."

"We've got Marta's son."

The silence that followed had a texture. Marta Quill's boy — Tam, a customs rider stationed at Gray Mouth, the son whose career we had already endangered by existing — was the reason we had left Marta's cottage at dawn with our throats tight and our thanks unspoken, because kindness has limits and we would not be the death of them. Naming him now felt like picking at a wound we'd promised to leave alone.

"No," Sable said.

"Not him. Not his name, not his face, not his hand. His *form*." I pulled the bread bag toward me and turned it over, showing her the customs franking stamp burned into the sacking — the mark every official packet traveling between stations carried, the little crown-and-anchor that said *this bag is the Crown's, touch it and hang.* "Every station keeps blanks. Every rider carries them. Marta said Tam keeps his saddle wallet stuffed with them because he's careless. If we had one blank courier form — one — and a fair hand to fill it in..."

"You have a fair hand."

"I have my father's fair hand," I said, and heard my own voice go strange. "Eleven years, Pike sat across from that handwriting. He copied it. He filed it. He condemned it." I met her eyes. "Let him see it one more time."

* * *

I want to be honest with you, whoever you are reading this — and I intend someday that you should exist, that these pages should outlive the fear that made them necessary — I want to be honest: there was a moment, pen in hand, firelight on the bothy floor, when what I felt was not resolve but something closer to communion. Because writing in my father's hand after two years was like wearing a dead man's coat. The loops came back to me first — the way he hooked his *e*s, the way he crossed his *t*s with a single impatient stroke — and then the cadence, and then, God help me, the *voice*, so that by the third line I could feel him standing behind my shoulder dictating, dry and tired and faintly amused, the way he'd dictated a thousand letters to that same treacherous clerk.

**To the Officer Commanding, Customs Station, Harrow Gate. Be advised: bearer travels under crown authority with dispatches material to the assize inquiry now convened at the county seat. Passage to be afforded without delay or examination of person. Disobedience of this instruction to be reported directly to the Hon. Commission. By hand of the Under-Secretary's office.**

Underneath, where a courier's warrant number belonged, I wrote a string of figures I invented from whole cloth, and beneath that, the seal line, which we faked with candle wax and the button of Sable's husband's coat — a plain brass button, no crest, but pressed hard and shown fast in lantern light, wax takes an impression that reads as *some* authority, and authority, I have learned, is mostly a matter of what people are afraid to question.

Sable watched the whole operation with her arms folded and her face unreadable.

"It will hang us," she said at last, conversationally, "if it fails. Forging crown documents. That's not a fine and a cell, Ruth. That's the rope."

"Yes."

"And it may not even work. Pike's not stupid. Stupid men don't run the best checkpoint on the coast."

"No. But frightened men do stupid things, and Pike is frightened. He's been frightened since the wreck at Widow's Rock. He knows the girl survived; Hobb's posters say so. Every traveler he stops is a chance to catch me, and every traveler he *doesn't* stop is a chance that I slip past. That's the trap he's in. Fear makes gates as well as walls — we just have to find the hinge."

She was quiet a long moment. Rain hammered the bothy's broken roof and ran down inside in bright threads. When she spoke again it was barely above the storm.

"Kenji used to say that about lighthouses. Everyone thinks they're built to keep ships away. But a light doesn't warn you off, not really. It tells you where the door is. The rocks don't move. The channel's always there." She took the finished document from my hands and studied it — my father's handwriting, my invented seals, her husband's button pressed into candle wax — and something moved through her face like weather. "All right. We'll make Pike open his own gate. But we do it at dusk tomorrow, in the last light, when faces are shadows and the rain is thick enough to drown a signature. And if he looks at you one second too long—"

"I'll ride on. Keep my hood up, keep my mouth shut, and trust the uniform to do the talking."

"The uniform," Sable said, "is the most honest liar in England. It never says a word and everyone believes it."

* * *

There remained the small matter of acquiring a customs franking stamp, a courier's oilskin, and a horse — for a crown courier arrives on horseback or not at all, and two women walking into Harrow Gate on foot would raise questions no forged letter could answer.

It was Sable who solved it, of course. While I slept — or lay with my eyes shut listening to the storm rehearse its arguments — she went out into the dark, and near dawn she came back soaked to the collar leading a wet, indignant pony, carrying an oilskin cape of the wrong size and a leather dispatch wallet with the franking stamp already burned into its flap.

"Where—" I began.

"Gray Mouth. The ferryman we crossed with owes me a debt he's been trying to pay since the river. This is how he's paying it. The pony belongs to his brother, who will swear by morning it was stolen, and the wallet—" she tossed it to me, and I caught it and felt the weight of officialdom in the leather, absurd and thrilling, "—the wallet came off a drunk rider in the ferry tavern who will remember losing it approximately never. Don't ask me more. The less you know of debts, the better you sleep."

I asked her one thing anyway, because it mattered. "If Pike stops me. If he pulls back the hood. What then?"

Sable stood in the doorway of the bothy with grey dawn light behind her and the storm still growling offshore, and she looked at me with those level keeper's eyes that had watched a thousand ships pass a dangerous shore.

"Then you give him the letter and say nothing, and you pray he reads the handwriting before he reads the rider. Because that's the truth of it, Ruth — the moment he recognizes his dead master's hand, he won't be able to stop reading. Guilty men always check whether the dead have left receipts." She picked up her pack. "Come on. The quarry first. We cross it today, in daylight, while we can still see the ledge — because tomorrow night, when we ride through Pike's gate, there must be nothing between us and the county seat but road."

* * *

The quarry took the whole day and nearly took more than that, but this is not yet its chapter — that comes next, and I will tell it honestly when I do, including the sound the water makes when it decides it wants you, which is no sound at all.

What matters here is how we stood at dusk on the quarry's north lip, bruised, soaked, shaking, alive, with the ledger still dry against Sable's heart and the forged dispatch sealed in its wallet under my arm, looking down at Harrow Gate glowing in the valley below like a lamp set to lure moths.

Lanterns at the barrier. Silhouettes of men. And one figure apart from the others, standing under the eaves of the customs house porch, motionless in the rain, watching the road south the way a spider watches the middle of its web.

Even at that distance I knew him. Eleven years of my childhood had included that silhouette in doorways — the slight forward lean of a man perpetually listening for advantage.

"There he is," I said.

"There he is," Sable said. And then, handing me the pony's reins, settling my hood forward, checking the wallet at my side one last time with fingers that trembled only slightly and only once: "Ride slow. Couriers never hurry — hurry looks like guilt. And when he asks your business—"

"I show him the letter."

"And when he asks your name?"

I swung up into the saddle. Below us, the rain was thickening into the dark, and somewhere out in it, the wall waited to become a door.

"Tell him," I said, "that the dead send their regards."
