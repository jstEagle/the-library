## 22. The Quarry Flood

There is a particular kind of arithmetic a keeper learns early, and it has nothing to do with oil or wicks. It is the arithmetic of water — how much the sea can lift, how fast it can take back what it lent, and how many seconds stand between "I have her" and "I never had her at all."

I did my sums badly that night. But I did them, which is more than I can say for the quarry.

We had come to the headland in full dark, because daylight was a luxury Hobb's riders could afford and we could not. Pike's customs house sat below us on the coast road, its windows lit like a man with his eyes open, and the only way past it was over — through the old slate quarry that the week's rain had turned from a hole in the ground into a lake with opinions.

"It won't be deep," Ruth had said, standing at the rim with the wind pulling her hood sideways. "It was a road once. The causeway's still there under the water — you can see it by day."

"Can you see it now?"

"No," she admitted. "But Marta said the water drops two feet by morning tide. We wait, we cross by day—"

"We cross now." I heard myself say it and recognized the voice as my husband's. Kenji had that tone when he talked himself onto the gallery in weather no sane man would climb into. "By morning, Pike will have riders on every path down from the moor. The flooded quarry is the one place they'll assume nobody's fool enough to go."

"And are we?"

"Foolishness is just courage that hasn't been introduced to itself yet," I said, which was something Kenji used to say too, though he'd stolen it from a book and I'd stolen it from him, and so it came down to me honestly worn.

Ruth looked at me a long moment. She had Vane's face — I saw it plainly now, in the dark, the same long jaw, the same stubborn set of the mouth that had signed its own confession in a cell two winters ago. Then she laughed, short and sharp, the laugh of a woman deciding to be brave rather than waiting to feel it.

"All right, Aunt Sable," she said. Our cover story, worn soft as an old glove. "Lead on."

* * *

The rain had stopped an hour before, which sounds like mercy and wasn't. Stopped rain means running water, and running water means the quarry lake was fed by three streams coming off the moor, all of them in a temper.

We went down the old haulage ramp single file, feeling with our boots for the tramway sleepers that ran along the floor of the pit. The water came first to our ankles, then our knees, then — where the pit bottom dipped toward the flooded workings — to our thighs. It was cold enough to make thought difficult, which I suppose was a kind of anesthesia. The ledger was sewn into the lining of my coat, against my breast, and every step I took I felt it there like a second heartbeat.

"Talk to me," Ruth said behind me. Her voice had gone thin. "Tell me about the lamp. Tell me anything."

So I told her about trimming wicks. About how you learn to read a flame the way a physician reads a pulse — the yellow tongue of a dirty wick, the blue heart of a clean burn. About how the great lens at Widow's Rock weighs more than both of us together and floats on a bath of mercury, turning all night on less effort than a child's finger, because that is what good design is: making enormous things obedient to small touches.

"You love it," she said. Not a question.

"I hated it for years," I said. "It took Kenji from me — not his life, his attention. He belonged to that light the way priests belong to their God. And then he died, and I stayed, and somewhere in the staying the hating wore through and there was just... the work underneath. Clean work. Work that keeps people alive." I stepped carefully over a sleeper. "You'll understand someday, if you live long enough to keep something."

Ahead of us the water darkened where the pit deepened. I had counted on the causeway — the old cart road that crossed the pit above the drowned workings, submerged maybe eighteen inches on a normal night. But this was not a normal night. Where the causeway should have shown as a shallow ripple, there was nothing but black water moving with a slow, muscular current, sliding toward the quarry's seaward breach where it poured over in a white ribbon.

The streams had raised the level. The causeway wasn't eighteen inches under. It was four feet under, and the water above it was moving.

"We can't," Ruth said.

"We can," I said, "if we go along the high ledge instead. There's a working cut into the east face — the men walked it, before the pumps stopped. It goes around the deep water."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll know quite quickly."

That got the laugh out of her again, thinner this time. We climbed out on the east side, hands finding the old tool marks in the rock, and began working along the ledge. It was wide enough for a confident goat and narrow enough to make a confident goat reconsider. Below us the quarry lake lay black and patient. Above us the slate rose sixty feet, wet and gleaming whenever the clouds tore open enough to let the moon through.

Halfway along, the ledge simply ended.

Not crumbled — *ended*, sheared away by some winter frost, leaving a gap of perhaps eight feet with the lake churning far below. On the far side the ledge continued, and beyond it, faintly, I could see the ramp rising toward the moor path and freedom.

"There'll be a plank," Ruth said. "Quarries always had planks. Scaffold planks, spare rails—"

And there was one. Wedged in the scree at the gap's edge, half-buried, green with moss: a scaffold plank, twelve feet of it, rotted gray. We dragged it out and laid it across, and it reached — barely — with six inches of overlap on each side.

I went first. I am heavier than Ruth and I figured if the plank meant to fail, better it learned on me. It bowed under my weight like a living thing, flexed, held. I crossed, stepped onto solid rock, and turned to guide her.

"Hold the middle," I called. "Don't look down. Walk like you're late for supper."

She stepped on. She was halfway across when the moon came out.

I want to be fair to the moon. It didn't mean anything. It found a gap in the clouds the way water finds a crack, and it lit the quarry lake below them, and Ruth — being human, being twenty-three, being told not to look down — looked down.

What she saw made her flinch. The flinch put her left foot on the rotten edge of the plank. The edge went through like biscuit. She pitched sideways into the gap, caught the broken stub of the plank with both hands, and hung there over forty feet of black water, her boots scrabbling at nothing, the current-visible surface of the lake waiting with the patience of all deep things.

"Ruth!" I dropped to my knees at the gap's edge. "Give me your hand — reach up—"

"I can't—" Her voice cracked across the pit. "My hands are slipping, Sable, I can't—"

Everything after that happened in the time it takes a lamp to swing through half its arc.

I remember thinking, with absurd clarity: *the ledger is against my breast; if I go in, it drowns with me; eleven days; Merrow leaves on the mail packet; Elias Vane died for a piece of paper and I am about to make it two deaths for one.* I remember thinking: *I am fifty-one years old and I have not been in open water since Kenji pulled me out of the harbor at Foulwind when I was nineteen and laughing.*

And I remember thinking — last, and loudest — *she is someone's daughter, and I know exactly what that costs, because I watched a father die for her already, and God will not get her tonight.*

I went in.

The cold was not cold. That's the lie cold tells you at the end — it stops feeling like anything, becomes a great neutral fact, the whole world reduced to pressure and dark. The lake closed over my head and the world went silent except for my own heartbeat, huge and slow, and somewhere above me a muffled screaming through forty feet of stone and water.

Ruth hadn't fallen yet. Her grip had failed at last and she'd dropped — but not into the deep channel. She'd hit the shallower shelf where the ledge collapsed, and she was underwater, tangled in the old scaffold chain bolted to the rock face, her hair a black weed streaming in the current, her hands still clawing upward at air she'd lost.

I found her by the chain. I followed the iron down because iron is honest, and at the end of it her wrist knocked against my knuckles, and I grabbed a fistful of sleeve and kicked.

We came up together. I got an arm around her chest from behind — Kenji again, some drill he'd made me practice in the harbor until I swore at him, *in case, Astrid, in case* — and hauled her toward the shelf. She fought me. Drowning people fight everything; it's not malice, it's the body's last stupid argument with death. She cracked my jaw with the back of her skull. I held on. I had held on to worse things for longer.

Somehow — I will never reconstruct it properly, and I've stopped trying — we got onto the shelf. Not the ledge we'd left; a lower one, a pocket in the quarry face, six feet wide, dry-ish, walled on one side by slate and open to the lake on the other. The plank was gone. The ledge we'd come by was ten feet up and sheer. The moor path might as well have been the moon.

Ruth was vomiting water and shaking so hard her teeth sounded like a loose lens-pan. I held her against the rock with my whole body, sharing what warmth I had, which was leaving me fast.

"Sable." Her voice was shredded. "The ledger—"

"Is fine." I pressed my hand flat against my soaked coat, felt the stiff rectangle beneath the lining. Waterlogged, yes. Dead, no. Ink on good rag paper forgives a drowning; ink on cheap paper does not, and Elias Vane had written his confession on good paper, because he'd known it would need to survive. "It's wet. Wet can be fixed. You can't, so stop talking and shiver."

She laughed then — a real one, wild and gasping — and put her forehead against my shoulder, and shook, and I shook, and the quarry held us in its cold palm and considered whether to close its fingers.

It decided not to. The rain held off. The current kept pouring over the seaward breach in its white ribbon, indifferent, and the clouds ate the moon, and we sat in our pocket of stone like two spoons in a drawer, and waited for morning.

* * *

Dawn came gray and unhurried, the way dawn comes when it knows you have nowhere to be.

We were blue-lipped and rigid by then. Sometime in the deepest part of the night Ruth had stopped shaking, which frightened me more than the falling had — I'd seen that stillness before, in men pulled from wrecks, and I'd beaten warmth back into her arms with my bare hands, rubbing hard enough to bruise, talking the whole time because a person who answers is a person who stays.

I talked about Kenji. I don't know why him, except that he was the warmest thing I owned.

"He used to sing while he polished the lens," I said, somewhere around the hour when the sky went from black to merely bruised. "Japanese songs, mostly, songs his mother taught him, and then English ones he'd learned wrong. He sang 'Rule Britannia' with entirely invented words. I never corrected him. Twenty-two years I let him believe he knew the words, because hearing him get them wrong was better than any version sung rightly."

"What were his words?" Ruth murmured. Her lips were against my shoulder.

"'Britannia rules the waves' became 'the lady loves the gravy,' and then something about potatoes ruling also, and after that I confess I couldn't follow. He claimed it was a translation issue. He claimed nearly everything was a translation issue." My throat tightened. "He translated himself for this country, Ruth. Gave up his language's music, gave up his name in company — Nakamura, spoken aloud only at home, worn like underwear, private. He did it so we could keep the light, because no board on this coast would give a Japanese keeper a station, so the station was given to *Mrs. Nakamura*, widow, English name, English face, and he stood beside her in the shadows and did half the work and none of the signing." I rubbed her arm harder. "And they killed him for it anyway. Or they let him die, which is the same thing when you're the one drowning. So understand me — I'm not doing this for justice. Justice is a word magistrates use. I'm doing it because somebody wrote my husband's name in a margin as a bought man, and my husband was never for sale, and I intend to make the men who lied about him read the truth aloud in a courtroom."

Ruth was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — steadier, older.

"My father wrote about you," she said. "In his letters. Before — near the end. He wrote, 'There is a keeper's wife at Widow's Rock who attended my trial. She sat in the back and she looked at me the whole time. Not away. Everyone else looked away. If I ever get free, I want to be the kind of man who deserves to have been looked at like that.'" She lifted her head. "He asked his clerk to find out your name. The clerk reported back to Hobb instead. That's how Pike first marked you, I think. That's how your name got into their papers at all."

The lake lapped at the base of our shelf. Somewhere above, a gull screamed the gull's eternal complaint.

"So it was me," I said. "One look in a courtroom, and it rippled all the way to a rock in the sea and killed a man."

"No." Ruth took my face in her cold hands and made me look at her, the way you'd steady a lamp. "It was them. They did the killing. Your look just showed them what honesty looks like, and they couldn't bear it." She almost smiled. "Keep looking at people like that, Sable. Right through to the end of this. It's worth more than the ledger."

I am not easily unmanned. Fifty-one years, a coast's worth of storms, a husband buried by other people's lies — I thought myself proof against sentiment. But sitting in that quarry pocket, frozen through, watching this girl — this stranger, this *daughter*, this dead man's living hand — tell me my grief had been witnessed and my witness had mattered, something in my chest that had been clenched for two years finally, quietly, unclenched.

Sisters are made in strange places. Churches, mostly, and kitchens, and sometimes — I can attest — forty feet up a quarry wall, soaked to the soul, holding each other against the dark.

When the light strengthened enough to see, I worked the ledger out of my coat lining. The pages had swollen into a soft brick, the ink feathered at the edges of every letter. I opened it with the care of a surgeon and read the first entry through the blur.

Legible. Every line legible. Rag paper and iron-gall ink — Vane had chosen well, or been lucky, and I sent up a word of thanks to whichever it was.

"Can you walk?" I asked.

Ruth tested her legs against the rock and stood, swaying. "If walking is required, I'll manage it."

"Good. Because the ledge resumes above us, and above that is the moor, and above that is Gray Mouth, and Marta's fire, and dry clothes, and — God willing — a peat flame and every page of this spread out where we can read what we're carrying." I looked up at the ten feet of sheer slate between us and the world. "And Ruth?"

"Aye?"

"When we get to Marta's, you're telling her it was your idea."

She grinned through cracked lips. "Aye, Aunt Sable."

Getting out took another hour and most of my fingernails, and I will not describe it, partly because terror makes poor prose and mostly because my hands hurt too much to type it twice. But we stood on the moor by seven o'clock, steaming gently in the weak sun like a pair of landed fish, the ledger wrapped in my oilskins against Ruth's chest, and below us the quarry lay flat and bright and innocent, pretending it hadn't tried to keep us.

Two days gone from our eleven. One checkpoint behind us. Pike's gate ahead.

And in the margin of a page I would not read until that night, drying page by page at Marta Quill's fire, a name waited for me — a name I would know anywhere, in any hand, in any century — and everything I believed about my husband's death was about to change.
