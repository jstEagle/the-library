## 23. Drying the Truth

There is a particular kind of intimacy that comes from sitting knee-to-knee with a stranger while you both handle evidence of treason. I had not anticipated it. Nobody warns you about that part. They warn you about the drowning, the riding, the checkpoints, the men with truncheons — all the dramatic hazards — and never once about the strange, unbearable closeness of reading a dead man's accounts aloud to a woman whose hands shake worse than yours.

We had crawled out of the quarry flood two nights past, half-drowned ourselves, the ledger soaked through and my father's confession gone soft as bread in milk. We'd sheltered since in a ruined bothy above the old peat cuttings — Marta had told us of it, back when she still had a son who wasn't due home, back when her kindness had no expiration date written on it. The bothy was four stone walls, a broken door, and a hearth that some long-dead shepherd had built to last. It was, at present, the most important building on the coast of England, though no map would ever say so.

The ledger lay open before us like a patient on an operating table.

"You know," I said, feeding another sodden page toward the fire's edge, holding it at the distance where heat dries without burning — a skill I acquired in roughly forty minutes of near-catastrophe — "my father kept accounts for everything. The household books, the church collections, even what he spent on sweets for me when I was small. Ninepence, March 1893, barley sugar, R.V." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "I used to tease him that he'd itemize his own funeral."

Sable didn't answer right away. She was working on a page from the middle of the book, wringing it gently first, the way you'd treat silk. She had taken off her boots. Her feet were bare on the cold flagstones and she seemed entirely unbothered by this, as if dignity were a thing other people needed and she had misplaced hers years ago along with her husband's name.

"He sounds like he'd have liked Kenji," she said at last.

I had learned by now not to ask. Every time her husband surfaced in her speech, he surfaced briefly, like a fish showing its belly, and then was gone again into deep water. But something in her tone made me bold, or foolish, or both.

"Tell me about him. While we work. It passes the time."

"It doesn't pass the time," Sable said. "It makes the time heavier. But heavy is fine. Heavy is honest." She smoothed her page flat against a warm stone and read. "Here. Listen. 'Received of T. Marlowe, per arrangement, twelve pounds.' Dated the fourteenth of January, '97."

"Who is Marlowe?"

"Keep listening and find out." She turned to the next entry. "'Received of T. Marlowe, fifteen pounds.' February. Then there's a gap — three months of nothing — and then, 'Paid to Sgt. Dunn, Gray Mouth ferry post, five pounds.'" She looked up. The firelight moved on her face and made her look younger and older simultaneously, which I was beginning to understand was simply how her face worked. "It's a business, Ruth. A proper one. Wages going down, shares coming up. Your father didn't stumble onto a smuggling ring. He stumbled onto a company."

* * *

We worked until the light failed and then worked on by firelight, because we could not stop. There is a sickness in reading such a book — the same sickness as picking at a wound — and we had it badly.

The shape of the ring emerged slowly, the way a coastline emerges from fog, and it was uglier for arriving piecemeal. At the top, taking the largest share, a name I did not know: *A. Greville & Sons, Shipping*, with entries recorded simply as "G" once the sums grew large enough that even a conspirator's discretion asserted itself. Greville's ships carried legitimate cargo south and illegitimate cargo north — French brandy, untaxed tobacco, and, in three entries that made my stomach turn over, crates marked "machinery" that were paid for at double rate by men whose names appeared alongside the word *arms*.

Below Greville sat Magistrate Corliss Hobb himself, receiving "consultation fees" that were neither consultations nor fees, initialing each one with that neat, self-satisfied *C.H.* I had seen those initials on my father's death warrant. Seeing them here, sixty times over, across six years of quiet theft, did something to me that grief alone had never managed. It made me angry in a clean, cold way, like well-water. Anger you could drink.

"Hobb wasn't the top," I said. "He thinks he's the top. Look — every entry involving Greville has a second mark beside it. Not Hobb's initials. Someone else countersigns."

Sable leaned close. Her hair had dried into a storm-cloud halo and she smelled of peat smoke and salt and, faintly, of the lavender water she rationed like a miser. "A solicitor's hand. See the loops? That's a man trained to write for a living."

"Greville's clerk?"

"Greville's conscience, more likely. Somebody keeps the true books separate from the false ones. This is the true one." She tapped the page. "Which means somewhere there's a false set, and your father stole the one that matters."

Below Hobb, the ring branched like a root system. Sergeant Dunn at the Gray Mouth ferry — the man whose lamp we had drifted past in the dark, our hearts hammering, our oars muffled with rags. A customs officer named Pell at the toll bridge, receiving quarterly sums so regular they might have been a pension. And at the third checkpoint, the fortified house we had gone over the flooded quarry rather than face, entries in a different ink, a different hand — smaller, tighter, the writing of a careful man.

"Aldous Pike," I read aloud. "Pike, Pike, Pike. He's in this book forty times. More than Hobb."

"He was your father's clerk," Sable said. "He knew where everything was kept. Which papers, which drawers, which witnesses." She said it without drama, the way she said most terrible things, and somehow that made it worse. "A man inside the house is worth ten men outside it. That's why the frame worked. That's why it took only a word from Pike to hang your father."

I put down the page I was holding, because my hands had begun to tremble and I did not want to drop it into the fire. Sixty pages of my father's vindication, and I nearly fed them to the flames because a dead traitor wrote in tidy little script.

Sable reached across without looking up from her own page and put her hand flat on mine. Just that. No words. She held it there for perhaps ten seconds, then withdrew and went back to reading, as though nothing had happened, and I understood that for her this was the whole vocabulary of comfort — brief, dry, exact — and that I had been given, by her standards, a speech.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight the rain found a new way in, down the chimney's throat, hissing on the hearthstones, and we shifted our operation deeper into the corner where the roof still held. My back ached. My eyes burned. I had never been so tired and so unable to sleep in my life, because every page turned was a piece of my father walking back into the room.

"Listen to this one," Sable said. Her voice had gone rough from reading. "'Received of C. Hobb, per instruction of G, the sum of forty pounds — marked: keeper's discretion.'" She frowned. "Keeper's discretion. That phrase recurs. Eleven times. Payments to someone called simply 'the keeper.'"

"Another lighthouse?"

"No. Lighthouses are Board property; their keepers are watched closer than vicars." She chewed her lip. "Could be a harbor master. Could be a bridge keeper. There's a toll-keeper at—" She stopped.

I looked up. In the firelight her face had changed. Not drained — I had seen her afraid, on the ferry, in the quarry, and fear empties a face. This was different. This was a face filling up, filling too fast, like a lock gate with the sluices jammed.

"Sable?"

She didn't answer. She was staring at the page in her lap, at a margin note in that tight, careful hand — Pike's hand, I thought distantly, Pike's tidy little hand — and her lips were moving slightly, reading it again, or refusing to read it again, I couldn't tell which.

The fire cracked. Rain ticked on the stones. Somewhere out in the dark a night bird cried once and gave up.

"Sable," I said again, and put my hand on her arm, and felt that she was shaking — not the way she'd shaken in the flood, all muscle and cold, but a fine internal trembling, like a struck bell after the sound seems finished. "What is it? What name?"

Her eyes came up to mine and there was something in them I had not seen before, not on the cliff, not in the water, not once in all these days of storms and checkpoints and lies. It looked, God help me, like the end of something. Like a woman arriving at a place she had unknowingly been walking toward for two years.

"The keeper," she said quietly. "Ruth. The payments are signed to K. Nakamura."

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. Nakamura. The name she never spoke. The name the fisherman in the market town had spoken kindly and she had nearly wept over her cabbages. The name buried under *Widow*, under *Mrs. S.* — the name she had wrapped in black wool and keeper's routine and carried like contraband for two years, and here it was, in her husband's own handwriting's opposite number, in the margin of the book that proved everything.

"Kenji," I whispered.

She closed the ledger very carefully, as though it were sleeping. When she spoke, her voice was level, which frightened me more than screaming would have.

"There's an entry," she said. "Dated the ninth of October, two years ago. Three weeks before my husband drowned." She opened the book again to the marked page and held it so the firelight fell across it, and read it out in a voice like iron being drawn slowly from a forge:

"'Paid to K. Nakamura, keeper — twenty pounds — for silence.'"

The rain came harder. Sable sat perfectly still, the ledger open in her lap, her husband's name and the price of his silence shining in the firelight between us, and I understood that we had not merely found the proof of my father's innocence.

We had found the reason her whole life had gone dark.
