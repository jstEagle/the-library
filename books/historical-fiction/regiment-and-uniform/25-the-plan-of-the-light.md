## 25. The Plan of the Light

The peat fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Sable said it, and I thought at first I'd misheard her. That was my mistake in those days — assuming that anything impossible was therefore unsaid.

"We go back to Widow's Rock," she repeated, in the tone of a woman announcing she'd decided to have eggs for breakfast.

I looked up from the ledger page I'd been copying out in case the original drowned or burned or was eaten by a customs horse. "Back. To the lighthouse. The one they searched."

"The one they *will* search." She fed a strip of dried kelp into the fire and watched it curl. "There's a difference, and it matters."

"Sable."

"Ruth."

"You were there when we read Pike's letter. *Search every keeper's station.* They're sealing the roads. They know a woman matching your description was seen on the coast road with a second woman. There is a reward for me with my own face printed under it, badly, but recognizably. And you propose we walk back to the single most obvious place on this coast and light a lamp in it like a signal fire."

"Yes," said Sable Nakamura.

I want to record, for posterity, that I did not throw anything at her. It was a near thing. There was a tin cup within reach and I had been raised better than that, which is to say worse, and only my father's ghost stayed my hand.

"Explain it to me slowly," I said instead, "as though I'm a magistrate. You seem fond of that style of reasoning."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been evicted and left a mark on the wall. She pulled her chair closer to the fire, and I recognized the posture. It was her lecture posture. I'd seen it twice before: once explaining the arithmetic of tides, once explaining why we do not sew valuables into the hem of a mourning skirt where a bored officer's fingers might wander. Both times she had been right, and I had hated it.

"A lighthouse is three things," she began. "It is a lamp. It is a sound. And it is a rock that kills ships."

"I had noticed the rock."

"Noticed it, perhaps. But you don't understand what it means to the men who sail these waters. Every master on this coast knows Widow's Rock the way you know your father's handwriting. They steer by it. They fear it. They trust the light that stands over it, because for eleven years that light has never lied to them. Not once. Not in the worst weather God has seen fit to invent." She turned the kelp strip over with a stick. "A keeper can say things with a light that no telegraph office will carry and no sergeant can intercept."

"Such as?"

"There are codes. Old ones. A steady light means safe water. A light occulting — going dark at intervals — means danger. And if the lens is turned away from the channel entirely and fixed on the wreck-stones, burning there—" She paused. "Every captain from here to the Humber knows what that means. It means *something is wrong here*. It means *do not come in*. And if the fog signal sounds the distress pattern beneath it — three blasts, silence, three blasts — then any ship of any navy in the world understands that the station itself is calling for help."

"And Justice Merrow's escort ship is sitting offshore waiting for exactly such an announcement."

"It's been riding at anchor two days," Sable said. "I've watched it through Marta's glass. It won't move until the weather clears enough for its captain to be certain the channel is honest. Its captain would very much like someone ashore to tell him the truth about the water. And I am the only person on this coast who can tell forty square miles of sea, all at once and in a language every one of them speaks, that something is wrong — and that the wrongness needs a judge."

I sat with that. The fire popped. Somewhere outside the wind worried at the loose shingle like a dog at a bone.

"All right," I said finally. "Say the ship comes. Say your light-language works and the boarding party puts ashore and finds us standing on the rocks like a pair of heroines in a ballad. Say Merrow takes the ledger and Hobb swings. Fine. Wonderful. Now explain to me the part where we get back to Widow's Rock alive, past sealed roads and a customs house full of Pike's men, to a tower that Pike has already ordered searched — because he wrote it down, Sable, in his own hand, and his own hand is not a thing I ever want to read again."

"He'll search it," she agreed. "Probably already has men watching it."

"*Probably—*"

"But he won't search it well." She set down the stick and looked at me straight on, and I saw that underneath the calm she was white-knuckled around something, holding it still by force. "Ruth. Do you know what a lighthouse is to the men who work for Magistrate Hobb? It's furniture. It's a landmark. It's the tall thing with the light on it that tells them where the smugglers' water ends. In eleven years, how many of them have climbed my stairs? Two. Once to complain that the fog signal kept them awake, and once because Sergeant Pike wanted to see the view for his map. They don't think of it as a fortress. They think of it as a candlestick." Her jaw set. "They are about to learn what it actually is."

"Which is?"

"A weapon I've been maintaining for eleven years without knowing why."

* * *

I did not sleep that night, which surprised me, because exhaustion had become a country I held citizenship in. Instead I lay on Marta Quill's spare bed listening to the storm rearrange the coastline and argued with Sable in my head, casting myself in the role of the sensible one, the one who pointed out that walking into a trap was generally considered a poor survival strategy among people who wished to continue surviving.

The trouble with arguing with Sable Nakamura in your head is that she wins there too.

Because here is what I kept circling back to, the way a moth keeps coming back to a flame it has already touched twice: everything else had failed or was failing. The roads were sealed behind us — we'd confirmed that at dawn, watching a mounted patrol turn back a cart at the crossroads with a casualness that curdled the stomach. The mail packet wasn't sailing; even if it did, the harbormaster at Gray Mouth was Hobb's creature, and we'd learned what happened to evidence that passed through Hobb's creatures' hands. We could not go forward by land. We could not go forward by sea. Every road between us and Justice Merrow now ran through the hands of men whose signatures were in that ledger.

Except one road. One road ran straight up out of the water, past the wreck-stones, to a door Sable had locked herself behind for eleven years.

"They'll be expecting us to run south," she'd said, before I went up to bed. "Fugitives run. Fleeing things flee. Everything Hobb knows about human nature tells him we'll keep moving away from him, toward the judge, by whatever hole we can squeeze through. So he'll thicken the net behind us and ahead of us and leave his flanks thin." She'd almost smiled then. "And his rear flank, Ruth, is a rock he's never once thought about, guarded by a widow he believes is a broken old woman who polishes glass."

"Are you?" I asked. "A broken old woman?"

"No," said Sable Nakamura. "But he is welcome to keep believing it right up until the moment it kills him."

* * *

Morning came gray and mean, the storm blown through to its sullen tail end. Marta made porridge without being asked and set the bowl in front of Sable with a look that said, plainly, that Marta had heard at least some of the plan through the floorboards and disapproved of all of it.

"You're mad," Marta said. She was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, with hands like mooring rope, and she said it the way people say *you're mad* when they've already started helping.

"Almost certainly," Sable said, and ate her porridge.

"The tower will be watched."

"The tower will be watched by two men in a boat, if Pike follows his own instructions, which he does, because he's a clerk. Two men in a boat cannot watch a rock in fog. Fog is coming back tonight — I can smell it under the rain."

"And if they're not in a boat? If Pike himself comes?"

Sable set down her spoon. For a moment she looked older than her years and younger than her grief, both at once, which is a trick I've never managed since.

"Then Pike climbs my stairs," she said, "and stands in my lamp room, and while he's doing it, the fog signal sounds distress behind him and the escort ship sends a launch, and by the time he works out what he's standing inside of, it's too late to climb back down. Marta. He thinks the lighthouse is a candlestick. Let him."

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked at me, and what passed across her face was something I've thought about often since — pity, mostly, and underneath it a kind of reluctant respect, the way you look at two women boarding a boat you're fairly sure will sink.

"You'll need dry oilskins," she said. "And food that doesn't spoil. And you'll need to go round by the headland path, not the coast road, and you'll need to leave before my son gets home, because I meant what I said about that."

"We know," I said. "And Marta — thank you isn't large enough, but it's what I have."

"Keep your thanks," Marta said gruffly, and then, softer, to Sable, in the voice of one keeper to another: "Kenji would have hated this plan."

"Yes," said Sable. "He'd have hated it and then improved it. I've done what I could in that direction. The valve on the fog signal sticks unless you warm it first — I've planned for that. What he'd have caught that I haven't, I don't know yet. I'll find out the hard way, probably."

"That's how he'd have found it too," Marta said, and something passed between them then, brief and old and salted, and I sat very still with my tea and let them have it.

* * *

We left at noon, into a sky the color of dishwater. Marta walked us as far as the stile at the edge of her land, pressed a parcel of oatcakes and salt fish into my arms, and stood watching until the hedge took her from view. I did not look back. Looking back felt like borrowing trouble against a debt we were already overextended on.

The headland path was a goat's opinion of a road — a thread of turf along the cliff top, flooded in places, the wind pushing at our shoulders like a drunk acquaintance who wants to tell you something important. Below us the sea worked itself against the rocks in long gray rollers, and I thought about the escort ship out there beyond the point, riding at anchor, patient as a debt collector, waiting for someone to tell it the truth.

"She'll see us coming," I shouted over the wind, at one point. "The ship. If your light works, she'll send a boat, and the boat will have sailors, and sailors will have rifles, and Hobb's men will have—" I stopped, doing sums I didn't like.

"Hobb's men will have a wrecked pursuit boat on the shoal and no way to explain to a judge's officers what they were doing chasing a launch in a gale at midnight," Sable called back. She walked the cliff path the way other people walk their own hallways. "Ruth. I've spent eleven years learning what that tower can do, the way you spend eleven years learning a person. Its faults and its strengths. Where it's blind and where it sees. Tonight it's going to speak for the first time since I've kept it, and it's going to say everything I've been too frightened to say out loud since Kenji died." She glanced at me sideways, rain streaming off the brim of her hat. "You of all people should understand that. You carried a dead man's letter for two years."

That stopped me. The wind filled the pause with spray and noise.

"I didn't choose to carry it," I said finally.

"No," Sable said. "Neither did I choose the light. But it's ours now, both of ours, and the only question worth asking is whether we spend it or hoard it." She faced forward again, into the weather. "I'm done hoarding."

* * *

We made the last mile as the light failed, and there it was.

Widow's Rock rose out of the dark water black and absolute, the tower above it pale as a bone, and my stomach dropped through the cliff path into the sea below, because standing off the point, maybe four hundred yards out, rode a small boat with a lantern in it. Two silhouettes. Watching. Exactly as Pike had instructed and exactly as Sable had predicted.

"Well," I whispered, crouched behind a gorse brake with my heart going like a drum in a parade. "There's your welcome party. Shall I knock?"

"Patience." Sable lay flat beside me, binoculars — Marta's, borrowed without ceremony — steady in her hands. "They're watching the seaward side and the landing stairs. Pike's order says search the station, so they'll row in at first light, walk up, find nothing, and row away. That's the whole of their imagination. Men like that check boxes, they don't ask questions." She lowered the glasses. "But we're not going in by the stairs anyway."

"There's another way in?"

"There's the oil store tunnel. Runs from the boathouse under the point, comes up inside the base of the tower. Kenji cut it the first winter — hauling oil barrels up those stairs in a gale nearly killed him twice, so he solved the problem the way he solved everything." Her voice did something complicated around his name, a knot pulled tight and then released. "Hobb's men don't know it exists. Nobody does. I've kept it clear for eleven years because keeping it clear was a habit, and I thought the habit was grief." She got to her feet, unhurried, rain flattening her clothes against her. "It wasn't grief. It was preparation. It just didn't know what for."

We came down off the headland in the last of the light, along a track only Sable could see, and she put her hand flat against a slab of granite at the waterline like a woman greeting a horse, and it swung — groaning, salt-seized, but swinging — onto a black passage that breathed cold stone and lamp oil.

"After you," I said, because chivalry is easy when the alternative is going first.

Inside, Sable struck a match and lit the smallest lamp I have ever seen, and by its bead of flame I watched her face as she looked up the spiral stair toward the room at the top where the great lens waited, all her years of polished glass and counted supplies and buried names stacked above her like ammunition.

"Eleven days became eight," she said quietly. "Merrow sails when the weather breaks. So we rest until the fog comes in tonight — and it's coming, I can taste it — and then, Ruth Vane, we make a lighthouse lie."

I followed her up into the dark, toward the lamp room, toward the light that had never once lied and was about to learn treason for love of the truth — and below us, far off in the black water, the lantern in the watching boat swung gently, patiently, marking nothing but its own ignorance.

*End of Chapter 25*
