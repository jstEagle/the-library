## 26. Pike's Letter

The boy from the village came up the cliff path at dusk, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with the climb.

I knew him — Tam Osgood, the cooper's son, fourteen and all elbows, who sometimes carried Marta's messages when his father's cart went that way. He was not supposed to be on this road. Nobody was supposed to be on this road. That was rather the point of it.

"He said you'd know what it means," he panted, holding out a folded scrap of paper as if it might bite him. "The old man at the fish sheds. He wouldn't give me a name. Just said a keeper would know."

"Did he say anything else?"

"He looked over his shoulder twice and gave me a penny to forget I'd come." Tam shrugged. "I kept the penny."

There was, I thought, a certain honesty in that. I paid him another one for his trouble and watched him go, and then I stood on the gallery steps with the paper in my hand while the wind worried at my collar like a dog wanting attention.

Ruth was inside, mending a seam by lamplight. She had gotten better at it these past days — her hands steadier, her lies less frequent, though she still told them out of habit the way some people hum. She looked up when I came in, read my face the way she'd learned to read weather, and put down the needle.

"What is it?"

"A message," I said. "Or half of one. The rest is in how it was sent."

* * *

Here is what we had learned since the quarry, since the peat fire, since the night I'd gone still over a name in a margin and Ruth had sat with me without saying anything foolish or kind, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in years.

We had learned that Sergeant Aldous Pike — once Elias Vane's clerk, now Magistrate Hobb's chief man and the turncoat whose testimony had hanged an innocent man — commanded the customs house at the Pike Gate. We had learned that the ring ran contraband through three checkpoints with the smoothness of a well-oiled clock, and that the toll bridge sergeant took his cut beneath the floor of a church, and that somewhere above them all sat Corliss Hobb, magistrate, signatory, murderer-by-proxy.

And we had learned, from the ledger's margins, that Kenji Nakamura had been written into their books as a bought man — a payment recorded against his name that he never took, entered there to bind him, and when binding failed, to bury him. My husband had died because he was honest and they needed him to be corruptible on paper. The sea got the blame. The sea gets the blame for most things on this coast. It has broad shoulders and no lawyer.

So: the message.

It was written in pencil, in a hand that hurried, on the back of a customs waybill — Pike's office paper, unmistakable, the eagle stamp in the corner half-smudged. It read:

*V.g. seen on coast road near Gray Mouth. Heading north. Seal roads. Search every keeper's station between here and the Rock. Do not let her reach water. — A.P.*

No salutation. No signature beyond initials. A man writing to his master in shorthand because even traitors grow careless with their own names eventually.

"The Vane girl seen near Gray Mouth," Ruth repeated. Her voice had gone very flat, which was how I knew she was frightened. Flat voices are what fear sounds like when it's been trained. "But we crossed at Gray Mouth nine days ago."

"Yes."

"And we've been here, or at Marta's, ever since. Nobody's seen us on any road since the ferry."

"No," I agreed. "Which means either Pike has eyes we haven't found, or—" I turned the paper over. The waybill number. The date column. "—or someone saw two women traveling north after the ferry crossing and made an assumption. A convenient assumption. One that happens to serve whoever wants Hobb looking busy and vigilant."

"You think it's a lie?"

"I think it doesn't matter whether it's true." I set the paper on the table between us, next to the lamp, where its shadow fell long across the wood. "What matters is what Hobb will do with it. And what he'll do is exactly what it says. Seal the roads. Search every keeper's station. Every station, Ruth. Including mine."

* * *

We spent the evening doing arithmetic.

Not sums of money — we were long past pretending money was the currency that mattered — but sums of time, distance, and men. How many riders does it take to seal a coast road in a storm season? Fewer than you'd think; a road only needs blocking at its narrow places. How long to search a lighthouse? An hour, if they're thorough. Less, if they're lazy, and men who serve magistrates through bribery are lazy by training. How many days until Justice Merrow's circuit leaves the county seat?

Four. Perhaps five, if the mail packet sailed late, which in this weather it might, which was both our hope and our terror.

"We can't go around them," Ruth said. She'd been pacing the kitchen, and now she stopped, hands flat on the table, leaning into the problem the way sailors lean into wind. "Three checkpoints, roads sealed, every keeper questioned. If we try to carry the ledger south again we're just walking the same gauntlet with more eyes on it."

"So we don't carry it south."

"Then what—"

"We make them bring the judge to us."

She stared at me. Outside, the wind had begun to build in earnest — the long moan off the northeast that meant the year's great storm was gathering its skirts, and I felt it in the glass of the barometer the way you feel a change in an old friend's mood.

"Explain," she said, "as though I'm simple."

"You're not simple. You're just not a keeper yet." I pulled the chart toward me, the old one with Kenji's pencil marks still ghosting the margins — soundings, hazards, the little crosses he'd put where the wreck-stones lay hungry below the tide line. Widow's Rock sat at the throat of the channel like a closed fist. Any ship coming to the harbor, any escort vessel, any mail packet riding out weather — they all passed within sight of my light. "Merrow's escort ship is anchored offshore waiting for the channel to be confirmed safe. Hobb knows the judge must leave soon. Hobb also knows — or will believe, thanks to Pike's letter — that the Vane girl is making for the coast. For water. For a ship."

"You want him to think we'll signal a ship."

"I want him to *know* it. Because it will be true." I tapped the chart, on the anchorage, on the channel, on the black cluster of marks that was the shoal. "A light shown wrong — shown on the wreck-stones instead of the safe channel — tells every captain in these waters that something is very wrong. Distress. Danger. Come investigate. It's old code, older than the Board. Merrow's people would answer it. And Hobb cannot allow that light to speak, so he must come to the tower himself, with enough men to be sure, and stand inside my walls while I—"

"While you what?"

I hadn't finished the sentence. I wasn't sure I could. But Ruth was watching me with her father's eyes — I'd begun to notice that, the set of the mouth when he must have argued law and lost, the stubbornness around the jaw — and she said quietly:

"While you do whatever keepers do when the light matters more than the keeper."

* * *

That night I did something I had not done in eleven years of keeping Widow's Rock.

I wrote a false log entry.

I have told you about the first falsification — the burned draft, the wreck of a girl I did not record, the small treason that started all of this. But that had been an omission. This was different. This was commission. I sat at my desk with the logbook open to the current week and I wrote, in my best round keeper's hand, the truth of everything except the truth: routine watch, lamp trimmed, weather deteriorating, no traffic.

Routine. As though my heart weren't hammering like surf. As though there weren't a dead man's confession sewn into the lining of my spare oilskin coat and a living man's treachery folded on my table.

Because that was the last piece, and I want to be honest about it, since honesty is the whole of what I have left to trade.

If we went back to Widow's Rock, we went back into the trap. Pike's letter was not a warning we'd intercepted — it was an invitation we'd accepted. Hobb would seal the roads, search the stations, and when the search reached the Rock, he would come personally, because magistrates always do when the prize is personal and the witness is a woman alone. He would walk into my tower with six men and a warrant and he would find either the Vane girl and the ledger, or nothing, depending entirely on how well I lied and how well Ruth hid and how much the storm favored us.

The odds were not good. I want to be clear about that too. I have never been a gambler; keepers can't afford to be. The sea takes gamblers. But there is a difference between gambling and reckoning, and the reckoning went like this:

Run, and we are hunted down on open roads with the evidence on our backs and nowhere to hide. Stand, and we fight inside walls I know better than any man alive — walls with a hundred and forty-one steps, a steam valve, a lens, and rocks beneath them that have been killing the unwary since before there was a crown to smuggle from.

"They expect a widow hiding," I said to Ruth, in the dark, with the lamp turned low and the storm beginning to test the shutters. "They expect a fugitive running. They do not expect the lighthouse itself to be against them. Nobody thinks of the light as a weapon. It's furniture. It's scenery. It's the thing that saves you, so it can't possibly hurt you."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we hang, or drown, and the ledger drowns with us, and Elias Vane stays a traitor forever, and Kenji stays an accident." I closed the logbook. "I've lived eleven years on the far side of that sentence, Ruth. I find I'm unwilling to live another."

She was quiet a long moment. Then she said, in a voice that had stopped being frightened and become something harder and finer: "My father used to say that justice delayed is justice denied, but that justice delivered by amateurs is justice entertained."

"He sounds like he'd have liked the plan."

"He'd have hated it. He'd have insisted on going first." She smiled — the first real smile in days, thin and fierce. "When do we leave?"

"At first light. Before the roads are sealed, while the storm keeps the riders close to their fires. Two women traveling home ahead of the weather — a widow and her sister, going back to tend the light. Nothing could be more natural on this coast."

"Nothing could be more natural," Ruth repeated, and then, wryly: "You realize we'll be walking directly into the arms of the men hunting us."

"I realize," I said, "that they've made one mistake, and we're going to make them pay for it."

"What mistake?"

"They think the trap is for us." I stood, and began pulling the oilskins from their pegs, and checking the wicks, and doing all the small domestic things a keeper does before a voyage, which is to say before a war. "They've forgotten that I've kept this light through eleven years of storms. They think Widow's Rock is where I live."

I swung the shutter closed against the rising wind and heard it take the gale's first full blow with a groan and a hold.

"It isn't. It's where I work. And tomorrow, I'm going back on shift."
