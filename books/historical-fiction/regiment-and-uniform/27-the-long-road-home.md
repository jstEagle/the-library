## 27. The Long Road Home

Marta Quill woke us before the gulls, which is a feat I intend to report to no authority, because it suggests either witchcraft or a woman who has spent forty years listening for weather in her sleep.

"Go now," she said, setting down two cups of tea that were more milk than tea and less kindness than either. "My Tam comes home tonight. If he rides through that door and finds you sitting at my table, he won't ask questions. He'll do his duty. And then they'll hang him for it, or hang me, and neither of those suits me."

Sable drank her tea like a woman taking orders from a superior officer, which, in Marta's cottage, she was. I drank mine because my hands needed something to do besides shake.

"You understand," Marta said to Sable, not unkindly, "that if you're taken on the road, I never saw you."

"I'd say the same of you," Sable said.

"That's the trouble with favors between keepers," Marta said. "We're all liars with good handwriting." She pressed a parcel of bread and cold mutton into my arms and looked at us both the way you look at people boarding a ship you're fairly sure will sink. "The coast road's under water past Harrow Point. You'll be walking in the sea's front garden. Keep to the cliff side. And whatever you do—" she stopped, and her voice did something I hadn't heard it do yet, which was soften, "—don't stop moving. Cold takes you when you stand still. Grief too. Same principle."

Then she shut her door on us, and we were in the dark, and the rain was coming at us sideways out of a sky that had given up any pretense of being above it all.

* * *

I want to describe the coast road honestly, because afterward — assuming there is an afterward — I expect people will imagine something picturesque. A lane, perhaps. Hedgerows. A view.

What there actually was: a track cut into the base of cliffs that had been shedding themselves into the sea since the beginning of time, flooded to the knee in places where the storm surge had come up over the shingle and decided to stay. The wind came off the water with the specific intention of removing us from the world, and the rain was not rain so much as the sea taking potshots. We walked in each other's blind spot, close enough to touch, far enough apart to see, and we did not speak, because speaking meant turning your face into the wind, and the wind punished vanity.

I counted my footsteps. This is a habit I developed somewhere around checkpoint one and have never managed to break: dread, measured out in steps. Two hundred steps between the last cottage and the bend at Harrow Point. Three hundred along the drowned stretch, water pulling at our hems like a beggar with opinions. Four hundred to the old signal station with its roof caved in like a broken promise.

At five hundred and some, Sable stopped.

I walked into her before I understood why, and she caught my arm and turned us both toward the cliff, into a fold of rock that smelled of bird lime and old iron, and we stood there pressed against stone while three riders went by below us on the road — going the other way, thank God, back toward Gray Mouth — in magistrate's livery, oilskins streaming, horses unhappy about the whole enterprise.

They were carrying lanterns. In daylight. Which told me two things: that the weather had made noon into dusk, and that they wanted to be seen. Hobb's men weren't searching quietly anymore. They were announcing themselves. *Look at us. Look how thorough we are. Every keeper's station, every cottage, every road.*

"They've been to Marta's already," I said, when the hoofbeats faded. It wasn't a question. The road behind us had been trampled into porridge.

"Or they're going to Marta's now," Sable said. "Either way, she knew what it would cost. Come."

And we came.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being hunted: most of it is walking. All the drama — the near-misses, the held breath, the ledger sewn against your ribs like a second heart — most of the time it's just putting one foot in front of the other while your mind does the real work of convincing your body that this is survivable.

My mind was losing the argument by Harrow Point.

The rain had found its way inside my oilskin through every seam I owned, and my boots had passed from damp through wet into some new state of matter that science has yet to name, and the ledger — wrapped in oilcloth, strapped flat against my stomach beneath my coat — was the only dry thing in England, possibly the only dry thing in creation, and I loved it the way you love a thing you would die for, which is to say resentfully and completely.

"How far?" I shouted, for perhaps the tenth time that morning.

Sable didn't answer at once. She had gone ahead to the lip of the point, where the land dropped away and the road bent inland toward the valley, and she stood there with rain running off her chin, looking at something.

"Sable."

"Toll bridge," she said. "There's a fire lit under the arch."

I came up beside her, and there it was, half a mile off across the flats: the bridge we'd crossed eleven days ago with our funeral faces and our mourning skirts, the bridge where a bored customs officer had nearly run his fingers straight into the seam of Sable's hem where the ledger used to live. Now there were men under the arch, four or five, a brazier throwing orange light against the wet stone, and a barrier across the road that had definitely not been there before.

"They've closed it," I said stupidly.

"They've closed every crossing into the peninsula. Pike's letter said as much — seal the roads, search every station. They're not looking for smugglers anymore. They're looking for us, and they know we have to come back this way eventually, because there's only one road home to Widow's Rock." She said it without drama, the way she reported weather. "Unless we swim."

"Do you swim?"

"No."

"Then I suppose we're walking through the toll gate."

"We are doing nothing of the kind." She turned, and even soaked to the skin and gray with exhaustion, she managed to look at me the way a headmistress looks at a girl who has suggested something catastrophic. "You don't walk through a door someone is holding shut. You find another door, or you make the man holding it open it himself."

"Which are we doing?"

"The second. Eventually. First we wait for dark, and first we get out of this wind, or we'll both be dead of exposure before anyone has the pleasure of arresting us." She scanned the cliff face above us, reading it the way she read everything — charts, weather, men — and then pointed. "There. Old quarryman's hut. My husband sheltered in it once. He said the roof held."

*Your husband*, I thought, climbing after her, *has been dead two years and is still the most useful man on this coast.* I didn't say it. There were things you could say to Sable Nakamura and things you couldn't, and Kenji Nakamura lived mostly among the things you couldn't, except in the dark, in bad weather, when she gave pieces of him away like a woman spending a hoarded inheritance.

The hut held. Barely. Half the roof was honest slate, half was honest sky, but the honest half covered a corner big enough for two, and there was a hearth with enough old gorse and driftwood in the grate to raise a smokeless fire, if you knew how — and Sable knew how, of course she knew how, she'd kept a light burning alone through six winters and had opinions about combustion the way bishops have opinions about God.

We ate Marta's bread. We dried our stockings by a flame the size of a cupped hand. And through the crack in the shutter we watched the toll bridge, and the fire under its arch, and the men who would cheerfully hang us both if we so much as sneezed within a hundred yards of them.

"He'll be there," I said, after a while. "The sergeant. From the first day."

"Bramley."

"You remember his name?"

"I remember everyone who has ever looked in my skirt seams." A ghost of the dry humor that surfaced in her maybe twice a week, like a fish rising. "He took a bribe in the chapel cellar, Ruth. Whatever else Bramley is, he's careful. If he's at the bridge tonight, it's because he wants to see every face that passes. Including ours, if we pass."

"Then we don't pass."

"Not as ourselves."

I looked at her. She was watching the bridge with an expression I was beginning to know and dread, the expression of a woman assembling something out of parts that should not fit together and making them fit anyway.

"What are you thinking?"

"I'm thinking," she said slowly, "that eleven days ago we went through that gate as mourners, and it worked because grief is invisible. Nobody interrogates grief. But we can't be mourners twice — Bramley has our faces now, or near enough. So we need to be something else that a sergeant doesn't look at twice." Her eyes moved to the brazier, to the men hunched around it, to the barrier. "Something official."

"Neither of us is official."

"No," Sable said. "But I've spent six years being mistaken for nothing at all. A widow, a servant, a keeper's wife — women are furniture to men like these, Ruth. They look through us. We're going to use that. We're going to walk through that gate as exactly what we are — a keeper and her hired girl, carrying dispatches — and we're going to let Bramley look straight at us and see nothing, because I am about to give him a reason not to look."

"And what reason is that?"

She reached into her coat and brought out her lighthouse logbook — the real one, the one she'd carried since leaving Widow's Rock, the record of every watch and every storm of the season — and set it on her knee.

"Authority," she said. "Paper frightens small men. And I am very good with paper."

* * *

Full dark came early, the way it does in storm season, arriving like a debt collector rather than a guest. By then Sable had done things to my appearance that I can only describe as punitive: my hair scraped back and pinned under a cap, my face smudged with peat ash, my posture corrected with a wordless pinch until I stood like a scullery maid who expected to be yelled at, which, having been one briefly and badly, I could reproduce from memory.

She had transformed herself differently. Not downward — sideways. She'd cleaned herself. Mended what could be mended, squared her collar, set her widow's cap at an angle of severe competence. She looked like what she was: a government employee of the northern lights, a woman whose signature carried weight on forms that mattered, a person who belonged to the Service and not to anyone's curiosity.

Under her arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the rain, she carried the logbook and a folder of papers she'd written by firelight for two hours — official-looking memoranda in a copperplate hand, addressed to *the Keeper, Widow's Rock Station*, concerning oil deliveries and lamp maintenance, stamped with a seal she'd improvised from a button and candle wax. Forgery, technically. Treason-adjacent, certainly. I was starting to understand that Sable's law-abiding nature was a load-bearing wall she could remove whenever the building needed to move.

"One thing," she said, at the hut door, with the wind trying to take it off its hinges. "If Bramley stops us — if anything goes wrong — you put your head down and you walk, Ruth. Not run. Walk. You get on the road toward Widow's Rock and you don't look back, and you don't wait for me, and you deliver the ledger yourself."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You're not hearing me." She took my chin in her cold fingers, and her eyes in the dark were the exact color and temperature of the sea. "Eleven days ago you washed up on my rocks half-drowned, and I pulled you out because I couldn't not. Since then you have become—" she paused, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to finish the sentence, "—the closest thing I have had to family since my husband died. Which is precisely why you will walk. Family isn't who you stay beside. It's who you carry forward. Do you understand?"

I understood. I hated it. I nodded.

"Good," she said, and released my chin, and opened the door onto the roaring night. "Then let's go pay a toll."

* * *

The approach to the bridge took twenty minutes and cost me approximately ten years of life.

We came down the valley road openly, because hiding was over — the roads were watched, the fields were watched, the only way through was through. Rain hammered the barrier, the brazier hissed and spat, and the men under the arch resolved out of the murk one at a time: two customs officers in oilskins, a magistrate's rider stamping his feet, a boy holding a lantern, and Sergeant Bramley.

Bramley. Eleven days older than my memory of him and somehow much worse. He stood slightly apart from the others, hands behind his back, watching the road both ways with the patience of a spider who has finally, finally gotten flies to walk toward the web on purpose.

Sable didn't hesitate. That was the whole trick of her, I realized, watching her walk — she never hesitated. Guilty people hesitate. She marched up to the barrier as though the barrier had personally inconvenienced her, and announced, in a voice pitched to carry through weather:

"Keeper of the Widow's Rock station. Open the gate, please. I've been delayed at Gray Mouth since Tuesday and I've oil and stores rotting on the quay."

The boy lifted the lantern. Light fell across her face, and mine, and I performed my scullery-maid slouch with what I can only assume was award-winning conviction, staring at Bramley's boots as though boots were the most interesting thing ever committed to leather.

Bramley stepped forward. Up close he was bigger and tireder than I remembered, with the complexion of a man whose conscience had recently started charging him interest.

"Widow's Rock," he said. "You're the widow up there. Nakamura."

"I am."

"Roads are closed. Magistrate's order."

"My papers say otherwise." Sable held out the folder with the button-sealed memoranda, and here is the moment I want recorded: Bramley took the papers, and looked at them, and then looked up — at Sable — and held the look.

One beat. Two. His eyes moved over her face the way a man reads a document he suspects has been altered, checking margins, checking seals. The rain filled the silence. Behind me I heard the rider shift his horse, bored, wanting his supper. My heart was performing some kind of percussion solo against the oilcloth strap, and I thought, with perfect clarity: *he knows, he knows, he's known since the chapel, he looked at her one beat too long that day too, and now—*

"Station log says you logged a wreck survivor three weeks back," Bramley said. Conversational. Almost gentle. "Young woman. Never accounted for."

For one insane instant I almost answered — I, the scullery maid, the furniture — and Sable's shoulder shifted a half-inch against mine, a pressure like a hand on a tiller, correcting course.

"The survivor," Sable said, "was delivered to the parish at Harrow Point. It's in the log. Would you like to read the whole entry? It's dull. Most of drowning is paperwork."

Something happened in Bramley's face then. I've thought about it since, the way you turn a stone over and over. Because he believed her — I'm sure of it now, sure the way you're sure of weather — and believing her, he had a choice, and I watched him make it. His eyes went from her face to the papers to the road behind us, dark and empty and full of rain.

"Oil and stores," he said. "Nothing else in the boat?"

"Nothing that concerns the revenue."

A pause. The brazier popped.

"Open the gate," Bramley said, stepping back. And then, as Sable took my arm to steer me through, quietly, under the wind, words meant for her alone and thrown away like something he'd decided not to keep: "Whatever you're carrying, keeper — walk fast."

We walked fast.

We did not look back. Sable had taught me that much. But I felt the look between my shoulder blades the whole length of the bridge, one beat too long and then another, and I understood that we had just passed through a door held open by a man who knew exactly what he was letting through it, and that sooner or later — probably sooner — someone would ask him why.

* * *

Beyond the bridge the road climbed, and the wind changed from enemy to merely hostile, and Widow's Rock lay somewhere ahead of us in the black — nine miles, maybe ten, a tower and a lamp and every door forced, every shadow pre-inhabited by men waiting for us.

Sable didn't speak until the bridge was a glow behind us. Then, without slowing:

"He recognized me at the chapel," she said. "Eleven days ago. I've been certain since the market town. Tonight confirms it."

"Then why let us pass?"

"Because Elias Vane was hanged on a lie, and Bramley helped build the lie, and some part of that man has been waiting three years for a chance to hand back the rope." She said it evenly, but her hand, where it gripped my arm, was trembling — whether from cold or from the arithmetic of having bet both our lives on the conscience of a bribe-taker, I couldn't tell. "It doesn't matter. What matters is that by tomorrow, when Hobb asks him, Bramley will either lie for us again — or he won't, and then every man Hobb owns will be on this road behind us."

Ahead, through a tear in the clouds, the sea flashed once, silver-black, and I thought I saw — or imagined I saw — the far-off point of rock where the tower stood, patient as a gravestone.

"How far to the light?" I asked.

Sable checked the sky, the road, the wind, and finally me.

"Nine miles," she said. "And every one of them already bought and paid for."
