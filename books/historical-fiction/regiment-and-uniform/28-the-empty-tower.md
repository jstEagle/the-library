## 28. The Empty Tower

The storm had spent its worst on the road behind us, which was a mercy, because I no longer had the strength to be afraid of anything larger than my own front door.

It stood open.

Not ajar. Not nudged by wind, though there had been wind enough to nudge cathedral doors. Open, and hanging with that particular wrongness of a thing moved by hands rather than weather — the hinges creaking their old complaint at an angle they'd never learned from me, the latch plate scarred where a crowbar or a boat hook had persuaded it. Six years of oiling and tightening, of treating that door like the first handshake a stranger got from my house, and someone had introduced themselves with iron.

Ruth stopped beside me in the rain, her hood streaming, and said nothing. She didn't have to. We had both done the arithmetic on the walk up from the coast path: if Hobb's men had come and gone, the tower would be shut. A searched building gets closed by men who want their searching kept private. An *open* building is a building someone means to come back to.

"Inside?" she asked.

"After you," I said, "no." I went first. It was my ground.

* * *

The kitchen looked as it always did, which frightened me more than wreckage would have. Wreckage is honest. This was tidiness performed by people who wanted me to think they hadn't been thorough — the chairs squared, the kettle set back on its hook exactly where I kept it, close enough to right that only a keeper would feel the wrongness humming off it like a bad bearing. They had measured my life while wearing gloves. Someone had read my logbooks; the shelf sat a finger's width out of true. Someone had opened my tea tin and put it back crooked, and I confess that small desecration angered me more than the door. A man who rifles your strongbox is a thief. A man who rifles your tea and can't be bothered to align the lid is telling you he has all the time in the world.

I lit the stove anyway. Habit is a kind of spine.

Then we climbed.

The lamp room told the rest of the story. The service hatch to the clockwork rotation stood open. My cleaning rags had been unfolded, refolded badly, left smelling faintly of pipe smoke — Pike's tobacco, I'd have wagered the lens itself, though I kept the wager to myself. And the storage locker beneath the mercury bath, where I keep the spare wicks and the log of oil consumption, had been pulled out and shoved back with one corner proud of its line.

But the oil. The oil was untouched.

Two hundred gallons of mineral oil in the reserve tanks, enough to burn the light through a month of the blackest weather God sends this coast, and not a pint disturbed. I stood there with my hand on the tank valve and understood what I was looking at, and the understanding settled over me like a wet coat.

They hadn't come to disable the light. They hadn't come to steal or smash or sabotage. Any fool sent to blind a lighthouse pours the oil down the rocks or cracks a valve, and it takes ten minutes and no courage. These men had opened every drawer in my house and then closed them again, gently, like undertakers arranging a body for viewing.

They were keeping the light alive. For us. A lit tower on a known route, with a known keeper, was bait sitting on a hook made of granite — and Hobb wanted the fish to swim home comfortable.

"They want us here," Ruth said quietly. Her eyes were doing the thing they did when fear and fury fought to a draw: going very bright and very still. "They've made it ready for us."

"They've made it ready for *themselves*," I corrected. "We're merely expected." I tested the rotation gear. It turned smooth as a sermon. "There's a difference, girl, between walking into a trap and walking into your own kitchen. One of those, you get to choose the moment."

"And the other?"

"The other chooses you. So we take the first."

* * *

I made Ruth watch the sea while I watched the land, and between us we found them inside an hour.

Not on the rock — Widow's Rock is bare as a bone and offers nowhere to hide but the tower itself, which they'd evidently judged better left empty. No, they were patient about it. From the gallery rail, wrapped in sleet and pretending to check the lightning rod, I picked out the details one at a time. A boat drawn up under the tarpaulin of the old wreckers' cave at the base of the far headland, half a mile east across the channel at low water. Smoke from a fire that someone had thought to screen with wet kelp — clever, that; it rose thin and white and might pass for spray until you knew to look. And once, briefly, a man on the headland path with a spyglass raised toward my windows, standing so casually against the grey sky that his casualness shouted.

Six, I made it. Possibly seven. Well armed, well fed — I could see the glint of a kettle over their fire — and positioned precisely where they could watch every approach by land and intercept any approach by sea, without ever dirtying their boots on my doorstep.

Waiting for the Vane girl. Waiting for the widow. Waiting, most of all, for whatever we carried that was worth a magistrate's personal attention.

"They know we'll come," Ruth said when I came down. "Why would they? Why not just search the whole coast?"

"Because searching costs men and time and finds nothing but cold trails," I said. "And because they're right. We did come home." I began pulling the canvas covers off the stores I'd need, and the work steadied my voice into something I almost recognized. "Which means everything they've planned is built on one assumption — that we arrive desperate, sneak inside, hide, and wait to be flushed. That we behave like quarry."

"And we won't?"

I looked around my lamp room. The great lens stood in its cage of brass, five feet of ground glass and silvered metal that Kenji had taught me to polish panel by panel until I could do it blind, in the dark, with my eyes stinging from salt. Every surface of this room knew my hands. Every bolt and valve and lever answered to a grip I'd been training for six years without knowing what for.

"We won't," I said, "because this isn't a trap they built. It's mine. They're just standing in it."

* * *

Here is what a lighthouse actually is, for those who have never lived inside one: it is not a light. It is a machine for making promises, and every part of it is a promise that can be kept or broken on purpose.

The lens gathers a single flame and throws it twenty miles. The clockwork turns it so that the flash comes at its appointed character — Widow's Rock, occulting white, three seconds bright, three seconds dark, the rhythm every coaster on this coast knows in their marrow the way you know your own pulse. Change the character and you tell every master at sea that something is wrong here. Show them a light where no charted light should be, and you tell them worse. Keep a light burning steady over rocks that should show dark intervals, and you will bring a ship onto the stones within the hour — which is why the old Board regulations treat a falsified character as near to murder.

And below all that, moaning through the fog on its steam whistle, the fog signal keeps its own speech: two blasts, fifteen seconds apart, the sound that says *here I am, here is safety, steer for me.*

I had spent six years keeping every one of those promises without fail. Tonight I intended to break all of them, in order, on schedule, and make each broken promise a weapon.

I inventoried aloud, partly for Ruth, mostly to hear my own plan take weight in the air.

"Mirrors first." The signal-room cabinet held the hand mirrors and the heliograph plates — polished steel, meant for signaling passing ships by sunlight, useless in a storm except for one thing: arranged behind and around the lamp, angled just so, they could throw stray light down the landward side of the tower. Down the headland path. Into the eyes of anyone watching from the rocks below, at the exact moment I needed them blind. "Oil next — the tanks are full, thanks to our guests' generosity, and the pressure reservoir for the fog signal is charged. It holds steam for four hours if I don't fire the boiler. Five if I cheat it."

"And the valve?"

I laid my palm flat on the brass wheel of the steam valve, warm even now with banked pressure, and felt the tremor of held force underneath, like a heartbeat in a sleeping animal. "The fog signal runs on a timer governor. Fifteen seconds, two blasts, faithful as church. But the governor is a mechanical courtesy, Mrs. Vane. Underneath it is a manual bypass, and underneath the bypass is a valve I can hold open by hand, and when I hold it open by hand, this tower doesn't speak its little polite sentence anymore." I met her eyes. "It screams. Continuously. As long as I hold it. Long enough for every ear on this coast — including a certain ship riding at anchor offshore — to hear a distress signal that cannot be mistaken for routine by anyone who knows the codes. Which Justice Merrow's escort captain does, or he shouldn't be wearing the anchor."

Ruth had gone quiet in the way she did when her mind was running ahead of her mouth. "And when Hobb's men hear it —"

"They'll come up the headland path to stop it. In the dark. Straight past my mirrors, straight onto the rocks at the channel mouth, where the tide is making and the Widow's shoal lies a boat-length under the swell." I took down the oilcan and began, with great tenderness, to oil the clockwork. "My husband used to say a lighthouse is the most honest building ever built, because it can't lie. He was nearly right. It can't lie — but it can be made to tell a different truth than the one it was built for. There's a difference, and the difference is a woman holding a valve."

"You'd be falsifying the light," Ruth said slowly. "That's — Sable, the penalty for that alone —"

"I know what it is." I had counted the cost already, in the nights since Marta's cottage, and the ledger of it balanced more easily than I'd expected. My name, my post, my clean record, possibly my liberty for a season. Against that: Elias Vane's signature, a magistrate's crimes, a daughter's life, and a dead man waiting two winters for someone to say *you were right and they killed you for it.* I had buried my husband twice — once in the ground, once behind a widow's black and a false calm — and I had learned this much about burial: some things you bury stay dead, and some things are only waiting.

"Girl," I said, "I have kept this light honestly for six years. If I am to lose it, I will lose it spending the last of its honesty where it counts. Now fetch me those mirrors, and mind the edges — they'll cut you quicker than Pike's men will."

She fetched them. And as we worked, side by side in the lamp room while the sleet ticked against the glass like impatient fingers, I caught her watching me with an expression I couldn't name at first. Then I could. It was the look of a woman watching someone else pick up a weapon she'd been carrying alone.

Outside, the storm gathered itself for the night's real work. The barometer had fallen all afternoon like a stone dropped down a well. By full dark the wind would be a gale, the sea would be mountainous, and six men under wet kelp-smoke on the far headland would be straining their eyes toward a tower whose keeper had finally come home.

Let them look. Let them count the windows, watch the lamplight move behind the panes, take their comfort from the ordinary rhythm of a keeper's evening. I wanted them comfortable. Comfortable men stand still.

I set the table in the kitchen for two, and put the kettle on, and waited for Magistrate Corliss Hobb to come and arrest me in my own house — while above his head, forty feet up in the dark, the mirrors waited, and the valve waited, and the light that could not lie rehearsed the only lie I would ever ask of it.
