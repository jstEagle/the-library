## 29. The Judge's Ship

We saw her masts before we saw anything else — three thin lines drawn against the bruised sky, like someone had scratched at the horizon with a knife and given up halfway.

"There," I said, though Sable had already seen them. She always saw things first. I'd stopped competing somewhere around the flooded quarry, when she'd hauled me out of black water by the collar of my coat and said, with the mildness of a woman remarking on the weather, "Don't fight the sea. It's a poor loser."

Now we stood in the lee of the headland above Widow's Rock, both of us soaked past any pretense of dryness, staring at that gray shape riding at anchor two miles offshore. The escort ship. *The Meridian*, if Marta Quill's gossip could be trusted, and Marta's gossip was more reliable than most men's oaths.

Justice Merrow's ship, delayed by the storm, waiting out the worst of it before attempting the channel.

Waiting for confirmation that the channel was safe.

And there was only one voice on this coast that could tell a captain whether the channel was safe or not. Only one light.

"Two miles," Sable said. Her eyes were narrowed against the spray, doing arithmetic I couldn't follow — distances, depths, the speed of a longboat through swell. "They'll have a pilot boat standing off. They won't bring a judge's escort through those rocks on rumor."

"So we give them more than rumor."

"We give them exactly what they'll believe." She turned to look at me, and rain ran down her face and she didn't bother wiping it away. "A keeper's signal. The old code. Every captain who ever worked this coast knows it. Light held steady on the safe water means come ahead. Light turned to the wreck-stones—" her jaw tightened— "means stay clear, something's wrong. And a keeper doesn't turn her light to her own rocks unless she has no other way to say it."

"And Hobb will know it too," I said. "He'll know what you're saying. He'll know exactly where we are and exactly why."

"Yes," Sable said. "That's rather the point."

I want to say I understood immediately. I didn't. It took me another hour of walking, another hour of cold working its way into my bones, before the shape of her plan opened up in front of me like a door into a dark room — and I stood on the threshold understanding that everything we had run from for eleven days was about to become the thing we ran toward.

*

Here is what I knew by then, which was nearly nothing and somehow enough:

My father wrote a confession before he died. Not a letter — a confession, forty pages of it, in the careful clerk's hand he'd used all his life, the hand that had once recorded other men's lies for Magistrate Corliss Hobb until he found the ledger beneath the lies and couldn't unknow it. The ledger was sewn into my mourning skirt now, against my hip, where it had ridden through a toll bridge, a ferry crossing, and one swim in a quarry that I still dreamed about. Forty pages of bribes and signatures and dates. My father's last work. His best work.

I had never known him well. That's the thing people don't understand about a hanged man's daughter — you don't lose a father, you lose a future tense. Everything he might have been to me got amputated the day they dropped him through the trapdoor, and I'd spent two years carrying not grief exactly but its stubborn cousin: the certainty that the story everyone told about him was wrong, without a single fact to prove it. Then his letter surfaced, half-drowned, clutched in the hands of a smuggler's boy who died getting it to the rocks below Widow's Rock, and the wrong story cracked open, and here I was.

Eleven days ago I would have told you I came back for justice. That was even true, mostly. But eleven days of Sable Nakamura had taught me the difference between justice and something older and less tidy — between proving a man innocent and finding out who killed him, and discovering those were two different errands, and you had to finish both or neither counted.

Kenji Nakamura's name was in the ledger's margin. A payment to a *K. Nakamura, keeper* — a bribe recorded to bind him in silence, taken or untaken, it didn't matter; the entry itself was a death sentence, because a dead bribe-taker tells no tales but a live one might. Sable had read that line by peat fire light and gone so still I thought she'd stopped breathing. When she finally spoke, her voice was level in the way ice is level right before it takes your weight.

"Now I know why," was all she said. "Now I know why the sea gave him back to me face-down."

She hadn't cried. I kept waiting for it and she never did, and I understood eventually that some women's grief isn't water. Hers was load-bearing. You don't weep a wall.

*

By late afternoon we had come down off the headland and crouched among the black boulders above the tower, watching.

Widow's Rock sat in its collar of surf, the lighthouse rising white and blind-eyed out of the murk, its gallery rail dripping, its lamp room dark. Dark in daylight meant nothing. Dark at night would mean everything. From this distance I could see the door hanging a little wrong on its hinges — forced, during our eleven days away, searched and left. But the glass of the lamp room caught the gray light and threw it back whole, unbroken. They'd gone through her home like burglars and hadn't broken her lens. Small mercies. Or perhaps not mercy at all — perhaps Hobb wanted the light operational, wanted the station alive, because a dead lighthouse draws inspectors and inspectors ask questions, whereas a living one just quietly guides contraband past the wreck-stones the way it had been guiding it for years, under a magistrate's protection, past a customs sergeant's toll bridge, while an honest keeper polished glass and buried her husband's name.

"They're not inside," Sable murmured. We'd watched for two hours and seen no smoke from the chimney, no movement at the windows, no boat tied at the landing. "But they're near. Pike wouldn't leave a station he suspects unwatched. There'll be men at the old coastguard hut, maybe in the boathouse. Waiting for us to walk up the path and knock."

"Then how do we get in?"

"The same way the oil gets in." She nodded toward the slipway, the iron rails running down into the boiling shallows where the supply boat would tie off in decent weather. Tonight there'd be no decent weather. Tonight the great storm of the year was assembling itself over the water like something being built plank by plank. "In this surf, nobody watches the seaward side. Nobody can. We go around the rock at slack low water, up the ladder to the oil store, and in through the storeroom hatch. If Kenji's maintenance held — and it held for six years after he died because I kept it — the ladder's sound and the hatch isn't rusted shut."

"If it doesn't hold?"

"Then we'll discuss it from the water."

That was Sable's idea of humor. I'd learned to recognize it the way you learn to recognize a specific bird call — rare, flat, easy to miss, and usually a warning.

*

We waited for dark, and dark came early and angry, the storm arriving like a debt collector. By full dark the wind was a solid thing you leaned against, and the rain came horizontal, needling, and the sea below the headland was white noise made solid, thundering and withdrawing and thundering again.

We went around the seaward side at slack water, which wasn't slack so much as briefly undecided. The ladder was there. The ladder held. I climbed it with the ledger pressing its rectangular promise against my hip and salt water trying to peel me off the rock, and Sable climbed behind me calling instructions in the voice of a woman describing a recipe, and we came through the oil store hatch into a smell of paraffin and rope and cold iron that was, unmistakably, *home* to her. I saw it hit her — that smell. She stood in the black storeroom with her hand flat against the wall and for a moment she wasn't with me at all. She was with the six years she'd spent here alone, keeping a light for ships and a silence for herself.

Then she lit the smallest lantern I've ever seen, cupped behind her fingers, and got to work.

I want to record what her work looked like, because I think people imagine lighting a lighthouse as striking a match. It is not. It is a discipline. She checked the oil reservoirs first — full, thank God; the searchers had left the fuel untouched, either from carelessness or design — and pumped pressure into the vaporizer system, listening to the gauge the way a doctor listens to a chest. She trimmed the spare mantles and laid them out like instruments on a tray. She tested the clockwork that rotated the lens, wound it, listened to its ticking heart. She went up to the lamp room and came down twice, once for a wrench and once swearing softly in Japanese, which was the first time I'd heard her use her husband's language and the second time I'd heard her say anything about him aloud since the peat fire.

"What's wrong?" I asked.

"Nothing. The fog signal's steam valve is stiff. It wants oiling and I haven't the time to do it properly." She worked the valve with both hands, jaw set. "It'll scream when it opens. That's fine. Screaming is what we want it to do."

Down in the watch room she spread her preparations out and showed me the plan entire, and this is what it was, laid plain:

At midnight — or whenever the storm peaked, whichever came — she would light the lamp. First she'd let it burn steady on the safe channel for a quarter of an hour, because the escort ship would be watching, and a light appearing after darkness is just a light; a keeper coming on duty. Routine. Nothing to alarm a pilot.

Then she would turn the lens. Not to the safe water. To the wreck-stones. Hold it there. Turn it back. To the stones again. The old code. *Something is wrong. Do not come in by the marked channel.* And no captain in the world reads that signal and thinks *weather*. Weather closes a harbor; it doesn't make a keeper aim her light at her own rocks. A captain reading that signal knows the keeper is telling him something a flag couldn't say — that the danger is human, that the channel is watched, that the light itself is speaking under duress.

"And then?" I asked.

"Then Ruth opens the fog signal. Full steam. Distress pattern — three blasts, pause, three blasts, pause. Not the scheduled fog signal. A distress pattern from a manned lighthouse means the keeper herself is signaling for help." She looked at me across the little lantern flame. "Justice Merrow's escort will put a boat ashore. They must — a distress signal from a crown station obligates them. And when they land, they will find a keeper and a young woman holding a dead man's confession, and a magistrate's riders hiding in the coastguard hut, and whatever happens next happens in front of witnesses a magistrate cannot buy."

"And if the ship doesn't answer? If the captain decides the risk is too great and weighs anchor and runs for open water?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. Rain hammered the lamp room glass overhead, and somewhere below us the sea detonated against the rock, again and again, patient as a siege.

"Then we've lit a lamp for nobody," she said, "and we're two women alone in a tower with a magistrate's men outside it, and we find out tomorrow whether I'm as good at dying as my husband was."

"That's not funny."

"No," she agreed. "It's arithmetic."

*

I took the first watch at the lamp room window, because she refused to sleep and I refused to let her stand there all night, and we compromised the way women compromise, which is to say somebody pretended to win. Through the streaming glass I watched the escort ship ride at anchor in the outer roads, her riding lights doubled against the storm, small gold points that vanished and reappeared as the swells rolled between us. Somewhere aboard that ship was a judge. An actual judge, with actual authority, the one man on this coast whose signature Hobb couldn't forge, whose questions Hobb couldn't answer with a bribe, whose presence turned our forty pages of evidence from a death sentence into a sentence pronounced on the guilty instead.

One signal. One light. That was the whole distance left.

And yet I stood at that window and understood, with terrible clarity, the knife-edge we were balanced on. Because the same signal that could bring the judge's boat ashore could also bring Hobb's men up that path at a run. The same light that said *come* to a ship two miles out said *here we are* to every man in the coastguard hut. We weren't choosing between safety and danger. We were choosing which danger arrived first, and betting our lives that justice traveled faster than malice through two miles of storm-lashed water.

There was also — and I made myself look at it straight on, because Sable had taught me that much — the rocks themselves. The wreck-stones. If the escort misread the signal, if her pilot misjudged the set of the current in this blow, if the light's timing confused rather than warned, then the judge's ship would end her career on the very shoals my father's killers had been navigating contraband past for a decade. We would trade a murder trial for a shipwreck, forty pages of truth for a hundred drowned men. The sea did not care whose cause was just. The sea kept its own ledger, and it collected in hulls and lives, and it never once accepted an apology.

I thought about my father's hands writing his confession by candlelight in a cell, knowing — he must have known — that the letter might never leave the prison, that the boy he paid to carry it might die on the rocks, that a stranger might fish it out of the surf and burn it unread. He wrote it anyway. That was the whole of his courage, compressed into ink and paper: *do the true thing even if the true thing drowns.*

Below me, Sable was checking the fog signal's boiler pressure for the fourth time.

Above me, the storm was deciding how much it intended to take.

And out on the black water, the *Meridian*'s lights swung gently at anchor, patient, waiting for the morning, for a pilot, for word that the channel was safe — not knowing that the only word that mattered was locked inside a tower with two women and a dead man's handwriting, waiting for a match to be struck.

I heard Sable climb the stairs. She came to stand beside me at the window, and together we watched the ship, and neither of us spoke for a long time.

"He'll wait for dawn," she said at last. "Captains always want dawn. Dawn is when they feel brave."

"And we can't wait for dawn."

"No. Because at dawn, Hobb stops searching the roads and starts searching the stations, and Pike comes up that path with thirty men instead of six." She touched the cold glass, over the distant gold points of the riding lights, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you needed to trust you. "It has to be tonight. At the height of the blow, when no pilot would dare the inner channel on his own judgment — when the only voice he'll trust is mine."

"You're certain he'll trust it?"

"I'm certain he has no alternative." She stepped back from the window, and in the dim red glow of the masked standby lamp her face was calm in the way I had learned to read as anything but calm. "Get the ledger ready, Ruth. Whatever happens tonight, those pages go into that judge's hands if I have to throw them to him in an oilskin wrapped round a stone. Wind the clockwork. Check the mantles. And then come and stand with me."

"It's not midnight yet."

"I know." She was already turning toward the stairs, toward the lamp, toward the thing she'd spent six years polishing and one lifetime earning. "But the storm isn't going to wait for the almanac. And neither, I think, is Hobb."

Far below, in the dark, a lantern flickered once along the cliff path — brief, deliberate, moving toward the tower.

They were coming.

Sable smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a keeper who has finally been given permission to do her duty.

"Good," she said. "Let them watch the light."
