## 30. Hobb Comes to the Light

They came at half past three in the afternoon, when the light of the world was going thin and gray, and they came the way men come when they want you to know exactly how many of them there are.

I counted them from the gallery before I went down. Six riders in magistrate's livery, dark coats beaded with spray, and two figures on foot who had dismounted at the causeway because no horse alive would cross those stones in a rising sea. The tall one walked like a man who had never once been cold. That would be Hobb himself. The other one — narrow, quick, head always slightly tilted as though listening for a door opening somewhere behind him — that one I knew by reputation alone, and it was enough. Sergeant Aldous Pike, the clerk who had sold his master's life by the yard.

Below me, Ruth was already moving. She had seen them before I had; she'd been watching from the lamp room with the telescope while I made tea, which is a sentence I never expected to write about my own kitchen. Now she stood at the bottom of the stair with her oilskin already dripping from the morning's work on the mirrors, and her face was doing what faces do when they are being held together by hand.

"That's him," she said. "Hobb. And Pike."

"I know."

"He'll search the tower. He'll search everything."

"Yes." I took the kettle off the stove and set it on its trivet, and I will confess that my hands were steadier than I had any right to expect, given what was in them. "Which is why he must find nothing, and be kept so long finding nothing that the tide turns against him. Men who come to bully hate nothing more than being made comfortable first. It takes the wind out of them. My husband taught me that. He said the Empire ran on tea, and that anyone who refused a cup was telling you something."

"Sable—"

"Go up," I said. "The lamp room hatch is behind the mirror frame. You know it. Don't come down for anything, Ruth. Not if you hear me scream. Especially not if you hear me scream."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide to obey, and I understood that this too was a kind of courage — harder, maybe, than climbing into flood water. Then she went up the stair on bare feet, quick and silent as smoke, and the hatch closed above the frame of the great lens, and I was alone with the kettle, the cups, and six hundred pounds of approaching magistrate.

I set out four cups anyway. Generosity is more unnerving than scarcity.

* * *

The knock, when it came, was not a knock. It was a statement of intent delivered in oak — three blows heavy enough to rattle the lamp oil on its shelf — and by the time I opened the door, Magistrate Corliss Hobb had arranged his face into an expression of weary civic duty, the look of a man burdened by the necessity of inconveniencing honest folk.

"Widow Nakamura," he said.

"Magistrate Hobb." I inclined my head precisely as far as courtesy required and not one inch further. "You've come far in filthy weather. Will your men shelter in the boathouse? There's dry wood there, and the stove draws well enough."

Something flickered behind his eyes. I had offered him organization. Bullies do not expect to be managed.

"We won't impose long," he said, stepping inside without waiting for the invitation to finish arriving. Pike followed him, and the door closed on the wind with a sound like a held breath released. Up close, Hobb smelled of wet wool and bay rum, a respectable smell, a church-and-county-fair smell, and I thought: *this man signed a warrant with hands that smell like Sunday.* "Reports of a woman passing through the coast road. A fugitive. We have reason to believe she may have taken refuge here, or passed through, or been seen."

"Here?" I let mild surprise lift my brows. "Magistrate, I see perhaps four souls a year who are not seabirds."

"Nevertheless." He unbuttoned his glove finger by finger, taking his time, making a small theater of it. "We'll need to search the station. All of it. Routine."

"Of course." I turned to the stove. "Tea first. You've crossed the causeway in a gale; I won't have a magistrate's death on my rocks for lack of five minutes' hospitality. It would spoil the logbook."

That last was a keeper's joke, dry as tinder, and Pike almost smiled at it — a quick, hungry little movement of the mouth, the smile of a man cataloguing. Hobb did not smile. Hobb sat down at my table as though he owned the deed, and spread his gloved hands flat upon it, and looked around my kitchen with the slow proprietary gaze of a man pricing furniture at an estate sale.

"You keep this station alone," he said. "Since your husband died."

"Two years this autumn."

"An accident, I understand. On the rocks."

"The sea keeps its own accounts, sir."

"Mm." He watched me pour. I poured well — I have poured ten thousand cups in this tower, and my hands know the work the way they know the wick-trim — and set his cup before him with the handle turned correctly, because I am petty enough to fight a war over the handle of a teacup. "You were born in these parts? I don't recall the name Sandoval in the parish registers."

Ah. There it was, then. Not a search for a girl at all — or not only that. A search for *me*, and for the name I had buried under widow's black and a keeper's routine.

"My people were from up-country," I said pleasantly. "My husband brought me to the coast. He liked the loneliness. He said a lighthouse was the only honest job left — you cannot lie to the sea, only to sailors, and the sea corrects you."

"A Japanese name," Pike said softly, from where he had drifted toward the stair door. "Nakamura. Unusual, for these parts."

"Unusual things wash ashore all the time, Sergeant. This coast collects them." I refilled Hobb's cup before he asked. "Your tea is getting strong. Take it before it turns bitter."

For a moment — just a moment — the magistrate's composure slipped, and beneath it I saw not anger but calculation, quick and cold, a man reordering his assumptions. He had expected either a frightened peasant woman or a defiant one. He got neither. He got a hostess.

"Begin the search," he said to Pike, without looking away from me.

Pike went to the stair door, and I felt the whole tower tilt around that single hinge — every plank, every stone, every hour of my husband's ghost leaning on it. Above us, ninety feet up, hidden behind the frame of the great lens, a girl was holding her breath in the dark among the brass and glass.

"The lamp room is locked," I said, and heard how flat my voice came out. "Keeper's rule. No entry during daylight hours except for maintenance. I can fetch the key if your sergeant insists, but I'll note it in the log, as required by Trinity regulations, and I'm afraid I'll also have to note the reason."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere above, the wind found the ventilator ball and moaned through it, low and long, like the tower itself clearing its throat.

"Regulations," Hobb said at last, tasting the word. "You keep a strict logbook, Mrs. Nakamura."

"To the minute, sir. Weather, lamps, oil consumed, vessels sighted, visitors received. Would you care to sign it? Most magistrates find it a comfort. It proves the coast is governed even when it's drowning."

And there — God forgive me — I nearly lost the whole game, because Corliss Hobb *did* laugh. A short, genuine bark of amusement, quickly strangled, but real. For one heartbeat we were two people who both understood paperwork, and then he remembered what he was and why he'd come, and the shutters came down again.

"You're a cool woman," he said.

"It's the trade, sir. Panic doesn't trim wicks."

Pike had stopped at the stair door. He was looking at the frame of it, at the lintel, at the floorboards near it — reading the wood the way he'd once read his master's correspondence. Then he crouched, unhurried, and pressed two fingers to the boards by the threshold.

"Dust's disturbed," he said. "Recent traffic. Someone's been up and down these stairs today, and often."

"Oil deliveries," I said. "The winter load comes in barrels. Two hundredweight a barrel. I carry what I can and drag the rest. The stairs remember it."

"In this dress?" Pike glanced at my skirts, at my hands. "You're slight, mistress."

"I'm a keeper, Sergeant. Keepers are measured in candlepower, not stone."

He rose slowly, and his eyes went past me, around the kitchen, touching everything — the dresser, the closed bedroom door, the oil store, the hatch cover in the corner that led down to the coal cellar. He was hunting for weight that didn't match its container. A clerk's instinct, sharpened into a weapon. I put the teapot down between us, gently, deliberately, and the china sang once against the saucer.

"More tea, gentlemen?"

* * *

What followed was forty minutes that I have decided, in retrospect, to describe as the longest tea service of my life, conducted under conditions of siege.

They searched while I poured. Pike went through the bedroom — my bed, Kenji's coat still on its hook (I had debated burning it; I had not been able to), the drawer where I kept thread and needles and the mourning skirt with its false seam, now empty, the ledger gone south with us and returned north again sewn where no needle had ever worked before. He held the coat a moment too long. I saw his thumb move across the cloth, testing it, and I said, from the doorway, "Careful with that, Sergeant. Wool shrinks," and he put it back.

Hobb did not search. Hobb talked. That was worse.

He talked about the weather, about the roads flooded south of Gray Mouth, about the difficulty of governing a coast that the sea kept cutting off from the county seat. He talked about duty, about order, about how the law was like a lighthouse itself — steady, impartial, fixed — and I listened with my eyes lowered appropriately and thought: *you built the darkness yourself, you vain old hypocrite, and now you've come to admire it.* Every few minutes he asked another question dressed as conversation. Had I seen boats at night lately, running dark? Had strangers passed? Did I know the Vane family, before?

"Vane," I repeated, as though tasting an unfamiliar fish. "The man who was hanged? The traitor?"

"His daughter has fled justice," Hobb said. "There's a reward. Fifty pounds." He sipped his tea, watching me over the rim. "Fifty pounds is a great deal of money to a solitary woman."

"It's a great deal of money to anyone, sir. That's rather the point of money." I gathered his cup and refilled it. "If I see her, I'll send for the constable. Though I'll tell you freely, Magistrate — fifty pounds spent on this tower's oil budget would light the coast for a year, and save more lives than one girl's capture ever will. But then, I don't set the reward. I only keep the light."

He studied me for a long time over that cup. And I want to record honestly what passed through me under that gaze, because it wasn't fear, or not only fear. It was the vertigo of the mask holding — the strange grief of it. He was looking straight at me, this man who had murdered my husband with a forged ledger line and a bribed inspector, and he could not see me at all. He saw a widow. A functionary. A teapot with a license. And some traitorous part of me mourned that invisibility even while it saved my life, because being unseen had been my armor for seven years, and armor grows into the flesh.

Then the clock in the corner struck four, and outside, the light changed.

It happens this way in late October: the day gives up all at once, like a debt called in early. The gray through the seaward windows thickened toward dusk, and Hobb glanced at it, and I saw him do the arithmetic — a search of a lighthouse after dark means either leaving across the causeway in black water or staying the night under a hostile roof.

"Time presses," he said, standing. "Sergeant. The upper works. Quickly now."

Pike was already at the stair door. He tried the handle. Locked, as I'd promised. He looked back at Hobb.

"The key, mistress."

"Regulations require me to log the inspection," I said, and went to the hook by the dresser where the key hung, and my heart was beating so hard against my ribs that I marveled neither man could hear it, a drum, a fog signal all its own — but my hands were hands, and they took down the key, and I walked to the stair door ahead of Pike, and I unlocked it, and I stepped aside.

Because that had been the plan, all along, since dawn. Not to refuse him the tower. Refusal makes men certain. Delay makes them doubtful, and doubt is a solvent. Ruth had needed one hour above, and the tide-tables and the mirror angles and the steam valve had needed one hour, and I had spent the afternoon buying minutes at fourpence apiece with tea and regulation talk, and now the account was drawn.

But as Pike put his boot on the first stair, two things happened in the same instant, and I have replayed them a thousand times since.

Outside, the wind swung hard to the northeast with a sound like a door slamming in heaven, and the first real gusts of the coming storm hit the tower and set the whole structure humming. And above us — faint, high, almost lost in that wind — came a single metallic clank. Brass on iron. The sound of a hatch cover settling, or a hand slipping on a rail, or nothing at all, depending entirely on what a man wanted to hear.

Pike froze on the stair, his head tilting — that listening tilt, the clerk's tilt, the tilt of a man who had spent years hearing doors open behind him.

"What was that?" he said.

"The tower talks in weather," I said. "Iron contracts. Glass ticks. If you spend thirty years here, Sergeant, you'll learn its language too."

He looked back at me over his shoulder, and his eyes were not stupid eyes, and for a moment the whole edifice of my courtesy balanced on a knife-edge of dust-disturbed floorboards and a clank in the wind. Behind him, Hobb had paused halfway to the door, watching, and the lamplight lay along his face and showed me something I had not seen before: not suspicion, but *haste*. The storm was coming down on him like a judgment, and Corliss Hobb, for all his power, was afraid of the sea — a man who forged ledgers and hanged innocents, afraid of the one auditor he could not bribe.

"Leave the lamp room till morning," Hobb said. "Lock it. We'll ride out the blow at the toll house and come back at first light with proper lanterns and a full party. Sergeant — the lower rooms are clean?"

"As clean as a widow's kitchen gets, sir."

"Then we're done here." Hobb settled his hat, and at the door he turned, and gave me a last long look — the look of a man filing a page he intended to return to. "Mrs. Nakamura. Thank you for the tea. You're a most unusual woman."

"So I'm told, sir. Mind the causeway. The third stone from the channel marker shifts in a northeast gale — it's taken ankles off better men than yours."

And they went out into the dying light, six riders and two walkers, and the wind took their voices and scattered them, and the door closed, and I stood with my hand flat against the oak and counted to one hundred before I let myself breathe.

Then I bolted the door, took the lamp, and started up the stairs toward the lamp room, calling softly as I climbed:

"Ruth. Ruth — they're coming back at first light. Whatever we mean to do with this tower—" 

The storm hit the glass overhead like the fist of God, and somewhere below, the sea began to move the third stone.
