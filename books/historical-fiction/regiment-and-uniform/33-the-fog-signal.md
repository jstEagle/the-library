## 33. The Fog Signal

I have never in my life been good at waiting. My father used to say I had the patience of a kettle — everything in me wanting to come to a boil at once, and loudly. He said it with love, mostly, and with that particular weariness men reserve for daughters who ask *why* too many times. So you may imagine what the last hour in the lamp room of Widow's Rock did to me: Sable below, pouring tea for Magistrate Corliss Hobb like some terrible comedy of manners, six of his men scattered around her tower like crows on a fence, and me crouched behind the lens apparatus with my heart going like a drum in a parade.

The lamp room smelled of hot brass and paraffin, and through the glass the storm came at us sideways, sheets of rain that hit the panes like handfuls of gravel. Below, the sea was a chaos of white teeth. Somewhere out in all that violence rode His Majesty's escort ship with Justice Merrow aboard, waiting — as Sable had put it, with the flat certainty of a woman who had read tide tables for twenty years — for someone to tell him the channel was safe.

Or unsafe. That was the trick of it. That was the whole beautiful, mad trick.

Sable's plan had sounded simple when she laid it out by peat fire light, the way all impossible plans do. A keeper has signals older than any magistrate's writ. A steady light on the safe channel says *come ahead*. But turn the lens away from the channel — fix it instead on the wreck-stones, the jagged teeth to the north where the Widow herself got her name — and every captain from here to Hull knows what it means: *danger, keep off, something is wrong.* And the fog signal, when the storm made sight useless, could scream it too. Not its ordinary note. The distress pattern. The one no keeper sounds except when his station is dying or his coast is killing men.

*Hobb will know what it means,* Sable had said. *That's rather the point.*

And now Hobb was in her kitchen, drinking her tea.

I pressed my ear against the cold iron of the lens housing and listened down the stairwell, which carries sound the way a speaking tube does. Their voices came up broken, but enough. Sable's voice, low and courteous, offering another cup. Pike's voice, oily as wet rope, asking after "the widow's household." And Hobb himself, booming, comfortable, a man who believed the world was a room he owned the furniture of.

"—no strangers passed this way, Magistrate," Sable was saying. "You've seen my logbook. One woman, one lamp, one fog signal. The sea doesn't send me callers."

"The sea," Hobb said, "sent us a wreck not three weeks past."

"Then the sea can account for it. I only keep accounts for the Trinity House."

There was a pause, and I could hear him smiling. Men like Hobb always smiled before they said the cruel thing; it saved them effort later.

"You're very cool, Mrs. Nakamura. Very composed, for a woman alone on a rock in the worst storm of the year, with two strange men tramping oil through her tower."

"I'd be cooler if your men wiped their boots," Sable said. "It's a long climb to sweep."

One of the men below laughed and then choked it off. Even Hobb's hired muscle, it seemed, could recognize when they were being scolded by their betters, whatever livery they wore.

I should explain what I was doing there besides listening, because God knows I asked myself the same question about forty times a minute. Beside me, bolted to the gallery floor, stood the steam pipe feeding the fog signal trumpet — that great brass throat that hung off the gallery rail and could be heard, so Sable claimed, four miles out in calm air. Tonight it would not need four miles. Tonight the whole coast was a bell and the storm was ringing it.

The valve was a wheel of red-painted iron, stiff with salt, guarded by a cotter pin Sable had quietly drawn an hour before Hobb ever knocked. She had shown me the motion twice: quarter-turn to free it, then full open — but *not* full open. Full open was the ordinary blast, ten seconds of foghorn every ninety seconds, the sound that says *all is well, I am here.* The distress pattern was different. Three short screams, then silence long enough to make your teeth ache, then three again. Over and over until someone came or the steam ran out. No ship's captain alive would mistake it. And no magistrate either, which meant we had perhaps minutes between the first blast and six armed men taking those stairs at a run.

Which was why the timing mattered, and why the timing belonged to Sable, and why I was to do nothing — *nothing*, Ruth, not even breathe loudly — until I heard her signal. What the signal was, she hadn't said. I suspected she didn't know yet herself. Keepers improvise. It's most of the job.

Below, chairs scraped. Boots moved. My stomach dropped through three floors of tower.

"Gentlemen," Hobb said, "we'll see the lamp room."

* * *

Here is a thing nobody tells you about terror: it makes you precise. In all my months of running — from the wreck site, along the coast road, through checkpoints and chapels and flooded quarries — the moments I remember clearest are not the big ones but the small ones. The exact grain of the wood under my palm. The exact number of seconds between lightning and thunder. Now, crouched behind the clockwork that turned the great lens, I counted the footsteps on the spiral stair the way other people count rosary beads. Seven treads. Twelve. Twenty.

Pike came first. Of course he came first; Aldous Pike had spent his whole life letting braver men open doors. He emerged into the lamp room blinking against the dark — Sable kept it dark now, the lamp unlit, the glass showing nothing but rain — and swept the space with eyes that had learned cunning in a clerk's chair. I flattened myself into the shadow between the lens pedestal and the wall, pulled Sable's spare oilskin over my head, and became, as much as a terrified young woman can become, a piece of station equipment.

Behind Pike came two of the men, and last Hobb himself, filling the doorway like weather. He was bigger than I remembered from the trial — or perhaps I had simply been smaller, sixteen years old and sitting in a gallery with my mother's hand crushing mine while men in wig and black described my father as though describing a disease.

Elias Vane. Guilty of treason. Condemned on evidence, hanged within the month. I had watched Corliss Hobb sign the warrant with a flourish, a man pleased with his own penmanship.

He looked around my father's confession — because that is what this tower was now, I understood suddenly; the ledger was the letter, but the letter needed delivering, and delivery needed a witness, and we had sailed eleven days through hell to fetch one — and he saw nothing but a lamp room. Rain on glass. Cold brass. A woman's shawl abandoned on a stool, which Sable had left there deliberately, the way you leave a stage dressed for the play you want the audience to believe.

"No one here," Pike said, and there was something in his voice I didn't like — not relief, but disappointment. Pike had wanted to find me. Finding me meant the loose end tied, and Pike was a man who slept badly beside loose ends.

"Check the gallery," Hobb said. "Check behind the lens."

A man went out onto the gallery walk and I heard him swear at the wind. Another circled the lens housing — passed within arm's reach of me, close enough that I could smell wet wool and the gin on his breath — and saw, apparently, nothing but shadow. I have thought since about what he must have seen: a heap of oilskins, a shape, a girl holding her breath so completely that she had briefly stopped being a person. There are worse ways to survive than being mistaken for laundry.

"Magistrate." Pike had gone to the valve, and my blood stopped. He touched the red wheel, tested it, found it locked without its cotter pin — and frowned. "This pin's been drawn."

"Drawn?" Hobb said.

"For maintenance," Sable called up from the foot of the stair, clear and unhurried. "Before a storm I draw the pins on all the working valves so they don't seize salted shut. Would you rather I couldn't sound a warning to ships, Sergeant? Or does the ring prefer a dark coast?"

The silence that followed had a blade in it. She had said *the ring* — lightly, carelessly, the way you'd say *the weather* — and I felt Pike go still the way a snake goes still.

"A figure of speech, Mrs. Nakamura," Pike said smoothly.

"Is it," Sable said. Not a question. Just placed there, on the floor of the lamp room, like something she'd set down and dared them to pick up.

Hobb laughed. It boomed off the glass. "You've a tongue on you, widow. Kenji Nakamura was said to be a careful man. Where did he learn such boldness?"

And then — I heard it, everyone heard it — the small sound of a teacup being set down on saucer downstairs, precisely, the way you set down a teacup when your hands want very much to do something else.

"My husband," Sable said, "learned boldness from watching careful men die anyway. Now. You've inspected my tower, Magistrate. Your sergeant has insulted my maintenance. Will you take supper, or shall I show you out before the tide takes the causeway?"

* * *

They went down. All of them. I listened to every boot-fall until it faded into kitchen sounds, and then I lay back against the cold iron and shook, silently, thoroughly, the way a horse shakes off water — grief and fury and relief all coming out at once through the skin.

Because that was the moment I understood what Sable had done. She hadn't stalled them. She had *baited* them. *Where did he learn such boldness* — she had put Kenji's name in Hobb's mouth, made him say it, made the murder sit in the room with them. She was keeping the wound open on purpose. Because a wound, she'd told me once, is how the truth gets in.

Downstairs, voices. Hobb deciding. I caught fragments: *the roads are sealed... nothing crosses the headland... wait out the worst of it at the toll house...* They were leaving. They were going to leave, and the storm would seal us in with our evidence and no way to deliver it, and Merrow's ship would ride out the gale offshore and sail for London with the circuit, and eleven days would come to nothing—

No. Wait.

They weren't leaving. Listen again. *Wait out the worst of it.* At the *toll house* — no. Hobb's voice again, lazy, certain: "We'll wait at the station below. If the Vane girl comes to this rock, she comes to us. Post two men on the causeway. The rest in the boathouse. Mrs. Nakamura—" the boom turning gracious, "—thank you for your hospitality. Do lock your door."

Boots. The door. The latch. Then wind, and nothing else, and the tower ours again.

I came down the stairs faster than sense allowed and found Sable standing at the kitchen window, watching lanterns bob away toward the boathouse below the point. Eight men, minus two posted at the causeway. Six in the boathouse, warm and dry and smug, waiting for us like spiders who had already built the web.

"They'll check the causeway crossing every hour," she said. "Pike will insist. He's thorough — it's his one virtue."

"So we're trapped."

"We're *surrounded*," Sable corrected. "It's different. Trapped means no options." She turned from the window and looked at me, and there was something in her face I had seen only twice before — once at Gray Mouth, once in the quarry water — the look of a woman stepping off a ledge on purpose. "Ruth. How is your nerve."

"Worse than yours, better than most," I said.

"Good enough." She picked up the storm lantern. "Come and see the engine room."

* * *

The fog signal house clung to the tower's seaward side like a barnacle: a low stone shed housing the boiler, the steam chest, and the great air receiver, all of it fed by a coal fire that Sable had banked high against the storm, because a keeper keeps her signal ready even when the signal seems useless. The machinery filled the little building wall to wall — gauges in brass cases, pipes running out through the wall to the trumpet on the gallery above, the whole thing breathing gently, a sleeping animal.

Sable put the lantern on a shelf and laid her hand flat on the boiler shell, reading its heat the way other people read a page.

"Full pressure," she said. "I stoked it at noon, knowing they'd come. Steam's at one-eighty. The trumpet will carry." She looked at me across the machinery, orange firelight in the little gauge-glass windows between us. "When you open that valve, every man on this coast knows something is wrong. Hobb knows what it means. The escort ship knows what it means. There will be no taking it back."

"I know."

"And the men in the boathouse will be here inside two minutes. Maybe less. After that it's done — we're either rescued or hanged, and the order of those events is anyone's guess."

"That's more optimism than you usually show."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one. "Ruth Vane. Open the valve."

I climbed to the gallery in the dark, rain hammering the boards, the sea roaring below like a crowd at a hanging — poor choice of image, Ruth, thank you — and knelt at the great red wheel with its missing pin. Through the trumpet's throat above me the wind moaned, a low lost sound, the tower itself whistling tunelessly in the gale.

I thought of my father's hands. That's what I thought of, kneeling there. Not the trial, not the rope, not the ledger with all its neat damning columns. His hands, ink-stained, turning pages, teaching me to write my letters — *a firm hand, Ruth, a firm hand tells people who you are.* Everything he'd written in secret, everything smuggled out in an oilskin packet that had traveled by drowning to reach this rock, all of it waited now on a quarter-turn of my wrist.

A firm hand tells people who you are.

I turned the valve.

The first blast went through me like a bone breaking. Four feet away and screaming, the trumpet made a sound less heard than suffered — three short shrieks torn out of the storm, raw and enormous, rattling the gallery boards, shaking rain off the roof in sheets. Then silence, sudden and vast, just the wind again, one aching heartbeat of quiet —

— and three more.

*Three short. Silence. Three short.* Distress. *Come. Something is wrong. Come.*

I held the valve open and let the machine scream. Below me, lights bloomed in the boathouse like a struck match — lanterns, shouting figures, men spilling out into the rain to stare up at the tower. I heard a whistle shrill. I heard Hobb's voice, even at this distance, even through the storm, doing what booming men do: ordering, threatening, promising. Two men started up toward the tower door at a run.

And then the sea answered.

Out in the black, past the wreck-stones, a light appeared — not the tower's, not ours. A rocket went up, hissing, red, bursting against the rain clouds in a bloom of crimson that lit the whole coast for one impossible second: the waves, the rocks, the white faces of Hobb's men frozen mid-stride, and me at the rail of the gallery with my hands on a valve, lit up like a saint on a bad church window. Then darkness again. Then a second rocket. Then a third — and beneath them, faint under the storm but unmistakable, a ship's bell, ringing fast and urgent, and the deep-throated bellow of a steam whistle answering our distress pattern blast for blast, like a call across a crowded room finally, *finally* answered.

The escort ship. Merrow's escort. Rockets of acknowledgment, boats putting out — I could see them now, two dark shapes dropping from the ship's side into the swell, oars flashing whenever the lightning obliged.

The world had heard us.

The men running for the tower door stopped dead in the mud, staring out at the rockets, and I watched the exact moment the arithmetic reached them — *distress signal, warship rockets, boarding party, magistrate's unsearched boat full of contraband* — because both of them turned around and ran the other way, back toward the boathouse, back toward Hobb, back toward the growing argument about whether to flee, fight, or drown.

I opened the valve wider and let the fog signal scream until my arms shook, three short and silence and three short, over and over, the dead man's daughter pulling the lever and the whole stormy coast forced to listen.

Below me, in the lee of the tower, Sable stood in the rain without her oilskin hood, face lifted to the red glare of the rockets, watching the boarding boats come on through the swell. She wasn't cheering. She was counting oar-strokes, judging the tide against the shoal, doing a keeper's arithmetic over a magistrate's grave.

"Hobb's launching," she shouted up to me, pointing. Out past the point, lanterns swayed at the ring's own boat as men tumbled into it — Hobb's silhouette unmistakable at the stern, huge, urgent, driving his crew out into the same storm, toward the same rockets, whether to intercept the judge's launch or to run, I couldn't tell.

The fog signal screamed. The ship's bell answered. And between them, out on the black water, two boats were now racing through the dark toward each other — and straight toward the rocks where the Widow had drowned a hundred sailors, with only Sable's light, and Sable's hand on the lens, to decide which of them she let live.
