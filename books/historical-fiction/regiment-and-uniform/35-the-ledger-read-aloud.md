## 35. The Ledger Read Aloud

There is a particular quality to light that turns, and I don't mean this philosophically. I mean the great lens of Widow's Rock, which my father's executioner's employer had come to smash, sweeping its beam around the lamp room in a slow white revolution, so that everything in the room existed in a rhythm: lit, then dark; lit, then dark. Justice Merrow's face. The ledger open on the chart table. Magistrate Corliss Hobb, soaked to the skin, his wrists bound before him with the same cord he'd brought to bind us, sitting on Sable's one good chair like a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy longer than expected.

And me, reading aloud.

I had imagined this moment, in the eleven days since I'd washed up half-drowned at the base of this tower clutching my father's confession. I had imagined it as triumph — trumpets, or at least the literary equivalent, which is a sentence you've rehearsed until it shines. What I had not imagined was how cold the lamp room would be with the storm still worrying the glass, how my voice would crack on the third entry, or how Hobb would keep looking at the beam when it passed over him as though it were a tide he could wait out.

"Begin again," said Merrow. He was a small man, dry as a sermon pamphlet, with spectacles that fogged each time the lamp's heat reached them. "From the top. Slowly. A record is only as good as its reading."

So I began again.

* * *

You should understand what we were working from. The ledger had been through the flooded quarry with me, which is to say it had drowned and been resurrected page by page over two nights of Sable's patient peat fire. The ink had run in places into grey ghosts of themselves; other entries stood sharp and black, written in a hand I knew better than my own heartbeat, because I had learned my letters copying it.

My father's hand. Elias Vane, customs clerk, hanged two winters past for a theft he did not commit and a conspiracy that did not have his name anywhere in it but as victim.

The first pages were almost boring, which is the thing no one tells you about corruption. Not devilry. Arithmetic. *Received of M. Trennick, per agreement, £4. Passage of goods, north checkpoint, unexamined.* Dates. Amounts. Initials. My father had kept accounts for these men for three years before he understood what he was keeping accounts *of*, and then — because he was my father, methodical to the marrow — he had begun keeping a second set. The true one. With names.

"*Corliss Hobb,*" I read, "*magistrate, quarterly retainer, twelve pounds ten, marked 'legal counsel.'*"

Hobb cleared his throat. It was a magistrate's clearing of the throat, the kind that preceded an objection in court, and I think he was surprised to find there was no bench for him here, no gavel, no deference waiting like a dog at heel. Only a judge in wet boots and a keeper's daughter holding her dead father's book.

"You are aware," he said, "that this document proves nothing. Anyone might write such a book."

"It's dated," said Merrow mildly. "Cross-referenced against harbor dues, bank drafts, and your own signature, which I have seen perhaps four hundred times on warrants and assize papers. You have a distinctive way of crossing your t's, Mr. Hobb. Like a man striking a match." He adjusted his spectacles. "Continue, Miss Vane."

Miss Vane. It was the first time anyone had said my name aloud — my true name — since the poster went up. *Wanted: Ruth Vane, accessory to treason, fifty pounds.* Hearing it now, in this room, from this dry little man with the fogging spectacles, it sounded less like a warrant and more like a christening.

I continued.

* * *

The beam turned. Lit, then dark. I read my father's careful columns into the dark and let the light confirm them, over and over, and somewhere in the second hour I stopped performing and simply read, and the room changed around me.

Sable stood by the south window with her arms folded, watching the sea rather than the prisoners, and I understood she was watching it for a different reason than habit. Out there, beyond the wreck-stones her signal had turned toward, the escort ship lay at anchor, and her boarding party was even now pulling through the swell toward us — six oars, a lantern shuttered, the justice's marines. Every entry I read bought them another minute of distance. That was the arithmetic under the reading. Hobb knew it too. I saw him do it behind his eyes: how long until the boat arrived, whether the storm would hold off the relief, whether a man with his money and his friends might yet buy back the night.

But he'd made one error, and it was sitting in the corner wrapped in a blanket, and his name was Aldous Pike.

* * *

Pike had been my father's clerk. I want to be fair about this, because fairness is the whole point of tonight, so let me be precise: Pike had been my father's clerk, his junior, the young man who fetched the ink and laughed at his jokes, and when Hobb had needed someone inside Vane's office to plant the forged bills and carry the whisper to the right ears, Pike had done it for thirty pounds and a sergeant's commission.

He had confessed it all in the stairwell, more or less, without meaning to — Sable told me later what he and Hobb had said within earshot of the lamp room stairs, about Kenji Nakamura and what had been done to him for knowing too much, spoken in the flat voices of men discussing drainage. But it was down in the tower's entryway, after the marines came, that Pike made his choice properly.

They had cuffed him alongside Hobb, and he had sat shivering in his soaked livery, and Hobb had looked at him once — just once, sideways, the look of a man calculating what a witness weighs versus what a witness costs — and something in Pike's face had gone out like a lamp with the oil spent.

"I'll speak," he said. To Merrow, not to Hobb. His voice was smaller than I remembered it; when I was nine he'd swung me round the customs yard by my wrists and called himself my poor relation. "For the crown. Full statement. Whatever protection's to be had."

"There's protection for honest witnesses," said Merrow, "and rope for dishonest ones. Choose which you'll be."

Pike chose. Of course he chose. Men like Pike always choose; that is the entire tragedy of them, and the entire use.

Now he sat in the corner and confirmed entries as I read them. "*Twelve pounds ten, Michaelmas quarter*—" "Paid, I collected it myself from his study, third drawer, under the sermons." "*Fifteen pounds to Sergeant Dawes, toll bridge*—" "Dawes takes it at the bridge, never in town, he's frightened of being seen near the magistrate's house." Each confirmation landed on Hobb like a stone laid on a grave — his own grave, dug in his presence, by the hands he'd paid to dig it.

At some point I realized Hobb had stopped watching the door and started watching Pike, and that the expression on his face was the loneliest thing I saw that night, and I include the sea in that accounting.

* * *

It was near midnight when I reached the page that mattered to me most, and I nearly couldn't read it.

The beam came round. Lit, then dark. In the lit half-second I saw Sable turn from the window. She had heard the date, I think, before I'd finished speaking it. Her face did nothing at all, which is how I knew.

"*October the eleventh,*" I read. "*K. Nakamura, keeper, Widow's Rock. Fifty pounds. Entered as 'chart correction fee.'*"

The wind took the tower and shook it like a dog shaking a rat. Nobody spoke. The beam swept round again, and in its light I watched Sable Nakamura stand very straight beside the window with fifty pounds of fiction hanging in the air between us, a bribe her husband had never touched, entered in a dead man's ledger to bind a living man's silence — and having failed to bind him, having been refused, having been *refused* —

"They killed him for saying no," Sable said. Not loudly. Almost conversationally, the way she said things that mattered. "Two years I thought the sea took him. The sea doesn't forge a winch cable."

"No," I said. "It doesn't."

She looked at Hobb then, and I am glad — truly glad, in the way one is glad of thunder at a safe distance — that I was not the object of that look. There was no grief left in it. Grief burns off, given enough time and enough truth, and what remains underneath is something older and cleaner and considerably more dangerous.

Hobb, to his credit or his ruin, held the look. "The man was a foreigner keeping a king's light," he said. "There were questions about him regardless. I resolved them."

"You wrote him into your book," said Sable, "and when he wouldn't be written, you cut him out of the world."

"The world," said Hobb, "is full of men who mistake their scruples for load-bearing walls."

"And you're finding out," said Merrow, without heat, "which ones hold."

* * *

We read until nearly two in the morning. My voice went hoarse around the shipping magnate's entries — Trennick, who moved contraband under legitimate manifest through three checkpoints, whose initials appeared forty-one times — and Sable brought tea without being asked, strong and bitter, and put it in my hands the way you'd reload a rifle. Once, passing behind Hobb's chair, she paused. Just paused. Long enough for him to feel the shadow of her across his shoulders. Then she moved on. It was the cruelest thing I saw anyone do that night, and I include the rocks.

Near the end, Merrow began making notes in a pocketbook, his pen scratching between the beam's rotations like some patient insect. "Trennick will require a separate warrant," he murmured. "Dawes at the bridge. The ferryman at Gray Mouth — no, he took no pay, he merely looked away; that's a caution, not a charge." He glanced up. "Your father indexed?"

"He was a customs clerk," I said. "Indexing was his religion."

"A good religion. Orderly. Forgiving of the paper and merciless about the facts." Merrow closed the pocketbook. "Miss Vane. How many copies exist of this ledger?"

"One," I said, and then, because lying badly runs in my family but honesty apparently does too: "One original. My father smuggled out transcriptions of key pages separately, in letters. I have those memorized. Some of them verbatim."

"Memorized," repeated Merrow, and something almost warm moved through the dry little landscape of his face. "Then the book can burn and the truth survives. That is the correct design of evidence, and I intend to say so at the hearing." He rose, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and crossed to the window where Sable stood. Outside, far down in the black, a lantern blinked twice — the boarding party's launch, coming round the headland at last. "Magistrate Hobb. You will go down to that boat, and thence to the customs hall at the harbor, where I shall hold a preliminary examination tomorrow — today, rather — God willing and the weather permitting. Your signature will be read to you. At length. I find repetition instructive."

Hobb stood. The cuffs caught the beam as it went round, flashing once, like a small insolent salute. "Justice Merrow," he said, and his voice had found its courtroom register again, smooth as varnish, "you should know that men of my standing have survived worse evenings than—"

"Yes," said Merrow. "That will be in the ledger too, I expect. Everything usually is."

* * *

They took them down the stair single file — Hobb first, two marines behind, then Pike with his blanket, hesitating at the door. He turned and looked up at me across the lamplit well of the stairwell, and for a moment he was nineteen again, swinging a child by the wrists in the customs yard, calling her his poor relation.

"He knew," Pike said. "Your dad. Near the end. He knew what I'd done, and he never once—" His jaw worked. "He asked if I needed anything. For my mother. That's the last thing he said to me before they took him. Asked after my mother."

"I know," I said, though I hadn't known, and do now, and will carry it.

"He'd have forgiven me," Pike said. "That's the worst of it. I could stand the rope easier than that."

Then the door closed behind him, and the wind came in around its edges, and the lamp room was ours again: Sable, the turning light, the ledger, and me.

I sat down on the chart table's bench and discovered my hands were shaking — late, useless, theatrical shaking, the body presenting its bill hours after the meal. Sable took the ledger from me gently, closed it, and set her palm flat on the cover as if quieting an animal.

"The dead man's hand," she said, "has finally had its say."

"It talked all night."

"Aye." She smiled — small, tired, real. "And it had a great deal to complain about, and it complained in perfect order, with dates." She looked at the closed book a moment longer. "Eleven days ago I pulled a drowned girl out of my rocks and wished her anywhere but my kitchen. Now I'd trust her with the last honest thing my husband—" She stopped. Corrected herself, deliberately, in front of me, in front of the turning light. "*My* husband. I'm done hiding him. Kenji Nakamura. Say it with me sometime. It's a good name. It's owed daylight."

"Kenji Nakamura," I said.

The beam swung wide, caught the rain on the glass, and threw it spinning across the ceiling like thrown rice — a wedding, I thought absurdly, the sky celebrating something — and below us, faint through the storm, the escort ship fired a gun. One report. Acknowledgment. Receipt of prisoner and evidence both.

Merrow reappeared in the doorway, cap in hand, spectacles fogged for the final time. "Miss Vane. Mrs.— Keeper Nakamura. The examination convenes at the harbor customs hall at nine o'clock. I'll want the fisherman who knew your husband. The woman Quill. The ferryman." He settled the cap on his head. "And I'll want you both, in your own names, telling it plain. Justice is a dull business, thank heaven. It has no use for disguises."

He went down. Sable blew out the stove and stood a moment in the rotating dark, and I saw her reach out and rest two fingers on the great lens as it came round past her, the way you'd touch the shoulder of an old horse before leaving the field.

"Nine o'clock," she said. "Then we'd best sleep, and mend our skirts, and walk into that hall like what we are."

"What are we?" I asked.

She considered it while the light went round — lit, then dark; lit, then dark.

"Witnesses," said Sable Nakamura at last. "Both of us. At long bloody last."
