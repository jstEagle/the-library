## 38. The Last Watch

The new keeper arrives on a Tuesday, which pleases me for no reason I can defend. Tuesdays are honest days. Nothing about them pretends to be anything else, and a man who comes to take charge of a lighthouse on a Tuesday is announcing that he intends no drama.

His name is Edmund Farrow. He is forty-some years old, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who have hauled things their whole lives, and he has the good sense to look frightened when he first sees Widow's Rock from the supply boat. That fright is the first qualification I check for. A keeper who isn't afraid of this rock is a keeper who will kill himself or someone else within the season.

"Mrs. Nakamura," he says, shaking my hand with both of his, which nearly swallows it whole.

"Sable," I say. "Just Sable now."

He blinks at that. Everyone does. The widow's black went into the sea two weeks ago — I threw it from the gallery myself, watched it spread out on the swell like a crow deciding against flight — and without it I am apparently a puzzle people can't quite assemble. A woman with a Japanese name and an English voice and no mourning and no story she's willing to shorten for anyone's comfort.

Farrow doesn't ask. He looks up at the tower instead, all one hundred and twelve feet of it, and says, "She's taller than the drawings."

"She lies about her height," I say. "Everything here does. Come inside before the tide turns. There's tea, and then there's work, and then — if you're still standing — there's everything you don't know yet."

* * *

I give him three days of routines before I trust him with the tower alone.

Not because he's slow. He's not. He writes everything down in a small leather book with a stub of pencil, asks each question once, and never asks it twice, which is rarer than you'd think among keepers. Most men either pretend to understand and drown later, or ask so many questions you begin to suspect them of collecting material for a ballad about your incompetence.

No — I take three days because the rock deserves it. Because Kenji took a month, and he was the finest hand with a lens I ever saw, and I have learned since his death exactly how long a month can be when you spend it waiting.

The first day: oil. How to test it, how to strain it through chamois when the weather's turned it cloudy, how much a cold night steals from the reservoirs that a warm ledger will deny. "The lamp drinks more than the books say it does," I tell him. "Log what you burn, not what you're supposed to burn. The Trinity House man will grumble either way. Better he grumbles at honesty than at darkness."

"The last keeper logged honestly?" Farrow asks, and there's something careful in it. He knows. Of course he knows. The whole coast knows by now what happened here — the storm, the signal, the magistrate's boat on the shoal, the assize, the pardon read aloud in a customs hall smelling of tar and herring. My name was in the newspapers for eleven days. Eleven days of being *the lighthouse widow*, printed in type too large for the truth to fit inside.

"The last keeper," I say, "logged one lie in fourteen years. Then spent a season paying for it." I let that sit. "We'll get to that."

The second day: the lens itself. I walk him around the great glass drum while it turns its slow daytime rotation, idle and gleaming, and show him how to listen to it. People think a light is seen. Wrong. A light is *heard* — in the clockwork's tick, in the whisper of the carriage wheels on the raceway, in the small complaints of mercury that mean friction where there should be float. Kenji taught me that. He used to close his eyes in the lamp room and diagnose the mechanism like a physician taking a pulse.

"Your predecessor had a gift for this machine," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than expected. "If the sound changes and you can't name it, stop the rotation and find out why. A dark minute is survivable. A light showing *wrong* — showing a false channel, a false flash character — that kills ships as surely as no light at all, and worse, because the masters trust it."

He nods and writes: *listen.*

The third day: the fog signal, the steam valve, the pressure gauge with the hairline scratch Kenji made beside the safe line so I could read it at a glance in the dark. I do not explain the scratch. Some inheritances shouldn't need narrating.

And on the evening of the third day, we trim the wick together, and I correct him, and it goes like this.

He's lit the lamp — a clean lighting, steady flame, no smoking — and I watch him reach for the trimmer with the confidence of a man who's done this at two other stations. He trims it round and even, textbook-perfect, the way the manual says.

"No," I say.

He looks up.

"You've trimmed it for the drawing-room." I take the trimmer from him. "This wick serves a lens, not a parlor. You want it slightly heavier on the seaward side — a hair, no more — because the draft comes off the water and leans the flame landward, and a leaning flame throws a thick arc and a thin one, and somewhere out there is a master reading your thin arc as his safe bearing." I make the adjustment. The flame stands straighter. Somewhere in the great drum behind us, the lens gathers it and begins to pour it out over the water in its slow white turn. "There. Now every degree weighs the same."

Farrow watches the beam sweep out across the darkening sea, and after a moment he says quietly, "Who taught you that?"

"A dead man taught me that."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be." I hand back the trimmer. "Be exact. That's how the living apologize to the dead here. Exactness. Everything else is flowers."

He takes it, and his grip is careful, and I think: *this one will do.* Not because he's brilliant. Because he wrote down *exactness* in his little book, underlined it twice, and didn't say anything sentimental about the sea or the lonely grandeur of the work. Sentimentality at a lighthouse gets people drowned.

* * *

On his fourth night, I send him up alone for the full watch while I sit in the kitchen below and listen to the clockwork through the ceiling. It ticks true. At midnight I climb anyway — old habit, and nobody's watching now to call it distrust — and find him standing at the rail, logbook open on the desk, watching the horizon the way you're supposed to: not staring but sweeping, quadrant by quadrant, patient as tide.

"Anything?"

"Fishing boat south by west, showing her lights proper. And a seal on the landing stage who objects to being counted as traffic."

"Note the seal. The inspectors love a bit of wildlife in the margins. Makes them feel the station is thriving."

He laughs, and it echoes strangely in the lantern, and I realize I haven't heard laughter in this room since — well. Since. The glass holds sounds the way it holds light; everything in here is remembered longer than you'd wish.

I leave him to it. On the stair, halfway down, I stop, because the thought arrives fully formed, the way important ones do:

*I could stay.*

Not as keeper — the transfer papers are signed, the Board has been remarkably gracious to the woman whose testimony toppled a magistrate, gracious in that particular official way that means they'd like very much for me to accept a comfortable post somewhere far away and stop being news. But I could stay in the cottage. Watch him work. Be the old woman on the rock who knows things, useful in emergencies, tolerated in calms.

I stand on those iron stairs and consider it honestly, which is more than I've managed some nights.

Then I hear, faint through the structure, Farrow's boots crossing the lamp room floor to check the mercury — thorough, unhurried, mine-no-longer — and I know the answer. A lighthouse with two keepers' hearts in it belongs to neither. Kenji taught me the light; I have taught Farrow the light; the teaching is done, and a thing fully taught must be fully released or it curdles into haunting.

Besides. Ruth is waiting in the harbor town with tickets bought and opinions packed, and the mail packet sails Thursday.

Thursday. Also an honest day.

* * *

My last morning, I write the final entry.

I've been dreading this the way you dread a confession you've already decided to make — knowing the words, fearing the weight. Fourteen years of logbooks sit in the cabinet behind me, and every one of them is true except for lines in one, and the truth I'm about to write has to be big enough to hold all of it.

Farrow is on the landing stage seeing off the supply boat's mate, who brought fresh bread and stared at me the entire visit with the frank rudeness of a man assembling a story to tell his wife. Let him. I'm done rationing myself into versions other people can swallow.

I open the logbook to the current page and dip the pen.

*October the 14th, 1899. Wind SW, freshening to gale after noon. Barometer unsteady. Lamp lit at 6:40 p.m., trimmed per station practice, burning well. Fog signal tested, in order.*

Then I stop writing like a keeper and start writing like myself.

*I, Sable Nakamura, born Sable Wren of Whitstable, keeper of this station these fourteen years, wife of Kenji Nakamura, keeper here before me, do record the following for whoever keeps this light after me.*

*On the night of September the 30th last, in the great storm, I showed this light upon the wreck-stones contrary to channel practice, and opened the fog signal in distress pattern, to bring His Majesty's escort vessel to aid and to confound Magistrate Corliss Hobb, who came to this tower with armed men to seize evidence of crimes he had committed. His pursuit boat struck the Widow's Rock shoal that same night. Men died. I did not steer them onto the rock, but I did not warn them off it, and I have made peace with the difference, mostly, on most days.*

*In the winter of '97 I entered a falsehood in this book, concealing the arrival of a survivor, Ruth Vane, to protect her life and the evidence she carried. It was the only lie I ever wrote in a station log, and it cost me more than any truth ever has, and I would write it again.*

*My husband, Kenji Nakamura, died on March the 3rd, 1897, in what was ruled an accident on the rocks below. It was murder. He was killed because he refused a bribe recorded in the Hobb ledger under his name — a bribe he never took, entered to bind him or bury him. He refused it because he was the most honest man ever to trim a wick, and because he believed a light should owe nothing to anyone but the sea. This station was his. I kept it for him. Whoever keeps it next: keep it for the sea, and for the ships, and for no one else. The day a light owes a man a favor is the day it starts killing them.*

*Final observation: the seal on the landing stage has pupped. Two pups, fat and insolent. Recommend the station continue to count them as traffic.*

I sign it. Full name. Both names, English and given, the whole improbable person, no armor anywhere in the ink.

My hand is steady until the last stroke, and then it isn't, briefly, and I let it shake, because there's no one in the room to perform steadiness for, and because fourteen years deserve one honest tremor at the end.

I close the book. The cabinet receives it with the others — spines worn, dates marching back through storms and calms and one murder and one miracle — and I rest my palm flat on the stack for a moment, saying goodbye the way you say it to graves: without expectation of reply.

When I come out, Farrow is waiting at the door with my single valise already carried down to the stage, and the morning is hard and clear, the kind of light that makes the water look newly invented.

"The pups are noted," he says solemnly.

"Good. That's the standard I'm holding you to."

He hesitates, then offers his hand again, both of his around mine. "Sable. What he said — your husband — about the light owing nothing." His ears go red. "Thank you. For teaching it to me straight."

I look past him at the tower, white against the blue, the lens turning inside its drum with its small patient tick, keeping its appointment with every horizon at once. Mine no more, and never really mine — only borrowed, the way everything beloved is.

"Mind the seaward trim," I say, because if I say anything else I'll disgrace us both.

The boat pulls away. I don't wave — waving is for people leaving something behind, and I find, sitting in the stern with the salt wind in my bare face, no black veil anywhere on me, that I'm not leaving anything behind at all. I'm carrying all of it forward: the glass and the grief and the ledger and the man who taught me to listen to machines, and a girl named Ruth who is standing on the harbor quay right now, presumably rearranging our itinerary by force of personality.

Ahead, the coast unrolls — the market town, the toll bridge, Gray Mouth, all the places that were gauntlets a month ago and are merely geography now. Behind me, Widow's Rock shrinks to a white tooth in the sea, and just as the bow swings toward the harbor, I see it happen: the sun dipping low enough that the lamp inside that distant drum will soon be lit by hands I trained, throwing its first careful arc across the darkening water.

Ruth meets me at the quay, takes one look at my face, and demands, "Well? Did you cry?"

"I wrote a logbook entry."

"That's worse," she says, and hooks her arm through mine, and steers me toward the packet and whatever a life looks like when you finally wear your own name in daylight.

The mail packet's whistle sounds. Somewhere behind us, on a rock in the gathering dusk, a light begins to turn.
