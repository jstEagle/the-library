## 39. Regiment and Uniform

**Sable**

I had worn black for six years. Long enough that the wool had memorized my shape — the way a uniform does, the way a regiment does. When I stood at the rail of the mail packet on the morning we sailed south, I was dressed in gray. Plain gray wool, nothing borrowed, nothing disguised, and it felt on my shoulders like a stranger's hand and like my own hand at once.

Ruth had bought it for me in the harbor town with the last of Marta Quill's kindness money, holding three bolts up to my face while the shopkeeper pretended not to know who we were, which was a courtesy nobody in that town had extended to me before.

"The black suits you," Ruth had said, "but you don't owe it anything anymore."

"I liked the black," I said, because some part of me had, the part that found armor comfortable, and honesty was the whole point of everything we'd done, so I might as well start practicing.

"You liked what it hid," she said. She was twenty-three and knew everything, the way twenty-three-year-olds do. "There's a difference."

The packet left on the morning tide with the storm season still growling offshore, but the harbor itself lay calm as poured pewter, and Widow's Rock stood out at the harbor mouth exactly where it had stood for all the years I'd hated it and loved it, which turned out to be the same years.

We'd said our goodbyes properly, which is to say awkwardly, two days before. The new keeper was a broad, earnest man named Hollis with salt-cracked hands and a habit of writing everything down twice, and I had walked him through the lamp room, the oil store, the fog signal's temperamental steam valve, correcting his trim of the wick three times until he got it right and looked wounded about it.

"It'll do," I told him finally.

"High praise," he said dryly, and I discovered I liked him, which made leaving easier and harder in equal measure.

Now, from the packet's deck, I watched his light come on. The sun wasn't down yet — the flare of the first watch is lit early in winter — but there it was, the great lens catching the low light and throwing it out in one long white breath across the water, sweeping, sweeping, finding us where we sat small and gray and civilian on the open sea.

Hollis would have polished it. I'd taught him that much if nothing else.

"Watching it won't keep it lit," Ruth said beside me.

"I know how it stays lit."

"I know you do. That's not what I mean." She pulled her coat tighter against the spray. Her hair was loose now, unbound since the assize, since the moment a clerk read the quashing of her father's conviction aloud and she walked out of that customs hall a free woman with a name again — Ruth Vane, daughter of Elias Vane, pardoned posthumously, his letters vindicated, his ledger entered into evidence under seal. "I mean you've been keeping other people's lights so long you've forgotten you're allowed to be a passenger."

I thought about arguing. I had gotten good at arguing with her over eleven days and one trial and one long winter of shared rooms, and I usually lost, which she counted as a virtue in me.

Instead I watched the light sweep past us again, and I let it.

* * *

**Ruth**

Here is what nobody tells you about being cleared: it takes an astonishing amount of paperwork.

For four days after the hearing, Ruth Vane did not exist. She existed in the ledger, in the warrant Hobb had signed with his own treacherous hand, in the poster still curling on village windows fifty pounds richer for her capture. But the clerk's ink saying so took its time drying, and meanwhile I sat in a harbor boarding house reading my father's confession for the ninth time and learning him by heart.

He wrote well, my father. He wrote the way he'd spoken — careful, a little formal, with jokes buried so deep you'd miss them unless you were looking. He'd written to me every week from prison, letters I'd never received because Pike intercepted them, and when they were returned to me at last in a government envelope I read them all in one night while Sable sat up with me pretending to mend a net she had no intention of mending.

*My dear Ruth,* the last one began. *If you are reading this, then someone has been braver than I deserved. I have spent a long time imagining who that might be. In my better imaginings, she is stubborn.*

"He got you right," I told Sable later.

"He flatters me," she said. "I'm worse than stubborn. I'm punctual."

But that was the winter's arithmetic, and this was the packet's deck, and the packet was taking us south — south to London, south to a publisher my father's solicitor had found, south to a small house in Islington with a garden too narrow for anything but herbs and a spare room Sable had not yet admitted she was going to take.

She would take it. I'd bet the reward money on it, and I'd earned that money honestly, or as honestly as anyone earns anything off Magistrate Corliss Hobb, who was sitting in a cell in the county gaol waiting for spring assizes and reportedly complaining about the tea.

On deck, Sable stood at the rail watching the Rock's light turn, and I watched her watch it, and I understood something I'd been circling all winter: she wasn't grieving the tower. She'd given the tower up clean, the way you set down a weight you've carried so long your spine has reshaped around it. What she was doing was checking. Counting the flashes. Making sure the world she'd built still worked without her.

It did. Four seconds dark, one flash white. Hollis kept a trim wick.

"He'll do," she said, as if answering my thoughts, which by then she could mostly do.

"He'll do."

"And in ten years he'll falsify a logbook entry for some half-drowned stranger, and it will ruin him beautifully."

"In ten years," I said, "you'll be telling this story at dinner parties and boring everyone."

"I have never bored anyone in my life."

"No," I agreed. "You haven't. It's very annoying."

* * *

**Sable**

The truth about belonging, which I learned late and paid full price for, is that it isn't a place. I had spent six years believing it was a place — that Widow's Rock held it, that if I kept the lamp trimmed and the log honest and my husband's name folded away like mourning clothes in summer, then belonging would stay put and keep me. It didn't work. Belonging is not a lighthouse. It moves. It goes where you go or it doesn't go at all, and for six years it hadn't gone anywhere with me because I hadn't gone anywhere at all.

Kenji had known this. He used to say it in his careful English, turning each word over like a stone on the beach: *A keeper keeps the light, Sable. Not himself.* I thought it was about humility. It was about luggage.

On the second day out, the weather came up from the southwest the way weather does, and the packet pitched and rolled, and most of the passengers went below to be miserable in the respectable dark of the saloon. Ruth and I stayed on deck because we were, by then, the sort of women who found bad weather companionable. We'd been introduced to worse and hadn't liked it as much.

A steward — young, freckled, seasick himself in the stoic way of stewards — brought us tea laced with enough brandy to qualify as medicine and lingered longer than tea required.

"Begging pardon, ma'am," he said to me. "But you're the Widow's Rock keeper, aren't you? The one from the papers."

I had been in the papers. That was still strange to say, even inside my own head. *Widow Keeper Exposes Coastal Ring* — the London dailies had made a meal of it, and made up half of it, and spelled Nakamura wrong in three different ways, which would have amused Kenji into next week.

"I was the keeper," I said.

"They're saying you turned the light onto the wreck-stones on purpose." His eyes were wide. "During the storm. To bring the judge's ship in safe and run Hobb's boat aground. They're saying no keeper's done that since—" He stopped, embarrassed.

"Since a very long time ago," I finished. "They're saying right. It's an old signal. Any captain worth his ticket knows it."

"Wasn't it against regulations?"

I looked at him over the rim of my cup. "Young man, I have recently learned what happens to people who are very strict about regulations. Would you like to hear?"

He laughed, decided he hadn't wanted the answer after all, and went below with his tray.

Ruth was grinning at me through the spray. "You're going to be insufferable in London."

"Almost certainly."

"Everyone will want the story."

"Then everyone will get it, and it will be accurate, which will disappoint them terribly."

* * *

**Ruth**

The book was her idea before it was mine, though neither of us said so at the time. Somewhere in Marta Quill's kitchen, drying the ledger page by page over peat smoke, Sable had looked up and said, "Someone should write this down properly. All of it. The parts the papers will get wrong."

And I had said, "I write letters," which was true and insufficient, and she had said, "Then learn," in the tone she used for trimming wicks, and that was that.

By the time we boarded the packet I had a satchel full of drafts — my father's letters, the confession, the ledger entries copied fair, my own account of eleven days written and burned and rewritten. The publisher's letter was in my pocket, agreeing to look at *a narrative of recent events on the northern coast*, which was the only title we could agree on that didn't sound like a melodrama.

Sable had vetoed *The Lighthouse Conspiracy*. ("No.") She had vetoed *The Widow of Widow's Rock*. ("Absolutely not. I am not a widow anymore; I'm just early-retired from marriage.") She had tolerated *Regiment and Uniform*, my father's phrase, from a letter I'd never received until it was too late for him to see me read it:

*They gave me a number and a uniform when I came to this country, and I have spent thirty years learning which parts of myself are regiment and which are mine. Keep yours, my dear. Wear what belongs to you openly. A hidden name is only half a life.*

On the third day out, with the coast long sunk behind us, she asked to see the letter. I handed it over and watched her read it standing at the rail with the wind pulling at her gray sleeves, and when she finished she folded it along my creases and gave it back and said nothing for a long while.

"He wrote like a man packing a bag," she said finally. "Everything chosen. Nothing wasted."

"Yes."

"I hid his name for six years and I told myself it was to protect the memory. It wasn't. It was to protect me. He'd have hated it." She said it steadily, without drama, the way she logged facts. "The fisherman, Aldren — he said his name on the dock at the hearing. Out loud. Kenji Nakamura. And half the harbor turned to look, and nobody flinched, and I stood there like a fool realizing I could have had that for six years."

"You couldn't have," I said. "Not then. You know that."

"I know it cost what it cost." She turned back to the rail, to the gray water, to the country we'd left. "I'm just saying the price was higher than the goods."

* * *

**Sable**

That evening the sky cleared in the west, and near dusk the packet passed within sight of a headland light — not ours, a southern one, a stranger's — and I caught myself counting its character automatically, the way you count another woman's children at church. Flash every five seconds. Fixed red above. A tidy establishment. Somebody's life's work, burning on schedule whether anybody watched or not.

Ruth appeared beside me with two cups of the medicinal brandy-tea, which by day three we had stopped pretending was for seasickness.

"To the regiment," she said, raising hers.

"To the uniform," I answered, because we'd been drinking to my dead husband's phrases all winter and it seemed only right to finish the set.

She clinked her cup against mine. Below us the engine turned over steady as a heartbeat, and ahead of us London waited with its publishers and its lawyers and its spring, and behind us a light I no longer tended swept its patient circle over water I no longer guarded.

And here is the thing I would have told Kenji, if the dead take post, and what I set down now because Ruth tells me a narrator owes her reader at least one plain sentence: I had spent six years wearing grief like a uniform issued to me — regulation black, correct buttons, proper mourning — and it had fit badly the entire time, the way issued things do. The gray dress Ruth picked out fit perfectly. Not because it was finer. Because I'd chosen it.

Belonging, it turns out, is not something the sea gives back. It's something you finally stop refusing.

The headland light flashed. Five seconds. Flash.

"Come below before you freeze," Ruth said. "You can tell the steward your story. He looks like he'd believe anything."

"Everyone believes anything," I said, following her down. "The trick is making them believe the truth."

The stairs took us into the lamplit warmth of the ship, and somewhere astern of us, small as a struck match, Widow's Rock burned on into the dark without me — kept now, and keeping, and none of it mine, and all of it, somehow, home.
