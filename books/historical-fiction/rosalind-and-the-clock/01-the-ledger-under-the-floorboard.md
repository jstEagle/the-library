## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The floorboard came up with a sound like a held breath being released.

Xanthe Iversen knelt in the dark of his surgery, coat still damp from the evening's rounds, and lifted the plank he had loosened and refitted so many times over twenty years that the nails had learned to slide without complaint. Beneath it lay a cavity between the joists, lined with oilcloth, and in that cavity — wrapped now in three layers of waxed linen against the damp — was the ledger.

He did not take it out every night. That was the discipline of it. A man who checks his secrets too often begins to need them checked, the way an itch becomes a wound. But some nights demanded it, and tonight was one of them, because tomorrow the last cart would cross the bridge, and after that the river would close like a book, and whatever was written inside that book would be read by no one until spring.

Except, perhaps, by him.

He drew the bundle out and set it on his knees. The surgery smelled of carbolic and lamp oil and the ghost of every illness the town had ever brought to his door: the influenza winter of '07, the typhoid summer, the slow cancers and quick accidents. Above him, through the ceiling, he could hear the house settling — the tick of cooling pipes, the wind working at the north shutters. Below him, beneath his own two knees, the river ran black and building its ice.

He unwrapped the first layer of linen.

The ledger itself was nothing to look at. An army field ledger, standard issue, its canvas cover gone grey-green with mould and handling, its spine stitched twice where it had split. Any quartermaster in any garrison in the country had handled a hundred identical books. It was what was *in* it that made Xanthe's hands go careful, the way they went careful over a wound that had never properly closed.

*Omitted names.* He had begun keeping them in the autumn of 1916 — or rather, he corrected himself as he always did, he had begun writing down what Aldous Vane ordered struck out. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole architecture of his conscience. Vane had commanded that names be omitted from the casualty reports; Xanthe had merely recorded the omissions, in his own small hand, in a book that officially did not exist, listing men who officially had not died, from wounds that officially had not been suffered, at a crossroads called Verchères Ridge that officially cost the battalion eleven men when it had in truth cost thirty-one.

Thirty-one minus eleven was twenty. Twenty families who had received no telegram, no pension, no grave, no grief that anyone official recognized. Twenty names in a dead officer's hand — Edmund Vane's hand — copied out by a dying man while Xanthe held the pen steady for him because the tremor had gotten too bad.

Edmund had dictated. Xanthe had written. That division of labour had seemed, at nineteen, almost holy. Now it felt like something he had been carrying in his chest ever since, a swallowed coal that never quite went cold.

He turned pages he did not need to read. He knew them the way he knew his own pulse. *Harrow, T., private, died of wounds received 14 October, report states returned to duty.* *Okafor, J., corporal, killed instantaneously, report states invalided home, discharged.* On and on, twenty entries, each one a small act of forgery committed by a colonel against his own dead, and beside each entry, in Xanthe's later additions, the evidence that proved the lie: field dressing station tallies, ambulance manifests, a chaplain's burial list, the sworn note of a stretcher-bearer who had since drowned himself in a canal in Ghent because, Xanthe suspected, the same silence had gotten into him like water into timber.

Twenty years. The war ended, the men came home or didn't, and Aldous Vane had come home to this river town as its benefactor — its saviour, if you asked the town council, which everyone did, because Vane had built the new wing of the cottage hospital and paid for the schoolhouse roof and endowed the winter coal fund, and a town that freezes for five months a year does not look too closely at the hands that warm it.

And Xanthe had said nothing. For twenty years he had said nothing, treating Vane's gout, drinking Vane's sherry, accepting Vane's patronage with a smile he had practiced until it stopped costing him anything visible.

He had told himself it was prudence. Who would believe a town nurse against a colonel? Against a man whose name was on the hospital lintel? And there was no proof that would survive scrutiny — the original reports were locked in regimental archives, and a copyist's word against a benefactor's reputation was a candle against a glacier. Prudence. Caution. The virtues of the survivor.

But he knew the truer name for it, and the true name was fear, and fear had a taste he knew better than carbolic.

* * *

He heard the cart before he saw the light of it.

It came up Mill Road slow, iron-rimmed wheels grinding the first rime off the cobbles, and Xanthe rose stiffly from the floorboard — the ledger rewrapped, reburied, the plank reseated, all of it done in the time it took a man to decide whether to answer his door — and went to the window.

Old Pieter Lund with his produce cart, lantern swinging, a single sack of late turnips aboard. Coming back from the last market run before the bridge shut. Pieter pulled up at Xanthe's gate and did not come in; he just sat there a moment, looking at the surgery windows, then raised a hand — not a wave, exactly. More a salute to a lighthouse.

Xanthe opened the door. "Trouble?"

"Rough ice coming," Pieter said, in the tone other men used for weather. "Ferryman says the channel's skimming already. You'll want your stores laid in."

"They are."

"And the garrison." Pieter lowered his voice without lowering it enough to matter; there was no one else on the street, but the habit was the town's habit. "They've doubled the watch. New faces. Colonel's orders. Men at the tollhouse day and night till the thaw."

Xanthe felt the coal shift in his chest. "Doubled. Whatever for? Nobody crosses a closed bridge."

"Nobody crosses an open one either, half the time," Pieter said, and laughed his dry laugh, and clicked his tongue at the horse, and the cart ground away toward the square, leaving Xanthe standing in his doorway with the cold getting at his ankles and the question sitting in him like a splinter.

Doubled the watch. At the bridge, and at the ferry crossing below it, which had been useless for years — the ferry boat hauled up and chained every November. Men posted at crossings nobody used, guarding against nobody, unless the colonel expected somebody.

Unless the colonel was afraid of something.

Or someone.

* * *

He slept badly, which was usual, and dreamed of Verchères Ridge, which was less usual lately. In the dream the ridge was green — it had been green once, absurdly, before the shells took the trees — and the wounded lay in rows among the grass and none of them would tell him their names, and when he leaned close to the nearest boy's face he saw it was Edmund Vane, aged nineteen forever, saying *write it down, nurse, write it down* in a voice going quieter with every word, and Xanthe wrote and wrote until the pen tore through the page and the page was the floorboard and he woke with his hand pressed flat against the planks beside his bed, as if checking the floor were solid, as if checking the whole house were solid.

Dawn came grey and late, the way it did now, the sun barely clearing the eastern hills before it began thinking about setting again. Xanthe lit the stove, put the kettle on, and opened the surgery for what the town called the morning queue: chilblains, coughs, a child with a boil, Widow Marsh with her heart drops, the cooper's apprentice who had caught his thumb in a vice and needed it looked at again because he kept picking at it.

Ordinary work. Ordinary day. He let the ordinariness run over him like warm water, and underneath it, all morning, the thought moved like a fish under ice:

*Last cart today.*

By four o'clock the light was failing blue. By five the lamplighter was making his rounds along the bridge road, and Xanthe stood in his doorway watching the last traffic of the season come up off the span: two wagons, a mail sleigh, a knot of garrison soldiers walking in loose formation with their collars up, and behind them, far back in the murk, one more vehicle that might have been a trap or might have been shadow.

He counted the soldiers. Fourteen. Yesterday there had been seven.

"Evening, Nurse Iversen." Sergeant Brandt, the garrison's senior man, touching his cap as the column passed. A decent sort, Brandt; Xanthe had sewn up his hand two winters ago after a barroom argument with a bottle. "Cold one coming."

"So I hear. You'll be busy, by the look of it."

"Colonel wants the crossing watched till the ice takes proper." Brandt said it easily, a man repeating orders he saw no sense in. "Anyone asks, we're preventing smuggling."

"Smuggling what? Turnips?"

Brandt grinned. "That's what I asked, sir, and got a look I didn't care for. Well. Good night to you." He hurried after his men, boots ringing on the frost-hard cobbles, and Xanthe stood a moment longer in the doorway, feeling the evening press in around the little pool of lamplight, and understood — with the calm certainty of a man who has diagnosed a great many things — that the colonel was afraid.

Vane did not double watches for turnips. Vane doubled watches the way he falsified ledgers: precisely, quietly, and only when something threatened him. Twenty years of silence, and now, in the first hard week of winter, with the river about to become a wall — now of all seasons — the garrison grew eyes.

Something was coming across that bridge. Or trying to.

* * *

That night he took the ledger out again.

This was against his own discipline, and he knew it, and he did it anyway. He knelt in the dark with the lamp turned low and lifted the plank and unwrapped the book and simply looked at it, the way you look at a letter you have not yet decided to answer.

Twenty years of silence. Twenty years of sherry and gout consultations and charitable subscriptions and *your service honours us, Colonel*. Twenty years of the swallowed coal. And he had made a promise — God, the promise — kneeling in the mud of a dressing station with a dying lieutenant's hand gripping his sleeve, and he had answered it the way young men answer dying men, instantly and absolutely and without any idea of the weight of what he agreed to.

*I'll see it done, sir. I swear it.*

And then he had buried the promise under a floorboard for twenty years.

He was not an old man yet — forty-three, sound of body except for the knee that ached before weather — but he felt old tonight, kneeling on cold boards with a dead man's handwriting in his lap, because he could feel something ending. Not the silence itself; silences don't end, they erode. But the *ease* of it, the comfortable weight of it, was lifting, the way a fever lifts — and a fever lifting can mean recovery or it can mean the crisis turning.

The doubled watch. The ferry posting. Vane's long fingers reaching even into this, tightening even now.

Xanthe wrapped the ledger back in its linen, lowered it into the earth-smelling dark between the joists, and seated the plank above it with the heel of his hand. Then he sat back on his heels and listened.

Wind at the shutters. The stove ticking. Far off, down by the river, the muffled tread of a sentry walking his post on the frozen approach to the bridge — walking, pausing, walking again, patient as a clock.

And beneath all of it, fainter than any of those sounds, another sound entirely: the river, deep under its new skin of ice, still moving. Still going somewhere. Ice could hold a town prisoner for five months, but it always broke in the end. Everything froze, and everything thawed, and what floated downstream in the thaw was never only water.

Xanthe banked the fire, trimmed the lamp, and went up to bed.

He had just blown out the candle when the dog at the tannery began to bark — not the idle barking of a cold night, but the flat, continuous alarm of an animal that has seen something on the road. Xanthe stood in the dark with his hand on the cold window latch and listened to it go on and on, out there past the last lamps, past the tollhouse, somewhere out on the bridge road where fourteen soldiers were watching for smugglers and turnips.

Then the barking stopped, all at once, the way it does when someone quiets it.

And a moment later, very faintly, carried on the north wind, he heard footsteps running — light ones, uneven ones, stumbling — coming toward the town.
