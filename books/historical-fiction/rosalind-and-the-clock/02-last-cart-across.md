## 2. Last Cart Across

The frost came down from the hills overnight and by morning the river wore its first white collar of ice along the banks, thin as spun sugar, breaking apart in the current like something the water was trying to swallow. I stood at my surgery window with a cup of tea going cold in my hand and watched it happen, because every year I watched it happen, and every year the watching did not get easier.

The Marlowe Bridge would close at sundown.

It closed every winter — had closed for as long as the town had stood here, since before the war, since before I came. The engineers who built it had never accounted for the ice floes that ground against the pilings from November to March, and so each autumn the tollkeeper walked the span with a red lantern, and each spring he walked it again to open it, and in between we were an island. Nine hundred souls, one road out, and that road shut like a book.

Twenty years ago I had learned what it meant to be trapped in a place with a man who could do anything he liked. I had promised myself I would never feel it again. And yet here I stood, counting the hours.

* * *

By nine o'clock they were already coming. They always came on closing day — the ones who'd put off their journeys too long, the ones who hoped the tollkeeper's lantern was only ceremony, the ones who wanted nothing more than to cross before the crossing ended, as if the act itself were a kind of medicine.

The first was Mrs. Ellery, whose lungs were failing her by inches each winter, come for a bottle of the cod-liver oil I mixed with honey and a spoonful of brandy she pretended not to notice. She sat in my chair by the stove and talked about her son in Manchester while I wrapped the bottle in sacking.

"You'll look in on me when the snow comes deep, Mr. Iversen?"

"I'll look in on you whether it comes deep or shallow, Mrs. Ellery."

"That's what you said last year." She smiled with most of her teeth. "And you kept to it. There's not many things in this town a body can count on, but you're one."

I said something modest and saw her out, and the thing she'd said sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. *There's not many things a body can count on.* Twenty years of being counted on. Twenty years of being the man who came, who looked in, who mixed the oil and set the bone and sat through the long fevers. It was a good life. It was a life built deliberately, plank by plank, over the pit of what I had done — or rather, over what I had failed to do, which is a different thing entirely, though the pit is the same depth either way.

* * *

The travelers came all morning. A drover with a gashed forearm where his horse had kicked, stitched and bandaged and grumbling that he'd miss the winter market at Harrowgate. Two sisters, elderly, traveling to their niece's for the season, both of them complaining of their hearts in the way elderly women complain of hearts — that is, constantly, and never quite fatally. A young man with a cough I didn't like the sound of at all; I told him flatly he should stay the winter rather than cross into the city with those pipes, and he thanked me and crossed anyway, because young men always do.

And then, just past noon, the soldiers began to arrive.

They came across the bridge in column, two files abreast, boots ringing on the iron plating in a rhythm I knew the way I knew my own pulse. Garrison blues, service caps, kit bags slung. I stood in my doorway and counted them past — twelve, twenty-six, forty — and they kept coming until there were better than sixty, marching up the high street toward the old customs house that Colonel Aldous Vane had converted into barracks six years ago, with his own money and his own insistence, for the good of the town, he'd said. For its protection. For the honor of having a standing garrison in a river town that had seen no enemy but weather in a hundred years.

Sixty men. Last winter there had been thirty.

I mentioned it to Tom Quill, the farrier, when he brought his cart around to collect a poultice for his mare's hoof.

"Vane's doubled his complement," I said.

Tom shrugged the shrug of a man who has decided not to think about a thing. "Colonel says there's talk of smugglers on the ice road. Runners going over to Harrowgate with untaxed goods." He laughed. "Smugglers. On our river. What's there to smuggle out of Ashcombe — turnips?"

"What indeed," I said.

But I watched the soldiers take up positions as the afternoon went on, and I did not laugh. Two at the ferry landing, where the ferryman had already hauled his boat above the waterline for the season. One pair posted at the head of the bridge itself, beside the tollhouse, where no post had ever been needed before — the tollkeeper closed the gates with a padlock and a chain, and that had always been enough. Another pair walking the river path below the mill. And at dusk, when I made my rounds to Mrs. Ellery's and back, I saw the lamplight burning in the customs house windows, row upon row, like a ship at anchor riding out a storm it alone believed was coming.

* * *

At four o'clock the last cart came.

I heard it before I saw it — wheels on frozen mud, and the particular complaint of a horse being driven hard after too many miles. It came up the high street at a trot: a farm cart, canvas sheeted over its bed, drawn by a bay gelding lathered gray with sweat despite the cold, driven by a woman I did not know, alone.

She reined in outside my surgery without appearing to decide to. The horse stood trembling. So, when she turned to look at me, did she.

She was perhaps thirty, tall, with dark hair escaping a wool cap in damp ropes, and a face that might have been handsome if it had slept recently or eaten well, and it clearly had done neither. Her coat was good quality once — good cloth, good stitching — but travel-worn now, hemmed with road dust, one glove missing entirely so that her right hand, holding the reins, was bare and chapped raw.

"Is this the doctor's?" she asked. Her voice was roughened by wind.

"I'm the nurse. Mr. Iversen. Dr. Prynne keeps rooms in Harrowgate half the year; the other half, people make do with me."

"Then you're the one." Something moved through her expression — relief, calculation, I couldn't say which — and then she seemed to catch herself and smooth it away, the way one closes a curtain. "My horse cast a shoe two miles back. I need him seen to before morning."

"There's a farrier three doors down. Tom Quill. He's sober until at least six."

"Yes." She didn't move. Her eyes went past me to the surgery window, to the light inside, and stayed there a moment too long. Then, quieter: "And if it were a person needing seeing to. Not a horse."

"Then I'd see to them," I said. "Closing day or any day. That doesn't stop with the bridge."

"No," she said softly. "No, I don't imagine it does. Not for you."

There was a strange weight in the way she said it — *for you* — as though she knew me, as though my reputation had traveled further than I'd ever supposed it could. I told myself it was nothing. Country nurses are known in country towns; kindness gets about the way gossip does.

"You've come far," I said. "You won't cross before the gates shut, driving at that pace. Where were you bound?"

"Across," she said. Just that. Then, gathering herself: "Or not. It hardly matters now, does it. The river shuts, and everything inside it stays."

She gathered the reins. The bay horse blew and stamped. And for a moment neither of us moved, and the wind came down the street from the direction of the bridge, carrying the smell of ice and iron, and I felt again that old sensation I have never found a better word for than *the wall* — the sense of the river rising up invisible between Ashcombe and the rest of the world, nine hundred people sealed in a jar, and one man holding the lid.

"You'll want lodging," I heard myself say. "The Ferry House will be full of garrison men by nightfall. Try the widow Crane's, on Chapel Lane. Tell her I sent you."

Something flickered in her face — gratitude, or its twin, caution. "Thank you, Mr. Iversen. Perhaps I'll do that."

She clicked her tongue and the cart rolled on, up the street, past the customs house where the lamplight burned in ranks, past the head of the bridge where the new-posted soldiers watched her pass with the idle attention of men paid to watch everything. I stood in my doorway until she turned onto Chapel Lane and was gone, and I noticed — because it was my trade to notice — that she had favored her left side getting down from the seat. Stiffly. Carefully. As a person moves when something under their clothes hurts them.

I told myself it was a pulled muscle. Riding stiffens anyone. I told myself a great many things that afternoon, and believed none of them, and the telling did no good whatsoever.

* * *

The tollkeeper walked the bridge at sunset with his red lantern, as he had done every November for longer than I had lived in Ashcombe. A small crowd gathered at the head of the street to watch, because watching is what this town does instead of speaking. The chain came rattling off its hook. The gates swung inward, groaning on hinges gone stiff with the first frost. Old Hobbes the tollkeeper locked them together and hung the lantern on the gatepost, where it would burn all winter as a signal — *closed, closed, closed* — visible clear across the water to the Harrowgate shore, where no one would see it.

The crowd dispersed quickly after that. Cold drives people home faster than grief does. Within ten minutes the high street was empty except for the soldiers' patrols and the drifting smoke from chimneys, standing straight up in the still air like columns holding up nothing.

I locked my surgery and walked the long way home along the river path, because I am a fool in certain reliable ways and one of them is that I go looking at the wall.

The ice had thickened along the banks since morning. Out in the channel the current still ran black and fast, grinding the floes, but the edges were sealing themselves shut seam by seam, and in three weeks — four, if the winter ran mild — even the channel would freeze solid, and then there would be the ice road, and carts would cross where the bridge could not, and for a few weeks each year Ashcombe would have two roads out instead of one.

That was how it had always been. That was how it was supposed to be.

But tonight, standing at the rail above the water with my breath smoking and my hands in my pockets, I looked downstream and saw the two soldiers Vane had posted at the ferry landing, stamping against the cold, their lantern throwing a small yellow circle on the mud. Watching the bank. Watching the ice forming. Watching the second road before it even existed.

Nobody posts guards on a road that isn't there yet. You post guards on a road you're afraid someone will use.

I thought of the woman in the cart, favoring her left side. I thought of the way she'd said *for you*, as though reading from a page I hadn't shown her. I thought of Edmund Vane, dead these twenty years, and the promise I made him with his blood cooling under my hands — *see it done, Xanthe, see it done* — and how I had buried that promise under twenty years of cod-liver oil and set bones and being counted on, plank by careful plank, over the pit.

The ledger was under the third floorboard from the hearth, beneath the loose board I could lift with a knife blade in four seconds. I had not opened it in a year. Its names waited down there in the dark the way the dead wait, which is to say patiently, which is to say forever, unless somebody doesn't.

I walked home through streets gone blue with dusk, past the customs house blazing with lamplight, past the sentries at the bridgehead who nodded to me with the courtesy of men who had orders to know every face in town. Colonel Vane's compliments, they would have said, if asked. His money built the barracks. His charity clothed the poor children at the mission school. His name was carved over the almshouse door and printed in gold on the flyleaf of every hymnal in St. Bride's.

A benefactor. A father to the town.

The river was becoming a wall, and the man who owned the wall had doubled his watch, and somewhere behind me on Chapel Lane a stranger with soldier's stiffness in her left side was asking the widow Crane for a room.

I reached my house and stood a moment in the doorway without lighting the lamp, listening to the wind come off the water, and I understood — with the calm, unhurried certainty of a man who has felt this exact dread once before, twenty years ago, in a field hospital lit by guttering candles — that the winter was not going to be quiet.

That same night, near midnight, when the frost had laid its silver hand over every window in Ashcombe, someone knocked at my surgery door.

Three knocks. Soft. Then, after a silence long enough for me to reach for the knife I keep by the floorboard, three more — weaker than the first, as though the hand making them was losing its argument with the cold.

I lit the lamp. I went to the door.
