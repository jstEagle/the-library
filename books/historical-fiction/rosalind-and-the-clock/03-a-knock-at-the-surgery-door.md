## 3. A Knock at the Surgery Door

The last night before the bridge closed was the kind of night that made a man want to bar his door and pretend the world had ended early.

Xanthe Iversen sat alone in his surgery with a single lamp turned low and a pot of tea going cold at his elbow. Outside, the wind had come down off the northern hills carrying snow in long grey banners, and it flung itself against the windows in gusts that sounded like hands demanding entry. The river beneath the town had gone black and restless, its ice still young and unreliable, and every so often he heard it groan — a low, tectonic complaint, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

He had spent the day doing what he always did on the final day: closing accounts, restocking shelves against the months of isolation, saying goodbye to travelers who would not return until thaw. Mrs. Petchett from the milliner's had brought him a tin of ginger biscuits and a look of pity he pretended not to notice. The ferryman's boy had come for liniment. And twice — twice — soldiers from the garrison had passed his window without hurrying, their collars up, their eyes on the street rather than the weather. The garrison had doubled since autumn. Everyone said it was routine, a winter posting, but everyone said it too quickly, the way people repeat a lie they've been given by someone important.

Xanthe poured himself more tea he didn't want and let his gaze drift, as it drifted most nights now, to the third floorboard to the left of the examination table. A board like any other. Scuffed oak, a knot near one edge shaped vaguely like a bird in flight. He had pulled it up perhaps forty times in twenty years, and each time his heart performed the same small treacherous stumble, as though the ledger beneath it were not paper but something alive, something that could be heard breathing if a man stood quietly enough.

*Twenty names,* he thought. *No. Twenty-one.*

He had added the twenty-first only this autumn — a boy named Corrigan whose death notice had never appeared anywhere at all, who had simply ceased to exist between one muster roll and the next. Xanthe had learned of him from a letter forwarded through three hands, written by a mother in Carlisle who refused to believe her son had never lived.

The wind struck the surgery hard enough to rattle the lamp glass. Xanthe looked up, listening.

Beneath the wind — or perhaps carried on it, distorted, made doubtful — came another sound. A knocking. Not at the front of the house but at the side door, the surgery entrance, the door patients used when they were ashamed or desperate or bleeding.

It came again. Weak. Irregular. The knock of someone who was spending strength she could not afford.

Xanthe rose slowly. Twenty years of careful living had built certain reflexes into him, and the first of them was *never open your door at night without knowing who stands behind it*. He crossed the room on quiet feet, lifted the lamp, and put his eye to the narrow gap beside the curtain.

At first he saw nothing but snow — a churned white smear where the lamplight reached, and beyond it darkness folding back into darkness. Then the smear moved. Resolved. Became a woman on her knees at the bottom of his steps, one hand braced against the doorframe, her hood crusted white, her face a grey oval with two dark eyes that were already losing their focus.

She saw the light move behind the glass. Her mouth opened.

"Please," she said, and the word was nearly torn away whole by the wind. "I was told — the nurse — please—"

Then her hand slipped from the frame and she went sideways into the snowbank, and Xanthe was no longer thinking about caution at all.

* * *

He got her inside by main strength, dragging her across the threshold while the storm poured in after them, and he kicked the door shut with his heel and got the bolt home. She weighed almost nothing, or seemed to; her coat was soaked through and frozen stiff in places, crackling like old parchment as he hauled it off her. Her lips were the color of slate. When he pressed two fingers to her throat he found a pulse running fast and shallow, a bird's pulse, a pulse that was burning its own fuel to stay ahead of the cold.

He worked without stopping to think, because thinking was a luxury for later and later might not come. Blankets from the warming cupboard. The big kettle filled and set on the ring. Hot water bottles wrapped in flannel tucked against her armpits and groin, nowhere bare skin, nothing scalding. He chafed her hands, wrapped them, spoke to her in the low continuous voice he used for men coming out of ether — nonsense words, reassurance words, words whose meaning mattered less than their rhythm.

Her eyes fluttered. Found his face. Held it with an effort that seemed to cost her everything she had left.

"You're him," she whispered. "You're Iversen."

"I'm the nurse," he said. "Whoever told you about me did you a kindness, but they should have told you to wait for morning."

"There is no morning." Her teeth were chattering so hard the words came out in pieces. "There's no — tomorrow for me unless — you listen—"

"Later." He eased her flat. "First we get you warm. Then we get you mended. Then you can talk my ear off until spring, if you like."

Something moved behind her eyes — not gratitude, exactly. Recognition, almost. As if the phrase had landed somewhere older than the present moment.

Then he drew back the blanket to examine her, and the world tilted.

The wound was high on her left side, just below the collarbone, and it had been dressed — badly, hastily, by someone who had bound it with strips torn from a petticoat and prayed. The cloth was stiff with dried blood, and around it her skin was mottled dark where the bruising spread. But it was not the wound that stopped Xanthe's breath. It was the wound's *character*. He had seen a hundred like it, had closed a hundred, in a war that had taught him ballistics the way other men learned scripture. Small calibre. Close range. An exit tear at the back that meant the round had gone through clean, missing the lung by a finger's width — a mercy, or a marksman's error, or an intention to wound rather than kill that somehow frightened him more than murder would have.

Someone had shot this woman deliberately. Recently. Within a day.

And she had walked — God knew how far — through a gathering blizzard to reach a stranger's door, because a stranger had once been a captain of the medical corps, because someone somewhere remembered his name.

"Who did this?" he asked quietly.

Her answer was to laugh — a terrible, cracked little sound with no humor in it at all.

"You don't know me," she said. "Do you. You really don't."

"I'd remember," he said honestly. "I remember most faces. It's a curse of the trade."

"No." She caught his wrist with sudden strength, her frozen fingers biting down. "Look again. Look properly. You knew my family. You knew—" Her voice snagged. "You knew Edmund."

The name went through Xanthe like a bayonet.

The surgery seemed very far away all at once — the lamp, the kettle just beginning to murmur, the wind clawing at the shutters. What remained near was a mud-walled dressing station eleven years dead, and a young lieutenant with a hole in his belly and his hands shaking too badly to hold a cigarette, and the smell of carbolic and cordite and the particular sweetness of a life ending faster than medicine could argue with it.

*Promise me,* Edmund Vane had said. *Not my father. Not the estate. The names. Make sure someone says the names.*

"You're Rosalind," Xanthe said.

Her grip on his wrist tightened, then failed all at once, her hand falling back to the table. "Rosalind Vane," she confirmed, each word dragged out of her like a nail from wood. "Presumed dead these six years. Which is why it was so very convenient to shoot me again. Nobody has to explain a second death when the first one already took."

Xanthe stood motionless. Somewhere in his chest, under the shock, an old mechanism that had been rusting for two decades shuddered and began, against all sense, to turn.

*The promise coming due.* He had imagined it a hundred times — as a summons, a letter, a tribunal, a knock from a government man with official questions. Never like this. Never as a half-dead woman bleeding through petticoat bandages on his table while the river froze and the colonel's soldiers doubled in the streets outside.

* * *

The bullet had missed the lung but torn muscle and nicked the edge of the scapula, and she had been walking on it for the better part of two days, which meant infection was already a guest at the table whether he liked it or not. He cleaned the wound with carbolic while she bit down on a rolled leather strap and refused, with a stubbornness he recognized from her brother, to make a sound. He debrided, sutured, packed, and bound. He forced laudanum on her and she took half a dose and fought the rest.

"I need my wits," she said thickly. "Whatever you give me, leave me enough to think."

"The thinking can wait until morning."

"No," she said, and her eyes, swimming though they were, fixed on him with terrible clarity. "It can't. Because in the morning there will be soldiers at your door asking if you saw anyone on the road tonight. There are already men at the ferry crossing. They shot at me an hour past Harrow Bend, and the only reason they lost me is that I went down the quarry track where horses can't follow. They'll work it out by dawn. So whatever happens, Nurse Iversen — whatever they ask you — I was never here."

"And if they search?"

"They won't find me, because you're going to tell me where to hide, and then you're going to forget me so completely that even you believe it." She tried to sit up; the attempt defeated her halfway, and she lay back, panting, furious at her own body. "I have documents. In my pack. My father will burn this town down looking for them, and he will burn *you* down first, because you're the one man in this valley who ever kept a record he couldn't control."

Xanthe went still.

For twenty years he had believed his secret was a private one — a stone kept in his own pocket, heavy only to him. Now this woman, this revenant from a buried decade, looked at him from his own table and spoke of records as though she had seen the floorboard lifted, as though she had read every page.

"How do you know that?" His voice came out lower than he intended.

"Because Edmund wrote to you. Eleven letters. I found them after — after everything. After they declared him missing instead of dead, after my father signed the papers that made his death into an absence and my uncle's estate into my father's property." Her breathing was ragged now, the laudanum and the fever pulling her down even as she clawed toward the point. "Edmund kept copies too. Muster rolls. Hospital returns. Dates that didn't match. Men listed as transferred who were never transferred anywhere except into the ground. He trusted you with the truth, and you kept it — I know you kept it, I know it the way I know my own name — and I have spent six years finding the other half of it."

The kettle had begun to sing. Neither of them moved to take it off the ring.

"My father thinks I died in a coach accident in 'thirteen," Rosalind whispered. "He arranged the presumption. It suited him. A dead daughter asks no questions about how her uncle's casualty ledgers came to be rewritten. But I survived, and I have spent six years being nobody — working under other names, following paper trails through three counties — and two weeks ago I finally held the proof in my hands. Original ledgers from the regimental depot. My father's own annotations in his own hand. Enough to hang him, if hanging were still done for this."

She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him, and in the lamplight her face was ghost-pale and fever-bright and utterly unyielding.

"So. You have a choice, Nurse Iversen. The same choice you've been avoiding for twenty years. You can carry me up to the fever ward, or the attic, or wherever old houses keep their secrets, and in the morning you can tell Colonel Aldous Vane's men exactly nothing, and go back to your ginger biscuits and your careful silence. Or—" her breath caught, and she pushed through it— "or you can finish what my brother made you promise. Because I am telling you: the bridge closes tonight, the river becomes a wall, and for five months you and I and the truth are all locked in here together with him."

Outside, the storm hurled itself against the surgery again, and beneath it, faint and vast, the young ice groaned along the river — the sound of the valley sealing itself shut for the winter.

Xanthe looked at the woman on his table, at her brother's jaw set in her feverish face, and felt twenty years of silence press down on him like snowfall — soft, accumulating, and finally, catastrophically heavy.

"Rest," he said at last, and his voice was not steady. "Sleep if you can. At first light, I'm taking you to Agnes Hale — she keeps the old fever ward, and no soldier alive suspects what that woman notices. And Rosalind—"

He paused at the footboard, one hand resting on the rail, the other unconsciously touching the pocket where he kept no key at all, because the key to the third floorboard had never existed anywhere but in his memory.

"When the soldiers come asking, you were never here. That much I swear. But understand me — the silence ends this winter. One way or another. I gave my word to your brother, and it seems his family has finally come to collect."

Her eyes were already closing, laudanum winning its argument at last, but she smiled — a small, fierce, exhausted thing, the first expression she had worn that looked like it belonged to the living.

"Good," Rosalind Vane murmured, sinking under. "Because they're already on their way."

Far off down the valley road, muffled by miles of falling snow, Xanthe heard — or thought he heard — the sound of horses.
