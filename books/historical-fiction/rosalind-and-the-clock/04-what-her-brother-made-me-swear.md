## 4. What Her Brother Made Me Swear

She was colder than any living thing had a right to be.

Xanthe carried her through the surgery and laid her on the examination table, and her weight in his arms was wrong — too light, too still, the way the wounded went light when the blood had left them. He stripped off her soaked coat and cut away the wool beneath, and there it was: a hole punched through the meat of her left shoulder, high, above the joint, weeping pink water into the table's cracked leather.

*Bullet wound. Small caliber. Through-and-through, by the feel of it — no, wait.* His fingers probed gently at her back and found no exit. *It's still in there.*

"Boiler," he said to the empty room, because his hands were already busy and his voice needed somewhere to go. "I need the boiler lit."

"I lit it." The woman's voice was a thread, but it held. Her eyes had opened — grey eyes, fever-bright, fixed on him with an intensity that had nothing to do with pain. "Before I knocked. I've been standing at your window for an hour, Mr. Iversen, watching you count bottles."

He went very still, a compress halfway to her shoulder.

"You know my name."

"You knew my brother's." She swallowed, and he watched the effort cost her something. "Edmund Vane. Captain Edmund Vane, Fourth Battalion, killed in the autumn advance of 1917. Except he wasn't killed, was he? Not according to anyone official. Because officially, Nurse Iversen — *Mr.* Iversen — Captain Vane never existed that week. No one died who could inherit anything from Colonel Aldous Vane."

The compress fell from Xanthe's fingers onto the table. For a moment the surgery swam around him — the green glass bottles, the enamel trays, the smell of carbolic and coal smoke — and underneath all of it rose another smell entirely: mud, ether, the sweet-rot stench of a field hospital in October, and a young man's voice saying *you have to promise me* —

"Who are you?" he asked, though he already knew. God help him, he'd known the moment she collapsed into his doorway, because she had Edmund's jaw and Edmund's way of looking at a man as though daring him to lie.

"Rosalind Vane," she said. "And you're going to stitch me back together, and then you're going to tell me what you promised my brother, because I've read enough of his letters to know he made you swear something before he died. He wrote to me about you. *There's a nurse here,* he said, *who counts everything, and one day the counting will matter.*"

Xanthe closed his eyes. When he opened them, twenty years had fallen away like a coat shrugged off.

* * *

October 1917 smelled of mud and iodine.

The casualty clearing station had been set up in a gutted château two miles behind the line, its ballroom ceiling open to the rain, its parquet floors running with blood-water that the orderlies squeegeed toward the broken windows every hour. Xanthe had been there eleven months. He had learned to sleep sitting up, to eat with one hand while the other packed a wound, to tell by the smell alone whether a man would keep his leg.

Captain Edmund Vane came in on a stretcher at dusk on the fourteenth, and Xanthe knew before the bearers set him down that this one was leaving them. Shell fragment, right lower abdomen. He'd seen a hundred of those and saved perhaps nine.

What he hadn't expected was for Edmund Vane to grab his wrist with a strength that had nothing left to spare, and hold it.

"Nurse Iversen." Edmund's voice was low, pitched under the noise of the ward — the moaning, the orderlies' calls, the rain on the broken glass. His eyes were clear. That was the worst part. Men dying of belly wounds got hours sometimes, lucid hours, hours to think, and Edmund had clearly spent his thinking hard. "You're the one who keeps the lists."

"Everyone keeps lists, sir."

"No." A bubble of blood at the corner of his mouth; he wiped it with the back of his hand as if ashamed of it. "The others write down what they're told to write down. You *count*. I've watched you. You counted the ambulances last Tuesday. Fourteen in, eleven recorded. You stood in the rain doing sums."

Xanthe said nothing. There was nothing safe to say.

"My father," Edmund said, "is Colonel Aldous Vane. You'll know the name. Everyone knows the name. Benefactor, hero, the man whose portrait hangs in every recruiting office between here and London." His grip tightened on Xanthe's wrist. "And my father has spent two years buying and selling the dead, Iversen. Do you understand me? Names moved. Dates altered. Men written out of the ledgers so that the battalions look stronger than they are, so that the casualties land where he wants them to land — on other officers' records, never his. And some of those men he's erased—" the breath caught, and Xanthe saw the pain come up through the morphine like water through a floorboard, "—some of them are men who would have inherited. My uncle's estate. Harrowgate. It passes through my cousin first, then me, then—"

"Sir, you should rest—"

"He wrote my cousin out three weeks ago." Edmund's voice cracked, not with grief but with fury, and fury is a terrible thing to hear from a dying man because it cannot be comforted. "Lieutenant James Vane, killed at Passchendaele, except the report says *wounded, returned to duty*, and James is in a grave the family will never visit because they'll be told he's convalescing in Boulogne. And when I'm dead, I'll be next, do you see? Dead men can't contest a paper. Dead men can't inherit. My father only needs me to die quietly, off the record, and Harrowgate is his entire."

"Captain—"

"*Promise me.*"

The ward noise seemed to fall away. Later, trying to reconstruct that night — and he would try to reconstruct it a thousand times over twenty years, in the small hours, staring at ceilings — Xanthe could never decide whether the ward had actually gone quiet or whether he had simply stopped hearing it.

"I'm a nurse," Xanthe said. "I'm nobody. I can't fight a colonel."

"You can count." Edmund's eyes were burning now, wet and fierce, the grey gone almost white. "You keep your lists, Iversen. Every name they strike, you write it down somewhere else. Somewhere they can't reach. Dates, regiments, next of kin — everything. One day someone will ask the question, and when they do, there has to be an answer that isn't my father's ink." His hand slid down Xanthe's wrist and gripped his fingers, soldier to soldier, though one of them wore no uniform worth the name. "Swear it. Swear it on something. On anything you believe in. I don't care what."

"I believe in very little, sir."

"Then swear it on the ones who die unwritten. That ought to be enough for anyone."

Xanthe looked down at the ruined belly of a twenty-six-year-old captain and thought of fourteen ambulances in the rain and eleven names on the sheet, and heard himself say:

"I swear it. By the ones who die unwritten."

Edmund Vane exhaled — long, shuddering, the breath of a man setting down a weight — and smiled, and it was his sister's smile exactly, Xanthe realized much later, the same crooked, defiant half-turn of the mouth.

"Good," Edmund whispered. "That's — good. Now write to my sister. Rosalind. Tell her I loved her, and tell her — tell her the counting matters—"

He was dead before midnight. And at dawn, when the adjutant came through with the day's casualty sheet, Xanthe watched him take up his pen, watch the nib hover over the line where Captain E. Vane should have appeared, watch the pen move on — past it, past it, to the next name, the next — and the sheet went up the chain without him, and somewhere in London a woman named Rosalind Vane would receive a letter telling her that her brother was alive and well and posted elsewhere, and would spend years waiting for a letter that would never come.

That night, in the latrine by candlelight, Xanthe opened a cheap ledger book and wrote, in his small careful hand:

*Vane, E., Capt., 4th Bn. Died 14 Oct 1917, 23:40, CCS No. 12. Cause: shell fragment, abdomen. Omitted from record.*

Beneath it, after a moment, he added: *First promise kept.*

By the armistice there were sixty-one names in the book. By 1919, eighty-three.

* * *

"Mr. Iversen."

Rosalind's voice pulled him back into the surgery, into the cold, into the present tense. She was watching him with that same fierce clarity, propped on one elbow despite the wound, blood seeping slow and dark through the pad he'd pressed against her shoulder without remembering doing it.

"You've gone somewhere," she said. "Where did you go?"

"To October 1917." He moved — finally, mechanically — to the cabinet, took down the ether bottle, the probe, the forceps. His hands were steady. Twenty years of practice at steadiness. "Your brother died at ten minutes to midnight on the fourteenth, in a château with its roof shot off, holding my wrist hard enough to leave marks. And the next morning the army decided he was still alive, and I decided to keep count."

"And you never sent word." Not accusation — worse. Curiosity, cold and exact. "Twenty years, Mr. Iversen. Eighty-three names, I'd wager, if you kept the habit. And you never once wrote to me."

"The post between here and the world passes through garrison hands eight months of the year." He set the instruments on a tray, arranging them with more precision than they required. "And a letter naming Aldous Vane's crimes, posted from a town he owns, signed by a man he commands — that letter would have reached two destinations. His desk, and the river."

"So you waited."

"So I *kept silent*, Miss Vane, which is not the same thing, though I've told myself it is for twenty years, and I no longer believe myself." He turned to face her. "Do you know what silence looks like from the inside? It looks like prudence. It looks like patience. Every year it looks a little more reasonable, and every year it costs a little less to maintain, until one morning you wake up and realize the silence isn't a wound you're guarding anymore. It's just — how you breathe."

Rosalind held his gaze. Blood loss had drained her face to paper, but the defiance in it burned like a lamp.

"Then stop breathing that way," she said. "Because I have documents, Mr. Iversen. Originals. My uncle's correspondence, my cousin's true death notice, three years of battalion returns in my father's own hand with the alterations visible beneath the corrections. I carried them across forty miles of snow with a bullet in my shoulder because every other road is watched. And I came to *you* — not to a magistrate, not to a newspaper, to you — because a dying man believed you were the kind of person who counts. So." She lifted her chin. "Stitch me up. And then show me whether my brother was right."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Then he lit the spirit lamp, warmed the ether mask, and began.

The bullet came out at a quarter past midnight, deformed, spent, lying in the kidney dish like a period at the end of a sentence. He cleaned and closed the wound, and she bore it with her teeth set and her hand fisted in the table's leather edge, and she never made a sound — and he thought, *Edmund didn't either, at the end. It runs in the family.*

Afterward, while she slept under a blanket on his cot, Xanthe knelt beside the surgery floorboard — the loose one, third from the wall, the one that had given up nothing in two decades of inspections and searches and one memorable burglary — and lifted it, and took out the ledger.

Eighty-three names. He turned the pages slowly in the lamplight, and each name was a small cold stone in his chest, and beneath the stones, faint but unmistakable, the old fire: *swear it, swear it on anything you believe in.*

Outside, the wind came down the valley carrying the first real taste of winter, and somewhere below the town the river was beginning to think about ice. Three days until the bridge closed. Three days until the town became a locked box with Aldous Vane holding the key.

And tomorrow — Xanthe felt it settle over him like a hand on the shoulder, courteous and absolute — tomorrow the garrison house would send for him. Because a stranger had been seen on the north road, and strangers were Vane's business now, and Xanthe Iversen had just been seen at his window lighting lamps past midnight.

He slid the ledger back beneath the floorboard and pressed the wood home, and stood in the dark listening to Rosalind Vane breathe, and understood that the silence he had tended for twenty years had just begun to bleed.
