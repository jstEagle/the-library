## 5. Rosalind's Evidence

The fever of the wound broke sometime before dawn, and Rosalind woke to the smell of carbolic and woodsmoke and the sound of a man moving quietly below her.

She lay still for a long while, taking inventory of herself the way she had learned to take inventory of everything since leaving her father's house: what she had, what she lacked, what could be taken from her in the night. The bandage at her shoulder pulled when she breathed deeply but did not burn. Her coat was gone — no. Folded over a chair near the stove, her satchel sitting atop it, buckled, untouched. She watched it for a full minute before she allowed herself to believe that.

Below, floorboards creaked. A kettle sang and was lifted from the fire. She heard drawers open and close with the particular softness of a man who did not want to wake anyone — or who did not want anyone to know he was awake.

She had come to this town because of a name on a letter eleven years old, written in a hand that had shaken. *If anything happens to me, go to Xanthe Iversen.* Edmund's hand. Edmund's shaking. And now she was here, under this man's roof, and the letter felt like a coin she had spent without knowing its worth.

She dressed slowly, one-armed, biting down on the pain where the bullet had torn through the meat below her collarbone. In the small looking glass above the washstand her face looked back at her: thinner than it had been in October, wind-scoured, the bruise along her jaw gone yellow-green. Her father would not know her. That had been the point of the last four months. Now she wondered if it still was.

The stairs complained under her weight, and when she came into the surgery, Xanthe Iversen turned from the stove with two cups in his hands and an expression she could not read — something between wariness and a grief he had packed away so long ago that the wrapping had grown into the wound.

"You shouldn't be up," he said.

"I've been up for years, Mr. Iversen."

Something moved behind his eyes at his own name. He set both cups down — tea, weak and over-steeped, the way tea gets when a household has been living alone too long to care — and pulled out a chair for her with a formality that belonged to another century, another army.

"Rosalind Vane," he said quietly. It was not a question. He had known since last night; she had seen him know, there in the doorway, snow to his knees, her blood freezing black on his step.

"My father told everyone I drowned," she said. "It saved him the trouble of a funeral."

* * *

He did not press her. That was the first thing she noticed about him, and it unsettled her more than interrogation would have. He drank his tea. He changed her dressing with hands that were steady and impersonal and very good, and all the while the morning light crept across the surgery floor and lit the dust motes, and outside the window the river lay grey and flat as hammered tin, and somewhere down the street a cart driver cursed his horse in language meant for warmer weather.

It was she who broke, in the end. She supposed he knew she would. Twenty years a soldier's nurse — he understood siegecraft, even the kind conducted over teacups.

"You knew my brother," she said.

"I carried your brother off the field at—" He stopped himself. "Yes. I knew Edmund."

"He wrote to me. Before the end." She watched his face carefully. "Did you know that?"

"I helped him write it." The words came out level, but his hands went still around the cup. "His right arm was— he dictated. I took the letter down."

"Then you know what's in it."

"I know what he asked me to promise."

"And have you kept it?" The question came out harder than she intended, edged with four months of cold roads and colder suspicion. "Twenty years, Mr. Iversen. Edmund died believing you'd see it done. I've read that letter until I can recite it. *Iversen keeps the true count,* he wrote. *Iversen will tell them, when it's safe.* Was it ever safe? Or was it only ever convenient?"

He set the cup down. When he looked at her, she saw the twenty years in his face all at once — they came up like water rising in a well shaft.

"Do you think I've forgotten a single name?" he said. "Do you think there is one night in twenty years I have slept without them?"

"Then show me."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. His jaw worked. Then he rose, crossed to the far wall, knelt at the edge of a floorboard near the dispensary cabinet, and pried it up with a knife from his instrument tray.

The ledger came out wrapped in oilcloth, tied with surgical tape gone brittle. He laid it on the table between them like a body on a slab.

"I started it the month after Edmund died," he said. "Every man whose name came off the rolls between the battle report and the final casualty return. Every death recorded as *desertion*, or *missing*, or struck entirely. I copied the original muster sheets when I could steal them from the adjutant's tent. I memorized what I couldn't copy." He untied the tape. "Two hundred and eleven names, Miss Vane. I can say them in order. Some nights I do, just to be sure I still can."

Rosalind did not touch the ledger. Not yet. Her hands were trembling, and she would not let him see it — though perhaps he already had, and was kind enough to look elsewhere.

"This is half the proof," she said.

"It is the better half. Yours is paper. Mine is men."

"Then we are going to make a complete document." She reached for her satchel at last, unbuckled it, and drew out the oilskin packet she had slept beside, walked beside, hidden inside the false bottom of a hymnal through three searches and one robbery at a coaching inn. "Because mine is not merely paper. Mine is my father's own hand."

* * *

She spread the documents out across the surgery table with the deliberation of a dealer laying out a winning hand, and let him look, and watched his face change as he understood what he was seeing.

"The estate," he said slowly. "Vane Holt. Your uncle's property."

"My uncle Charles died childless in the spring of 1899. Under the entail, the estate should have passed to the eldest surviving line — which, after Uncle Charles, was my father's younger branch. My father inherited nothing but debts. But there was a condition, buried in the old settlement deeds: the estate passed intact only to a son of the family *unimpaired by dishonour* — a phrase the lawyers argued over for a generation, but which in practice meant unconvicted, unbankrupt, and unscandalous. My father stood to lose everything if any charge of fraud or falsification ever attached to his name."

"So he needed to look clean."

"He needed to look immaculate. And then the war gave him the means to buy his immaculateness with other men's blood." She put her finger on the topmost document — a casualty return, official stationery, the War Office crest embossed in the corner. "This is the return my father submitted for the autumn engagement of 1901. Look at the totals."

Xanthe bent over it. His breath had gone shallow. "Fourteen killed," he read. "Nine wounded. Three missing."

"Now look beneath it." She peeled back the corner of the page — she had learned, in her father's study, at fourteen, that he always pasted new returns over the old drafts rather than destroy them; some superstition of his, some horror of blank spaces — and there, ghosting through the paper, the ink of the original bleeding faintly upward like a bruise through skin. "Thirty-one killed. Fourteen wounded. Six missing. He reduced the dead by seventeen, Mr. Iversen. Do you know why?"

"Compensation."

"Pensions. Widow's allowances. And more than that — accountability. A colonel who loses thirty-one men in a badly planned action faces inquiry. A colonel who loses fourteen has fought a creditable action. My father traded seventeen dead men's names for his reputation, and his reputation bought him Vane Holt, because the estate's trustees required a commander of unblemished record to stand as guarantor of the family's standing. There are letters here from the trustees' solicitor saying precisely that. In black and white. Dated, signed, witnessed." Her voice had gone quiet and very cold, and she recognized the tone as her father's own, and hated that too, and used it anyway. "And here —" she drew out a slim bundle of correspondence, tied in red tape, "— is the adjutant who kept the original returns. Captain Merrick. He wrote to my father twice asking whether the corrected figures might be restored 'once matters had settled.' Once, you understand, the widows had stopped writing. This is my father's reply."

Xanthe read it. His lips moved slightly, the way a chaplain's do over a burial service.

*"Merrick — you were paid to transcribe, not to remember. The figures are as reported. Should your memory trouble you again, I shall arrange for it to trouble you in a place with worse weather than England."*

"He sent him to a garrison post in the hills," Rosalind said. "Merrick died there within two years. Drink, officially. I have never believed *officially* in my life."

* * *

Xanthe sat back. For a long time he said nothing, and the clock on the mantel ticked through the silence, and outside a crow called once from the bare plane tree by the bridge road.

"Your father," he said at last, "is coming to dinner at the garrison house on Thursday. He asked me — politely — whether I'd seen any strangers on the north road."

"Then we have days. Perhaps less." Rosalind gathered the documents back into their packet, squaring the edges, aligning the corners, because her hands needed work or they would shake. "Listen to me. I did not come here to hide in your attic until spring. I came here because this is the last place on earth my father cannot reach quickly — the bridge closes, the river closes, and for five months the world beyond it may as well not exist. I thought that made this town a refuge. Now I see it makes it a trap — for both of us. So we use it. Five months, Mr. Iversen. No magistrates, no post, no telegraph. No one he can bribe, threaten, or erase. For five months, the only law in this valley is whatever we can build out of paper and witnesses."

"And in spring?"

"In spring the ice breaks, the bridge opens, and we walk across it carrying enough truth to bury him." She met his eyes. "You promised my brother. I am here to collect."

He looked down at the ledger — at the oilcloth, at the brittle tape, at two hundred and eleven names sleeping under the floorboards of a modest surgery for twenty years — and when he raised his head again, something in his face had shifted, the way ice shifts on a river before it breaks: no crack yet visible, but the whole mass of it suddenly, secretly, in motion.

"There's a woman named Agnes Hale," he said. "Matron of the old fever ward. She nursed beside me through the whole campaign. She remembers more names than I do — and she remembers your father's face from the day he reviewed the wounded and stepped over a dying boy to keep his boots clean." He rose and began gathering cups, briskly, a man giving his hands orders his mind hadn't caught up with. "If we're building a web, we start with her. But understand what you're asking, Miss Vane. Everyone you draw in, you endanger. In a town this size, a secret has the lifespan of a match in wind."

"Then we light several at once," Rosalind said, "and let him spend the winter chasing sparks."

Through the window, far down the white street, two soldiers in her father's colours walked the bridge road with rifles slung, unhurried, thorough, already looking. She watched them pass the surgery without pausing — this time.

"Thursday," she said quietly. "You said he's coming Thursday. He'll ask about strangers on the north road."

"Yes."

"Then when you dine with him, Mr. Iversen, smile, drink his wine, and tell him nothing at all." She touched the packet at her breast, the paper heart of it warm against her ribs. "And come home quickly afterward. Because I think we have less time than the weather suggests — and I did not survive four months and a bullet to die waiting for spring."

Outside, the first flakes of the season's second snow began to fall, fine as ash, drifting down toward the frozen river and the bridge that would soon close like a door.
