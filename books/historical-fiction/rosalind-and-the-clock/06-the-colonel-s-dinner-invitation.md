## 6. The Colonel's Dinner Invitation

The summons came by a boy who did not knock so much as let himself in, the way boys do when they have been told their errand outranks courtesy. He stood in the doorway of the surgery with his cap crushed in one fist and an envelope in the other, and he would not meet my eye.

"From the garrison house, sir. Colonel Vane's compliments." He said it the way children recite scripture — words learned whole, meaning never once considered. "He asks for you at seven. Dinner."

Dinner.

I took the envelope. It was heavy stock, cream-colored, sealed with wax that had been pressed with the Vane crest — a heron standing in water, which had always struck me as an odd choice of emblem for a man who preferred to do his wading in other people's blood. The boy was already backing out the door.

"Shall I say you'll come, sir?"

"I'll come," I said, and heard how flat my voice went, like a coin dropped on stone.

He left. I stood a long moment with the invitation in my hand while outside the window the last carts of the season rattled over the bridge planks, and I thought: twenty years, and he has never once asked me to sit at his table. Twenty years of salutes across the parade ground, of nods in the churchyard, of the colonel pressing my hand at the memorial service each autumn and saying, *We remember them, Doctor* — *we remember them*, when we both knew perfectly well that remembering was precisely what I had been hired, in every sense that mattered, not to do.

Now, on the last night before the river closed, he wanted me at dinner.

There is a particular quality to fear that arrives wearing good manners. A shout, a blow, a bayonet — these are honest things; the body knows how to answer them. But an engraved card asking you to dine at seven o'clock sets off no alarm at all. It simply sits in your hand, warm from the boy's pocket, and lets your imagination do the work that terror cannot be bothered to finish. All evening I treated a fisherman's gashed palm and a child's croup and Mrs. Prewitt's endless complaints about her heart, and all evening the envelope sat in my coat pocket like a second pulse.

Rosalind was not in the surgery. She was across the lane in Agnes Hale's back room, sleeping in fits behind a locked door, and I could not go to her, could not warn her, could not even look too long in the direction of the fever ward lest some idle eye note where my attention went. That was the first cruelty of the invitation: it separated me from the very secret I needed guarding, and made me carry it alone into the lion's parlor.

At half past six I shaved, which I confess I did badly because my hand was not steady, and put on the dark suit I keep for funerals and tribunal days, and stood before the small mirror above the basin looking at a man I barely recognized. Gray at the temples now. Lines around the eyes like cracks in porcelain. *You were a lieutenant once,* I told the reflection. *You followed this man into three campaigns and buried his brother beside a road in Flanders and swore an oath over the body.* The reflection said nothing. Reflections are cowards; they only show you what is already there.

The garrison house stood on the rise above the mill race, the finest building in town after the church, all pale stone and tall windows, its chimneys smoking generously against the coming cold. Vane had bought it with army money and furnished it with estate money, and the townspeople pointed to it with pride — *our colonel's house* — never quite asking themselves what a colonel needed with a fortress in a river town of four hundred souls. Two sentries flanked the door tonight. That was new. Last month there had been one, and in the spring none at all.

"Doctor Iversen," the sentry said, and opened the door without taking my name, which meant my name was already expected, already written down somewhere, already filed.

* * *

The hall smelled of beeswax and roast pheasant and, beneath both, faintly, of pipe smoke — Vane's tobacco, a dark Turkish blend I had last smelled in a dugout outside Ypres while the world came apart in flashes. A manservant took my coat and hat. Another appeared to lead me through to the drawing room, and I noted, because a nurse notes everything, that the second man wore boots polished to a soldier's shine beneath his servant's clothes. The house was garrisoned to the carpets.

Vane stood at the fireplace with his back to the door.

He had aged well, the way monuments age — deliberately, with attention. Seventy next spring, and still straight-backed, still silver-haired in the disciplined way of a man who has his barber come to him rather than trusting himself to chance. He wore civilian tweeds, but he wore them like a uniform; even at ease, Aldous Vane looked like a man reviewing troops. On the mantel above him stood photographs in silver frames: young officers, dead men mostly, smiling out of sepia at a future they did not keep. I knew three of the faces. I had closed two pairs of eyes myself.

"Theo." He turned, and his face arranged itself into warmth the way a stagehand arranges scenery — quickly, professionally, with visible ropes if you knew where to look. "You're punctual. You always were. Sit, sit. Brandy?"

"Water, thank you, Colonel."

"Still the abstainer." He poured himself two fingers of amber without waiting for the servant, which was itself a message: tonight we are informal, tonight we are old comrades, tonight there are no servants' ears worth minding. "Twenty years I've known you and you've never taken a drink in my house. One of these days I'll cure you of it."

"One of these days," I agreed, and sat where he indicated, on the sofa, in the lower position, facing his chair.

He settled opposite me with the fire at his back, which meant I had to squint slightly to read his face, and I understood that too was deliberate. Old soldier's trick. Keep the light behind you and the other man spends his strength guessing.

"You've been busy," he said. "The whole town through your surgery this week. Colds, coughs, broken axles, broken hearts. You work too hard, Theo."

"There's illness enough to go round."

"And now the bridge closes tomorrow and you'll have nothing but chilblains until April." He smiled. "How do you bear it? This little town, shut up like a box every winter. I confess I find the isolation restful. No newspapers for months. No visitors. Nothing comes in." He lifted his brandy. "And nothing goes out."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock struck the quarter hour with the patience of a thing that has counted worse hours than these.

"No," I said carefully. "Nothing goes out."

"Remarkable, really." He swirled the brandy, watching it climb the glass. "A town cut off from the world, and yet everyone in it knows everyone else's business inside a day. A stranger can't buy a loaf of bread without six people reporting what she paid for it." His eyes came up, mild as milk. "Speaking of strangers."

Here it was, then. I had known it would come and had rehearsed my answers all afternoon against the ceiling of my bedroom, and still my mouth went dry as paper.

"Have you seen anyone on the roads?" Vane asked. "Anyone passing through? The garrison reports a woman may have crossed the bridge Tuesday night, or Wednesday — the sentries are vague, they see a hundred travelers a week and remember none of them, poor devils. A woman traveling alone, middle-aged perhaps, or young and dressed down to look it. Possibly injured." He said *possibly injured* the way another man might say *possibly carrying letters*. "It occurs to me that if such a woman fell ill or met with misfortune on the road, she'd find her way to your door eventually. Everything in this town finds its way to your door eventually, Doctor. You're rather the town's confessor."

"I haven't treated any strangers," I said.

It was true. I had treated no stranger; I had treated Rosalind Vane, whom I had known since she was nine years old and fearless on horseback, and I clung to the distinction like a rope over a drop.

"No one at all?" Vane's voice stayed gentle. That was always his gift, the thing that made men follow him and women trust him and governments overlook him: he could make interrogation feel like concern. "No one knocked? No one half-frozen, no one bleeding? In this weather, a traveler caught out overnight would be lucky to reach shelter at all. Lucky," he repeated, softly, "to reach anyone's door."

"If someone reached mine, Colonel, I'd have treated them and told you. There are no secrets in a surgery."

"No," he said. "There aren't. That's what I've always admired about you, Theo. You of all people understand that everything comes to light in the end." He rose, crossing to the window, and stood looking down at the town below — at the lamplit lanes, the smoke rising straight in the still air, the black ribbon of the river beginning to rim with ice along its edges. "Do you know what I think about, on nights like this? Legacy. What a man leaves behind him. I've given this town thirty years, Theo. The school roof. The hospital wing. The winter coal fund. Every family here eats because of arrangements I made, debts I quietly forgave, wages I advanced. And in return they give me their loyalty, which costs them nothing and comforts me greatly." He turned. The firelight made his shadow enormous on the wall behind him. "Loyalty is a strange currency, isn't it? It keeps its value only as long as nobody tests it. The moment a man is asked to choose — really choose — he discovers whether he was ever loyal at all, or merely comfortable."

"I wouldn't know, Colonel. I'm not a man of property."

"No," Vane said, with something almost like fondness. "You're a man of ledgers."

My blood stopped moving. I am certain of it. For one full second my heart declined to beat, and in that second the whole architecture of twenty years — the floorboard, the oiled cloth, the names written in my own careful hand, forty-one names, forty-one men erased — stood naked in the drawing room between us, glowing like phosphorus.

Then I understood he meant the surgery accounts, and made myself breathe.

"Accounts must balance," I said. My voice sounded distant to me, spoken by someone else. "Even a country doctor learns that."

"Indeed they must." He smiled again, and refilled his glass, and the moment passed the way weather passes — leaving everything outwardly unchanged and everything underneath rearranged. "Come. Dinner's getting cold, and Cook will sulk for a week."

* * *

We ate pheasant and potatoes and a heavy plum pudding, served by the boot-shined manservant, and Vane talked the whole time, which I gradually realized was the entire design of the evening. He talked of the war, of campaigns we had shared, of Edmund.

That was the cruelty beneath the courtesy. He spoke of his brother easily, warmly, raising his glass to Edmund's memory halfway through the meal — *"the best of us, Theo, the best of us"* — and watched me over the rim as he drank. Testing the name against my face the way a jeweler tests gold against a touchstone, looking for the flicker, the flush, the telltale tremor.

I gave him nothing. Twenty years of practice gave me nothing, though inside I was nineteen years old again, kneeling in the mud at Passchendaele with Edmund Vane's head in my lap while the life went out of him in bright, terrible pulses, listening to the last coherent words a dying man ever said to me: *They'll write us off the rolls, Iversen. My brother will write us off the rolls to get the estate, and no one will ever know our names. Promise me. Promise me someone will know.*

I promised. God forgive me, I promised, and then I came home to this town and worked for that man for twenty years and kept my promise folded under a floorboard like a letter I was afraid to post.

"He'd have liked this weather," Vane was saying. "Edmund loved a hard winter. Said it sorted the serious men from the decorative ones." He laughed, a rich, genuine-sounding laugh. "Do you remember the Argonne? No — of course you remember it. Everyone remembers it who survived it. The things we did, Theo. The things we saw. A man carries a great deal, and says nothing, and that silence is the truest fellowship there is." He leaned forward, and for a moment the warmth dropped away entirely, and what looked out at me from behind his eyes was the thing itself, the cold engine underneath the charm, and I understood that everything else — the brandy, the memories, the raised glass to his brother — had been scaffolding around this single sentence:

"You and I, Doctor, have always understood the value of keeping certain records closed."

"Yes," I said. "We have."

He held my gaze a moment longer, weighing whatever he found there, and then the warmth returned like a lamp being relit, and he was the genial benefactor again, ringing for port, asking after Mrs. Prewitt's heart, laughing at his own joke about the vicar's sermon. And all the time, beneath the tablecloth, my hands were folded tight enough to whiten, and my mind was running the calculation it had been running all evening: *he suspects; he does not know; he is fishing with a wide net; the net has not closed.*

Yet.

Because there was one moment, near the end, when he rose to escort me to the door and paused at the drawing-room threshold, and said, casually, terribly:

"Oh — one more thing. The fever ward. Old Miss Hale's attic domain. It's been disused since the epidemic, hasn't it? Drafty old place. Full of dust and history." He smiled down at me, and the heron on the wax seal flashed in my mind, standing patient in shallow water, waiting for whatever moved beneath the surface. "I've been thinking of having it inspected. Structural survey. Soldiers make thorough inspectors — they check every corner. Perhaps next week, once the snow settles in and there's nothing better to occupy them. Goodnight, Theo. Mind the ice on the steps."

* * *

I walked home through streets gone blue with frost, past shuttered shops and curtained windows, and the town felt suddenly like a held breath. Lamps burned low. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. Above the mill race the garrison house windows glowed, every one of them lit, and I knew that behind at least one of them a map of this town lay open on a desk, and my surgery had been penciled onto it, and the fever ward beside it, with a small neat cross.

Nothing goes out, he had said. And nothing goes out — except, tomorrow, when the bridge shutters closed for the season, the last thing to cross would be the ice itself, creeping inward from both banks, sealing the river like a lid on a jar.

I let myself into the surgery and did not light the lamp. In the dark I knelt and put my hand flat against the floorboard, feeling through the wood the faint rectangular presence of the ledger beneath, forty-one names lying quiet as bones.

Tomorrow the bridge closes. Next week, soldiers with orders to check every corner.

Whatever was going to be done with the truth in this town would have to be done before the snow finished falling — and done by lamplight, in attics, page by page, by hands steadier than mine felt tonight.

Above the lane, in the dark of the fever ward, a candle flickered briefly at the attic gable and went out.

Someone had just heard me come home.
