## 7. Snowbound

The bridge closed at noon on the third of December, and the whole town turned out to watch it happen, the way other towns turn out for a hanging or a wedding.

Rosalind watched from behind the fogged glass of Agnes Hale's back room in the old fever ward, her breath making a small ghost on the pane that she wiped away with the heel of her hand. Below, on the frozen mud of the approach road, two garrison engineers in greatcoats swung the last of the barrier chains across the span's northern throat. The chains were new — she could see the brightness of them even at this distance, galvanized links catching what little light the sky offered. Someone had paid for new chains. Someone had wanted this closing to be official, ceremonial, photographed if any photographer had been fool enough to make the journey.

Her father, she thought, had always understood theater.

The crowd below was smaller than she'd expected — forty souls perhaps, fifty, bundled to the eyes, their exhalations rising in a single gray cloud over the road. A boy rang the church bell, badly, out of rhythm, and nobody corrected him because there was nothing left to mark time against now. The river itself lay flat and white from bank to bank, ice locked down since the first hard frost a fortnight past, and beyond it the far shore was only a smudge of black pine under a sky the color of dishwater.

From here to the county seat was eleven miles by bridge. By the river road, around through the gorge — if the gorge road still existed, which no one would confirm — it was better than sixty.

She had come three hundred miles to reach this town, and now she could not leave it.

"You'll want to step back from that window." Agnes Hale's voice came from the doorway, unhurried, carrying the particular authority of a woman who had spent forty years telling people what to do with their bodies and had never once been argued with successfully. She came into the room carrying a tray — tea, bread, a rind of cheese, a boiled egg gone gray at the edges. "Light carries further than you'd think on a day like this. And there's a man across the lane who watches this building."

"Which man?"

"The one who thinks I don't know he watches it." Agnes set the tray on the washstand and looked out herself, briefly, without appearing to look. "Sergeant Croft's boy. He stands at the corner of the tannery yard most afternoons. He thinks he's being subtle. He is not. His mother was a Merrick and subtlety skipped the whole family."

Rosalind drew back from the glass. Three days in hiding had already begun to teach her the grammar of the town: which windows commanded which streets, which doors stuck and which opened silently, which names were spoken freely and which dropped to a lower register when they passed between lips. *Vane* was spoken low. She had heard it twice in the market before Agnes smuggled her here through the back lanes, hood up, hands chapped like any workingwoman's, an old nurse's coat over her shoulders that smelled of carbolic and camphor.

"He's posted men at the ferry crossing too," Rosalind said. "I saw them yesterday. Four of them, at the boathouse."

"Six now." Agnes poured the tea without asking whether she wanted it. "They came up from the garrison this morning with a stove and a crate of coal. Making themselves comfortable. Your father means to hold the river."

*Your father.* The words landed strangely, the way they always did. Rosalind had spent nine years learning not to say that word at all — had trained it out of her mouth the way one trains out a stammer — and here it came back easily, handed to her by a stranger as though it were nothing, as though it hadn't cost her everything.

"He's not holding the river," she said. "He's holding me. There's a difference."

"There is," Agnes agreed, "and it will matter very much later. But for now the effect is the same, so drink your tea while it's hot."

---

The fever ward had been empty since the influenza winter, two years gone, and it wore its emptiness like an old coat: iron bed frames stacked along one wall, mattresses rolled and tied, the great ward room upstairs a cavern of dust sheets and pale light. It sat at the edge of town where charity had put it — close enough to serve the poor, far enough that respectable noses wouldn't be offended — and its isolation that had once marked it as a place of contagion now made it nearly invisible. Nobody came here. Nobody had reason to.

Except, Rosalind noted on her second morning, someone had reason now.

She discovered it by accident, coming down the outside stair from the attic where Agnes had made her a bed among the Christmas decorations — crates of paper garlands, a plaster angel missing one wing, the accumulated pageantry of twenty forgotten Decembers. From the stair she had a clear line southeast across the frozen allotment gardens to the ferry crossing, half a mile off, and she had made a habit of checking it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

That morning there was a fifth figure at the boathouse. Not one of the soldiers — wrong coat, civilian wool, a hat with a fur brim. He stood apart from the garrison men at the edge of the ice and did something she couldn't resolve at distance: bent, straightened, bent again. Through Agnes's ancient opera glasses, salvaged from a drawer and thick with grime she'd cleaned off with vinegar, she resolved him at last.

He was measuring the ice. Driving a chisel bar down at intervals, kneeling to read the fracture, marking points with a stick of colored chalk. An engineer's work. Or a surveyor's.

Or a man determining how much weight the river could bear, and where.

She lowered the glasses and stood very still in the cold, her breath smoking, and felt the shape of the trap with new clarity. It wasn't just that the bridge was chained. It was that every possible way across the river was being catalogued, tested, priced. Her father had not simply shut the door. He was walking the walls.

---

*You are dead,* the letter had said, in her aunt's cramped hand. *Officially. There was a telegram. Your father wept — or produced weeping, which amounts to the same thing in that house. Do not write. Do not come. He believes it, or has arranged to believe it, and either way you are safer as a ghost.*

Safer as a ghost. She had been a ghost for nine years — governess under another name in Prague, then a clerk in a Geneva shipping office, then nothing at all, moving like smoke through cities that didn't ask questions. She had gotten good at it. Good at being no one. And then Edmund's package had arrived last spring, forwarded through three hands, containing the ledger pages and the letter in his careful draftsman's script, and ghosts, she had learned, cannot testify.

So she had crossed back into her own country on false papers and traveled north through the late autumn with a satchel of documents sewn into her petticoat, and she had meant to cross the river at this town because it was the only crossing, and she had known — she had *known*, some cold rational part of her had laid it all out plainly — that the last cart across the bridge would be her last easy chance.

She had missed it by one night. One night of caution, of lying up in a hay barn rather than pressing on through the dark, because her wound had gone bad and she could feel the fever climbing her spine like a ladder.

And then Xanthe Iversen's door, and the knife-stitch of the probe, and the strange mercy of a man who recognized her name and went white as the snow outside and said, *I knew your brother.*

She touched her side now, unconsciously, through the layers. The wound was knitting. Nurse Iversen — *Mister* Iversen; she kept tripping on it, the soft voice and the sure hands arguing with each other in her head — changed the dressing every second day and said little, but his silences were informative ones, and she had begun to read them. He was afraid. Not of her. Of the arithmetic of her presence: one more variable in a town already held in a closed fist, one more thing that could go wrong, one more debt coming due on a promise made to a dying man twenty years ago.

She had heard him say it the first night, feverish, half-dreaming on the table: *"I promised him."*

She intended to collect.

---

In the afternoon the snow began — not the theatrical sort, not the swirling curtain of storybooks, but a fine dry sift that fell straight down for hours and erased the world by degrees. Rosalind stood at the attic window watching the ferry crossing dissolve. First the boathouse roof went soft-edged, then the soldiers' stove pipe stopped showing smoke, then the figures at the ice's edge became suggestions, then became nothing. The river vanished. The far shore vanished. For a quarter hour the entire world was the window frame and six feet of falling white beyond it, and she felt a wild, irrational lift of spirits — *they can't find what they can't see* — before reason reasserted itself.

Snow covered tracks. Snow also preserved them. And in the spring —

In the spring everything would melt, and every footprint, every dropped button, every buried thing would surface at once.

She heard Agnes on the stairs — the woman moved quietly for her age but the fourth step sang, always, a worn board with a voice of its own, and Rosalind had already memorized the house's small chorus: the singing step, the latch on the linen cupboard that needed lifting before turning, the draft under the attic door that made the candle flame lean east.

"They've lit lamps at the crossing," Agnes said, coming in without preamble, shaking snow from her shawl. "Fixed lanterns on posts, four of them, strung along the ice road where it starts. That's not for finding their way. That's a message."

"That the road is watched."

"That the road is *theirs*." Agnes set down the basket she carried — bread again, and a jar of preserves, and beneath a cloth, Rosalind saw, a folded newspaper from two towns over, a week old, smuggled the way contraband always moved in winter: inside the laundry. "There's more. Tom Merrick — the watcher's brother, mind, family full of watchers — came by the surgery today to have a tooth drawn. Loose-lipped, the whole lot of them. Your father's called a meeting of the constables and the harbor board for tomorrow night. Closed session."

"About what?"

"About strangers on the road, is what Tom heard through the wall before he was told to move along. About whether anyone's seen anything that ought to be reported." Agnes's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Twenty years I've lived in this town, girl, and I will tell you how Aldous Vane governs it. He doesn't post proclamations. He asks questions. Gently. At dinners. And every merchant and magistrate in the valley spends the rest of the week answering them, whether they mean to or not. The meeting tomorrow isn't to decide anything. It's to remind everyone there's a decision to be made, and which side their bread is buttered on."

Rosalind turned the newspaper over in her hands without reading it. "And which side are you on, Mrs. Hale?"

Agnes looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the snow went on falling, patient as arithmetic.

"I nursed through the typhoid summer of 'ninety-eight," she said finally, "when the fever ward was full and half the town wouldn't come within a mile of this building, and the man who funded the medicines and sent the coal was Colonel Vane, and everyone said what a benefactor he was. And I'll tell you what I learned that summer, hauling corpses to the lime pit while the town toasted its benefactor. Charity is a fine thing. But you want to watch close what the charitable man gets for it." She picked up the empty tray. "He got a town that can't say no to him. He bought it cheap, and he's held it ever since, and the interest is paid in silence. I've been paying it twenty years myself. I'm tired of the installments."

She paused at the door.

"Eat the egg before it goes cold. And stay off the window after dusk — the lamp at the tannery corner burns till ten now. They added it Tuesday." 

Then she was gone, and the singing step faded down the stairs, and Rosalind was alone with the snow and the newspaper and the slow tightening of things.

---

Night came early, the way it did this far north in December — by four o'clock the dark was total and the wind had found its evening voice, moaning in the ward's broken eaves. Rosalind sat in the attic by candle stub, sewing, because her hands needed occupation or they would start shaking, and because a woman seen mending in a window read as furniture, as background, as nothing worth reporting.

Through the floor she could hear the town settling into its winter sleep: a dog somewhere, a sleigh bell on the far road, the groan of the river ice contracting in the cold, a sound like a giant shifting in sleep that she was learning to hear without flinching. Somewhere south of her, in a surgery on Chandler Street, a man was lying awake above a hidden ledger, and she found she could picture it precisely — the narrow bed, the ceiling he stared at, the floorboard he had lifted and replaced so many times the edges had gone soft.

She should have felt guilt about what she was asking of him. Some part of her, distantly, did. But the larger part felt only the old cold engine of purpose turning over, the one Edmund had built in her before he died, the inheritance he'd actually left her: *the truth is a debt, Rosalind, and debts don't care whose comfort they cost.*

Below, faint through the wind, a whistle sounded from the direction of the crossing. Long, short, long. Then, after a pause, answered — long, long.

A signal. Repeated. Something moving on the ice at night, or something expected to.

She pinched out the candle and went to the window, careful to stand to the side of the glass, and looked south toward the river she could no longer see. Far off, tiny as sparks, the four lanterns at the head of the ice road burned in a row — and now, she saw, there was a fifth light among them, swinging, mobile. A lantern carried by a walking man, moving out onto the white.

Toward the far shore. Or toward something on it.

Rosalind stood in the dark with her hand pressed to the cold glass and understood, with the clarity that comes when the last ambiguity drains out of a situation, that the trap was no longer being set.

It was sprung. It had been sprung the moment she crossed into the valley — perhaps before that, perhaps from the beginning, perhaps her aunt's letter and her careful ghost's route and every cautious delay had all been played exactly as someone else had written them. Her father had not doubled the garrison because he feared her coming.

He had doubled it because he knew she was already here.

The lantern light on the ice paused, far out, and seemed to turn — a slow sweep, deliberate, the beam dragging a pale finger across the frozen river until it pointed, and held, directly at the fever ward on its hill.

Then it went out, and the snow fell on, and Rosalind Vane reached for her boots.
