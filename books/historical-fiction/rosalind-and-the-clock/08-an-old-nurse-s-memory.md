## 8. An Old Nurse's Memory

Agnes Hale had spent sixty years learning to read people the way other folk read weather, and the girl who came into the dispensary on the third of December, wrapped in a man's coat and a lie, was carrying a storm behind her eyes.

"Come in out of the cold," Agnes said, as she said to everyone. "You'll want your hands warmed before anything else."

The girl hesitated on the threshold — that was the first thing. People who were merely cold came straight at the stove like moths. This one stood in the doorway and took the measure of the room: the rows of labeled bottles, the cot behind the curtain, the window with its view of the frozen river, the door to the back stairs. She was mapping her exits.

"You're the matron," the girl said. Her voice was low, careful, sanded down so it wouldn't catch on anything. "Mrs. Hale."

"Everyone in this town is somebody's nurse eventually." Agnes held out her hands for the girl's coat. "Give it here. You'll be wanting tea, and you'll be pretending you don't, but you'll drink it all the same."

It was when the coat came off that Agnes saw the bandage — a rough wrapping at the left shoulder, gone stiff at the edges, stained the brownish rust that meant it had bled a day or two past and been tended by someone who knew what they were doing but hadn't done it well. And beneath the bandage, when the girl turned toward the light of the lamp, Agnes saw the face properly for the first time.

Sixty years of faces will do things to a memory. Most of them blur together like watercolors left in the rain. But some stay fixed as daguerreotypes, and Agnes Hale carried a whole gallery of them: the men who had died under her hands in the fever ward of '99; the children she'd pulled through scarlet fever; and, sharpest of all, a family portrait from a happier age, when she had still taken private nursing work up at Vane House and the colonel's wife was alive and laughing and his two children ran shrieking down the long gallery on rainy afternoons.

Edmund had been the loud one, all elbows and schemes. The girl had been quieter. Watchful. She used to sit on the kitchen steps while Agnes skinned rabbits, asking questions no child should ask — *why do we keep the ones we can't save comfortable instead of trying harder?* — and Agnes had answered her honestly, because there seemed no sense lying to a child who would find out anyway.

That child was standing in her dispensary with a bullet wound and a dead woman's name, calling herself Mrs. Ferris of somewhere up-country, and Agnes Hale felt the years fall away from her like a coat slipping off a shoulder.

* * *

She did not say it. That was the discipline of a lifetime. She hung the coat on its hook, set water to heat, measured out tea leaves with hands that did not tremble, and let the silence do what silences do best.

"The wound wants looking at," Agnes said at last. "Whoever dressed it meant well but tied it like a hay bale. Sit."

"I can manage—"

"No doubt you can. Sit anyway."

The girl sat. Agnes unwound the bandage with the unhurried competence of decades, and studied the wound beneath: a clean through-and-through, high on the shoulder, angry but not poisoned. It had been washed. It had even been stitched, clumsily, with thread too heavy for flesh.

"Nurse Iversen's work?" Agnes asked mildly.

The girl went very still. Stillness itself was an answer; most people would have blurted something. "I don't know any Nurse Iversen."

"Mm." Agnes threaded a fresh needle, dipped it in carbolic, and bent to her work. "Then whoever stitched you has a rival in town, and I'll have to have words with him about technique." She began closing the ragged edges, stitch by small precise stitch, and spoke without looking up. "I nursed for the Vane family. Before your time, likely. Before most everything. There was a daughter in that house who used to sit on my kitchen step and ask me why God allowed fevers."

The needle paused. Resumed.

"She'd be a grown woman now," Agnes went on. "If she lived. They say she didn't. Drowned, they say, or dead abroad — the story changes depending on who tells it and how much they've had to drink. But stories about the dead are cheap in this town, because Colonel Vane pays for the good ones and the bad ones come free."

"Mrs. Hale—" the girl began, and stopped, because her voice had cracked open like river ice, and underneath it was not the sanded-down stranger's voice at all.

"There now," Agnes said gently. "Hold still. Two more stitches. You've been holding yourself together with string and pride since before you crossed the bridge, and pride doesn't hold a wound shut, whatever the young believe."

When the last knot was tied and the fresh dressing smoothed down, Agnes sat back and looked at Rosalind Vane full in the face, and Rosalind looked back, and neither of them said the obvious thing, because between certain kinds of women the obvious thing is the only thing that never needs saying.

"You knew," Rosalind said finally. "From the moment I walked in."

"I knew from the moment you stood in the doorway counting the exits. Your mother used to do that. In rooms full of friends, mind you. She'd count the doors like they might turn into enemies." Agnes gathered up the soiled linen. "She was a frightened woman married to a frightening man, and nobody ever said it aloud, least of all her. I wonder if you remember her at all. You were young when she died."

"I remember her teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass." Something moved across Rosalind's face — grief, or its ghost. "I remember more than I want to."

"Memory's a cruel inheritance in that family." Agnes poured the tea, pressed the cup into the girl's good hand. "Drink. Now. You'll tell me nothing tonight, and I won't ask. But tomorrow, or the day after, you'll need to tell someone, and you should know before you choose: I'm old, my hands shake on cold mornings, and my discretion is the only part of me that's never failed. Sixty years in this town and I have never once repeated what was told to me over a sickbed. Not to husbands, not to priests, not to constables. Especially not to constables."

Rosalind drank. Her eyes over the rim of the cup were her father's eyes, and yet nothing like them at all — the same grey, lit by an entirely different fire.

"Why?" the girl asked. "Why help me? You owe my family nothing. Less than nothing."

Agnes settled into her chair by the stove, joints announcing themselves, and considered the question honestly, because the girl deserved honesty and because Agnes had earned the right to take her time answering.

"When I was a young nurse," she said, "I worked the fever ward. Down by the mill — you'll have seen the ruin. Typhoid, two winters running, and half the town's poor shut up in there dying by inches while the fine houses up the hill kept their windows shut against the smell of us. We lost forty-one souls those two winters. Forty-one. And I wrote every name in my own hand, because the registrar couldn't be troubled and because I thought — foolish girl — that names mattered. That if you wrote them down, someone, someday, would have to account for them."

She looked at her hands, spotted and knotted as tree roots.

"Nobody ever did. The ward burned in '04 and the records went with it, and the town built a memorial fountain with the Vane subscription money, and the fountain has no names on it, just a stone lady pouring water that freezes every winter. Forty-one people, Miss Vane, reduced to a statue that doesn't work." She lifted her chin. "So when a young woman comes to me half-dead, hunted by the same family whose charity paved over forty-one graves, I find I am not neutral. I find I have been waiting sixty years to not be neutral."

The fire ticked. Outside, wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere far off a dog complained at the cold. Rosalind set down her cup.

"They're searching the town," she said quietly. "Soldiers at the ferry crossing. Men going door to door by Friday, asking after a woman traveling alone. My father doesn't leave loose threads."

"Your father," Agnes said, "has been leaving loose threads in this town for twenty years and paying us to pretend we don't see them. He thinks he owns the loom." She rose, creaking, and took a lamp from its shelf. "But he's never understood the thing about thread, child. It runs through everything. Pull one end and you never know what comes unravelled."

* * *

The fever ward ruin stood at the edge of the mill district, a long low building of dark brick with boarded windows and a sagging roofline, half-swallowed by briars. The town called it haunted, which suited Agnes perfectly; she had started the rumor herself, years ago, and found it the most useful piece of gossip she'd ever planted. No children dared it. No lovers lingered there. Even the soldiers Vane had billeted in the mill gave it a wide berth, crossing themselves with the casual superstition of men who mocked God on Sunday and feared ghosts on payday.

Agnes unlocked the side door with a key she'd kept thirty years, and led Rosalind up the back stair into the attic.

It was dry, at least. The roof above the old ward had been reslated after the fire scare of '09 — Vane's own money, ironically, though he'd have forgotten — and someone, in some forgotten year, had stored up here the furniture the town couldn't bear to throw away and couldn't bear to look at: bedframes, a lectern from the chapel, crates of hymnals gone soft with damp, and rows of canvas screens that had once given the dying their privacy. Dust lay thick as snowfall. A single round window at the gable end looked east, over the roofs of the poor quarter, to where the frozen river gleamed like a blade laid across the valley.

"It's not comfortable," Agnes said, setting the lamp on a crate. "But it's warm enough if we bring a brazier, and it's invisible, which is worth more than comfort. The floor below is solid — I had it checked when I still climbed stairs easily. There's a hatch to the coal cellar at the far end, and the cellar door opens onto the lane behind, and the lane is a rabbit warren the constabulary has never successfully patrolled, being mostly too drunk to try." She turned. "I'll come mornings, with food and dressings, in my delivery basket. Nobody questions an old woman with a basket. I've been invisible myself these fifteen years — it's remarkable how thoroughly a town stops seeing its nurses once they stop being useful for births."

Rosalind walked slowly along the row of canvas screens, trailing her fingers through the dust. At the gable window she stopped and stood a long moment, looking out at the river ice.

"My brother died in a place like this," she said, without turning. "Not here. A dressing station near Amiens. Xanthe — Nurse Iversen — wrote and told me. Edmund lived four hours after they brought him in. Long enough to make a friend promise something." She turned then, and her face in the lamplight was terrible and young. "He made Iversen swear he'd see justice done for the men my father erased. And Iversen kept that oath quiet for twenty years, the way everyone keeps everything quiet in this town. And now I've arrived and broken his silence open, and he's terrified, and he's helping me anyway. Do you know what that costs a man? To spend two decades being careful and then stop?"

"I know what it cost me to start again just now," Agnes said. "At my age, with my rheumatism, knowing exactly what Vane's men do to interfering old women." She smiled, and was surprised to find the smile steady. "And I find I don't care a fig. Isn't that something? Sixty years of caution, and it turns out it was only ever waiting."

"For what?"

"For the names to matter again." Agnes opened her basket and began unpacking it with the economy of habit: bread, cheese, a jar of rendered fat, clean linen, a small tin of carbolic crystals, a candle stub. "There's a blanket in the cedar chest by the wall, wool, army surplus, warmer than it looks. Burn the candle low and only after dark — the chimney draws badly and the light shows through the boards. If anyone comes up the stair that isn't me, there's the coal hatch, though I'll be honest, child, I'd rather you fought than crawled. A cornered woman with a poker can do remarkable arithmetic."

Rosalind almost laughed — a short, startled sound, the first unguarded noise Agnes had heard from her. "You'd arm me with a poker against soldiers."

"I'd arm you with a poker against the Devil himself, and I'd expect you to win. I've seen your temper." Agnes snapped the basket shut. "You were four. Your father took a switch to Edmund over some mischief, and you bit him. Drew blood. He never touched either of you again without thinking twice, though he'd have flayed me for saying so."

"I don't remember that."

"Doesn't matter whether you remember it. It remembers you." Agnes paused at the head of the stair, lamplight catching the deep lines of her face, and for a moment the old matron looked out at the young woman with an expression that had waited a very long time to be worn. "Forty-one names, Miss Vane. I wrote them once and the world burned them. If you've brought proof of what your father buried — and I think you have, or you wouldn't have crossed an icy river with a hole in your shoulder — then you and I are going to see those names carved somewhere that lasts. Understand? Not a fountain. Not a statue that freezes. Somewhere with the actual letters."

Rosalind stood among the dust and the dead ward's furniture, and Agnes watched something settle in her — not comfort, nothing so gentle as comfort, but resolve finding its footing, the way a wounded animal tests a leg until it holds weight.

"Tomorrow," Rosalind said. "I'll show you tomorrow. And Iversen. There are documents that have to be copied, and copies that have to be placed, and I can't do it alone and neither can he."

"Then tomorrow it is." Agnes lifted the lamp and started down. "Sleep if you can. Dream if you must. And mind the fourth stair — it groans like a scandalized vicar."

She locked the side door behind her and stood a moment in the falling dusk, breath smoking, listening to the wind off the river. Across the water, at the ferry crossing, she could see the small orange eye of a brazier where Vane's soldiers kept their watch, and beyond it, upstream, the lit windows of the garrison house where the colonel sat warm and certain behind his bought town.

Old women, Agnes thought, tucking the key into her mitten, had buried more dangerous secrets than this.

She had not gone twenty paces up the lane when she saw the lantern coming toward her — two men in garrison greatcoats, moving door to door through the mill district already, a day ahead of any schedule anyone had mentioned. The taller one raised his light.

"Evening, ma'am. Constable's rounds. You seen anyone unfamiliar pass this way?"

Agnes Hale, who had lied to husbands and priests and constables for sixty years and never once been caught, smiled her best grandmother's smile and prepared to earn her keep.
