## 9. The First Search

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, and Xanthe knew before he opened the door that it would not be a patient.

Patients knocked the way weather did — without ceremony, without apology, arriving already inside his day. This knock was measured. Three raps, evenly spaced, with a pause between each as though someone on the other side were counting. He stood in the surgery with the kettle just off the boil and looked at the door for the length of one full breath, and in that breath he heard the boots on his step shift their weight — two men, maybe three, standing not close together but arranged, the way soldiers arrange themselves when they have been told to stand somewhere rather than having chosen to.

He had spent twenty years learning to read silences. This one spoke plainly.

"Coming," he called, and made his voice the voice he used for old Mrs. Tarrow and fretful mothers and drunkards with split brows: unhurried, faintly bored, the voice of a man whose only concern this morning was whether he'd remembered to order more laudanum from the wholesaler in Marlow Bend.

He opened the door.

Two soldiers of the garrison stood on his step, and behind them, at the gate, a third leaned against the fence post with the studied idleness of a man under orders to look relaxed. The two at the door were young. The nearer one had a face like a peeled apple, all shine and no texture; the other was older, perhaps thirty, with the flat grey eyes of a man who had done this kind of work before and expected to do it again. Neither smiled. The older one held a folded paper.

"Nurse Iversen." Not a question. "Lieutenant Harkess." He did not offer his hand. "Colonel Vane's compliments. We're conducting a search of premises along the river road."

"A search." Xanthe let mild surprise sit on his face like frost on a window. "What for? Has there been a theft?"

Harkess unfolded the paper with the crispness of ceremony. It was an order, written in a clerk's careful hand, bearing the colonel's signature at the bottom — a signature Xanthe knew intimately, having watched Aldous Vane sign death warrants dressed up as casualty reports across three years and two hundred miles of front. The hand had not changed. Even now, seeing it, he felt the old field hospital smell rise in him: carbolic and mud and the sweet rot of gas gangrene. He put the smell away.

"—any persons entering the town since the first of December," Harkess was saying, "and any correspondence, documents, or papers relating to such persons. You'll admit us to search the premises. The colonel's compliments again."

"The colonel is thorough," Xanthe said, and stepped back, and held the door wide. "Come in out of the cold, Lieutenant. There's tea just boiled. I won't have it said that Vane's men froze on my step while I warmed my hands at my own fire."

Something flickered in Harkess's grey eyes — the small irritation of a man who has rehearsed a scene and finds the other player improvising. "That won't be necessary."

"Nonsense. My surgery is open to any man freezing to death, and you look half gone already. Your corporal's ears are white as candles." The apple-faced soldier touched his ear self-consciously, and Xanthe saw, with the particular satisfaction available only to men who have spent a lifetime managing the dying and the difficult, that courtesy could be a weapon as surely as a bayonet. "Sit by the stove. The kettle will want five minutes. A search takes as long as it takes; there's no sense in your catching pneumonia over it and costing me a week's nursing."

It was, he reflected, as he busied himself with cups and the good tea — the loose leaves he kept for patients' relatives, not the tin dust he drank himself — the oldest trick in his book, learned in a dressing station outside Ypres where a staff major had once arrived demanding to know why a captain's son had been listed among the dead when the family swore they'd had a letter. *Make them sit down,* Edmund Vane had murmured from his cot, grey-lipped and amused even then. *A seated man argues slower than a standing one.* Edmund had known his father's methods the way a son knows a father's fists.

Edmund. Xanthe poured the water and did not let his hands shake.

* * *

They searched while they drank, which told Xanthe something about how Vane ran things — nothing wasted, no motion without purpose. The apple-faced corporal went through the dispensary drawers while Harkess sat with his tea, and the arrangement meant that whatever the boy found or failed to find would be reported through the older man's filter. Xanthe catalogued it and filed it away. Men who filtered were men who could be managed.

"You keep a tidy establishment," Harkess said.

"I keep any establishment tidy. Untidiness kills. A mislabeled bottle has murdered more men than most regiments." He said it pleasantly, watching the corporal lift down bottles and squint at labels. "Mind the brown glass ones, Corporal. The third shelf is carbolic and the fourth is laudanum, and if you confuse them your colonel will lose a searcher and I'll lose a Tuesday."

The boy flushed and set the bottles back more carefully. Harkess almost smiled.

They worked through the front room. Through the consulting room, where the corporal pulled open every drawer of the desk and riffled patient records with fingers that smelled of boot polish. Xanthe drank his tea and talked — about the bridge closing, about the price of coal, about the influenza year and how the town had buried forty-one souls and he could name every one because he kept the register himself — and every sentence was a stone laid in a road leading away from the one thing he could not allow them to find. Talk, Edmund had said. Talk is a smoke curtain. Papa's officers never once looked past a man who wouldn't stop talking.

Under the floorboards beneath the examining table lay the ledger. Twenty years of names. Two hundred and eleven entries in his own upright hand: name, rank, regiment, date of death, date of official report, and in the final column, in a different ink, the single word that had cost him sleep for two decades — *omitted*, or worse, *altered*. Beneath it, sometimes, what the alteration had purchased. He had not opened the boards since the night Rosalind Vane collapsed bleeding on his doorstep. He had not needed to. He could feel the ledger through the floor the way a man feels a cracked rib — constantly, painfully, only when he moved wrong.

And Rosalind herself — that was the other wire stretched tight beneath the morning's civility. She had left before dawn, Agnes Hale walking her through the back lanes to the fever ward attic, wrapped in one of Agnes's ancient shawls, her wound still weeping through its dressing. If the soldiers found so much as her hairpin here, both of them were dead. If they found the ledger, everything Edmund had died believing in died with it.

"There's a cellar?" Harkess asked.

"Through there. Mind your head; the joists are low. I keep it for stores — the town gives me a side of pork each Christmas and I'm ashamed to say last year's is still hanging."

Harkess went himself. Xanthe noted that too: the lieutenant trusted no one's eyes but his own for the places that mattered. He followed him to the cellar door and stood talking in the doorway — blocking it, casually, a host showing off his larder — while Harkess walked the rows of shelving with a candle stub lifted high. Jars of preserved plums. Bandages in oilcloth. The pork, black with pepper. And against the far wall, under sacking, the crates Xanthe had carried down himself two nights ago, his arms burning, at three in the morning: Rosalind's documents. Her father's letters. The original casualty returns with their columns struck and rewritten. The proof.

Harkess stopped in front of the sacking.

Xanthe's heart did not speed up. Twenty years of practice had taught it not to; instead everything in him went very quiet and very clear, the way a ward goes clear at two in the morning when the breathing changes. He heard himself say, in the same bored tone: "The plum preserves are the third crate. Take one home to your mother if you've a mind — I put up forty jars and there's no wife here to eat them."

Harkess lifted the sacking.

The crates were there, four of them, stenciled in faded paint: *SURGICAL SUPPLIES — ST. BARTHOLOMEW'S, MARLOW BEND*. Inside the nearest, visible to the candlelight, lay bandages. Rolled, packed, ordinary. Because at four in the morning, after carrying the crates down, Xanthe had gone back upstairs, taken every document out again, and hidden them where no searching officer raised on regimental order would ever think to look: sewn into the ticking of his own mattress, page by page, flat as pressed flowers, with Rosalind's covering letter from her solicitor folded around them. The crates had been refilled with exactly what their stencil promised. A lie, he had decided, should be boring. Boring lies survive searches; interesting truths do not.

"Bloody heavy, these," Xanthe said. "Bandage wadding weighs a ton. The wholesaler cheats on the shipping weight and charges me for cotton and sends me bricks, near enough."

Harkess lifted a roll of bandaging, turned it over, set it down. He looked at the crates a moment longer — and Xanthe watched him weigh something in those grey eyes, some instinct, some itch — and then the itch lost to the arithmetic of a morning with thirty houses left on the list and a lieutenant who wanted his lunch.

"No papers down here?"

"Only these." Xanthe handed him a sheaf from a nail by the door: invoices, three years of them, dog-eared and dust-furred. Harkess glanced at the top one and gave it back.

They climbed the stairs. In the front room the corporal had finished the dispensary and stood waiting with nothing in his hands, and the third man came in from the yard stamping snow, reporting the shed empty, the coal house empty, the privy — he permitted himself a smirk — empty.

Harkess drained his tea and set down the cup with a click. "You'll understand, Nurse, that we may call again. Orders are orders."

"Orders always are." Xanthe saw them to the door. And there, on the step, with the snow falling fine and dry as sifted flour, he committed the small act of folly that he would later recognize as the hinge of the whole winter: he said, mildly, "You'll tell the colonel he's welcome to search me personally whenever he likes. An old army nurse has nothing in his pockets but lint and peppermints."

Harkess paused. Something moved behind the grey eyes — not suspicion exactly; recognition. The look of a man hearing an echo he can't place.

"You served under the colonel," Harkess said. "In France."

"Every man in this town served somebody somewhere, Lieutenant."

"Yes," Harkess said. "But you knew him. I saw it when you signed the visitor's book at the garrison dinner. You wrote your old regiment under your name." He settled his cap. "He didn't mention you. Good day, Nurse."

The boots crunched away down the path. The third man fell in behind. At the gate the corporal looked back once, touching his ear, and then the lane took them, and the snow began filling their footprints almost at once, as though the world itself were helping cover tracks.

Xanthe stood in the doorway until the lane was empty. Then he went inside, locked the door, and sat down slowly in the chair by the stove, and only then did his hands begin to shake — great, useless, animal shaking, the delayed bill for an hour of borrowed calm. He watched them tremble and thought: *that is fear, and fear is information.* Fear said they would come again. Fear said the next time there would be no tea, and Harkess's echo-recognition would have ripened into memory, and Aldous Vane would remember exactly who Xanthe Iversen was and what he had been positioned to see, twenty years ago, in a dressing station that officially never lost a man.

But beneath the fear, settling into it like sediment into water, was something harder and older and cleaner. He had sat through that hour performing calm, and the performance had worked, and working it had taught him the thing twenty years of silence had let him forget: he was good at this. Lying to power was a craft like any other, and he had been apprenticing at it his whole adult life without admitting it — every soothing word to a dying man, every gentle fiction to a grieving widow, every official form filled in with the truth carefully bent to fit what the living could bear.

No more bending. Not for Vane.

He knelt, pried up the floorboard, and drew out the ledger. Two hundred and eleven names. He laid it on his knee and did not open it. Instead he fetched pen and ink and a fresh quire of paper, and began, in a hand disguised into a schoolboy's roundness, to copy the first entry.

*Private Thomas Ellery. Died 14 March 1917. Reported 22 April 1917. Cause altered.*

By tonight, Agnes would carry the first pages to the fever ward. By tomorrow, the schoolteacher would have others. One copy might burn. Ten copies scattered through a snowbound town could not be burned by any colonel alive.

Outside, the snow thickened against the windows. Somewhere toward the garrison, Aldous Vane was reading Harkess's report, and somewhere in the disused fever ward, a woman with a bullet wound was waiting to learn if the winter would swallow them both.

Xanthe dipped his pen and wrote the second name.
