## 10. What Power Buys in a Small Town

The fever ward's attic had one window, and through its clouded glass Rosalind Vane spent her third morning in hiding watching her father buy a town.

She had not thought of it as buying before. That was the word Agnes Hale used, delivered without malice, the way one might describe weather or the price of eggs. *He buys them, dear. He's been buying them for twenty years.* Rosalind had bristled at it then — filial instinct dying slowly, like a fire that wouldn't take — but from the attic window, with the whole of Millbrook laid out below her like merchandise on a counter, she could no longer deny the arithmetic of it.

Below, on the frozen high street, Mr. Ellery Prewitt was sweeping snow from the steps of his general store. A small enough act, an honest act, the kind of thing a shopkeeper did every morning of his life without thinking. But he swept only the left side of the steps, and when Rosalind followed his gaze she understood: the right side belonged to the garrison. Two soldiers stood there in their grey coats, stamping against the cold, and Prewitt swept around them as carefully as a man mopping around royalty. When he finished, he went inside and came back out with a paper sack — coffee, by the way he held it, precious coffee, the real stuff that hadn't been seen in shops since the war — and offered it up to the soldiers with both hands and a bow of the head that was almost ecclesiastical.

One of the soldiers took it. The other patted Prewitt's shoulder, twice, like a man soothing a dog.

*There,* Rosalind thought. *That's what it looks like.*

* * *

She had come down to the town believing herself prepared. She had spent three years preparing — since the letter, since the solicitor's confession, since she'd sat in a rented room in Calais with her father's handwriting spread across the bed and understood at last what she'd always half-known about the shape of him. She knew the figures. She knew the dates. She knew which casualty reports bore his corrections and which signatures beneath them were traced rather than written. She could recite the names of the men whose deaths had been unmade so that her uncle's estate might pass cleanly into her father's hands.

She knew all of it, and she had believed that knowing would be enough. That truth, properly presented, properly witnessed, was a key that turned any lock.

Millbrook had been disabusing her of that belief for three days.

"Here." Agnes Hale came up the attic ladder with a tray — tea, bread, a heel of cheese — and set it down on the crate that served as a table. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a church pew, and she moved through the derelict ward below with the proprietary ease of a woman who had run it through two epidemics and a war. "Eat before it goes cold. And get back from the window; light shows."

Rosalind withdrew but did not leave the glass entirely. Below, Prewitt's son was hitching the store cart, and across the street Constable Brearley stood talking with the garrison sergeant, and the conversation had the particular posture — shoulders easy, hands loose, laughter shared at some private joke — of men who were not adversaries but colleagues. Brearley wore the town's blue. The sergeant wore her father's grey. Between them, on the trampled snow, lay the single set of footprints they'd made standing together, indistinguishable.

"When did it start?" Rosalind asked. "The — " she searched for a word that wasn't *bribery*, though bribery was precisely what it was. "The arrangement."

Agnes lowered herself onto the old cot with a grunt, arranging her skirts over knees gone thick with age. "Arrangement," she repeated, tasting the word. "That's kinder than mine. I call it the tithe." She poured the tea, steam rising between them. "It started small, the way these things do. The year after the bridge was built. Vane paid for the bridge, you know — or his money did. Then the school roof. Then the new wing on St. Ambrose's, though I'll note he named it after himself, which told me everything I needed to know about the man's piety. Then loans. Prewitt's store nearly went under in 'eleven; Vane carried the note. Brearley's house — same. Half the merchants on that street owe him more than they'll earn in a lifetime, and the other half owe him less but fear him more, which comes to nearly the same thing in the end."

"And they know," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question. "They must know what he is."

Agnes looked at her over the rim of her cup, and her eyes — milky at the edges, sharp at the center — held something between pity and contempt, and Rosalind couldn't tell which of them it was aimed at. "Miss Vane. What do you imagine a man is, that knowing would matter?"

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she had forbidden herself to do: taking inventory of her enemy's holdings.

It was easier than it should have been, because her father's generosity was not hidden. It was displayed. From the attic window she could see the brass plaque on the fountain — VANE MEMORIAL FOUNTAIN, though nothing about it memorialized anyone she recognized — and the banner over the schoolhouse door announcing the Colonel Aldous Vane Winter Relief Fund, and the new shutters on the widow Cottrell's house, painted that autumn, the paint still bright against the grey weatherboarding. Widow Cottrell's husband had died in the second year of the war. Rosalind wondered, with a coldness that surprised her, whether the woman knew that her husband's name had appeared on a list her father had personally amended — struck through, annotated, reclassified — before it ever reached the War Office.

Probably not. Probably Mrs. Cottrell blessed the Colonel's name every night over her new shutters.

Toward dusk, the garrison bell rang the change of watch, and Rosalind watched the evening transaction begin. It happened everywhere at once, a choreography so practiced it had become invisible to the town itself. Prewitt sent his boy across to the garrison with a hamper. The butcher — Halloran, she remembered his name from childhood summers here, a red-faced man who'd once given her a sausage end as a treat — carried out two geese, plucked and dressed, holding them aloft like offerings. Dr. Marsh, the physician who should have been Xanthe Iversen's rival and instead drank with him once a month out of professional courtesy, closed his surgery early and walked past the garrison house, tipping his hat to the sentry. Even the vicar, little Reverend Fenwick, paused on his rounds to exchange words with the duty officer, and whatever he said made the young man smile.

No money changed hands. No money needed to. The currency was older than coin: gratitude, obligation, fear wearing gratitude's coat.

"He gives them winter," Agnes said behind her. Rosalind hadn't heard her climb the ladder again; the old woman moved quietly for her size. "Coal, food, work for their sons in his timber concern. And in return they give him silence. Not because they're wicked, child. Because the ledger balances, and men will forgive a great deal to keep a ledger balanced in their favor."

"My brother's death didn't balance anything," Rosalind said. "Edmund's death was the entry. Everything since has been the erasure."

"I know it." Agnes said it simply, without softness, which Rosalind found herself grateful for. Softness would have undone her. "I nursed in that war too, before my hands gave out. I've read your Mr. Iversen's face when certain names are said in his presence. Whatever's under those floorboards of his — and don't look shocked, girl, I've known that man kept something down there since the day he moved in; a man doesn't check a floorboard with his boot heel every morning unless it matters — whatever it is, it weighs on him the way your papers weigh on you."

"You shouldn't speak of it. Even here."

"I'm seventy-three years old and I live above a dead fever ward. Who would I tell?" Agnes snorted. "But hear me, because this is the part you haven't learned yet, sitting up here with your documents like they're scripture. Papers don't convict men. People convict men. And every person in this town who could stand up and say what your father did owes him their livelihood, their house, their children's schooling, or their freedom. You've brought a bill of sale into a town where everything worth selling is already sold." She gathered the empty cup. "You need to find the one thing he hasn't bought."

"And what's that?"

Agnes paused at the top of the ladder, silhouetted against the grey window. "I don't know yet. But I knew your Uncle Thomas, rest him, and I knew Edmund, God keep him, and I'll say this much: a man who falsifies the deaths of soldiers is a man other soldiers remember. And there are more men in that garrison than the Colonel thinks who signed their names under his and never saw their friends' names printed after." Her voice dropped. "Grief keeps longer accounts than charity does, Miss Vane. Charity gets renewed every winter. Grief compounds."

* * *

Night came early that far north in December, and by four o'clock the streetlamps were lit — gas, another Vane gift, the pipes laid at his expense — and the town below became a stage of yellow circles in blue darkness.

Rosalind watched a constable make his rounds. Brearley walked slowly, unhurried, pausing to chat at doorways, and at each doorway the pattern repeated itself: warmth, deference, a small gift pressed into his hand or offered up — a twist of tobacco from Prewitt's, a mince pie from the baker's widow, a flask from the publican at the Ferry House. None of it was large. All of it was constant. Brearley accepted each offering with the weary graciousness of a man performing an office, and Rosalind realized she was watching not corruption in the vulgar sense but a system of tribute so thoroughly normalized that neither side any longer perceived it as a transaction. The constable was fed because the constable was fed. The Colonel was obeyed because the Colonel was obeyed. Ask any soul in Millbrook whether they had been bought and they would be genuinely, righteously offended — and then they would go on exactly as before, because the alternative required imagining a town without the fountain, the fund, the shutters, the coal, and imagination, Rosalind was learning, was the scarcest commodity in a place that lived eight months of the year inside a wall of ice.

Her father had understood something she had never grasped until now. She had thought power meant force — the garrison, the rifles, the men posted at the ferry crossing since the bridge closed. Force was the least of it. Force was expensive and visible and bred resentment. What her father had built was cheaper and stronger: a town that policed itself. Every merchant was a sentry. Every debt was a wall. If she walked into Prewitt's store tomorrow and asked for help, Prewitt would feed her, warm her, weep over her father's cruelty even — and send his boy straight to the garrison before her tea went cold, because his store, his house, his son's position at the timber works all hung from a single thread, and the thread ran through Colonel Aldous Vane's hand.

Justice, she thought, pressing her forehead to the cold glass, justice has no purchase here. You cannot spend truth in a town whose entire economy runs on its absence.

And yet.

She thought of Xanthe Iversen's face when she'd pulled the documents from her coat that first night, his hands shaking as he read — not with fear, she'd realized later, though there had been fear too, but with recognition. With relief, almost, terrible relief, the look of a man who has carried a weight alone for twenty years and sees someone else finally put their shoulder under it. He owed her father nothing. That was what made the nurse dangerous, and why her father's summons to dinner — Agnes had heard of it, the whole town heard of everything within a day — was not generosity but reconnaissance. He was trying to determine whether Iversen's floorboards held anything besides dust.

She watched the surgery's windows across the square, lamplit, curtains drawn. Somewhere behind them, a man was deciding — had perhaps already decided — whether twenty years of careful survival were worth a stack of names written in fading ink.

Below, a figure emerged from the garrison house and crossed the square toward the surgery. Tall, upright despite his years, fur-collared, moving with the unhurried certainty of ownership. Rosalind's breath fogged the glass and she wiped it with her sleeve, heart slamming.

Her father climbed the surgery steps and knocked, and after a long moment the door opened, lamplight spilling out across the snow, and Colonel Aldous Vane stepped inside and closed the door behind him.

The curtain in the surgery window twitched. Then the lamp went out.

Rosalind stood in the dark of the attic, her breath loud in the cold, and understood that whatever was decided in that room tonight, the winter — and everything hidden beneath its floor — had just begun to move.
