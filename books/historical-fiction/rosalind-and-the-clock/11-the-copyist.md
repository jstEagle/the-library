## 11. The Copyist

The fever ward's attic smelled of camphor and dust and forty years of boiled linen. Xanthe sat at a child's desk meant for a pupil half his size, his knees cramping against its legs, and by the light of a single shaded lamp he opened the ledger for the fourth time in twenty years.

Outside, the wind worried the eaves of the disused ward like a dog worrying an old bone. Below him, three floors down, Rosalind Vane slept in what had once been the nurses' quarters, her wound knitting under Agnes Hale's stubborn care. And across the river, invisible beyond the ice and the dark, Colonel Aldous Vane sat in his garrison house with a glass of brandy and a telephone line that still worked, because the colonel's comforts were always the last things winter touched.

Xanthe turned to the first page.

He had written it in 1899, in a dressing station that stank of chloroform and gangrene, in a hand far less steady than the one he wrote with now. *Private Thomas Ellery, 2nd Platoon. Died of wounds received 14 October. Reported: missing, presumed deserted.* The ink had browned. The lie beneath it had not aged at all.

He took a fresh sheet of paper — plain stock bought from the stationer's in lots of ten, spread across three months so no single purchase would seem odd — and began to copy.

*Name. Rank. Date of death. Manner of death. What was reported instead. Who signed the report.*

It was the last column that made his pen hesitate each time. *Who signed.* Because every falsified entry bore either Vane's own signature or the signature of a man who owed Vane his commission, and there was no way to copy those names without understanding what he was building. Not a record anymore. An indictment.

His hand did not shake. He noticed that with something like surprise. Twenty years ago, writing the original entries by lamplight while men moaned two cots away, his hand had shaken so badly he'd blotted half a page. But fear, he was learning, was like morphine: the first dose overwhelmed you, and after enough of it you could function inside it, think inside it, even work.

He copied until the lamp oil ran low. Then he folded the pages into an oilskin packet, blew out the light, and sat in the dark listening to the wind, feeling the strange new thing growing in his chest where dread had lived so long.

It took him a moment to name it. Hope. Fragile as a match flame in this drafty attic, but there.

* * *

Agnes Hale climbed attic ladders the way she did everything else — without ceremony and without apology, one hand on the rail, her breath arriving in efficient gusts. She was seventy-one years old and had nursed through three epidemics and one war, and she regarded the colonel of the garrison with the same clinical eye she brought to a suspicious sputum sample: interesting, potentially dangerous, best handled with gloves.

"Three pages tonight," Xanthe said, passing them over.

She read them standing, by the stairwell window's grey light, her lips moving slightly. When she reached the third page she stopped. "Wexford," she said. "Danny Wexford. His mother kept the bakehouse on Mill Lane when I was a girl. She used to give me the burnt loaves."

"He wasn't deserted," Xanthe said. "He bled out on a stretcher while they argued about whether he was worth the morphine. Vane wrote him up as a deserter because Wexford had seen the requisition books."

Agnes closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, they were dry, which somehow made it worse. "Mrs. Wexford died believing her boy ran. Believing it for forty years." She tucked the pages into the false bottom of her sewing basket, beneath thirty years of mending. "My sister lives in Ashcombe. She writes to me monthly and I write back. Nobody intercepts an old woman's letters about her rheumatism."

"You understand what happens if they find these on you."

"I've buried more people than the churchyard has room for, Xanthe. They can add me whenever they like." She patted his arm once, briskly, the way she'd pat a patient who was healing but not yet out of danger. "Copy your names. That's your work. Let mine be the hiding of them."

That night she sewed three pages into the lining of a shawl she was sending to Ashcombe for repair, and mailed it from the post office with a smile for the clerk, and told him her sister complained terribly about the cold.

* * *

The schoolteacher's name was Mr. Prewitt, and he was afraid of everything.

Xanthe had chosen him anyway, or rather had failed to choose anyone else, because Prewitt was the only man in town besides himself who could write a fair hand quickly, and because Xanthe remembered — from a conversation two winters past, beside the stove in the empty schoolhouse — the night Prewitt had drunk too much sherry at the harvest supper and said, apropos of nothing, that his brother had died in the war and the letter home had never seemed right, and that he'd written twice to the regiment asking how a man listed as killed in action could also be listed in the same dispatch as having deserted, and that no one had ever answered him.

Prewitt had been describing Private Reginald Cobb. Entry nineteen in the ledger.

Xanthe came to the schoolhouse on a Thursday evening, when the children had gone home and the janitor was elsewhere, carrying a tin of tea biscuits as pretext. He found Prewitt grading arithmetic at his desk, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the permanent wince of someone who expected the world to shout at him.

"Mr. Prewitt. Might I borrow a hour of your evening?"

"For — medical reasons?" Prewitt looked at his own hands as though they might have developed symptoms.

"I want to show you something. And then I want you to decide whether you ever saw it. Either answer is honorable."

He laid the ledger open on the desk between the arithmetic papers. Prewitt read the way he taught, Xanthe observed — carefully, moving his lips, going back — and when he reached the nineteenth entry his face went the color of the chalk dust on his sleeve.

"That's Reggie," he whispered. "That's my brother."

"Yes."

"They said he deserted." Prewitt's voice cracked on the word. "My mother went to her grave — she never said his name again. She said we wouldn't speak of a coward in this house." He pressed both hands flat against the desk as though the floor had tilted. "And it's — it says here he was shot trying to carry a wounded man out of the line. It says he was recommended for—"

"A commendation. Which Vane converted into a desertion charge, because Cobb had witnessed the requisition falsifications. There are thirty-one names in that book, Mr. Prewitt. Every one of them erased so that Aldous Vane could inherit an estate that belonged to his dead brother's line."

The silence stretched. Down in the yard a sled bell jingled past, some child being hauled home through the snow, and the ordinary sound of it made the extraordinary thing on the desk seem impossible.

"What do you want from me?" Prewitt asked finally.

"Your hand. You copy better than I do, and faster, and no one watches a schoolteacher sharpening his pens. I'll bring you entries. You'll make copies. Copies go where you'll never know, and that protects you — if you're questioned, you've seen nothing, done nothing."

"And if they search here?"

"They won't. Vane's men don't search schoolhouses. In their world, education is furniture." Xanthe held his gaze. "But I'll not pretend the risk away. If this unravels badly, it will unravel onto whoever holds the thread. Think on it a day. Tell me no, and I'll never mention it again."

Prewitt looked down at his brother's name a long moment. Then he pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him, squared it against the edge of the desk with the automatic precision of thirty years' teaching, and uncapped his ink bottle.

"Dictate," he said.

* * *

They built the web the way a spider builds, in the dark, patiently, each strand tested before weight is put upon it.

Agnes carried pages out in her sewing basket and her medicine parcels — she still compounded simples for half the widows in town, and no soldier alive would rummage through a package labeled *camphorated ointment, for Mrs. Dunmore's chest*. Three pages went to Ashcombe in January's second week. Five more followed. Her sister's farmhouse, Xanthe understood, was becoming an archive disguised as a correspondence about rheumatism and the price of wool.

Prewitt copied at night after marking homework, and hid his work inside the school's own records — blank examination booklets, folded flat, filed among seven years of their fellows. When a soldier had come to the door one afternoon asking whether the schoolmaster had seen strangers on the road, Prewitt had chattered at him for twenty minutes about attendance figures and the heating allowance until the man escaped, visibly grateful.

And Xanthe himself memorized.

This was the part he told no one, not even Agnes. Each night in the surgery, after the lamp was out, he lay on his cot and recited the ledger silently, name by name, date by date, lie by lie — all thirty-one entries, in order, forward and then backward, the way he'd once memorized pharmacology. If the originals burned and the copies were found and Agnes's letters were intercepted and Prewitt's exam booklets confiscated, the truth would still exist in one final, unsearchable location: the head of a tired nurse in a river town, waiting for spring.

Rosalind, when he visited the attic, watched him copy with an intensity that unsettled him. She sat wrapped in two blankets with her shoulder still bound, and she read over the fresh pages as though she could burn them into herself the same way.

"My father thinks silence is a structure," she said one evening. "Like a bridge. Load-bearing. Every person who keeps quiet is a pier holding it up." She touched the page where Private Ellery's name now existed in duplicate. "You're cutting piers."

"One strand at a time."

"How many strands before it matters?"

Xanthe thought of the town below — the miller who took the garrison's flour contract, the constable whose salary Vane quietly doubled, the innkeeper whose mortgage the colonel had bought and never called due. Power, in a small place, was simply debt arranged politely.

"Enough that when the thaw comes," he said, "there are more people holding the truth than holding their tongues."

Rosalind almost smiled. It was the first time he'd seen anything approach a smile on her, and it transformed her face the way sun transforms a frozen field — suddenly there was soil under there, and seed.

* * *

On the last Friday of January, Xanthe walked home from the fever ward through falling snow, and the walk itself felt different. For twenty years the streets of this town had been a map of his silences — here was the corner where he'd once changed the subject when a widow asked about her son; there was the garrison wall he'd learned not to look at. Now, walking home with nothing but the memory of fresh-copied pages warming his coat against his chest, the streets seemed to rearrange themselves around a different architecture. Agnes's kitchen. Prewitt's desk. A farmhouse in Ashcombe filling up with other people's griefs, faithfully transcribed.

It was not safety. He knew precisely how unsafe it was. Vane had doubled the patrols at the ferry crossing; soldiers stopped travelers now and examined cart beds with bayonets, politely, thoroughly. The trap was tightening by degrees, and Xanthe could feel it the way one feels weather change — pressure behind the ears.

But for the first time in twenty years, the silence was not his alone to carry. It had been broken into pieces, and the pieces were hidden in sewing baskets and examination files and an old woman's mail, and there were four people in the world now who knew the whole shape of the thing, and knowing, they had chosen not to look away.

Fragile hope, he thought. Hope like river ice — solid enough to cross if you moved carefully, treacherous the moment you forgot it was frozen water, and underneath it all, the current, running.

He let himself into the surgery, lit the lamp, and found the note on his desk.

It was unsigned. The handwriting was small and agitated, the letters leaning as if written in haste or in drink:

*I believe I am being followed. I have the pages you asked for but I dare not keep them another night. Meet me tomorrow at the ferry house, noon. Burn this.*

Beneath it, in the same unsteady hand: *P.*

Xanthe stood holding the note while the snow ticked against the window glass, and slowly, the warmth of hope drained out of him and left behind the older, colder familiarity of dread. Prewitt was careful. Prewitt was timid. Prewitt did not arrange meetings at the most exposed spot in the entire valley — a single building on the river's edge, surrounded by the very soldiers Vane had posted there, in broad daylight.

Unless Prewitt hadn't written it at all.

Xanthe fed the note to the stove and watched the flames take it, and outside, far off across the white fields, the ferry house stood dark against the river ice, waiting for noon.
