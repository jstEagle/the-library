## 12. Betrayal at the Ferry House

The schoolteacher's name was Miss Elspeth Wren, and she had hands that could not keep a secret.

Rosalind noticed it the first evening in the attic of the fever ward, when Agnes Hale brought her down through the back stair to meet the small circle of the willing: Xanthe Iversen, gray and steady as river stone; old Agnes with her knuckles like walnut shells; and a thin woman of perhaps thirty-five, spectacles fogging in the cold room, who kept smoothing her skirt as though the fabric might confess something if she didn't.

"Elspeth teaches at the parish school," Agnes said. "Forty children under her thumb. She can copy a page faster than any clerk in the county, and she's kept her mouth shut about worse than this."

"I haven't," Elspeth said, and laughed — a bright, brittle sound that had no bottom to it. "I mean — I have. Kept quiet. About things. What I mean is—" She pressed her lips together and nodded hard, twice, like a woman agreeing to a price already named.

Rosalind watched her across the candle stub burning on the ward's dusty table, and felt the first cold thread of dread go through her, fine as wire. She knew this kind of courage. It was the courage of people who had never been tested, who believed themselves brave because they had only ever imagined the test. Edmund had been like that in his letters from the front, before the letters changed.

*You'll see,* he'd written. *When I come home I'll tell them everything. Names and dates. They can't bury all of us.*

They had buried him without a stone.

* * *

By the second week of December the copying was well along. The ledger's pages had been photographed in Xanthe's memory and now lived again in four different hands: Xanthe's precise medical script, Agnes's cramped schoolroom capitals, Rosalind's own slanted pen, and Elspeth Wren's beautiful copperplate, which was the finest hand of the lot and which made each page look, Rosalind thought uneasily, official — look real, look like something worth killing for.

The plan was simple because simple plans were the only ones that survived. Copies would leave the town one or two at a time, carried by people with ordinary reasons to travel: the fishmonger's boy with his delivery sled, a farmer's wife going to market on the ice road before the thaw closed even that. Pages would reach the magistrate in Harrowgate, the newspaper man in Dunmore, a solicitor Rosalind trusted from her years before the estrangement. By spring there would be truth scattered so widely that no fire, no search, no colonel's signature could gather it all up and burn it away.

"It's like seeding a field," Agnes said, licking her thumb to turn a page. "You don't put all your grain in one sack."

Xanthe said little. He sat with his ledger open before him — the original, recovered from beneath the floorboards and now hidden somewhere Rosalind had not been told, deliberately not told, for her own protection — and he read names aloud sometimes, quietly, the way a man reads over a grave. Sergeant Thomas Merrick. Private Alfred Soames. Lieutenant James Vane.

That last name always stopped the room. Rosalind had learned not to flinch at it. Her uncle. The brother whose estate her father had inherited on the strength of casualty reports that said *missing, presumed dead* when they should have said *killed by his own colonel's order, left at the wire, denied his medals and his pension and his mother's mourning card.* Xanthe had been there. Xanthe had written the true numbers in a book no one was ever meant to read.

"You're certain about the ferry house tomorrow?" Xanthe asked Elspeth that night, folding the first bundle of copies into an oilcloth packet no larger than a prayer book.

"Certain." She pushed her spectacles up. "Tommy Brand goes across every Thursday with fish for the toll keeper's family. I'm to bring the school's mending basket — wool and such — and swap it at the toll house. Nobody looks in a mending basket. Nobody has ever looked in a mending basket."

*Nobody has ever looked*, Rosalind thought, *because nobody has ever had reason to.* But she said nothing. She had been running for three weeks; she had slept in ditches and burned her own photograph; she had learned that the world did not require her approval to proceed. And Elspeth Wren was kind, and loyal, and terrified, and those three qualities were pulling her in directions she couldn't yet feel.

* * *

Thursday came gray and windless, the kind of cold that made the river groan in the night like something turning over in its sleep.

Rosalind watched from the fever ward's high window, a gap in the frost she'd cleared with the edge of her thumbnail. Below, the town moved through its frozen morning — smoke from chimneys standing straight up in the still air, the black line of the bridge shuttered at both ends, sentries stamping at the gatehouse. And out past the last houses, the white plain of the frozen river, and on it two figures small as stitches: the fishmonger's boy hauling his sled, and behind him, hurrying, the mending basket swinging from Miss Elspeth Wren's arm.

She was too fast. That was the thing Rosalind noticed, the thing that turned her blood to ice water. Elspeth walked like a woman late for everything, like a woman whose body was trying to outrun her conscience. A person carrying nothing but wool does not walk that way.

At the edge of the ice road, where the garrison had posted its checkpoint since the bridge closed — two soldiers and a brazier, ostensibly watching for smugglers, actually watching for everything — the figures stopped. One soldier stepped forward. Routine. Rosalind told herself it was routine. The soldier waved the boy through after a glance at the fish crates; the boy went on toward the ferry house, small and unremarkable against the snow.

Then the second soldier took hold of the mending basket.

"No," Rosalind whispered, her breath blooming on the glass. "Put it down. Put it down and smile, you fool, smile—"

From this distance she could not hear what was said. She saw it instead, the way you see a wreck through dark water. Elspeth's free hand rising, fluttering — the universal gesture of *oh, it's nothing, nothing at all*. The soldier setting the basket down on the ice. Bending over it. Elspeth's other hand flying to her mouth. Then both soldiers looking up at her at once, and Elspeth taking a step backward, and another, her boots skidding slightly, her whole thin body saying in every language but words: *take me instead, take me, I'll tell you anything.*

"She's talking," Agnes said behind her. Rosalind hadn't heard the old woman climb the stairs; her joints cracked like green wood but her feet were silent as a cat's. Agnes stood beside her at the window, and Rosalind watched the old nurse's face do something terrible: nothing at all. Twenty years of wards and fevers had taught Agnes Hale to watch death approach with her hands still.

"They'll question her," Rosalind said. "She'll fold in ten minutes. She folded when you asked her to lie about the attendance register, she told me so herself, she thought it was funny—"

"Aye." Agnes's breath fogged the glass between them. "And now she'll give them Mr. Iversen inside of an hour, and us inside of two, unless we move first."

* * *

They did not move first. There was nowhere to move to, and moving was confession.

Rosalind spent the afternoon in the attic with the remaining pages, feeding them one by one into the stove while Agnes stood watch below, and the paper curled and blackened and let go of its ghosts — *Merrick, Soames, Vane* — into smoke that rose straight up the cold flue and vanished into a sky that kept all secrets. It was like drowning kittens. It was necessary. Her father had taught her, without ever meaning to teach her anything, that sentiment was a luxury purchased by other people's courage.

Xanthe came at dusk, hooded, letting himself in through the coal chute with the ease of a man who had been sneaking into buildings since before either of them was born. His face told her before he spoke.

"The courier got through," he said. "Tommy Brand. He's at the toll keeper's with the fish and none the wiser. The pages are on their way to Harrowgate in the boy's own sled, because the soldiers searched the basket and found wool." He almost smiled. "Elspeth's mending saved half our harvest."

"And the other half?"

Xanthe sat down heavily on the old ward cot, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and several he hadn't earned. "They took her to the garrison house. Not the cells — the parlor. Tea and a fire and Colonel Vane himself, being kind to her. Being *gentlemanly*." He rubbed his eyes behind his smudged spectacles. "I've seen Aldous Vane be gentle before, Rosalind. In Flanders. He was gentle with the chaplain too, right up until the chaplain asked one question too many about the casualty returns."

"He won't hurt her," Rosalind said. "He doesn't need to hurt her. She'll drown herself in apologies and tell him everything just to make the kindness stop." She knew it the way she knew her own pulse. Kindness, in her father's hands, had always been the sharpest instrument in the drawer.

* * *

It took less than a day.

Rosalind learned of it the way the town learned of everything: through the shopkeepers' mouths, through the changed rhythm of boots on the high street. On Friday morning there had been two soldiers at the ice road checkpoint. By Friday evening there were eight, and a lantern swung all night at the ferry house, and Constable Pell — who had spent twenty years finding reasons not to see things — was seen walking to the garrison house with his cap in his hands like a schoolboy summoned before the headmaster.

Saturday dawned white and silent, and with it came the knock.

Not at the surgery door this time. At the fever ward's back stair, one soft rap, then two — Agnes's signal, but wrong, hurried, the knock of someone whose hands had stopped obeying. Agnes opened the door on Xanthe, hatless in the cold, frost in his eyebrows, and behind him the street lay empty in that particular way streets lie empty when they are being watched.

"They've named me," he said simply. He came in and stood with his back against the door, as if holding it shut against what he'd brought with him. "Last night. Elspeth held out — I'll say that much for her, she held out through the tea and the sympathy and the offer of a transfer to a school in Dunmore. Held out until Vane showed her Tommy Brand's statement. The boy admitted swapping baskets at the toll house. After that she seems to have decided honesty was the last virtue left to her." He drew a breath. "She gave them the surgery. She gave them my name. She wept, I'm told, very prettily, and Vane comforted her, and this morning at nine o'clock I am summoned to the garrison house. Summoned, mind — not arrested. Summoned. To discuss, the note says, 'certain irregularities in the town's correspondence.'"

The silence in the attic was total. Somewhere below, the river boomed — the great plates of ice shifting, settling, testing their own weight.

"He knows about the ledger?" Rosalind asked.

"He knows pages exist. He doesn't know how many, or where, or whose handwriting is on them — Elspeth never saw the original, thank God, and the ones she copied she copied blind, thinking them hospital records. But he knows I wrote something once. He's known for twenty years that I kept a private book. He teased me about it, in France. Called it my book of grudges." Xanthe's laugh was dry as ash. "He thinks it's a grievance. He has never understood that it's a debt."

Rosalind looked at him across the ruined ward — at this gray, deliberate man who had carried her brother's dying request through two decades of careful, cowardly, honorable silence — and felt the noose draw tight around more than one neck.

"If you go to him," she said slowly, "he will offer you a bargain. Pardon for silence. He'll dress it up as friendship, as the war finally being over, as your pension and your practice and your good name. And part of you—" she held his gaze, refusing to soften it— "part of you wants to take it. You've wanted to take it for twenty years. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise."

Something moved behind Xanthe's eyes — pain, and acknowledgment, and beneath them a bedrock of something harder. "Part of me has wanted to take it every single morning since 1917," he said. "And every single morning I've gotten up and written one more name in that book instead. Go and tell Agnes to get you off the ice road tonight, whatever the weather does. Because by this time tomorrow, Rosalind, either I'll be sitting in your father's parlor drinking his sherry and lying beautifully—" he straightened, and buttoned his coat, and for a moment he was twenty-eight again and standing in a dressing station that smelled of carbolic and mud— "or I'll have told him no, and then neither of us will be safe in this town again. Either way, you cannot be here when he comes looking."

He paused at the coal chute, half in shadow, and looked back at her.

"For what it's worth," he said, "Edmund told me once that you were the bravest Vane of the lot. He said the rest of the family bought their courage, but yours was home-grown." A ghost of a smile. "Let's hope he was right. We're about to spend it all."

Then he was gone, down the chute and into the white street, walking — Rosalind saw through the frosted pane — not quickly, not slowly, but exactly like a man with nothing to hide, which was the most frightening walk of all.

Behind him, at the far end of the high street, the garrison house door opened. Two soldiers stepped out into the snow and fell in behind him, at a courteous distance, escorting him to his fate.

Rosalind turned from the window and began packing the last of the ledger copies into her coat.
