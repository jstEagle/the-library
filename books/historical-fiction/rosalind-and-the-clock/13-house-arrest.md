## 13. House Arrest

They came for him at dusk, when the lamplighter was still making his rounds and the snow had that soft blue quality of a wound gone numb.

Two soldiers at the door — Xanthe knew them both; the tall one, Brandt, had been in his surgery last March with a hand mangled in the sawmill's gears — and behind them, unannounced and unhurried, Colonel Aldous Vane himself.

"Doctor Iversen." Vane stood on the step with his greatcoat collar up and his breath smoking, looking for all the world like a neighbor come to borrow a cup of flour. "You'll dine with me tonight."

"I wasn't aware—"

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "You weren't. But you will dine with me all the same. Bring your coat. The walk is short, but it's bitter."

There were four more soldiers leaning against the surgery wall in the shadows, their rifles slung loose, their faces carefully blank. Xanthe looked at them, then back at the colonel, and understood with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water poured down his spine, that he was not being invited anywhere.

He fetched his coat. He paused at the floorboard where the ledger lay — or what remained of its hiding place; he had moved the pages days ago, distributed them like seed grain into other men's keeping — and he let himself feel the old reflexive fear rise and then settle. Nothing there. Nothing to find. He locked the surgery out of habit and followed the soldiers into the dark.

* * *

The garrison house sat above the town like a judge above a courtroom, its windows lit gold against the blue-black river valley. Once it had been the customs house, back when the bridge tolls mattered; Vane had taken it over during the war years and simply never given it back, the way he took everything, gently and permanently.

Inside, warmth and candlelight and the smell of roast pheasant. A table set for two. Silver polished bright. A decanter of wine the color of garnets.

"You honor me," Xanthe said, taking the chair Vane indicated.

"Nonsense." Vane poured without asking. "I honor myself, if we're honest. It has been years since anyone at my table could discuss the campaign at Gallipoli without flinching." He smiled. He had aged well, the colonel — silver at the temples now, a deeper set of lines around the mouth, but the eyes unchanged: pale, patient, and absolutely certain of their own rightness. "Sit. Eat. You're too thin. Nurses always are. They give away their own rations."

Xanthe ate, because refusing would have been a statement, and statements were ammunition. The pheasant was excellent. The wine was better than anything he'd tasted since before the war. That was the point of it, he understood. Everything about this evening was the point of it.

They talked of nothing for an hour. The ice on the river, thickening by the day. The miller's boy who'd broken his arm sledding — Vane had heard, of course; Vane heard everything in this town, which was precisely why they were here. The price of coal. The state of the road east. Vane spoke the way a chess player moves pawns in the opening: each remark innocuous, each remark also a probe, mapping the shape of the man across the table.

It was nearly ten o'clock when he set down his glass and said, "Do you remember Corporal Denham?"

Xanthe did not let anything move in his face. Twenty years of practice went into that stillness. "Denham. Third platoon?"

"Second section, but close enough. Big fellow. Farm hands. Took shrapnel at—" Vane waved vaguely southward, "—that place with the mill. You wrote to his mother."

"I wrote to many mothers."

"You did. It was noted. Noted favorably." Vane turned his glass slowly on the cloth, leaving a wet ring like a small pale moon. "His mother died in October. Did you know? There was no one left to write to. No one at all. That is the thing about time, Doctor — it solves so many problems on our behalf, if only we have the patience to let it."

"Is there a question in that, Colonel?"

"There is always a question in everything I say. It's why people find me tedious at dinner parties." Vane leaned back. "A stranger came over the mountain before the bridge closed. A woman. Wounded, I'm told. Someone sheltering her, someone feeding her, someone perhaps carrying messages for her. And I find myself thinking: who in this town would do such a thing? Who has the skill to dress a bullet wound, the house to hide a fugitive, and — this is the important part, Xanthe — the grudge?"

The name hung between them, though Vane hadn't spoken it. *Rosalind.* And beneath it, unspoken still but louder: *Edmund.*

"The roads bring all kinds," Xanthe said. "Peddlers. Deserters. Pilgrims, once, going to the shrine at Marlowe Ford."

"Pilgrims." Vane laughed softly, genuinely amused. "Yes. Well. If such a woman exists — and I am a reasonable man, I deal in probabilities, not certainties — then whoever is helping her should consider very carefully what she is. She is not her brother. Edmund was honest. Edmund believed things. This one believes only in grievance, and grievance is a fire that burns the hands that carry it." He refilled Xanthe's glass, uninvited. "And whoever is helping her should consider what she offers them. Justice, probably. The word costs nothing. It's the finest currency in the world because it never has to be minted."

"And what does the Colonel offer?"

Vane smiled again, slower this time, and Xanthe felt the trap close with the soundless elegance of everything the man had ever done.

"Ah. Now we arrive somewhere." He rose, crossed to the sideboard, and came back with a leather folder, which he laid beside Xanthe's plate without opening it. "Your lease. On the surgery. Which, as you know, I hold through the charitable trust. Renewed for life — your life, not the trust's. Backdated to September, so the matter is settled regardless of what any solicitor says. The rent forgiven. In perpetuity."

Xanthe looked at the folder and did not touch it.

"There is more," Vane continued, settling back into his chair with the ease of a man laying out cards he already knows will win. "In the spring, Dr. Hargreave retires from the county hospital board. I control three seats. I can put you on that board. A salary. A title. A warm room in winter and colleagues who address you as *Doctor* with the capital letter audible. You could leave this freezing river town forever, if you wished. Or stay in it, comfortable, respected, untouchable. Your choice. All of it yours. All of it, in exchange for—"

"Don't," Xanthe said quietly.

"—for nothing," Vane finished smoothly. "For silence, which is not nothing; silence is the most expensive commodity in the world, and I am offering to buy yours at a fair price. For forgetting, which is cheaper. For a signature on a witness statement, if it comes to that, attesting that no stranger passed through your surgery in November. One line of ink, Xanthe. I've seen you write longer prescriptions."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a clock counted the quarter hour with the patience of geological time.

"You think I can be bought," Xanthe said.

"I think," Vane said, "that you have already been paid. For twenty years. Paid in safety, in quiet, in the respect of this town, in the privilege of pretending. I am not buying something new from you. I am continuing a subscription." He leaned forward, and for the first time all evening the pleasantness dropped away entirely, and Xanthe saw beneath it the officer who had signed the orders, the heir who had burned the letters, the father who had disinherited a daughter and called it discipline. "I know about Edmund's promise. You think I don't, but I was there when he died. I held his hand. He made everyone swear things in his last week — the chaplain, the orderly, the girl who brought his water. Dying men distribute promises like a drunk distributes coins. Nobody keeps them. It would be charming if it weren't so tiresome."

Something went very cold and very still in Xanthe's chest. Not fear. Something older and harder than fear.

"He trusted you," Xanthe said. "He told me you'd changed. He told me the reports were a mistake, a clerk's error, that you'd fix it."

"And I did fix it. I fixed it exactly as much as fixing was possible." Vane spread his hands, reasonable to the last. "War is a ledger, Doctor. You of all people understand ledgers. Some columns balance and some don't, and a commander who cannot bear to look at the red ink gets everyone killed. I saved lives by falsifying those reports. The estate was the mechanism — the money, the influence, the standing to protect the regiment, to protect this town, to protect *you*, sitting in your warm surgery writing to grieving mothers while I did the arithmetic nobody else had the stomach for." He picked up the folder and slid it across the cloth until it touched Xanthe's knuckles, cool leather against cold skin. "Take it. Take the lease, take the board seat, take the spring. Tell me in the morning that no one came to your door, and live out your life as the good man this town already believes you are. Or refuse, and become a footnote. I have buried more significant men than you under less significant circumstances."

Xanthe looked at the folder. And here was the terrible thing, the thing he would carry with shame for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be: part of him wanted it. Part of him — the tired part, the part that had been afraid every single winter for twenty years, that had hidden the ledger beneath the boards and lain awake listening to the wind and thought, *one more year of silence, one more, and then I'll speak* — that part of him reached for the warm room and the safe spring and the blessed ending of fear.

Twenty years of safety, offered back like a bribe. Except it was worse than a bribe. It was an accounting. Vane was showing him the invoice for two decades of complicity, itemized and totalled, and asking him only to keep paying.

He took his hand off the table.

"Colonel," Xanthe said, "when I was nineteen years old, my father sold our farm to cover his debts and told us it was for our own good. I have spent my whole life recognizing that voice." He stood. His legs were steady, which surprised him. "I'll thank you for the dinner. The pheasant was excellent. As for your papers — I don't sign things I haven't read, and I don't read things offered at gunpoint, even politely."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. Then he sighed, and the mask of geniality settled back over him like snowfall, soft and complete.

"Splendid," he said. "Dinner conversation is so rare these days. Someone who disagrees with me." He rang a small bell on the sideboard. "You'll stay the night. The east room is prepared — it has the best view of the bridge, I always thought. Consider it hospitality."

"Consider it what it is," Xanthe said.

"I shall consider it whatever is most convenient," Vane agreed pleasantly. At the door, the soldiers waited. Brandt wouldn't meet Xanthe's eyes. "Oh — and Doctor. Your surgery. I've taken the liberty of posting a man outside it. The streets are treacherous this time of year. We wouldn't want anyone breaking in and stealing your instruments."

Or finding what isn't there anymore, Xanthe thought, and was almost grateful for the cold walk upstairs, because it kept his face from doing anything at all.

* * *

The east room was beautiful. That was the cruelest detail. A wide bed with a quilted counterpane, a fire already laid, a ewer of water, a window overlooking the frozen river where the ice stretched white and vast toward the far shore, and beyond it, faintly, the black line of the woods where Rosalind had crossed.

House arrest dressed as courtesy. He set his pocket watch on the sill and sat in the chair by the window and did not sleep.

He thought of Edmund instead. Edmund at the end, gray-faced and burning, gripping his wrist with a strength that had come from nowhere and would last only minutes. *You keep the names,* Edmund had said. *Someone has to keep the names.* And Xanthe had kept them — oh, he had kept them, copied in his careful nurse's script, thirty-one names, thirty-one men who existed everywhere except in the official record, whose mothers had received letters about wounds instead of deaths, whose pensions had been denied because officially they had never fallen. He had kept them under a floorboard like a disease he couldn't cure.

And now the ledger was ash in Vane's hands — no. Not yet. Not yet. The original still lay in its new hiding place, three copies of its pages scattered among Agnes Hale's linen press and the schoolmaster's lesson books and a tin of tea in the ferry house. Vane thought he was holding Xanthe's silence. He didn't know he was holding nothing at all.

Toward dawn, Xanthe heard boots on the stair, measured and unhurried, and a soft knock.

He opened the door on Agnes Hale, bundled in wool, a covered basket on her arm, and behind her in the corridor a soldier who looked profoundly embarrassed to be escorting an elderly lady at five in the morning.

"Matron Hale," Xanthe said. "What brings you out in this cold?"

"Fever ward inventory," Agnes said clearly, loudly, for the soldier's benefit. "The colonel asked for a full accounting before the thaw." Then, quieter, meeting Xanthe's eyes with a steadiness that had survived forty years of wards and dying men: "And I brought you breakfast, since I heard you were staying. Bread, butter, and — you'll want to check the bottom of the basket. The eggs are fresh."

She handed it over. Their fingers brushed the handle. Her face gave away nothing at all.

Under the cloth, wrapped in a napkin still warm from her oven, Xanthe found bread, butter, two brown eggs —

and a folded slip of paper in a handwriting he knew, sharp and slanted and unmistakably hers:

*They're moving me tonight. Ice road. Don't follow. Finish it here.*

Rosalind.

Xanthe folded the note into his sleeve and looked out the window at the frozen river, white and silent and beginning, at its edges where the current ran fastest, to whisper.
