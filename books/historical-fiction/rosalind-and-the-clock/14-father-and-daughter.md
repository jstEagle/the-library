## 14. Father and Daughter

They came for her on the ninth day of her confinement at the garrison house, when the snow had stopped pretending it would ever stop and lay against the windows in grey drifts like something buried.

Rosalind heard him before she saw him — the particular cadence of his boots on the corridor boards, unhurried, deliberate, the walk of a man who had never once needed to hurry in his life. She set down the book she hadn't been reading and stood. It seemed important, suddenly, not to be sitting when he entered. Not to be smaller than she had to be.

Colonel Aldous Vane closed the door behind him with a soft click, as though the room were a sickroom and noise might disturb a patient.

"Rosalind."

"Father."

He looked older than she remembered. That was the first treachery of it — she had spent eleven years building him out of fury and ink, a monster drawn large across the whole of her grievance, and here was only a tall man of sixty-one with silver coming in unevenly at his temples and the same tired grey eyes that had once watched her ride without holding the reins. He wore civilian clothes. She had expected the uniform; somehow that was worse. The uniform would have been an admission that this was war between them. The tweed jacket insisted it was family.

"You've been keeping well," he said. It was not quite a question.

"As well as one keeps under guard."

"The guards are for your protection." He crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back, and looked out at the white street, the shuttered ferry house, the river lying under its ice like a held breath. "The town has moods in winter. Confined men drink. I wouldn't have you jostled."

"I wasn't jostled when I was shot, Father. I was shot deliberately, by your own man, on your own road."

"By a poacher who has since been dealt with." He said it mildly, the way he might correct a child's arithmetic. "You were seen leaving Harrow Lane at dusk in a stolen coat. In 1913 that was enough to ruin you. In 1919, with half the country's sons unaccounted for, a woman walking away from a house is merely assumed dead. Grief is efficient that way." He turned from the window. "I paid for the stone myself. Quite a handsome one. You'd have liked the inscription."

"I read it," Rosalind said. "You wrote *beloved daughter*. You hadn't spoken to me in three years when you wrote it."

"Grief is also generous." A faint smile touched his mouth and did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Sit down, Rosalind. Please. This will take some time, and you'll want your legs under you."

She did not sit. He inclined his head slightly, accepting the point, and took the chair himself instead — arranging his long frame into it with the ease of a man settling before his own hearth. That ease enraged her more than any threat could have. He owned the room. He owned the house, the street, the frozen river, the constable's silence and the miller's gratitude and the schoolmaster's careful blank face. Eleven years of remittances and subscriptions and charitable endowments had bought every door in this town, and now he sat down inside hers as if it were the most natural thing in the world, because it was.

"I'll come to it plainly," he said, "because I know how you dislike being managed. You came here carrying papers. Letters of administration from the War Office, copies of casualty returns, the 1916 ledgers from the field hospital at Saint-Aubin — yes?" Her face must have moved, because he nodded, satisfied. "And a nurse named Iversen, who kept his own accounts. I've known about Iversen's little ledger for years, incidentally. Longer than you have. It's why he was permitted to settle here rather than being posted somewhere inconvenient. A man who keeps dangerous paper is best kept where you can watch the drawer."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the season. *Xanthe.* All those nights at the surgery, all those pages copied and smuggled — had they been watched from the beginning? Had Agnes's attic been a cage with a kindlier name?

"You're wondering how much I know," Vane said, reading her again, always reading her. "Enough. Not everything. I am not omniscient, whatever this town believes. But I knew you were alive within a month of your so-called death. A girl with your handwriting doesn't vanish; the pawnbroker in Ashford recognized the script on a railway parcel label. I let the stone stand. It was convenient for both of us — you dead could move freely where you living would be pursued. And I confess—" here he paused, and something almost human flickered through the weariness of his face— "it spared me having to explain you to anyone. Your disappearance was the last honest thing in my life, Rosalind. Everyone believed it. Even me, some days."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because we are finally in a position to speak honestly, and I find at my age I'm starved for it." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here is the truth you came to dig up, laid out by my own hand so that you needn't waste the winter excavating. Yes. I altered the returns. Edmund's battalion was withdrawn from the line at Verdun on the fourteenth of September, 1916 — withdrawn, not destroyed — and the men lost in the covering action were written off under another company's number, a company whose muster rolls had already burned. Dead men with no paperwork are infinitely useful, Rosalind. They can die twice. They can die wherever the arithmetic requires."

"And their mothers," Rosalind said quietly, "received no pension. No notification. Some of them wrote to the War Office for years. One of them is buried in the churchyard here under a stone that says *missing*."

"Yes," said her father, without flinching, and she hated him for the absence of flinch. "Mrs. Corbett. I endowed the almshouse wing partly in her name. She believes it was charity. It was bookkeeping."

"Why? Edmund was alive. His inheritance — the estate passed to you regardless, since he was disinherited before the war. What did falsifying his reports buy you that grief wouldn't have?"

For the first time, the composure shifted. Small as a tremor in still water, but she saw it, and she understood suddenly that she had asked the one question he had prepared no answer for.

"Edmund was not disinherited," Vane said at last. "That is what you were told. What your mother told you, God rest her, because it was kinder than the truth. Edmund refused the estate. Formally, in writing, from the front — a letter through his solicitor renouncing his claim, dated August 1915. He wanted nothing that came from me. He said—" and here the colonel's voice did something she had never heard it do, something dry and cracked, like paper folding— "he said the land was soaked in other people's money and he could feel it through his boots. So the renunciation was genuine. But a renunciation can be revoked. If he survived the war, he could come home at twenty-three with a chest full of medals and a legal claim, and there was nothing I could do to stop him except be glad to see him. And I would have been glad to see him. Do you understand that? Whatever else I am — and I am everything you think I am, and worse — I would have opened that door."

Rosalind found she had gone very still. Outside, wind dragged a fine smoke of snow along the eaves.

"But he didn't survive," she said.

"He did not survive the covering action, and the covering action did not officially exist, and therefore my son's death — which broke me, Rosalind, which broke something in me that has not healed and will not — occurred nowhere at all. No death notice. No effects returned. No grave with his name. Because I signed the alteration myself, on the twentieth of October, 1916, in an office in Rouen, with a pen I still own." He looked at his hands. "I told myself I was protecting the estate from litigation. I told myself a great many things. The truth is simpler and fouler: I could not bear for Edmund's death to be real, and so I made it administratively imaginary. And then I discovered that an imaginary death was worth nine thousand acres."

The room was silent. Somewhere below, a soldier coughed; a log settled in a grate.

"So you see the shape of the trap," Vane went on, recovering himself, the composure sliding back over him like a visor. "If those documents go to a tribunal, the tribunal does not merely learn that I falsified reports. It learns that Colonel Vane erased his own son from the rolls of the living to secure an inheritance — and every man who died unnamed becomes a monument to my monstrousness, and the estate passes to the Crown or the creditors or whichever committee of clerks adjudicates these things, and the Vane name ends in a filing cabinet. That is what your justice costs, Rosalind. Not my disgrace. My *extinction*. And yours with it. Your mother's name too."

"Good," she said.

He smiled at that — genuinely, briefly, and it was the most frightening thing he had done yet. "Yes. I thought you'd say that. Your brother would have said it too. You have his jaw when you're angry." He rose, walked to the bureau against the wall, and unlocked the lower drawer with a small key from his watch chain. From it he took two things: a leather folio, and a folded newspaper cutting gone soft at the creases. He set them on the table between them but did not push them toward her, as though offering food to a bird he didn't want to startle.

"Inside the folio: full deed to Harrow Lane, free and clear, transferred to you by instrument already drafted and witnessed — notarized Tuesday last, before either of us spoke a word. The estate entire. Nine thousand acres, the house, the tenancies, the timber rights. Yours. Not because you recanted — I won't insult you by making the deed conditional, you'd smell the condition and refuse on principle. It is unconditional. It exists. It has existed since before you reached this town." He tapped the newspaper cutting. "And this is the obituary of Captain Edmund Vane, printed in the county gazette, November 1916 — the version I suppressed. The true one. With the action named, the date, the place. I wrote it myself and then locked it in that drawer for three years because I could not bear the world to know my son had died in a battle I had officially declared never happened."

Rosalind looked at the cutting and did not touch it. To touch it would be to let him see how badly she wanted it. Edmund's name in print. The action named. The proof that her brother had lived and died and mattered — the very thing she had crossed half the country in a stolen coat to obtain, offered to her on a table by the man who had hidden it.

"What do you want in exchange for things you've already given me?"

"Nothing so crude as recantation. Recantation is for cowards and you'd despise it and so would I." He stood by the window again, his back to her, watching the ice-bound river. "I want silence with a deadline. Give the winter to the tribunal's convening authority — a letter, unsigned even, simply stating that the documents in question were examined and found to be forgeries of postwar manufacture. One page. Then take your estate and live on it however you please, and I will take the blame to my grave with a dignity the record allows, and the families of those men will go on believing what believing has cost them less to believe. Mrs. Corbett will keep her almshouse. The town will keep its benefactor. Nothing will change except that you will be rich, and warm, and *home* — truly home, Rosalind, in the house where your brother's boots are still by the door, because I never let them move them."

Her breath caught despite herself. The boots. She could see them — cracked leather, left by the kitchen door, mud from a summer lane that no longer existed anywhere on earth except in her father's stubbornness.

"That is the seduction," Vane said, turning, and his eyes were clear and terrible. "Not the acres. The boots. I know what you've lost, because I lost it too, and I am offering you the only cure that has ever worked for either of us: a house that remembers. Come home. Be my heir. Let the dead rest in the fiction that shelters them. It is what I chose, and look — I am still standing. Still respected. Still *your father*, which is more than most men in this century can claim."

Rosalind walked to the table. She picked up the newspaper cutting — slowly, letting him watch her want it — and read Edmund's name in the old black type until she had memorized the shape of every letter. Then she folded it along its worn creases and put it in her pocket.

"The obituary I keep," she said. "It belongs to me. It always did."

"And the deed?"

"Keep it. Frame it. Nail it over the fireplace at Harrow Lane, next to the boots." She met his eyes. "Because here is what you have never understood, Father, in sixty-one years of buying things: the deed isn't the trap. The trap is that you believe everyone can be bought, and so you have spent eleven years purchasing witnesses instead of answers, and now the tribunal comes in spring and there will be no one left in this town whose loyalty you actually trust — including, I suspect, yourself. You didn't offer me this because you love me. You offered it because you are afraid. And fear, in my experience, is the one currency that inflates."

His face did not change. That was the horror of him; his face never changed. But his hand, resting on the back of the chair, tightened until the knuckles went the color of the snow outside.

"You'll freeze this winter in that attic above the fever ward," he said softly. "Did you imagine I didn't know about Hale's attic? I have known since the night you arrived. Iversen's lamp burned past two o'clock, and old women gossip even when they think they're conspiring. I could end this tonight with a word to Major Reeve. Remember that, when you weigh my generosity."

"Then why haven't you?"

"Because I wanted my daughter back," Aldous Vane said, "before I destroyed her. Is that not what fathers are for?"

He left the way he had come, unhurried, the door closing with its soft click, and Rosalind stood alone with the fire dying and Edmund's obituary pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

It was only after a long while, when the light had gone blue and then grey, that she noticed what his parting words had confirmed without meaning to: he knew about the attic. Which meant he knew about Agnes. Which meant the search, when it came, would come there first.

She was moving toward the bell-pull to summon her guard — to stall him, to ask for tea, anything — when she heard, far off across the frozen town, the fever ward's bell begin to ring.

* * *

It rang wrong. That was Rosalind's first coherent thought as she pressed her face to the frosted glass: the fever ward's bell had hung silent for six years, and now it rang fast and ragged, no rhythm to it at all, the way a bell rings when the person pulling it is frightened — or performing fright. Down in the yard, sentries were spilling out of the guardhouse buttoning their coats. Someone was shouting about water. About quarantine. About typhoid.

Rosalind put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the folded obituary, and beneath the fear — sharp and bright and utterly new — she felt something she had not felt since the night she collapsed half-dead on a nurse's doorstep.

Hope.

Old Agnes Hale was ringing hell out of that bell, and Rosalind intended to be gone before it stopped.
