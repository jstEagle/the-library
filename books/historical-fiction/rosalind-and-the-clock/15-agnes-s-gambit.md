## 15. Agnes's Gambit

Agnes Hale had buried more people than the town had streets, and she had learned long ago that the dead kept better secrets than the living.

She sat in her kitchen at the edge of the charity grounds, hands wrapped around a cup of tea that had gone cold an hour ago, and considered the shape of the thing she was about to do. Outside, the snow had stopped at last, and the light coming off the drifts was so clean and white it made the inside of the window glass look dirty by comparison. Somewhere down the hill, in the garrison house with its lit windows, Colonel Aldous Vane was drinking port and believing himself safe behind his walls of bought loyalty. And above the disused fever ward, in a room that smelled of dust and camphor and forty years of old sickness, his daughter was hiding with a bullet scar on her shoulder and a satchel full of papers that could hang him.

Agnes had known Rosalind Vane since the girl was six years old, all elbows and scraped knees, sneaking into the fever ward to "help" — which meant sitting with the dying children and reading them stories in a voice too loud for sickrooms and exactly right for frightened ones. The colonel had forbidden it, of course. Vane forbade everything he couldn't control. But the girl had come anyway, slipping past the kitchens like smoke, and Agnes had let her, because a ward full of dying children needs someone who isn't afraid of them.

That was thirty years ago. The child was gone. What sat in Agnes's attic now, wrapped in two blankets and burning fever from a wound that wanted proper care, was something harder and colder and more dangerous — and Agnes found she loved her no less for it.

The problem was simple, and simply impossible: Rosalind Vane could not stay where she was, and she could not leave the way any ordinary person would leave. Xanthe Iversen was under house arrest in the garrison guest room, drinking Vane's sherry and smiling Vane's smiles while the noose drew tight around the whole enterprise. The schoolteacher had been questioned. A courier was in custody. Soldiers walked the river road in pairs, and the ferry crossing had two men posted day and night with orders to turn back anyone heading toward the county seat without papers signed in the colonel's own hand.

Vane thought he had closed every door.

Agnes set down her cold tea and smiled at it, a small private smile, the one she used when she was about to tell a family news they didn't want to hear and had decided not to soften it.

He had forgotten one door. He had forgotten that Agnes Hale was seventy-one years old, that she had survived three epidemics and two wars' worth of wounded men passing through her wards, and that in this town there was no authority on God's earth — not constable, not garrison, not colonel — that would overrule her judgment in a matter of contagion. Fear of disease outranked fear of officers. It always had. It was the one law everyone obeyed without being asked.

If you wanted to move a person through a locked town, you didn't disguise her as a soldier or hide her in a cart. You made her a plague.

* * *

She went up to the attic that evening with a tray of broth, knocking twice softly, then once — their old signal from the epidemic winter of '89, when the ward had been full and the girl too young to see what Agnes saw when she looked at the rows of small faces.

Rosalind lifted the blanket away from her face. The fever had broken in the night, thank God, but it had left her hollow-eyed, the skin stretched over her cheekbones like paper over a drum frame. She looked, Agnes thought, remarkably like her uncle Edmund must have looked in his last hour — and she pushed that thought away, because Edmund Vane was not hers to mourn. He was Xanthe's ghost, carried twenty years.

"You've been crying," Rosalind said.

"I'm old. My eyes water. It's a mechanical failure." Agnes set the tray down and lowered herself onto the crate beside it, joints complaining. "Eat. All of it. You'll need your legs tomorrow."

Something moved behind the girl's eyes — hope trying to surface through exhaustion and suspicion, like a swimmer under ice. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow." Agnes looked at her hands. They were steady. Good. Old hands lied often enough; it would be a poor time for hers to start. "There's a supply wagon comes to the charity grounds Thursday mornings. Flour, coal, lamp oil. It goes out through the north gate past the garrison checkpoint, because the north road is the only one the drifts haven't fully closed, and it turns south at the crossroads before the ferry landing. Two drivers. Both of them owe me their mothers' funerals, which in this town is a debt of some weight." She paused. "You'll be on that wagon. And you'll be the most contagious woman in the county."

Rosalind stared at her. "I don't—"

"Typhoid," Agnes said, and enjoyed, just slightly, the way the word landed. "A case of typhoid fever has appeared in the old fever ward. Highly infectious. The matron — that's me, girl, keep up — has declared quarantine and ordered the immediate evacuation of the charity grounds for fumigation. Everyone will be moved out in a panic, which means nobody will count heads, which means one extra body under a tarpaulin in a supply wagon will be seen by no one. The soldiers at the gate won't come within ten feet of us. They'll wave us through with their handkerchiefs over their faces and thank their stars we're leaving."

"And after?"

"After the crossroads, the wagon slows where the road bends by the Hollis fence line. You drop off, cut east across the fields to the mill track, and walk the river path north. It's eleven miles to the county seat. You can make it before dark if the wind is kind and your legs are willing." Agnes fixed her with the look she'd used on forty years of nurses who wanted to skip hand-washing between patients. "Which brings me to your feet. Let me see them."

"They're fine."

"They are not fine; I can smell the neglect from here. Boots off."

It was absurd, and they both knew it — the disinherited daughter of the most powerful man in the valley, sitting in a dusty attic having her blisters dressed by a septuagenarian matron as though she were a charity child with chilblains. But Rosalind pulled off the boots, and Agnes worked in silence for a while, wrapping the worst heel in linen, and the silence was companionable in the way only silence between women who have both outlived their illusions can be.

"He offered me the estate," Rosalind said suddenly, to the wall. "My father. Three nights ago. Through an intermediary, then in person. He knew I was alive the whole time, Agnes. He's known for months." Her jaw tightened. "All I had to do was recant. Sign a statement saying the documents were forged, say I'd been manipulated by Nurse Iversen, go home, be his daughter again. The estate, the name, everything restored."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'd rather inherit nothing than inherit him." A pause. "He said I'd change my mind by spring. He said everyone does."

Agnes tied off the bandage and sat back. "Your father has spent thirty years buying this town one loyalty at a time, and he believes that's how the world works — that everything is purchasable if you find the right price. He bought the merchants. He bought the constables. God help us, he very nearly bought Xanthe, offering him back his safety like a man returning a borrowed coat." She stood, slowly, knees cracking like green kindling. "But he never bought me. Do you know why? Because I was burying his soldiers' names off the books before he finished falsifying the ledgers. He thinks charity is a leash. I have watched too many people die to mistake ownership for kindness."

Rosalind looked up at her, and there was something raw in her face, something younger than the woman with the satchel and the fury. "Why are you doing this? You could lose everything. Your position, the charity, your pension—"

"My dear girl." Agnes gathered the tray. "In 1892 I held a boy named Thomas Wren while he died of a wound that should never have killed him, because his company was written off as deserters so an officer wouldn't have to explain a failed advance. I have spent my whole life sweeping up after powerful men's arithmetic. I am seventy-one years old. I am owed one act of open defiance, and I intend to enjoy it thoroughly." She paused at the attic ladder. "Sleep. Tomorrow we make this town believe in ghosts."

* * *

Thursday came grey and windless, the sky the color of dishwater, perfect weather for a lie.

At half past seven, Agnes Hale threw open the doors of the disused fever ward, rang the charity bell — the great cracked bronze bell that hadn't sounded since the epidemic winter — and began, in a voice pitched to carry across the frozen grounds and into every listening kitchen on the lane, to raise hell.

"Typhoid! Typhoid in the old ward! Get your things, get the children, get OUT!"

It was, she reflected later, the finest performance of her long career, and she had once talked a drunken army surgeon through an amputation by describing, in loving detail, his mother's opinion of drunkards.

By eight o'clock the charity grounds were chaos. Mothers bundled children into blankets. The cook wailed about her stores. Two elderly residents had to be physically carried, protesting, into the courtyard. Agnes moved through it all like a general — barking, directing, draping herself theatrically against the ward doorframe whenever anyone came near, pressing the back of her hand to her brow and announcing that *she* would stay with the contaminated building until it could be limed and fumigated, because someone had to, because duty was duty.

Word traveled faster than the bell. Within the hour, half the town knew the fever ward had opened its curse again, and fear did the rest. Merchants shuttered their shops rather than risk customers walking past the charity grounds. The constable sent word he was keeping his distance pending instructions. And at the garrison—

At the garrison, Agnes knew, a messenger had already arrived, because she had sent one: a trembling charity girl, instructed to breathe hard and look pale, informing the colonel's adjutant that there was typhoid at the Hale charity, that Matron Hale had quarantined the entire grounds, and that the supply wagon scheduled for the morning would need to pass through the checkpoint along with the evacuees, as it was carrying contaminated bedding to be burned at the lime kilns.

Let Vane chew on that. Let him weigh it. A typhoid scare in a town cut off by ice was not a matter for pride or politics; it was a matter for the garrison doctor, and the garrison doctor — a tired, decent man named Prewitt whom Agnes had once saved from a charge of drunken negligence — would confirm that typhoid was indeed possible, indeed plausible, indeed something he strongly advised keeping at arm's length until the fumigation was complete. Prewitt would not ask questions. Prewitt owed her too. In this town, it turned out, half the population owed Agnes Hale something, and she had never once called in those debts until now, and now she called them all at once, and found they were good.

The wagon left at nine.

Agnes rode in the back of it, upright among the tarpaulined bundles, her face arranged into grim martyrdom, one bundle larger than the others and breathing very quietly beneath the canvas. Rosalind lay flat between rolls of bedding, a damp cloth — vinegar-soaked, to redden the eyes and lend authenticity to any close look — folded in her pocket, her father's stolen documents sewn into the lining of her coat where no search short of scissors would find them.

At the north gate, the checkpoint. Two soldiers, muffled to the eyes, standing well back from the wagon with the studied nonchalance of men who had been briefed and did not like their briefing.

"Morning, ma'am," the sergeant managed through his scarf. "Doctor Prewitt's compliments. He says—" a glance at the tarpaulins, a visible swallow "—he says you're to keep the canvas tied down the whole way and burn it all at the kilns. Don't unload it anywhere else. Don't touch it more than necessary."

"I have been touching it all morning, Sergeant," Agnes said icily. "Someone in this town must do the necessary work while the rest stand about being careful. Open the gate."

The gate opened. No one counted heads. No one looked under canvas. Fear, Agnes thought, settling her shoulders, is the finest porter ever invented; it opens every door and asks no questions.

The wagon groaned north through the drifts, past the frozen millrace, past the dark windows of houses where families watched them pass and shut their children indoors. At the crossroads, where the road bent by the Hollis fence line, the driver slowed — a coughed word, a shift of the brake — and Agnes shifted her weight, blocking the view of the far side of the wagon with her own broad old body, and felt rather than saw the canvas stir, the shadow slip over the wheel and down into the ditch and away through the frozen bracken toward the mill track.

Eleven miles. One girl. One satchel of truth.

"Drive on," Agnes said.

* * *

They did not get far enough.

Agnes learned it at noon, standing in the lime-dusted doorway of the emptied ward, watching a rider come up the north road at a pace no errand justified. He passed her without a glance, spurring hard toward the garrison, and ten minutes later the garrison bell began ringing — not the alarm pattern, but the assembly call, sharp and urgent over the rooftops — and men began pouring out of the barracks gates, mounting horses, taking the north road at the trot.

Agnes stood very still in the doorway and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do all morning.

Rosalind had left at nine. On foot, across fields, she might reach the mill track by half nine, the river path by ten. Eleven miles in winter, alone, fever-thin, on bandaged heels — even walking well, she would not reach the county seat before four o'clock. The soldiers had perhaps caught sight of her already. Perhaps a farmer had seen a figure cutting east and mentioned it, the way farmers mention things. Perhaps the courier in custody had finally given up something after all — a route, a plan, a name.

Or perhaps Aldous Vane had simply done the same arithmetic Agnes had done, hours ago, and concluded that a typhoid scare convenient enough to empty his enemy's stronghold was no scare at all.

The riders went north. Then more of them. Then, last, a single horseman in a grey coat, riding not fast but steadily, the way a man rides when he is certain the quarry has nowhere to go — and Agnes recognized the set of the shoulders even at that distance, and her heart went cold in her chest.

The colonel himself was going to the river.

Agnes turned back inside the empty ward, to the lime bucket and the fumigation pots and the magnificent, useless machinery of her deception, and for one moment — one long, bitter moment — the old fear rose in her, the fear that had kept her silent through twenty years of small compromises. She was seventy-one years old. She had done what she could. If it failed now, it had cost her everything for nothing.

Then she thought of Thomas Wren, dying on straw while his name was struck from the books. She thought of Edmund Vane's promise extracted in a field hospital, and of Xanthe Iversen carrying that promise like a stone in his chest for two decades. She thought of a girl of six reading too-loud stories to dying children, grown now into a woman carrying her father's crimes in a coat lining across the ice.

"No," Agnes said aloud, to the empty ward, to the lime dust hanging in the cold light. "Not nothing. Not yet."

She took down her coat and her stoutest stick, and she set off down the hill toward the garrison, at a speed unbecoming a matron, to find out exactly how much trouble a town full of debtors could make when she asked them all at once.

Out on the white river, faint in the grey distance, a small figure was moving north across the ice — and behind her, gaining, came the horsemen.
