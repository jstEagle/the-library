## 16. The Ice Road

The river had stopped being a river sometime in January. It was a road now — a white road with no banks visible in the dark, running from the fever ward's back steps to the far shore and the county road beyond, and it would bear her weight or it would not.

Rosalind stood at the top of the ice-house ramp with the satchel strapped across her chest and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, how much she did not want to do this.

Then she counted the seconds gone and stepped down onto the frozen river.

The cold came up through the soles of her boots like a living thing. Not the cold of rooms and coats and fires — that was cold as an idea. This was cold as a fact, patient and enormous, the cold of a mile of water held still beneath her feet by nothing but its own stubbornness. She could hear it settling under her, faint groans traveling out from each footfall like the complaints of some vast sleeping animal she was walking across the spine of.

*Don't think of it as water,* she told herself. *Think of it as ground.*

But she had grown up on this river. She knew what lay under the white. She had been nine years old when a drover's horse went through near the mill race, and she remembered the sound it made — not a splash, nothing so gentle — a *boom*, like a door slammed in the earth, and then the black water standing up around the hole and the men on the bank shouting and the horse screaming until someone put a pistol to its ear.

She walked.

Agnes had given her the last hour before moonset, and the typhoid scare was doing its work behind her: lanterns swinging in the fever ward yard, Agnes's magnificent voice cracking across the snow — *typhoid, gentlemen, do you want to explain to Colonel Vane why his garrison is down thirty men by Sunday?* — soldiers falling back from the outbuildings as if the very air were poisoned. It had been beautiful, in its way. The colonel's own fear of disease had opened the door his soldiers had shut.

Rosalind had slipped out through the ice-house, where the frost kept everything honest, and down the ramp the drovers used in winter, and now there was nothing between her and the county seat but a mile of frozen river and whatever men Aldous Vane could put on it once he understood what had happened.

She gave herself until the far shore to be afraid again. For now, she walked.

* * *

The moon was a thin nail-paring low in the west, giving less light than the snow reflected back up at her. That was the strange arithmetic of the ice road: the world lit from below, shadows thrown upward, the dark above her like a ceiling. Her breath plumed and drifted sideways. The wind came down the valley from the north, straight along the river's course, so that walking into it was like wading.

At two hundred yards out she allowed herself one look back. The town was a scatter of orange windows on the rise, the garrison house the brightest of them, and — yes. There. A lantern moving fast along the quay road. Then another.

They had found the empty attic, then. Or the ice-house ramp, with its footprints going down and not coming up.

*Run, then,* said the cold practical voice that lived somewhere below her heart, the voice that had gotten her out of her father's house at seventeen and across half of France in the war and into this satchel's worth of proof. *Grief later. Fear later. Legs now.*

She ran. There was no graceful way to run on river ice; she ran the way frightened animals run, arms out, weight low, boots skidding and catching, and every stride sent a shock through her knees and a sound out ahead of her across the white — the crisp squeak-crunch of dry frost, which was good, dry frost meant thick ice — and under it, occasionally, something else. A groan. A long, low note like a bow drawn slow across a bass string, rising and fading somewhere off to her left, in the black channel country where the current ran hardest under the bridge span.

The ice talked all night; she knew that. Every old riverman said so. Ice expanding, ice contracting, ice settling its accounts. A singing river was not necessarily a breaking river.

Knowing this changed nothing. Each groan went through her like a hand closing.

* * *

Halfway across, she found the stakes.

They were old ones — willow wands planted by the ferrymen decades ago to mark the safe lane in hard winters, most rotted to stubs, leaning at drunken angles out of the snow. But they meant something: that this line had been walked before, year after year, by men who knew the river's temper. She steered for them gratefully, counting them as they passed — three, four, five — and it was at the sixth stake, leaning hard to starboard, that the ice spoke differently.

Not a groan. A *crack* — short, sharp, whipcrack loud, directly ahead of her, and she froze mid-stride with her arms flung out like a tightrope walker's.

Silence. Her own heartbeat. The wind.

Then, faintly, behind her: voices. Carried flat and clear over the ice the way sound traveled on winter nights — she could pick out words now. *There — on the ice — the woman—*

A lantern swung out onto the river behind her, then a second, then a third, spaced wide, the way you spread men when you're hunting something that might go through the surface and you don't want to lose more than one of them.

So Vane had ordered them onto the ice after her. Rosalind felt something turn over in her chest that was almost — *almost* — satisfaction. He had spent twenty years buying this town, polishing it, endowing it, holding it up like a jeweler's setting around himself. And now he was spending his own soldiers' lives on a midnight river to stop one woman with a satchel.

He must be more frightened than she'd guessed.

Good. Let him be frightened. Fear was the only honest thing she'd ever seen him feel.

She ran again, and this time the ice answered her every few strides — cracks chasing her footsteps, long splitting reports that seemed to travel faster than she did, racing ahead along invisible flaws and detonating somewhere out in the dark. Once, twice, she felt the surface *give* under her — a softness, a shudder, like stepping on the lid of a box — and she threw her weight forward and was past it before her mind finished forming the word *thin*.

The lanterns behind were gaining. Of course they were. They were young men in army boots who hadn't spent the day barricaded in a cold attic eating biscuit crumbs. She heard one of them bellow — *Stop! In the name of Colonel Vane!* — and the words went rolling away upriver and came back off the hills sounding absurd and enormous.

In the name of Colonel Vane. As if the river cared whose name anyone invoked.

*Keep the stakes,* she told herself. *Count the stakes.* Seven. Eight. The ninth was missing entirely — snapped off at the root, and she remembered suddenly that Edmund had told her about this stretch, years ago, laughing, fourteen years old and invincible on skates: *the spring runs under there even in February, Roz, the fish hold their noses in it all winter—*

Edmund.

It came over her the way it always did, without permission — not grief exactly, or not only grief, but his voice, his laugh, the specific gravity of him, and underneath it the promise she had made on his behalf without ever meeting him, the promise that had dragged a dying stranger's sister out of an attic and onto a frozen river at midnight. Xanthe Iversen had sworn it to Edmund. But she was carrying it now. It was in the satchel against her ribs, heavier than paper had any right to be: the casualty returns in her father's own hand, the altered dates, the neat little transposition that had moved forty dead men off one ledger and onto another so that Ashcombe Court would pass untainted to the surviving son.

To Edmund. Who was dead too, though the ledgers didn't know it yet. Dead at the front while his father's clerks were deciding which of the dead were officially dead and which were merely inconvenient.

Forty names. Somewhere behind her in the dark, in a surgery with loose floorboards, a man had spent twenty years writing those names down because no one else would. She thought of his face when he'd opened the door to her — the recognition arriving before surprise, like he'd been waiting twenty years for precisely this knock.

*You're the reason I'm on this ice,* she told him silently, and meant it as a thank-you.

The tenth stake was bent double. Beyond it the ice looked different — rougher, wind-scoured, patched with gray. The far shore. She could see the county road now, or the pale seam of it, and the dark mass of the tollhouse pine.

And between her and the shore, moving out from the eastern bank at an angle that made her stomach drop, a fourth lantern. Cutting the chord. Heading for the shallows near the outflow, where any fool knew the ice went rotten first.

They weren't trying to catch her. They were trying to *steer* her — herd her off the ferryman's lane toward the middle channel, where the current never fully slept, where the ice might be a foot thick or two inches thick depending on the week and the whim of the water.

Rosalind stopped running and stood still, chest heaving, breath tearing out of her in rags, and made herself think.

Behind: three lanterns, gaining slowly. Ahead-right: one, moving to intercept. Ahead-left: the open channel country, black-veined, treacherous, and beyond it — eventually — the same far shore. Straight ahead: the old lane, the stakes, the rotting ninth, and whatever the spring current had done to the ice above it this year.

Three choices and none of them good, and the men behind her closing at a walk now, deliberately unhurried, because they had decided she had nowhere to go.

*They think I'll break,* she thought. *Father taught them to think that. He's been teaching everyone to think that for twenty years.*

She turned left. Toward the channel. Toward the black veins.

She heard the shout go up behind her — confusion first, then alarm, then Vane's sergeant bellowing *not the channel, you madwoman—* — and she put her head down and ran at the worst ice on the river because it was the one direction they would be slowest to follow.

* * *

Later, she would never be able to reconstruct those minutes in order. They came back to her forever afterward as fragments, the way bad dreams do.

Running with her eyes fixed on the snow ahead, reading it like text — the difference between snow lying on solid ice (dry, squeaking, trustworthy) and snow lying on ice that had water moving close beneath (damp, dull, a faint glassy sheen like the skin on milk). Jumping the first black vein — a crack a hand's width wide showing naked water below, exhaling cold like an open cellar — and landing badly and sliding and getting up. Hearing behind her the *crack-boom* of a man's weight finding soft ice, and the shouting, and the lantern swinging wildly, and the sergeant's voice gone high: *Get back — get BACK, you'll have him through—*

Someone had gone in. One of hers or one of theirs — theirs, it had to be theirs, they were the ones on the soft line — and the pursuit behind her dissolved into rescue, lanterns converging, ropes, the whole machinery of the chase tearing itself apart to keep a soldier from drowning.

She should have felt triumph. What she felt instead, absurdly, was the memory of a horse screaming by the mill race, and she hated her father a little more for making her feel it.

The channel country was fifty yards of hell and then it ended — she came up over a pressure ridge where two floes had rammed and frozen together, a small white mountain range, and slid down the far side onto ice that sang *solid* under her boots, dry and thick and true, and there were the stakes again, the outer markers, and there was the shore.

She reached the county road as the moon set.

For a moment she simply stood in the shadow of the tollhouse pine, shaking, her lungs full of knives, and looked back across the river. The town glimmered on its hill. Out on the ice, lanterns clustered around a dark hole in the world, and thin voices carried to her across the frozen mile — urgent, frightened, human. Men pulling one of their own out of the water because he'd been sent onto a night river to stop a woman carrying papers.

*This is what it costs,* she thought. *This is what Father has always spent — other people's blood, other people's sons, spent quietly and written off cleanly. Forty names once. One name tonight.*

The satchel pressed against her ribs. She pressed back.

Then she turned north, toward the county seat and the magistrate's dark house and the morning, and began to walk the six miles of snow-choked road with her clothes freezing stiff and her heart going like a bird's.

She had maybe three hours of darkness left. Her father had maybe three hours of ignorance left. After that, whichever of them moved faster would decide what happened to forty names and one town.

Rosalind Vane picked up her pace, and behind her, far out on the black-and-white river, the ice groaned once, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep.
