## 17. The Burning of the Ledger

They came for me at dusk on the fourth day of my confinement, when the light through the garrison house windows had gone the color of old pewter and the lamps below were being lit one by one along the parade ground. I heard their boots on the stairs — two men, heavy, unhurried — and I knew before they opened the door that this was not another summons to tea.

Colonel Aldous Vane stood in the corridor beyond them, dressed for the occasion in his dress uniform, medals pinned with a precision I remembered from twenty years ago, from field hospitals where he had come to read names off lists while I wrote them down and other men carried them out feet-first. He looked older now. He looked, if anything, more certain than he had ever been.

"Nurse Iversen," he said. "You'll walk with me."

"Am I under arrest?"

"You are my guest," he said, "and you are walking with me. There is a distinction, and I'd thank you to observe it. It matters to the tribunal that will never convene."

I went. What choice was there? The house was ringed with his men; the river was a white plain beyond the windows, locked and silent, and the bridge — the bridge was a black spine of timber standing over it, useless as a closed book until spring. We crossed the hall where a maid kept her eyes fixed on the floor, and out through the servants' door into the yard, where a brazier had been set up beside the woodshed. Not a large fire. A domestic fire, the kind a man lights to warm his hands.

He had brought the ledger.

I saw it in his gloved hands — my ledger, my true ledger, the one I had kept beneath the floorboards of my surgery through nineteen winters — and something in my chest went cold and still, like the river itself. They must have taken up the boards during the first search after all, or during the second; I had told myself the copies were safe, had told myself the original was only paper, only ink, only memory made stubborn. But seeing it in his hands was different. Seeing it was watching someone hold your own heart at arm's length and weigh it.

"Do you know what this is?" Vane asked. He turned it over as though inspecting merchandise. "My adjutant found it yesterday. Under the floor by the stove, which shows a certain lack of imagination, I'll grant you, but then you were never an imaginative man, Xanthe. You were always a faithful one. That was your whole tragedy, if we're being honest about it." He opened it. Pages turned. His voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Forty-one names. Do you know how many times I've read them since yesterday? Forty-one men who died at Harrow Ford and Ferren Crossing and the ridge above Malden, whose deaths were entered nowhere, whose pay was drawn and pocketed, whose families received nothing but silence. Forty-one entries in a dead man's hand."

"Not a dead man's," I said. "Edmund's hand is in there too. You might look at it. You might remember what your son's handwriting looked like before—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and quick, a blade laid flat across a table, and for one moment the polish cracked and I saw the man underneath: not a colonel, not a benefactor, just an heir who had spent twenty years guarding a theft. Then it sealed over again, and he smiled the smile he used at charity dinners.

I thought of Edmund then, as I had thought of him every hour since Rosalind collapsed at my door with her father's bullet in her shoulder. I had held him while he died, in a dressing station that smelled of carbolic and mud, and he had gripped my wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, *They'll write us out, Xanthe. My father's already written us out. Promise me someone will read the names aloud someday. Promise me they'll be said.* And I had promised. I had promised a dying boy because it cost nothing to promise a dying boy, and then I had come home to this town and buried the promise under my floorboards and called it prudence for nineteen years.

The ledger was going into the fire. Very well. I had known since Agnes's typhoid scare emptied the fever ward, since Rosalind went out across the ice toward the magistrate, since the courier was taken at the ferry house and broke under questioning — I had known this moment would come, and I had done my arithmetic long before Vane did his.

"Before you burn it," I said, "you should understand what you're burning."

"A book," he said. "Paper and ink."

"The only original, yes." I let that stand in the air between us. "And copies?"

He paused. It was a small pause, no longer than a heartbeat, but I had watched this man's face across casualty tables for two years of war and I knew every weather it could make. He had not considered copies. Or rather — he had considered them and dismissed them, because in his arithmetic paper was power only when it was scarce, and he could not imagine a nurse in a river town having the patience or the audacity to multiply himself.

"Copies," he repeated. "Of what use? A forgery can be denounced. A copy without the original proves nothing. You're a nurse, Xanthe, not a lawyer. You've been reading too many of the schoolmaster's pamphlets."

"There are eleven copies," I said. "There have been eleven since the week after your men searched my surgery the first time. One went to Agnes Hale, sewn inside the ticking of her mattress, because no soldier in this town will search an old woman's bed while she watches. One went to Thomas Merrick the schoolteacher, though I'll own that one was a mistake, given how he folds under pressure — I expect that's the page your courier was carrying. One is in the parish register box at St. Brannock's, under the christening records, because Father Ellery has not yet decided whose side God is on, but he knows better than to let soldiers open church property. One is buried in a tobacco tin under the third apple tree in Marta Kessler's orchard, and Mrs. Kessler hates you with a purity I have never seen matched in any church. Two went out on the last cart before the bridge closed, addressed to solicitors in the county seat, and God and the postal service know where those are by now. One is with the ferryman's wife, one under the floor of the cooperage, one—" I stopped. Smiled, for the first time in days. "You don't need the full list, Colonel. The point of eleven is that neither of us needs the full list."

The brazier crackled. Somewhere beyond the wall, a sentry coughed.

Vane looked at the ledger in his hands for a long moment. When he spoke again, the gentleness was gone entirely, and what remained was almost refreshing in its honesty. "You understand what happens to you. Whatever you think you've accomplished — whatever little network of grannies and schoolteachers you've assembled — the tribunal cannot sit until the thaw, and I control the roads until the thaw. Your Miss Vane may have reached the magistrate, or she may be at the bottom of the river with my men's boot prints around her. Either way, you are here, the town is mine, and in the morning everyone will have forgotten a book ever existed. Except you. You'll remember. You'll remember every night for the rest of your life, which I intend to be long, because killing you would make a martyr, and martyrs are so much harder to manage than cowards." He weighed the ledger once, twice, like a man testing the pull of a fishing line. "So burn it with me, Xanthe. Stand here, watch it go, and tomorrow I tear up your house arrest, restore your practice, and we both go back to being respectable men. Twenty years of silence bought you safety. Another twenty can buy you peace."

*Promise me,* Edmund had said, his blood cooling on my hands. *Promise me.*

"I made a promise," I told him, "to your son. You should know that it wasn't to destroy you. He didn't ask for vengeance. He asked that the names be said. That somebody would read them aloud, all forty-one, somewhere they couldn't be unwritten. Everything else — the tribunal, the estate, your fall — that was your doing, Colonel. You built all of it yourself, out of the same materials you built the falsified reports. You simply never believed anyone would stack them against you."

"Burn it yourself, then," Vane said quietly. "If you're so brave. Take it from my hand and put it in the fire, and let's see whether courage keeps you warm."

I took it. Of course I took it. It was mine; it had been mine through nineteen years of floorboard damp and lamp-oil fumes and the small nightly terror of hearing boots in the street outside my window. I held it over the brazier, and I felt its weight — less than a pound of paper, and heavier than any load I'd ever carried.

Then I let it go.

The flames took it slowly, at first — the cover curling, the edges browning, then catching all at once with a sound like a drawn breath. I watched the first name go. Corporal E. Whitlock, drowned at the ford, age nineteen, his mother still living on Chandler Street and buying her bread from a baker who takes the Colonel's charity. I watched the second and the third. Private Halloran. Sergeant Boyce. The twins, Anselm and Peter Rook, listed in my hand on a single line because they had died within an hour of each other and I could not bear to separate them even on paper. Page after page. The fire ate them greedily, and the heat came up against my face, and I did not step back.

Vane watched me watch it. I want to record, in whatever account of this survives, that he expected grief. He wanted grief; he had arranged the brazier and the dusk and the dress uniform to produce grief, the way other men arrange flowers. And I gave him none, and I could see the absence of it troubling him more than defiance would have, because a man can fight defiance. He cannot fight a man standing warm at his own bonfire looking satisfied.

"Why aren't you grieving?" he demanded at last. It sounded almost like a plea.

"Because you're burning ash," I said. "That book stopped being the truth three weeks ago. It's been the truth in eleven places at once since before the ice closed. You can't burn a truth that's already been said, Colonel. You can only burn your copy of it." I watched the last page catch — the final entry, Edmund's own hand, the note he had pressed on me at the end: *If I don't come back, count us. Someone count us.* — and as it flared and whitened, I felt something loosen in my chest that I had carried so long I'd mistaken it for bone. "Twenty years ago you taught me that reports can be altered. You never learned the other lesson. Paper burns. Witnesses don't. You've got eleven of them now, and every one of them is a person who chose, knowing what choosing costs. You never once had to buy me, Colonel. Imagine what the others cost you."

He straightened. For a moment — I will swear this to my dying day — for a moment he looked afraid. Not of me. Of the yard, the town, the lit windows beyond the wall, the whole quiet settlement of people he had fed and clothed and purchased, waking one by one from a sleep he'd sold them. Then the discipline came down like a shutter.

"Go back to your room, Nurse Iversen," he said. "Enjoy your last night of comfortable imprisonment. In the morning I'll decide what a coward costs these days." He turned to his men. "Put him back."

They walked me across the yard, past the brazier where the last flakes of ash rose and drifted, gray moths over black snow, and up the stairs, and locked the door behind me with a courtesy that was worse than violence. I sat on the edge of the bed in the dark and listened to the wind coming off the river, and I did the thing I had not allowed myself to do in nineteen years.

I said the names aloud. Quietly, in order, all forty-one, starting with Corporal Edward Whitlock, drowned at the ford, aged nineteen, and ending with the Rook twins, and finishing with Edmund Vane, lieutenant, who counted them first and asked me to keep counting. The room was cold and the words made no breath-cloud in the dark that anyone would ever see, and it was the most honest prayer I have ever spoken.

Near midnight, I heard it.

Not boots this time. A scratching at the shutters — soft, deliberate, three taps and a pause, three taps and a pause — the signal Agnes and I had agreed on weeks ago and I had begun to fear we'd never use. I crossed to the window and eased the shutter a finger's width, and the cold poured in, and below me in the blue snow-light stood a figure muffled to the eyes, holding up a lantern with its shutter half-closed so that only a knife of light showed.

The figure pulled down its scarf, and I saw Rosalind Vane's face, chapped raw by the ice road, alive, impossibly alive, and behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of the woodshed, two men in the dark coats of the county magistrate's provosts.

"Open the back stair, Mr. Iversen," she whispered up to me. "We've come to fetch you. The magistrate's men reached the ferry house an hour ago — and by morning, half the town will know what's in those pages. He doesn't yet."

Behind us, down the corridor, a door slammed, and boots began to run.
