## 18. The Town Decides

The thaw came the way a fever breaks — not gradually, but all at once, in a single night of wind off the western hills that set the river groaning like something waking from a long sleep.

Xanthe heard it before dawn on the fourth morning after the burning: a deep, percussive report from downriver, then another, then a rolling series of cracks that traveled the length of the valley. He stood at his surgery window in his shirt and watched the ice shudder against the piers of the bridge. By lamplight it looked black and restless, breathing. In twenty winters he had never once been glad to hear that sound. This morning he pressed his hand flat against the cold glass as though he could hurry it along.

They had taken the ledger two nights ago. Vane's men had come for it with lanterns and a tin basin, and Vane himself had fed it page by page into the garrison house fire while Xanthe stood at attention and watched twenty years of careful ink go up as light and smoke. *You see how little it was*, Vane had said, almost tenderly, watching him. *Paper. Ash. Nothing.*

*Nothing,* Xanthe had agreed.

He had counted the hours since. Sixty-one. Long enough for Agnes Hale to have walked the copies to three houses, for the schoolteacher Miss Prewitt to have posted her envelope to the county magistrate under cover of a school-trust correspondence, and — this was the part no one had planned, the part that had happened on its own, the way water finds its level — long enough for the pages themselves to have begun moving through Harrow's Crossing like a contagion passed hand to hand in a crowded ward.

Because Agnes had not stopped at three houses. Agnes, who had spent forty years reading faces over sickbeds and knew precisely which ones could hold a secret and which would crack with the strain of holding it, had made her own triage. She had copied further pages in her own careful copperplate — Xanthe hadn't known until later; she'd sat in the fever ward attic with Rosalind through two whole nights doing it — and she had distributed them the way she distributed medicine: according to need.

By morning, everyone in town would know. And everyone in town would have to decide.

* * *

Xanthe saw it first at Cobbett's store.

He went in for lamp oil and bread, an errand so ordinary it felt like a disguise. The store was warm and smelled of paraffin and rope and the cured hams that hung from the ceiling beams all winter. Herbert Cobbett stood behind his counter weighing sugar with the grave attention he gave all transactions, and pinned to the wall beside the calendar of the Harrow's Crossing Agricultural Society — the calendar Colonel Vane had paid to have printed every year since 1911, the colonel's own orchard on the front page — was a single sheet of paper.

A ledger page. A copy, in a hand Xanthe recognized as his own younger self, the entries sharp and unhesitating where his current script had gone cautious. *Pvt. T. Ellery, 2nd Platoon, died of wounds received at—* and beneath it, in different ink, the word that had been struck from every official record in the province: *KILLED*. Not missing. Not transferred. Killed, and buried in a field grave whose coordinates existed only because a young nursing orderly had written them down by candlelight while the man was still warm.

Cobbett caught him looking. For a moment neither man spoke. The stove ticked. Outside, a cart went past toward the bridge road at a pace too fast for the mud.

"You knew," Cobbett said at last. It wasn't quite an accusation. It was the sound of a man recalibrating twenty years of a relationship in real time.

"I kept the record," Xanthe said. "That's all I ever did."

"Vane gave me my first stock loan when the flood took my cellar stock in '13." Cobbett said it flatly, like a confession. "Every merchant in this town has a debt to that man. You know it. He knows it. I expect that's rather the point of him." He looked at the page on his wall, then reached up — and instead of taking it down, he took a second sheet from under the counter and pinned it beside the first. Two names now instead of one. "My brother served under him," Cobbett added, not looking at Xanthe. "Came home without his left arm and wouldn't say why he was the only one of his section to come home at all. Wouldn't say for eleven years. Then he drank himself quiet in the spring of '17." He tied the apron string behind his back with his one good habit of precision. "Bread's on the second shelf. Oil you'll have to carry yourself; my boy's not come down yet."

When Xanthe left, the bell over the door rang behind him, and through the window he saw Mrs. Cobbett standing before the wall, her hand at her mouth, reading.

* * *

The constable was harder to watch.

Brack had been town constable for as long as Xanthe had been town nurse — a big, slow, decent man who broke up tavern fights with his palms rather than his fists and who had, Xanthe happened to know, once driven forty miles through a February storm to fetch a doctor for the miller's daughter. Brack also drew his wage from a fund the garrison administered, lived in a cottage on garrison land, and had looked directly through more than one thing in his years of service that a braver man would have had to look at.

Xanthe found him at midday standing at the corner of Mill Street and Bridge Road, where the mud was already running in brown threads along the gutters, watching four of Vane's soldiers take down the notice that had stood there all winter: *BRIDGE CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE GARRISON COMMANDER UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.*

"They're taking it down," Brack said, without turning. "Ice isn't out yet. Bridge won't bear a cart for days. But they're taking it down." He worked his jaw. "You know what that means?"

"It means someone's told him he'll want the road open soon," Xanthe said. "For leaving."

"For leaving." Brack turned then, and his face was doing what faces did in Harrow's Crossing that week — trying to settle around information that didn't fit the shape of the life built on top of it. "There's a paper going round, Mr. Iversen. My wife's cousin has it. Names on it. Fellows listed as deserters, some of them, or transferred, or died of fever in a hospital that turns out—" He stopped. Started again. "One of those fellows, Ellery, taught me to shoot. Back before everything. Cheerful lad. Used to whistle while he cleaned his kit."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "I remember him whistling."

Brack looked at him for a long moment, and Xanthe watched the decision move through the big man like weather — the fear coming first, then the shame at the fear, then something underneath both that had been waiting a long time for permission.

"If the colonel asks me to arrest anyone," Brack said slowly, "on account of papers, or nurses, or old ladies from the fever ward — I'll be obliged to be very slow about it. Painfully slow. You understand me? I'm a slow man. Always have been."

"I've always found you very thorough, Constable," Xanthe said.

Brack nodded once, hard, like a man setting a bone, and walked away down Mill Street with his hands clasped behind his back and his boots throwing up little fans of meltwater.

* * *

Not everyone decided well. That had to be recorded too; Xanthe had spent twenty years writing down what actually happened, and he wasn't going to stop now.

Tobias Renwick, the auctioneer, went to the garrison house on Thursday morning and came out an hour later with a signed certificate of commendation and a look on his face like a man who had sold something precious cheap and knew it. By evening Renwick was telling anyone who would stand still that the papers circulating were forgeries, Communist fabrications, the work of outsiders — and Xanthe noted, without pleasure, that Renwick's voice cracked on the word *outsiders*, because everyone in town knew exactly which outsider he meant, and everyone knew the auctioneer's warehouse lease came from the Vane estate office at half the market rate.

And the young soldiers were their own weather system entirely. They were farm boys mostly, conscripted into the garrison service on wages that Vane's charity made generous, boys who stacked firewood for widows in December and called Agnes Hale *ma'am* with genuine feeling. Xanthe passed two of them on the lane outside the churchyard, and they were passing a folded page between them the way boys pass a cigarette, heads together, and when they saw him they didn't hide it — one of them, a red-eared boy named Sorrel whom Xanthe had treated for frostbite in January, folded the page away and then said, unprompted:

"Nurse. If it comes to — if there's trouble on the bridge, when the ice goes out." He swallowed. "Some of us have fathers on that list. We only just worked out we might."

"I know," Xanthe said. "Ellery's mother lives past the tannery. Someone should tell her before she reads it off a shop wall."

Sorrel looked at the ground. "We'll tell her," he said. "Me and Denny. We'll go tonight."

* * *

Rosalind came to the surgery after dark, hooded and mud-hemmed, having crossed half the town through back gardens with Agnes walking fifty yards behind like any elderly lady taking her constitutionals. She was thinner than when she'd arrived, and harder-lined, and there was a new stillness in her that Xanthe distrusted and admired in equal measure.

"The magistrate's reply comes with the mail boat," she said, standing at the window with him, watching the river. "If the ice lets the boat through. Agnes thinks Saturday. Maybe Sunday."

"And if Vane is on the bridge before the tribunal can convene?"

"Then we stand on it first." She said it without drama, the way she said most things now. "I've been counting soldiers' loyalties all week. It's remarkable what a piece of paper does. Half the garrison won't follow him across that bridge if they believe what's written on the pages. Some of them are his men body and soul and always will be. But half—" She turned. "Half are just boys who enlisted for the coat and the wage, and boys follow whichever adult in the room seems least afraid. That will be us, Xanthe. Do you understand? When the moment comes, the whole town will decide in about ninety seconds, and they'll decide by watching whether we flinch."

"I don't flinch," Xanthe said. "I write things down. It's slower but it lasts longer."

Something moved across her face that was almost a smile. "Edmund used to say that about you. That you were the only man in the medical corps who treated a wound and then wrote down the truth about it, even when the truth was embarrassing to the surgeon." Her voice caught slightly on her brother's name — it always did, Xanthe noticed, on the way in but never on the way out, as if the grief hit a threshold and then steadied itself. "He'd have liked this week. All these people choosing. He believed people wanted to be good and only needed the arithmetic laid out plainly enough."

"People are complicated arithmetic," Xanthe said. "Cobbett pinned the pages to his wall next to Vane's calendar. Renwick took a commendation. Brack has decided to be slow. The boy Sorrel is going to tell a mother her son didn't desert." He rubbed his eyes. "It's not a clean verdict, Rosalind. It's a town."

"It's a town deciding," she corrected softly. "That's not the same thing. That's better."

Downriver, the ice gave a long moaning report, and they both turned to the window. In the darkness they couldn't see it, but they could hear it plainly now: the river breaking itself apart, plate by plate, all along its length, the whole frozen wall coming down.

"One more thing," Rosalind said. "Agnes had a letter from the ferry house. Vane's ordered the boats chained and posted a picket at the crossing — six men, doubled at night. He's not planning to wait for the tribunal. Whatever he means to do with the deeds, he means to do it the moment the bridge takes weight." She pulled her hood up. "Which means Saturday, or sooner."

She let herself out the back door into the dark, and Xanthe stood alone in the surgery, listening to the river come apart, and understood with complete clarity that the next time he heard that sound in daylight, he would be standing on the bridge.

He took out a fresh sheet of paper, and at the top of it, in his present hand — the older hand, the cautious one that had finally run out of reasons for caution — he wrote the date. Beneath it, the first line: *On the morning the ice went out, the town chose.* There would be more to write. There would be names. But the silence was over; whatever came across that bridge, it would happen in the open, witnessed.

Outside, far off toward the garrison house, a lantern was moving — down the drive, onto the bridge road, stopping. Another lantern joined it. A third.

Vane was counting his men in the dark, the way a gambler counts his chips before the last hand.

Xanthe put on his coat.
