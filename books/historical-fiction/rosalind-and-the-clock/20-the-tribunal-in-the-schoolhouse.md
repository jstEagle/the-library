## 20. The Tribunal in the Schoolhouse

The tribunal convened on the third Tuesday of April, in the schoolhouse, because it was the only room in town large enough to hold what was coming and because the magistrate from the county seat — a narrow man named Corliss with spectacles that magnified his eyes to the size of coins — refused to sit anywhere Colonel Vane's money had touched. That ruled out the garrison house, the assembly hall above the bank, and the reading room Vane had endowed the winter before I came to town. The schoolhouse smelled of chalk and floor wax and forty years of children, and I thought that was fitting. If names were going to be spoken aloud, let them be spoken where children learned to speak.

I had not slept. Nobody had slept, I think. The thaw had come all at once, as thaws do in river towns — one day the ice groaning like a dying animal, the next the brown water running fast and free beneath the bridge for the first time in five months. The sound of it carried through every open window, a rushing, indifferent noise, the river minding its own business at last.

Rosalind came for me at dawn. She wore her grey coat, the one with the burnt hem she'd never had mended, and she stood in my surgery doorway with her hands folded like a woman waiting at a graveside.

"It's time," she said.

"I know."

"Xanthe." She hesitated. In five months I had watched this woman cross a frozen river under rifle fire, watch her father burn a book of the dead before her eyes without flinching, and here she hesitated over three words. "You don't have to read them all. The tribunal has the copies. You could simply confirm them. Corliss told me — he said testimony is enough, you needn't—"

"He's wrong," I said. "Or he's kind. Either way he doesn't understand what a ledger is."

I took the copy from its place inside my coat — the third copy, the one Agnes had copied out in her careful schoolroom hand while I dictated, page by page, through those weeks of snow. The original was ash in a garrison grate. But ink remembers. That was the whole of my faith, these twenty years: that ink remembers even when men choose to forget.

We walked down through the town together. Word had gone round — there is no faster telegraph than a small town preparing for judgment — and people stood along the street the way they stand for funerals. Mrs. Petch outside the bakery with flour still on her apron. Old Tam Wiley leaning on his gate. The constable, Hobbes, who for twenty years had looked through me whenever soldiers were mentioned, standing very straight beside his own front door with his cap in his hands. No one spoke. The river spoke for all of us, running loud under the bridge.

The schoolhouse benches were full. I had expected officers, the tribunal's clerks, perhaps a dozen of the curious. Instead there were merchants and widows and two of Vane's own sergeants sitting awkwardly among civilians, their caps on their knees like penitents. At the back, in the last row, sat Agnes Hale, ancient and upright, her hands flat on her lap, watching me with eyes that said: *Go on, boy. Say it.* She had waited out a war and a winter for this. We all had.

The tribunal sat at the teacher's desk, raised up on the platform where the headmistress used to stand. Corliss in the center. To his left, a barrister from the county, thin as a blade. To his right, a major of engineers — a soldier judging a soldier, which some called fairness and others called theater. Colonel Aldous Vane sat apart from them, at a small table near the window, in civilian clothes that fit him like a disguise. They had taken his rank from him pending the finding; they could not take his posture. He sat the way stone sits, grey and permanent, and when I entered he did not look at me. He looked at the river.

That was the only time in twenty years I ever saw Aldous Vane avoid anyone's eyes.

* * *

Corliss opened proceedings with the dry machinery of law — the commission's authority read aloud, the charges enumerated, falsification of military records, theft of inheritance, conspiracy in the deaths of men whose deaths had never officially happened. It sounds absurd, put so plainly. Men died twice: once in the mud, once again in the paperwork. The second death was the crime.

Then the barrister turned to me. "Dr. Iversen. You were regimental nurse attached to the Third Column, 1899 through 1901. Is that correct?"

"I was," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. Twenty years of practice at keeping it steady, I suppose. Even silence trains a man for something.

"You kept a private record of casualties during your service. A ledger."

"A ledger of the omitted," I said. "Yes."

"Describe it for the tribunal."

And here is the thing I had rehearsed a hundred times in the dark, in the surgery, walking the frozen streets — the speech, the framing, the lawyerly architecture of it. None of it came. What came instead was Edmund Vane's face, the last time I saw it, young and already half-ghost, asking me for a promise in a tent that stank of iodine and rain.

So I set aside the speech. I took out the copy, opened it, and began.

"The first name is Corporal Elias Brandt," I said. "Died the fourteenth of October, 1899, of wounds received at Harrow Ford. His death was recorded in my field book. It does not appear in the column's official casualty return. The return lists him as transferred."

The barrister leaned forward. "Read the entry as you recorded it."

I read it. All of it. *Brandt, E., corporal. Wound to the abdomen, shell fragment. Lucid until evening. Spoke of his mother in Leeds, asked whether letters reach the dead. Died at 19:40. I closed his eyes.*

The room made no sound. Not even the river, though I knew it was still running; sound simply stops mattering at certain moments, the way pain does.

"The next name is Private Samuel Oduya—"

"Doctor," Corliss said gently, "you may summarize. The tribunal has the document."

"With respect, sir," I said, "no one has heard these names. Not once in twenty years. The tribunal having the paper is not the same as the town hearing them. These men lived here or near here, or their mothers wrote here. Their names have been unspoken since the year they died, and an unspoken name is a second burial, and I am done burying men twice."

I looked at the major of engineers. He nodded once, slowly, a soldier granting permission the way soldiers do — economically, meaning it completely.

"Private Samuel Oduya," I said. "Died the second of December, 1899. Fever, following a wound at the crossing at Meldrum. Official record: deserted."

Someone in the third row made a small noise, quickly swallowed. Oduya had been real to someone in this room. Of course he had. Every name would be real to someone. That was the arithmetic I had feared and craved all winter.

I went on.

* * *

It took two hours. The light moved across the schoolroom floor the way it moves over any working day — slow, indifferent, golden by the end — and I read thirty-one names. Thirty-one entries in a hand that started out steady and grew cramped and then steady again, because that is what grief does to handwriting, it comes in waves. Some entries were long. Some were one line, because the man had died too fast for anything but the fact of it. For each I gave the date, the cause, the disposition — what the official record claimed instead. Transferred. Deserted. Recovered and posted elsewhere. One man, Lieutenant Harrow — no relation to the ford — listed as killed in action against the enemy when in truth he had been shot at dusk by his own picket in a confusion no report ever acknowledged, because acknowledging it would mean admitting the pickets had been placed by an order signed—

Here the barrister stopped me. "By whom?"

I looked at the window. Aldous Vane sat unmoving, his profile carved against the spring light, the river roaring behind him like something impatient.

"By Captain Aldous Vane," I said. "Acting commander, Third Column. Later colonel. Later the benefactor of this town."

"Captain Vane signed orders placing the pickets?"

"He did."

"And why would a casualty list omit Lieutenant Harrow?"

"Because Captain Vane's elder brother, Major Edmund Vane, held title to the family estate," I said. "An estate entailed such that if Edmund died without direct heir, it passed not to Aldous but to a cousin. Unless — and the entail allowed this — Edmund's death occurred while he was formally listed as commanding in the field, in which case succession reverted to the next brother. Edmund was wounded in October of 1899. He did not die of it. He recovered, slowly, and was invalided home, alive. But the returns that autumn, altered under Captain Vane's signature, listed Edmund Vane as deceased in command. The estate passed accordingly. When Edmund learned of it — months later, from a solicitor's letter — he confronted his brother. Within the year, Edmund was dead. Fever, the record says. He was a strong man of twenty-nine, and I nursed him through that fever, and I will say before God and this tribunal that I do not believe fevers kill strong men of twenty-nine unless someone helps them along."

The words came out of me like splinters leaving flesh, each one a small tearing, and after each one more room.

I had never said it all together before. Twenty years I had carried the pieces separately — the promise, the suspicion, the ledger, the fear — because pieces are bearable and the whole shape of it is not. Now I stood in a schoolhouse with chalk dust in the sunlight and said the whole shape of it aloud, and found, to my astonishment, that it did not crush me. It emptied me. There is a difference. A crushed thing stays broken. An emptied thing can be filled with something else.

Edmund, I thought. Your brother is sitting right there and cannot look at me. I promised you justice. I am twenty years late and I am keeping it anyway.

* * *

They broke at midday. Bread and cheese brought in from Mrs. Petch's, eaten in silence at the desks like children kept late. Rosalind sat beside me and did not touch me and did not need to; her shoulder an inch from mine said everything a hand could have.

"You left nothing out," she said quietly.

"There was nothing left worth keeping."

"My father hasn't spoken all morning."

"He spoke for twenty years," I said. "In charity galas and dedication plaques and the naming of the reading room. A man gets his allotment of speaking. Today it's ours."

When we resumed, the afternoon belonged to questions. The barrister took me through the alterations one by one — dates, signatures, the tell-tale ink where a return had been amended after the fact. I showed them how the alteration of Edmund's status required three documents changed in concert, and how all three bore the same clerk's hand, a corporal named Finch, who had died in 1903 in circumstances the record described as accidental. I watched the major of engineers write *Finch* in his notes and underline it twice.

Then, near four o'clock, Colonel Vane's counsel rose — a hired advocate from the city, smooth as varnish — and asked leave to cross-examine.

"Dr. Iversen. You allege a conspiracy spanning decades, implicating a decorated officer, based upon a private notebook. Unofficial. Uncountersigned. Kept hidden — by your own admission — for twenty years. Why should this tribunal credit it?"

"Because you may send for the parish registers of Leeds and find the grave of Elias Brandt's mother, who buried an empty coffin," I said. "Because you may send for the muster rolls and find Samuel Oduya's pay stopped the month he 'deserted,' though no desertion was ever prosecuted — the army prosecutes deserters, sir, always, except when the deserter is conveniently dead. Because you may summon the solicitors who handled the entail and ask them why the succession papers cite a death notice dated eleven days before the man actually died. And because—" I lifted the copy, and my hands, which had been steady all day, shook now, and I let them shake, "because there were thirty-one men, and I have spent twenty years being the only person alive who knew their true ends, and no man invents thirty-one ghosts and then carries them for two decades out of ambition. Ambition would have burned the book. Love kept it."

Silence. The advocate sat down. Corliss removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, a long unnecessary minute of it, buying himself composure.

"Colonel Vane," he said at last. "The tribunal will hear your account tomorrow morning. This session is concluded."

Vane rose. For the first time all day he looked at me — across the schoolroom, past thirty-one names hanging in the air between us like frost — and what was in his face was not rage. It was something older and sadder, almost relief, the look of a man who has carried a weight so long he has forgotten it is a weight, seeing the ground where it might finally be set down.

He did not speak. He walked out past the rows, past the town that had eaten his bread for twenty years, and not one person met his eyes, and I understood that whatever remained of the trial, this walk was already its verdict.

* * *

Afterward the room emptied slowly, people lingering the way they linger at a wake. Agnes Hale came down the aisle with her stick tapping and took my hand in both of hers.

"Well," she said. "Twenty years I've wondered what you kept under those floorboards. Worth the wait, boy."

"You knew about the floorboards?"

"Dear heart, I've known about your floorboards since before you had them. I knew your predecessor hid brandy there during the cholera summer. Nothing in this town stays secret, Xanthe. It only stays unspoken." She patted my hand. "There's a difference. You've just ended one of them."

Rosalind and I walked out into the evening. The bridge stood black against the orange water, open at last, traffic already moving over it — carts, a mail coach, ordinary life resuming its ordinary crossings. Tomorrow the tribunal would hear Vane. The day after, the finding.

But tonight, as we reached the corner by the bakery, a boy came running up the street with a lantern, breathless, and skidded to a stop before us.

"Beg pardon — Nurse Iversen? Magistrate Corliss sent me. He says—" the boy gulped air, "he says the colonel's gone. His quarters are empty. And there's papers burned in his grate, and the estate deeds are gone off his desk, and Sergeant Doyle says he saw him take the north road toward the ferry landing not an hour past — with the river still high and the night boat the last one till Friday."

Rosalind went white, then hard, the way steel goes. "He won't run," she said. "Not him. He's not fleeing. He's going to the county seat ahead of the finding — to friends, to lawyers, to men who owe him. If he reaches the capital before the tribunal reports—"

I looked north, toward the landing, where the last light was going off the water.

"Then tomorrow's verdict arrives too late," I said. "Get your coat, Rosalind. The bridge is open. Let's see if twenty years of silence can still run fast enough to catch a guilty man."

We ran.
