## 1. The Weight of Names

The rain had been falling since Tuesday, and by Friday it had stopped being weather and become a condition of the world—something the men lived inside the way fish live inside water, something they no longer remarked upon because remarking required a self outside it, and none of them had that anymore.

Jonah Vane counted the drips coming through the canvas roof of Ward Two out of habit rather than need. Eleven leaks, eleven tin basins and dented mess tins catching them at different rhythms, so that the ward made its own broken music: *plink, plonk, pause, plink.* He had learned to sleep through artillery but not through silence. Silence meant a man was dying, or already dead, and in either case someone would have to write it down.

He was the someone.

Not officially. Officially he was a nurse, third class, attached to the 4th Provisional Hospital of the Ninth Army, which meant he changed dressings, held basins under chins, carried the dead to the wagon when the wagon came—which was rarely, because the wagon preferred officers—and kept his hands clean enough to be trusted near open wounds. But there was no official capacity for what Jonah did after lights-out, after the orderlies finished their cards and the last lamp guttered down to a thumb of flame. There was no form for it. No dispatch column. No line in any ledger the army kept, because the army's ledgers were not interested in certain kinds of men.

Jonah's ledger was.

It lay beneath the floorboard by his cot—a loose plank he'd pried up the first week, when the mud outside was too soft for burying anything you wanted to keep. A book of cheap ruled paper, board-bound, bought from a sutler who'd sold him also a tin of rancid butter and a lie about the war ending by spring. On its pages, in his small, upright hand—the same hand he used for recording pulse rates and fluid intake, deliberately unremarkable, the handwriting of a man with nothing to hide—were the names.

Forty-one names now. He added the forty-second that night.

* * *

The man in the end bed had come in two days before with a belly wound that the surgeon, Major Hessler, had pronounced "a matter of hours" and then, when the man refused to die on schedule, "a matter of days." The men called him Tolly. His real name—his whole name, the one his mother had shouted across a yard somewhere east of Vellmark, the one a girl might have embroidered on a handkerchief—was Bartholomew Rees. Jonah knew this because he had asked, on the first night, while the morphine still worked and Tolly could still answer in complete sentences.

Nobody asked anymore. That was the thing nobody said out loud. In a hospital like this, names were overhead; they took time, and time was triage. A man came in, he got a number chalked on the bed frame—*Bed 14, abdominal, expectant*—and if he died, the number went into the day book, and the day book went to the adjutant, and the adjutant sent up a count. Forty-seven men lost this week. Numbers moved like weather. Names stayed where they fell.

But some men didn't even get numbers. Some men got nothing.

Tolly was one of those. Jonah had learned it on the second morning, when the paymaster's clerk came through with the muster roll to confirm identities against the regimental lists—a thin, fastidious man named Ostrow who smelled of ink and held his notebook away from the beds as though death were contagious to paper.

"And this one?" Ostrow had said, nodding at Bed 14 without looking at the man in it.

"Tolly," Jonah said. "Bartholomew Rees."

Ostrow ran a finger down his list. Ran it again. Closed the notebook with a small, satisfied snap, as if the mystery had resolved itself neatly. "No such man."

"He's lying right there."

"I'm sure he believes he is." Ostrow had smiled then, a clerk's smile, apologetic and absolute. "Deserted in August, most likely. Or transferred and misfiled. Either way—" a little shrug, the shrug of a man whose conscience had been professionally streamlined— "he isn't mine to count."

And that was that. Ostrow moved on to Bed 15, and Tolly—Bartholomew Rees, twenty-two years old, a farrier's son who could shoe a horse blindfolded and who talked in his sleep about an orchard—became, in the eyes of the Ninth Army, a rounding error. When he died, no dispatch would carry his name home. No pension clerk would trouble a widow who didn't exist, because officially there was nothing to be widowed from. Somewhere east of Vellmark a mother would wait for letters that had never been authorized to be written.

Jonah had stood there holding a basin of bloody water and understood, with the cold clarity of a bone snapping, that somebody had to keep the count. Not God—God's books were closed to auditors. Somebody with a pencil.

* * *

So now, past midnight, with the rain working its eleven rhythms above him, Jonah knelt on the packed earth floor beside his cot and lifted the plank.

The smell underneath was of damp and old straw and the particular mineral stink of ground that had absorbed too much of everything. He slid the ledger out, wiped its cover with his sleeve, and carried it to the one table in the nurses' quarters, where the stub of candle gave light enough to write by and not enough to be seen writing.

He turned to the last written page. Forty-one names, each with its small biography in the margins, because a name alone was just a stone; it was the details that made a man weigh something.

*Tomasz Grell, Pvt., Co. B, 11th Rifles — left thigh, gas gangrene. Played the concertina. Asked twice whether his letter reached his sister. It hadn't; I posted it myself.*

*Anselm Pike, Cpl. — head wound, Lucerne field. Kept a photograph of a dog in his breast pocket, which the surgeons removed and lost.*

*Wren Odile (given name uncertain; answered to "Dutch") — typhus. No regiment would claim him. Possibly a deserter from both sides. Sang beautifully.*

On and on. Forty-one men the army had misplaced, mislaid, disowned, or simply declined to notice, and beside each name the small facts Jonah had gathered like a man gathering coins off a battlefield: mothers' towns, trades, jokes, last words, the things they'd asked him to remember. He was not a sentimental man—he'd have denied sentimentality the way he'd deny theft—but he had discovered early that grief, unwritten, curdles into something worse than grief. It becomes doubt. It becomes *did he ever exist.* Writing was the only preservative he trusted.

Tonight's entry waited in his memory, patient as the dead always were.

He dipped the pencil stub, licked its point out of old habit, and wrote:

*Bartholomew ("Tolly") Rees, Pvt., nominal roll unknown — enlisted under a name the army has since lost interest in. Abdominal wound, Marrow Ford action. Farrier's son. Shod horses for the baggage train the week before he was hit, unpaid. Talked about an orchard. His last coherent words were "tell them I stood," and since the army will not tell them, I am telling them here:*

*He stood.*

He sat back and read it over, and his throat did the tight thing it did every night, the thing he had learned to breathe through rather than feel, because a nurse who wept at every bed was a nurse who soon couldn't work at all. Quiet grief disguised as routine—that was the trade. You folded the sorrow small, like a letter into a coat pocket, and you carried it, and once a night you unfolded it and let it breathe, and then you folded it again and went back to the basins and the bandages and the eleven dripping rhythms of the roof.

Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep—not dying, only dreaming; the dreams were often worse than the wounds. Jonah listened until the moan settled back into breathing.

Then he heard footsteps in the corridor.

He closed the ledger in one motion, smooth as closing a surgical kit, and set it face-down on the table, and by the time the blanket across the doorway swung aside he was standing with his hands loose at his sides, looking for all the world like a man who had risen to check on patients, which was true, and like a man with nothing on the table behind him, which was not.

It was Sergeant Brann, the night orderly, carrying a lantern and a face full of bad news.

"Vane." Brann's voice was pitched low, the way voices went in wards. "You're wanted. Orderly room. Now."

"At this hour?"

"At this hour." Brann set the lantern on the table—directly next to the ledger, close enough that its heat touched the boards, and Jonah felt his stomach drop through the floor and into the earth beneath it. "Dispatch rider came in half drowned. Whole army's moving. They're pulling us back off the river."

"Back where?"

"West, they say." Brann rubbed his jaw. Rainwater dripped off his coat onto the boards. "Evacuation orders at dawn. Everything that walks walks. Everything that can't—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Every nurse in every army learned that arithmetic in the first month: what can be carried, and what must be left for the enemy to find, and which of the two categories the wounded fall into.

"The men in Ward One can't walk," Jonah said. "Half of Two can't."

"That's not why they want you."

"No?"

Brann hesitated. Lantern light moved on his face and made him look older and more tired than a man of thirty ought to look, which was the general condition of everyone in the province that autumn. "They want a list," he said finally. "Of the walking wounded who can be pressed as bearers. And they want it by dawn. Written. In your hand." He almost smiled. "Congratulations, Vane. You're the only man here who writes straight."

He picked the lantern back up and swung out through the blanket, and his boots faded down the corridor, and the ward settled back into its music—*plink, plonk, pause*—as though the world outside the canvas had not just tilted.

Jonah stood a long moment.

By dawn he would have to write another kind of list, an official one, in the same careful upright hand, and the two lists would sit in the world side by side like twins separated at birth—one counting men the army acknowledged, and one counting the men it had chosen to forget. And between them, spreading westward with the retreating army, would come the question he could feel arriving already, cold as the river fog: *what do you do with a book of names when there is no longer any ground safe enough to hide it under?*

He looked at the ledger, face-down on the table, ordinary as a hymnal.

Then he knelt, lifted the loose plank, and began to decide.
