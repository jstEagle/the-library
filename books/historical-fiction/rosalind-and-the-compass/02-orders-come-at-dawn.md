## 2. Orders Come at Dawn

The order came before the light did.

Jonah Vane heard the hooves first—three horses moving fast along the ridge road, which meant staff officers, which meant bad news. In eleven months of war he had learned that good news traveled on foot and arrived late, if it arrived at all. He set down the forceps he was cleaning in a basin of gray water and stood in the doorway of Ward Two, listening to the riders slow, dismount, and begin shouting for Colonel Harrow's adjutant before their boots had fully found the mud.

Behind him, thirty-one men lay in rows on straw pallets, breathing the wet-smoke smell of a field hospital at three in the morning. Some of them slept. Most of them did not. The ones who could not sleep were the ones who had been here longest; they had developed the particular stillness of men who had learned to read a building by its sounds, the way sailors read weather. Jonah felt their attention settle on his back like a hand between his shoulder blades.

The lantern in Ward Two was turned low to save oil. He kept it burning anyway, all night, every night. A dark ward bred despair faster than gangrene bred fever, and despair killed men whose wounds should not have killed them. That was the first thing he had written in his ledger, in fact—not a name but a note, dated the third week of the campaign: *Darkness is also a casualty.* He had crossed it out afterward, embarrassed by its poetry. But it was true, and truth was the only thing he trusted himself to keep anymore.

The adjutant's voice carried across the yard, thin and cracked with cold: "Evacuate at first light. Everything portable. The army falls back to the Halven line."

*Falls back.* The phrase was doing a great deal of quiet work. It meant the front had broken at Kessel Ridge. It meant the wounded who could not walk would be left, or moved too late, or moved and died on the carts. It meant that everything Jonah had built in this barn over four months—the scrubbed tables, the boiled-linen screens, the floorboard under the third cot in Ward One—would be abandoned to whichever army came up the valley behind them.

He went back inside and began, without letting himself think about it, to triage.

* * *

Triage was the word the surgeons used and hated. It came from the French, *trier*, to sort—to divide a crowd of suffering men into three columns: those who would die regardless, those who would live regardless, and those whom intervention might tip from one column into the other. The arithmetic was merciless and it was honest, and Jonah had come to believe that honesty of this kind was the only mercy left on the plateau. To pretend all men could be saved was to save none.

But there was another sorting to do tonight, and it was not honest, and he had invented it himself.

At half past three, with the orderlies striking tents in the yard and the first of the walking wounded forming a shuffling column by the gate, Jonah knelt beside the third cot in Ward One and lifted the loose floorboard with the edge of a spoon.

The ledger came up wrapped in an oilcloth, dry, warm from the ground. It was not large—a book no bigger than a hymnal, bound in the leather he had cut from an old saddle skirt—and it weighed almost nothing. He knew this because he had weighed it once against his palm and told himself so. And yet when he held it now, in the blue hour before dawn, it pulled at his arm like something drowned.

Forty-one names.

That was the count as of last night, when Corporal Dunmore had died at ten minutes past nine of a thigh wound that should have taken a month to kill him and instead took six days. Forty-one names of men who had been struck from the rolls before they ever properly entered them—men recorded as *missing*, *deserted*, *transferred without record*, or, in the most elegant cases, never recorded at all. Men whose deaths were inconvenient. Lieutenant Sarr, shot by his own sentry during the confusion at the mill and logged as lost to enemy fire because the sentry was the major's nephew. The two privates of the Fourth Company who had drowned crossing the ford on a night march ordered by an officer who refused to wait for the moon and whose report placed them safely on the far bank. Boys whose mothers would receive letters saying nothing at all, or worse, letters saying *deserter*, and spend their lives waiting for a son who had chosen, in the army's telling, to run.

Jonah had started the ledger in September with a pencil stub and no philosophy. He had simply been unable to bear the arithmetic going unwritten. By November it had become the one discipline he kept more faithfully than his rounds. Each entry took the same form: name, regiment, date, place, cause of death where known, and beneath it a single line of witness—*I saw him fall. I dressed his wound. I closed his eyes. He was here.* Because that was the whole crime, wasn't it? Not the dying. The *here*. The refusal to admit these men had been here, under someone's command, in someone's care.

He sat back on his heels now with the book in his hands and listened to the evacuation gathering itself outside like a storm changing direction.

His kit lay open on the cot above him. He inventoried it by touch and habit: two dressings rolled tight, a tin of carbolic crystals, the small saw, the probe, a spool of silk ligature, laudanum—half a bottle, precious as blood—morphia tablets wrapped in waxed paper, a scalpel with a blade worn thin as a fingernail. It fit into a satchel designed to hold exactly this much and no more. He had packed and repacked it a dozen times over the months until the contents lived in his hands the way prayers live in the mouth.

There was room in the satchel for either the morphia and the carbolic, or the ledger.

Not both.

He measured this fact the way he measured a wound: without flinching, because flinching changed nothing. The satchel's flap would close over the book alone if he left out the drugs, and it would close over the drugs alone if the book stayed under the floorboard for whoever found this barn—Russian cavalry, or their own rear guard looting, or the rats and the rain, which were patient and thorough.

Morphia and carbolic were lives. He could say that plainly. There were men on the carts ahead of him tonight who would need both within a day, men whose wounds would fester without antiseptic and whose pain would tear the strength out of them without the poppy. Every surgeon on this plateau worked with a fraction of what he needed; to abandon any part of that fraction was to choose, in advance, a specific number of specific men and mark them for death.

And the ledger? The ledger was dead men. It saved no one. It fed no one. It was ink and grievance, a hymnal full of ghosts.

Except.

Except that a man who died erased was not merely dead—he was unmade. His mother received a lie. his debts went unpaid, his children learned shame instead of grief. And the officers who did the erasing learned that erasure was free, that it cost less than honesty, that the machinery of record-keeping could be bent quietly and no one would ever straighten it. Forty-one names was not the whole of the evil. Forty-one names was only the evidence. If the evidence burned, then every future battle would produce its own forty-one, and the next commander would erase them even more smoothly, because the practice would be established, customary, safe.

The ledger was not dead men. The ledger was the argument that dead men counted—that they had weight, that they pressed on the world, that they could not be subtracted by a clerk's pen. It was, he realized, kneeling there while the barn emptied around him, the only weapon he owned.

And weapons, in war, outranked medicine. That was the bitterest thing he had ever thought, and he thought it slowly, testing it for flaws, and could not find one.

* * *

"Surgeon Vane."

He closed the board with his knee and stood, turning the ledger against his chest inside his coat before Sergeant Brakel's lantern swung through the doorway. Brakel was sixty, built like a door, and had been a stonemason before the war; he handled the wounded with the gentleness of a man accustomed to things that could crack.

"They want the non-walkers loaded by five," Brakel said. "Carts for twenty. We've got thirty-one."

"I know."

"The colonel's staff says the worst go to Halven. The rest—" Brakel's jaw worked. "The rest stay comfortable as we can make them."

*Comfortable.* The word had a smell to it tonight, like flowers in a sickroom. Jonah nodded anyway. There was no other nod available.

Brakel lingered. His lantern light moved along the row of faces, and Jonah watched him do the same arithmetic everyone did, involuntarily, counting the living. Then the sergeant's eyes dropped to Jonah's coat, where the oilcloth made a faint rectangular shadow under the wool, and rose again, and said nothing. Brakel missed nothing. Jonah filed that away among the night's many debts.

"Your kit ready?" Brakel asked.

"Nearly."

"Don't be long. Rear guard burns what they can't carry." He said it flatly, a mason stating the weight a wall will bear, and went out.

Jonah stood alone in Ward One with the satchel open on the cot.

He took out the morphia first. Held it up to the lantern. Half a bottle, maybe eighty doses. Eighty nights of mercy, or eight crises, depending how you spent it. Then the carbolic, which was not mercy at all but prevention, which was the difference between a wound that healed and a wound that became a story ending in amputation or worse. Then the ligature silk, without which a bleeder died in minutes. Then the scalpel, which was nothing, which was only a tool, except that without a blade he was not a surgeon at all but a man in a bloody apron.

He laid them on the blanket in a row and looked at them the way you look at people at a funeral, knowing some of them are about to be spoken of in the past tense.

The ledger sat warm against his ribs.

*Choose,* said the night.

He thought of his father, a customs clerk in a river town whose name he had not spoken aloud in years, who had taught him that a record is a promise made to the future—that the whole architecture of decent life rested on the assumption that what is written will be honored. His father had believed in ledgers the way other men believed in God. *A man can lie,* the old man used to say, tapping the customs house ledgers, *but paper keeps faith.* Jonah had laughed at it once, young and sure of himself. Paper keeps faith. As though paper had ever stopped anyone with a rank and a reason.

But his father had also been the kind of clerk who, when ordered to falsify a shipment, resigned rather than comply, and had spent his last working years copying shipping manifests in a rented room, unpaid, because someone had to keep the true numbers somewhere. Jonah had been ashamed of him then. He understood him now with a completeness that felt like inheritance.

Outside, the first cart groaned into motion. A man cried out—a short sound, quickly smothered, the way the long-term wounded learn to cry out—and the wheels found the ruts of the valley road.

Jonah made his choice the way he made all his choices: quickly, so that the self that wanted to hesitate could not get a word in.

He put the ligature silk back in the satchel. He put the scalpel back. He put in the dressings, because dressings were cheap and everywhere and yet somehow always short. Then he wrapped the carbolic tin and the morphia bottle together in the last clean towel, knelt, lifted the floorboard, and laid them in the earth beside the ledger's old resting place, tucking the towel around them like bedding. He poured the last inch of his private flask of spirit over them—not for preservation, but because a soaked bundle wrapped in cloth looked, to a hurried looter, like something already ruined.

Then he hesitated, one breath, two—and added the morphine tablets, the waxed packet, deep in the corner where the board's shadow would hide them.

He was leaving the drugs. He heard the sentence complete itself in his head and felt it land in his chest like a swallowed stone. Somewhere ahead of him on this retreat was a man he would not be able to help, a pain he would have to talk to instead of treat, a fever he would fight with cool cloths and lies. He had just decided that man's fate, sight unseen, in a barn at dawn, and the deciding had taken less time than choosing a boot.

For forty-one names. For a book of the erased.

*If it survives,* he told the stone in his chest, *it saves more than the morphia ever would.* But he was enough of a surgeon to know when he was administering a placebo, and he administered it to himself anyway, and rose, and buttoned his coat over the book, and went out into the dark to load the carts.

* * *

The column moved at first light, and the light was the color of dishwater.

Jonah walked at the rear, beside the last cart, where Private Ostrander lay with his abdomen bound and his eyes open to the sky, counting clouds the way Rosalind—no. He corrected himself. No census woman yet. She was a rumor he had heard at the staff tent, a name attached to paperwork: some ministry sending enumerators up the line to take account of personnel, of stores, of God knew what. *Account.* The word snagged in him like a fishhook and he pushed past it. There would be clerks enough at Halven. There were always clerks.

Behind them, the hospital barn stood empty in the mist. He had done what he could for the eleven men who stayed: fresh dressings, water within reach, the morphia—no. The water. Water within reach, and the orderly Fennel, who had volunteered to remain, given instructions and the location of the buried tin, sworn to secrecy with a look rather than words. Fennel had gripped his hand and said, "You'll send for us," and Jonah had said, "We'll send for you," and both of them had known the sentence was a coin neither could honor, and had spent it anyway, because some currency exists only to be spent between people who know its worthlessness and pay it regardless.

Two miles down the valley road, the rearguard's smoke rose behind them—thin at first, then greasy and black. They were firing the stores they couldn't move. Jonah did not look back. Looking back was a luxury of men with clear accounts.

Ostrander's hand found his sleeve. "Vane." The private's voice was papery. "Did we leave anyone?"

"We left no one," Jonah said, which was true, and then, because the man's eyes held on him with the terrible literacy of the dying: "Everyone who could be moved was moved."

Which was also true. Both sentences true, and between them, in the white space where ink wasn't, the eleven men in the barn, and the tin under the floorboards, and the forty-one names riding against his heart toward a capital that did not know it was waiting for them.

The cart lurched. Ahead, the road bent toward the river and the town called Halven, and somewhere beyond Halven, three checkpoints between him and the tribunal, though he didn't know that yet—all he knew was that the road ran downhill toward the water and that every mile of it was a mile the ledger survived.

He kept his hand flat over his coat the whole way down, the way a man guards a wound.

It was, he reflected, becoming one.
