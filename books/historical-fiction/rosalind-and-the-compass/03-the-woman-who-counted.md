## 3. The Woman Who Counted

The road into Halven was a brown ribbon stitched with cart ruts and the bones of last autumn's harvest, and Rosalind Ashe walked it the way she walked every road: counting.

Four hundred and eleven paces from the milestone to the first cottage roof. Three chimneys smoking in a town that had once, by the census of seven years past, held ninety-one hearths. Two dogs, one lame. A dead horse by the drainage ditch, its belly swollen to the size of a hayrick, and no one sent to move it.

She recorded all of it in the ledger she carried against her ribs — the official one, bound in grey cloth, stamped with the seal of the Bureau of Population and Provision. *Halven district: population diminished, cause unrecorded, provision requisitions exceeding registered households by a factor of four.* The arithmetic did not lie. Arithmetic never lied; only people did, and they lied most fluently when war gave them an audience that did not check their figures.

Rosalind checked figures.

It was what she was for. Twenty-nine years old, widowed on paper if not in fact — Tomas Ashe had been swallowed by the army three winters ago along with his younger brother, and neither had come home — she had taken the Bureau's examination out of necessity and discovered, to her own quiet astonishment, that she was very good at it. Good at standing in a doorway with her notebook and asking the questions other people flinched from. *How many slept here before the shelling? How many sleep here now? Where is your husband? Your son? Your daughter?* People hated her for it, at first. Then, more often than she expected, they gripped her sleeve as she turned to leave and whispered the names they hadn't dared say aloud.

That was how she had learned about Halven's hospital: not from any military dispatch, but from a widow named Frau Ostrick, who said, in the tone of a woman reporting weather, *They bring them down to the tannery yard when the boats come. They don't put them in the rolls. My sister's boy went down that river alive and I have had no word since.*

*No word*, in wartime, was a country all its own, and Rosalind had been travelling through it for months.

So here she was, at half past ten in the morning, standing in the mud outside the tannery yard of Halven, looking up at a building that had once cured hides and now cured men, or tried to. The smell hit her first — carbolic laid over old blood laid over, faintly, stubbornly, the ghost of the tanning vats beneath it all, as though the building itself remembered what it had been made for. Smoke rose from two stovepipes. Orderlies in stained aprons moved between the door and a line of litters under an awning. And everywhere, everywhere, the sound: moaning low and constant like wind in a chimney, punctuated by the sharp bark of someone giving orders.

Rosalind opened her grey ledger, wetted her pencil, and wrote: *Field hospital, Halven. Time of inspection: 10:31.*

Then she squared her shoulders, settled her satchel, and walked toward the door as though she had every right in the world — which, by the authority of the Bureau seal in her pocket, she did. Whether anyone inside would honor that authority was another matter entirely. War made its own laws, and she had found that its officials regarded hers the way mules regard rain.

* * *

Inside, the tannery was dim and cavernous, lit by windows fogged with condensation and hung with canvas where the curing racks used to be. Rows of cots ran the length of the floor, close enough together that a nurse had to walk sideways between them. The air was warm and thick, breathing itself. Rosalind counted as she came down the aisle because she could not help it — forty cots visible, thirty-eight occupied, six of those occupied by men so still she marked them mentally in a separate column without yet letting herself write it.

A matron intercepted her near the middle of the room, a broad woman with flour-white forearms and eyes that had seen everything twice. "Visiting hours are for the chapel side," she said. "This is the surgical ward."

"I'm not visiting." Rosalind produced the letter of commission, unfolded it with the crispness of long practice. "Rosalind Ashe, Bureau of Population and Provision. I'm conducting a personnel audit for this district. I'll need your admission registers, your discharge rolls, and your correspondence with the divisional command."

The matron read the letter slowly. Around them a man began to cough, deep and wet, and someone farther down murmured to him in a dialect Rosalind half knew. When the matron looked up, her expression had not changed, which was itself informative.

"Registers are kept by the head nurse."

"Then I'll see the head nurse."

"He's busy."

"Then I'll wait for him to be less busy." Rosalind let the silence do its work. She had learned early that officials tested visitors the way dogs tested strangers — a growl first, to see if you flinched. "I can conduct my count of the ward while I wait, if that's agreeable. Forty cots, thirty-eight occupied. Shall I note the names?"

Something flickered behind the matron's eyes then — not guilt, exactly. Wariness. The look of a woman doing sums of her own. "Wait there," she said, and disappeared through a canvas flap at the far end.

Rosalind waited. She did not stand idle while she waited. She drifted along the cot rows with her pencil moving, recording what could be recorded openly — bandages, stumps, fevers — and noting what could not: the way two orderlies paused their conversation when she passed; the way one cot, number twenty-seven by her count, sat empty but freshly made, its blanket tucked with a care the others didn't receive; the way a young private with his arm in a sling watched her pass and then watched her some more, as though memorizing her for someone.

By the time the canvas flap moved again, she had filled three pages.

The man who came through it was not what she had expected. She had pictured the head nurse of a field hospital as either a harried surgeon's shadow or a fortress of a woman like the matron. Instead he was lean and dark-haired, perhaps thirty, with ink stains on his fingers and a tiredness worn so deep it had become almost elegant, the way a stone worn smooth becomes something other than rough. He moved down the aisle between the cots with a strange, gliding economy, touching a fevered forehead here, adjusting a pillow there, and each man he touched seemed to settle slightly, as though his hand carried something in it besides carbolic.

"Miss Ashe." His voice was quiet, pitched under the ward's noise rather than over it. "Jonah Vane. I keep the books here — such as they are." He glanced at her grey ledger without pretense of not noticing it. "The Bureau sends us auditors now? We can barely spare lint, let alone paperwork."

"The Bureau sends auditors wherever the numbers stop adding up," Rosalind said. "And in this district, Mr. Vane, they have stopped adding up magnificently."

"War is hard on arithmetic."

"So I'm told. Repeatedly. By everyone whose figures don't survive my questions." She held his gaze. He held hers back, and she noted — filed away, the way she filed everything — that he did not do what most men did when she stared at them, which was either bluster or retreat. He simply stood there, patient as a well, waiting to hear what she'd draw up.

"Your matron tells me you keep the admission registers," she said. "I'll need to see them. All of them. From the establishment of this facility to today."

"They're not exciting reading."

"Neither am I, Mr. Vane. We should get on well."

For the first time, something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, buried under the ward's weight. He turned without a word and led her toward the back of the tannery, to a partitioned corner that had once been the foreman's office. A desk, a lamp, shelves of files swollen with damp. He pulled a register from the shelf and set it before her, and Rosalind noticed that he chose it without hesitation, without looking through the stack — as though he already knew exactly which volume she would ask for and had it ready.

Or had it ready regardless of what she asked for. She noted that too.

* * *

The register was meticulous. That was the first thing she felt, before she even thought it: the handwriting was beautiful, small and upright, each entry ruled and dated, causes of death specified in a precise clinical hand. *Pvt. Aldric Meunier, 22nd Rifles, gunshot, left thigh, died 14th, gangrene.* Page after page of it, hundreds of entries, the whole terrible harvest gathered in with a care that was almost devotional.

It was also wrong. Not sloppy-wrong. *Shaped*-wrong.

Rosalind worked through it with her lips slightly parted, running totals in her head, cross-checking against the casualty abstracts the Bureau had received from divisional command — abstracts she had copied out herself in the capital and carried folded in her satchel like scripture. Entry by entry, day by day, she reconciled the two documents, and entry by entry, day by day, they diverged.

"Mr. Vane," she said at last, not looking up, "how many men does this facility hold at capacity?"

"A hundred and forty, stretched. A hundred and sixty if we put them in the drying sheds."

"And how many deaths have you recorded since the eighth of the month?"

He told her. It matched her total exactly, which surprised her, and then annoyed her, because she had wanted to catch him out on it.

"Now," she said, turning the register around to face him, her pencil resting on the page like a compass needle finding north. "Here is the abstract divisional command submitted to the Bureau for the same period. Sixty-two deaths from this facility." She tapped the second figure. "You have recorded ninety-four."

The lamp hissed softly between them. Somewhere beyond the canvas wall, a man called out for water in a voice like a gull's.

"Command counts differently than we do," Vane said. His voice was still quiet, still level. "Men who die in transit aren't always entered here. Men who die within an hour of arrival are sometimes returned to the rolls of their own units, and their units report them or don't according to their own customs. The Bureau's abstract is assembled from unit returns. Mine is assembled from bodies." He met her eyes. "Bodies are harder to argue with."

"It's a lovely answer," Rosalind said. "You've clearly given it before."

"Once or twice."

"To whom?"

"To the last auditor." Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice — gone before she could name it. "There wasn't one. But there would have been, sooner or later. Everyone gets audited eventually. It's the century we live in."

Rosalind studied him for a long moment. Then she turned back to the register and did the thing she had actually come to do — the thing she had rehearsed on the road, walking, counting paces, fitting the question into her mouth until it sounded like nothing more than routine diligence.

"One more reconciliation, and I'll trouble you no further today." She drew the unit abstracts toward her again. "I track missing personnel as well as excess deaths. Desertions, stragglers, men lost between units. It's tedious work, but the Bureau likes to know where its soldiers' names have gone." She let her pencil drift down the column of names. "I've noticed several men from the 22nd Rifles listed as deserted in the weeks after the action at Kessel Bridge. Curious number of them. Eleven, by my count. Desertion tends to run in waves after a rout, of course, but these eleven have a pattern — they were all wounded before they vanished. All passed through dressing stations. Several, according to the unit returns, were evacuated by river."

She looked up. "Did any of them pass through here?"

And there it was. She saw it happen — saw the change come over him, subtle as frost forming on a window. Nothing about his posture altered. Nothing about his face, strictly speaking. But the warmth went out of the air between them, the way it does when a door closes somewhere deep in a house, and Rosalind understood that she had just watched Jonah Vane decide exactly how much of himself he was going to show her.

Which was none.

"I couldn't say," he said. "We get hundreds of men off those boats. I don't track them after they leave the ward. If a man recovers, he goes back to his unit. What his unit writes about him afterward is the unit's business."

"Even when the unit writes *deserted*?"

"Especially then. Units write all sorts of things." He closed the register gently, both hands flat on the cover, the gesture of a man closing a coffin lid. "Desertion is convenient, Miss Ashe. For everyone except the deserter. It requires no inquiry, no tribunal, no pension, no letters home. A man who deserts costs the army nothing but ink." His voice stayed perfectly level. "A man who dies badly costs it considerably more."

The words hung in the lamplit little office. Rosalind heard what was inside them, underneath them, the way she heard everything — the fury banked down to coals, the grief pressed flat like flowers in a book. This man had counted the same discrepancy she had counted. He had gotten to it first. Perhaps he had built his beautiful, meticulous register precisely so that no one like her would ever be able to say the numbers didn't add up — while the true numbers lived somewhere else entirely.

Somewhere she intended to find.

"You've been very helpful, Mr. Vane," she said, rising, closing her own ledger with a snap. "I'll want copies of these pages. I'll return tomorrow with the Bureau's copying forms. And I may have further questions about individual cases — names, dates, boat manifests. I find the river traffic particularly interesting."

"Everyone finds the river interesting," Vane said. "That's why they dammed it."

She paused at the canvas flap and looked back at him. He had already picked up a pen, already bent over his papers, already the picture of a busy man with no further time for auditors — and yet she would have sworn, by the set of his shoulders, that he was listening to her leave with every nerve he owned.

"Good afternoon, Mr. Vane."

"Good afternoon, Miss Ashe. Mind the step. The yard floods when the boats come in."

Outside, the cold hit her like clean water. Rosalind walked to the end of the tannery yard, stopped beside the drainage ditch, and allowed herself, for exactly one minute by her pocket watch, to feel the shaking that had been building in her hands since she'd said her brother's name without saying it.

*Eleven men. Wounded. Evacuated by river. Listed as deserters.*

Elias Ashe, private, 22nd Rifles, wounded at Kessel Bridge, evacuated by river, subsequently recorded as deserted. Her brother, erased from the rolls of the living by a single dry word, and no one — no clerk, no commander, no census — accountable for the arithmetic of him.

She breathed until the minute expired. Then she opened her ledger to a fresh page and wrote, in her smallest, firmest hand:

*Head nurse, field hospital Halven (name: J. Vane). Keeps excellent records. Records incomplete by design. He knows where the missing names are. He knows I know. Tomorrow he will try to decide whether I am dangerous enough to matter.*

Below it, after a moment, she added:

*He lies the way honest men lie — badly, and only about the things that matter.*

She snapped the ledger shut. Behind her, in the tannery, the ward breathed on, forty cots strong, and somewhere in that building — beneath a floorboard, behind a brick, inside a man's coat — she was certain the rest of the list was waiting.

Tomorrow, then. She had one name to find among the missing, and she had just met the man who kept them.

She counted her steps back into town. There were two hundred and six of them, and she did not miss one.
