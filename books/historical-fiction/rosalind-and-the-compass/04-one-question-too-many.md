## 4. One Question Too Many

The tea was terrible, and that was the first thing Rosalind learned about the field hospital at Halven: they stretched their stores thin enough to read through. The liquid in her cup was the color of river water with ambitions, tasting faintly of bark and something burnt. She drank it anyway. Refusing hospitality was a kind of arithmetic too—offense given, cooperation lost—and Rosalind Ashe had never once in her life made an error she could have avoided by counting.

She sat across a plank table from Jonah Vane in what had been, before the war, the parlor of a merchant's house on the edge of Halven. Now it was a receiving ward for officers well enough to sit upright but not well enough to be sent back over the river. A wounded lieutenant snored on a cot against the far wall, his bandaged leg propped on a stool like something borrowed. Rain worked at the windows in long gray strokes. Somewhere deeper in the house, a man moaned in a rhythm that had stopped meaning anything an hour ago.

Jonah poured himself tea he did not drink. She had noticed that about him already—he poured, he held, he set it down untouched, as though the ritual were the point and the drinking an afterthought. His hands were steady. That interested her. Most men's hands told on them within ten minutes of conversation; hers did, if she let them, though she never let them.

"Your credentials are unusual," he said. "A census taker this close to the line."

"The census doesn't stop for wars." She said it evenly, because it was true, or true enough. "People die where they live, Mr. Vane. Someone has to record them."

"And you've come to Halven to record them."

"I've come to reconcile them."

He looked up at that. It was a small movement—a lift of the eyes, no more—but she caught it the way she caught everything, catalogued it, filed it under *later*. Reconcile was not a word civilians used. Reconcile was a word for ledgers.

"You'll find the war has made a mess of the count," he said. "Towns empty overnight. Parishes burn their registers when they evacuate. Half the province is somewhere other than where it was last spring."

"That," said Rosalind, "is precisely my difficulty."

She opened her satchel—not all the way, just enough to draw out a folded paper, and she watched him watch the satchel itself, not the paper. Another entry for the file. Men who were hiding things always looked at containers.

"This is the muster abstract for the Fourth Provincial Regiment," she said, smoothing the page flat between them. "Transcribed from the War Office rolls in the autumn. Nine hundred and fourteen men entered as present and effective." She turned it around so it faced him. "In the winter dispatches, the same regiment reports six hundred and two. I am not asking where three hundred men went to, Mr. Vane. I know where men go in wartime. I am asking why the difference appears nowhere. No casualty returns. No discharge papers. No transfer orders. Three hundred and twelve men, vanished off the page as if the ink were ashamed of them."

The rain filled the silence for a while. On his cot, the lieutenant turned over and muttered something about the bridge at Marrow Ford, then slept again.

"Paperwork," Jonah said at last, "is the first casualty of any retreat."

"It isn't paperwork I'm concerned with."

"No?"

"No." She folded her hands around the warm cup, and let the pause stretch until it began to hurt him—she could see it begin to hurt him, a tightening at the corner of his jaw—and then she said the name. "I'm concerned with one man. Elias Ashe. Private, Fourth Provincial, entered present and effective in the autumn muster. Listed in the winter rolls as—" she recited it without looking at her notes, because she had not needed notes for weeks now— "*deserted in the field, whereabouts unknown.*"

Something happened to Jonah Vane's face.

It was nothing a casual observer would have caught. He was good—better than good; there were moments when she thought he might actually be better at this than she was, which was a thought she did not enjoy. But she saw the half-second delay before he reached for sympathy, the way his eyes went first to the middle distance where memory keeps its files, and then, only then, arranged themselves into the soft blankness men wear for grieving sisters.

"Ashe," he repeated. "You're related."

"He's my brother."

"I'm sorry," he said, and the words came out smooth as poured oil, and that was the third thing she filed away, because grief spoken smoothly is either very old grief or no grief at all, and he had known Elias Ashe for exactly as long as it took her to say the name.

"You knew him," she said. Not a question.

"I knew of him." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. "This is a hospital, Miss Ashe. Men pass through. I couldn't speak to every face."

"But you remember his."

Another quarter-turn of the cup. "Why would you say that?"

"Because when I said his name, you looked at the window. Toward the river. Toward the direction a man would have gone if he crossed over." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, the tone one uses for reading aloud. "Deserters run toward home or toward safety. Your eyes went neither left nor right. They went straight through the wall, due east, like a compass needle. Like a habit."

For the first time since she had entered the house, Jonah Vane stopped performing. It lasted perhaps two seconds—the mask didn't drop so much as go still, like a pond freezing over—and in those two seconds she saw what lived underneath: exhaustion, yes, and caution, but underneath both of those, something that startled her with its plainness. Grief. Real grief, unarranged, the kind that has been carried alone so long it has grown into the bone.

Then the mask thawed, and he was the careful nurse again.

"You've done this work a long time," he said.

"Census work? Six years."

"Not census work." He nodded at her satchel, at the muster abstract lying between them like a body on a slab. "Counting work. Finding work. Whatever it is you actually do, Miss Ashe, because I don't believe for a moment that the provincial census office sends women to war zones to ask after individual privates."

Rosalind felt the ground shift beneath her, the particular vertigo of a hunter realizing she has walked into a clearing with another hunter already standing in it. She had prepared for suspicion, for bureaucratic stonewalling, for the dull hostility men reserved for women who asked questions near uniforms. She had not prepared to be seen through.

"The census office," she said slowly, "keeps records because records are how a country knows itself. When a country starts losing records—deliberately losing them—it stops being able to know itself. Someone has to notice. I notice." She lifted her chin. "Elias wrote to me every week from the front. Every week, Mr. Vane, without fail, even from the worst of it. His last letter came in the second week of December. In January the rolls list him deserted. My brother spent four years keeping our mother's accounts to the penny after our father died. He alphabetized his socks. Elias Ashe did not desert anything in his life, including arguments, including funerals, including me."

"And yet the roll says—"

"The roll," said Rosalind, "says what someone wanted it to say. That is the entire point of my being here."

The moaning from the inner ward had stopped. Somehow that was worse.

Jonah rose, took both cups, and refilled them from the dented pot on the sideboard. It was, she understood, a maneuver—time bought, faces rearranged in privacy—but she allowed it, because her own pulse had climbed and she wanted the interval too. When he sat down again, he set her cup before her and said, quietly:

"What do you want from me?"

"The truth about your brother," she said, and heard herself say *your* instead of *my*, and saw him hear it too, and understood in one cold drop what her tongue had just confessed: that whatever Elias had become to the army—to the rolls, to the dispatches—he had become, in some part of her, a question this man was holding. "I want to know if he passed through here. If he was wounded. If he died. If he deserted." Her voice caught on the last word and she hated it, hated the catch, hated that he saw it. "I can bury a brother, Mr. Vane. What I cannot do is spend the rest of my life reading a lie in government ink and pretending it's a grave."

Jonah looked at her for a long time. Outside, the rain thickened, and a lantern swinging in the corridor threw moving shadows across the ceiling like slow birds.

"Elias Ashe," he said finally, "came through Halven in November."

Her breath went out of her all at once, and she was glad, fiercely glad, that she had both hands wrapped around the cup, because otherwise they would have shown everything.

"Wounded?" she managed.

"Grazed. Shell fragment along the ribs. Ugly, but not mortal." He spoke in the flat cadence of a report now, and she recognized it for mercy—he was giving her facts in the shape facts take when a man has decided to hand over a few and hoard the rest. "He was lucid. Talkative, even. He talked about a sister who counted things. Doors, steps, spoons." A ghost of something moved across his mouth. "I remember thinking it sounded less like a habit than a prayer."

"It is a prayer," Rosalind said, before she could stop herself.

"He was evacuated east with the walking wounded on the twenty-ninth of November. There should be a transfer record." Jonah met her eyes, and held them, and said with deliberate clarity: "There is no transfer record. I looked."

"You looked."

"I look at a great many records. It's a habit of mine." He turned his cup again—that quarter-turn, always the same quarter-turn, clockwise, like winding something. "Men vanish in this war, Miss Ashe. Some of them desert. Some of them die unnamed in the mud and get written down wrong. And some of them—" he paused, and she watched him choose, watched the decision move through him like weather— "some of them are written down wrong on purpose. I don't know which Elias Ashe is. I wish to God I did."

"Why?" The word came out sharper than she intended. "Why does a field nurse wish to know the fate of one private among hundreds? Why do you look at a great many records, Mr. Vane? Why did you pour tea you won't drink, and turn your cup the same direction every time you're deciding whether to lie to me?"

The color left his face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"I don't know what you mean," he said, and it was the worst lie she had heard from him yet, and the fact that he offered it anyway—that a man this careful would reach for something this flimsy—told her more than competence ever could have. He was protecting something. Something bigger than Elias's file. Something that made even this conversation, with this woman, this dangerous.

"Here is what I know," Rosalind said, rising, folding the muster abstract along its worn creases. "I know my brother was here. I know he left on the twenty-ninth of November and arrived nowhere. I know that three hundred and twelve men of the Fourth Provincial are missing from the record without explanation, and I know that you flinched—I watched you flinch—when I called them vanished off the page. I know you are a careful man, Mr. Vane, and careful men do not flinch at arithmetic unless the arithmetic belongs to them."

She slung the satchel across her body and stood looking down at him, at this tired, guarded man with his steady hands and his untouched tea, and she felt the strange double motion of her own heart: the sister in it wanting to seize him by the collar and shake the truth loose, and the counter in it, colder, wiser, whispering: *not yet. You have found the shape of the hole. Do not fill it in haste. Mark it. Return.*

"Thank you for the tea," she said.

"Miss Ashe." He stood too, and for a moment they faced each other across the plank table with the rain coming down outside and the dead and broken sleeping all around them, and his voice dropped so low she had to lean in to keep it. "Whatever you think you've found here—whatever you intend to do next—there are men in this province whose names appear in places you haven't looked yet. Powerful men. When they erase a soldier, they don't stop at the soldier." His eyes held hers, and in them she saw not warning exactly but something rawer—an appeal, almost, as if he were pleading with her to be careful for reasons he could not say aloud. "Ask your questions carefully. Count slowly. Please."

"Is that concern for me," Rosalind asked, "or concern for yourself?"

And Jonah Vane smiled—a real smile, brief and crooked and terribly sad, the first honest thing she had seen him wear all afternoon.

"Yes," he said.

She walked out through the corridor of cots, past the lantern, past the men who watched her go with eyes that had seen too much to be curious anymore, and out into the rain, where the mud of Halven took her boots and held them. Behind her, in the parlor, she left a man sitting alone with two cups of tea he would not drink.

But she did not leave empty-handed. Walking back through the town toward the counting-house where she lodged, she turned the afternoon over and over, the way she turned everything, and itemized what she had taken from him without his knowing:

He knew Elias. Knew him well enough to remember the letter, the counting, the *prayer*. He had checked the transfer records—checked them before she ever arrived, or within hours of her asking, which meant he had known to look, which meant he suspected what the missing pages meant. He feared names appearing in places she hadn't looked—feared them enough to warn her, which meant those names were written down somewhere, kept somewhere, by someone.

By him.

It was in the way he touched the table when he thought she wasn't watching—inward, toward the floorboards, twice in one hour, the small unconscious gesture of a man confirming that something hidden beneath him was still there.

Rosalind Ashe stood at the corner of the flooded street, rain running off her hood, and permitted herself, for exactly five seconds, the luxury of certainty.

Then she began to plan how she would come back tomorrow, and the day after, and however many days it took, to sit across from Jonah Vane and drink his terrible tea—until one of them finally asked the question the other could not survive answering.

Behind her, in the darkening house above the river, a floorboard settled softly into place.
