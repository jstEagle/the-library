## 5. What the Floorboard Kept

The fever ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool, and Jonah Vane moved through it the way he moved through everything now—counting. Fourteen men breathing. Two not expected to see morning. One whose bandage needed changing before the light failed entirely.

He had learned, in three years of war, that counting was the only prayer he trusted. Not the chaplain's Latin, not the colonel's speeches about glory and province and the honor of the fallen. Numbers. Fourteen breathing. That was a number you could carry in your chest like a coal, and it kept you warm on nights when nothing else did.

He changed Private Hollis's dressing by lamplight, working quickly because quick hands hurt less than slow ones. The boy was nineteen and had taken grapeshot through the thigh at the ford, and he talked the whole time about his mother's bakery in Corven, how she made rye loaves heavy as paving stones, how when the war ended he would eat one every day until he sickened of them.

"You'll never sicken of them," Jonah said. "That's not how bread works."

"That's what my father said. About my mother's cooking." Hollis laughed, then winced, then laughed again anyway, which told Jonah more about the boy's chances than any chart could.

Jonah tied off the bandage, noted the hour in the margin of the treatment sheet, and went out into the blue dark between the wards.

* * *

His quarters were a repurposed linen closet at the end of the surgeons' corridor: a cot, a crate for a table, a candle stub, a window no wider than a hymnal that looked out onto the latrine ditch. He had lived in worse. He had lived in this same room during the winter retreat from Kessel with frost in his boots every morning, so he had no complaint to make now.

But when he opened the door, he knew before his boot crossed the threshold.

It was small things. Small things were always how a room betrayed its secrets. The candle stub had been moved—half an inch, maybe less, its wick now pointing toward the door instead of away from it. He always turned the wick outward after lighting it; some old habit of his mother's, trimming tapers in the chandlery in Halven before the war ate her savings. The crate-table sat a finger's width from the wall. He kept it flush; he had knocked the plaster loose there once and never let it shift since.

And the floorboard.

Third from the wall, second from the door. It looked exactly as it should look—the scuffed pine, the nail heads worn to dull moons, the seam filled with the grey dust that gathered in every corner of this building. But the dust in the seam had been disturbed. Not removed—disturbed. Someone had run a knife along it, or a fingernail, probing, testing, and then pressed the board back down with care, but dust does not forgive being pressed. Dust remembers.

Jonah stood in the doorway a long moment, letting his eyes move over the room without letting his body follow. The cot blanket was smooth but folded wrong—one corner tucked under instead of over. His spare shirt hung differently on its nail. The water jug had been refilled, and he had not asked anyone to refill it.

Someone had been here. Recently. Within hours, perhaps. The lamp oil smell was still faintly fresh in the corner where someone had knelt—he caught it now, under the carbolic, the ghost of a hand-lamp burning close to the floor.

He thought: *the provost.* The military police had been through the hospital twice since autumn, hunting deserters and hoarded morphine, and they searched the way oxen plowed—everything overturned, everything obvious. This had not been that. Whoever came here had been quiet, patient, methodical. They had lifted the board, seen what lay beneath, and put it back.

Or—they had lifted the board and not known what they saw. A ledger among medical ledgers. A book is only a book unless you know how to read it.

He crossed the room in four strides and knelt.

The board came up easier than it should have—someone had worked the nails loose already, saving him the effort, and that felt almost obscene, like coming home to find your bed warm and turned down by a stranger's hands.

Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the ledger.

He unwrapped it right there on his knees, fingers moving fast now, faster than he wanted them to, and checked it the way a man checks a wound—by feel first, then by sight. The binding held. The pages held. He fanned them in the lamplight and counted the entries out of habit: forty-one names in his own cramped hand, each with a date, a unit, a cause of death, and the single word that bound them all together—*omitted*. Forty-one men who had died in this war and appeared nowhere in it. No dispatch. No roll. No letter home. Forty-one men whose deaths would have embarrassed a colonel here, a supply officer there, a staff major who had ordered an assault everyone above him had forbidden and whose failure needed witnesses who could no longer witness anything.

Forty-one names. And the page after the last one, blank, waiting, because Jonah had learned long ago that the war was not finished making its list.

Everything intact. Everything undisturbed.

So why did his hands shake?

He sat back on his heels in the middle of his narrow room, ledger open across his thighs, and let the fear arrive properly, the way he let pain arrive in the ward—fully, honestly, because a fear denied only grew teeth in the dark.

Someone had found it. Someone had found it and left it.

Those two facts did not sit comfortably together. If the provost had found it, it would be gone, and so would he—there was a shed behind the guardhouse where men who wrote inconvenient things spent their last night, and Jonah had carried two of his own orderlies past that shed and heard nothing from them again. If a thief had found it, it would be gone too—a ledger of blank paper was worth something to a man short of paper, and this one wasn't blank.

Which meant whoever had knelt on this floor tonight had read enough to understand what they held, and had chosen—chosen—to leave it where it lay.

Why? Three answers came, and he turned them over like coins.

First: they wanted to know if he noticed. A test. Let the keeper discover the search and reveal himself by his panic—move the book, burn it, run. Any of those confirmed the book mattered.

Second: they wanted to follow it. Leave the bait in place and watch where the keeper carried it, and to whom.

Third—and this was the coin that cut his palm worst—they were not from the army at all. They wanted the ledger themselves, for their own reasons, and they were willing to wait.

Every answer ended the same way: someone was watching him, and he had walked into this room like a man walking into an open palm.

* * *

He wrapped the ledger and replaced the board and did not sleep. He sat on his cot with the lamp unlit and listened to the hospital breathe around him—the groans from the fever ward, the creak of Sister Aldous's shoes doing her rounds, the far-off challenge of a sentry at the river gate. At some point he took out the little notebook he kept for treatments and, on a blank leaf, wrote down every person who had passed through the surgeons' corridor that day, as best he could reconstruct it. It was a census of suspicion, and it shamed him even as he wrote it.

Sister Aldous. She had brought clean linens to his corridor at midday; she did most days. Fifty years old, hands like tree bark, had nursed through two wars before this one and wept only once that Jonah had ever seen—for a boy from her own village. Could she kneel on a floor for twenty minutes with a lamp? Her knees ached; she complained of it daily. Unlikely.

Corporal Fenwick, the clerk. He came twice a week with requisition forms and stayed to gossip. Fenwick had a memory like flypaper and a resentment against Captain Iles over a denied promotion, and Jonah had seen him reading things left face-down on tables when he thought no one observed. Possible. But Fenwick was a coward, and cowards do not kneel in the dark with lamps.

The new orderly, the quiet one assigned last week from the convalescent pool—young, watchful, always where the work wasn't. Jonah had marked him already as strange. What was his name? Renn. Orderly Renn. He had been sent to fetch Jonah three times today, and each time Jonah had already been gone. Or each time, Jonah had been meant to be gone.

And then there was her.

Rosalind Ashe. Census taker. Arrived Tuesday with her credentials and her questions, precise as a surgeon's probe. She had walked these corridors, asked about personnel rolls, asked about men transferred and men discharged and men whose paperwork had gone astray. She had taken tea in the mess while Jonah sat across from her and lied about a soldier named Elias Ashe, and lied badly, and watched her file the lie away behind those grey eyes like a specimen pinned to cork.

She was quartered in the annex. The annex connected to the main building by a covered passage. The passage door did not lock; Jonah knew because he had oiled its hinge himself in the first week, tired of its shrieking.

He did not want it to be her. That surprised him, the strength of the wanting. He had known her four days. He owed her nothing and suspected her of everything. And yet some part of him—the part that had watched her count the ward beds with her lips moving slightly, the part that had seen her stop at the bed of the dying Hollis and stand there a moment too long with something raw crossing her face—some part of him had decided she was not the enemy, and now that part argued loudly against the evidence.

But wanting did not make a thing true. He was a keeper of records; he dealt in what was written, not what was wished. And the evidence said: someone patient, someone careful, someone with reason to seek omitted names.

A census taker counts people. That was all. That was her trade. But Jonah had learned that in wartime, every honest trade grew a dishonest shadow. Censors read letters. Quartermasters sold boots. Chaplains informed. And perhaps census takers hunted.

Or perhaps she was exactly what she seemed, and the searcher was Renn, and Renn reported to someone whose name would one day fill a page of the ledger.

He could not know. That was the truth of it, and the truth was useless, and useless truths were the kind that killed you.

* * *

Dawn came up grey through the hymnal window, and with it came the decision, arriving not with thunder but with the plain arithmetic he trusted.

If he left the ledger beneath the floorboard, it belonged to whoever knelt next. Tonight, tomorrow, within the week—it was only a matter of time. A hiding place that had been found once was not a hiding place anymore; it was a trap he was sitting inside.

If he destroyed it, forty-one men became nothing. Not forgotten—worse than forgotten, because forgetting happens to the innocent, and these men had been erased deliberately, their deaths amputated from the record by officers who signed the amputations with comfortable pens. Jonah was the only place they existed. Burn him and the book and they had never lived at all. He had promised himself, the first night he wrote the first name—Private Tomas Wren, drowned in the Kessel canal, officially "deserted," actually dead of a beating ordered by his own captain—that he would be their ground to stand on. He could not unmake that promise without unmaking himself.

So: move it. Tonight. Before the watcher returned to check whether the bait had stirred.

Where? Not on his person—the wards were searched, the orderlies went through kits looking for contraband brandy, and a man who carried one book everywhere might as well wear a sign. Not with Sister Aldous; not because he doubted her, but because involving her made her guilty, and she had given enough to this war already. Not in the chapel—too obvious, too often used.

He turned the problem over through morning rounds, through Hollis's fever spike and break, through the amputation list the surgeons posted at noon (three names; he memorized them; he would write them tonight if any were missing from the dispatches). And somewhere between the second dressing change and the third, the answer arrived, simple and ugly and perfect:

The dead house.

Behind the surgical ward stood a low stone building where the bodies waited for burial details. It was cold, it was grim, and it was the one place in a hospital that no one searched—no officer wanted to inventory the dead, no thief wanted to rob them, and the provosts, superstitious to a man, entered only at bayonet-point. There was a shelf there for the belongings of the deceased, awaiting transport to their families, and among the Bibles and lockets and unsent letters, one more oilcloth bundle would be invisible. Hidden among the possessions of men already erased, the ledger of their erasure would lie safer than anywhere in the province of the living.

It was not sentimentality, he told himself. It was tactics. He told himself this twice, and on the third telling admitted that it was both, and that he no longer saw much difference between the two.

* * *

Night came down at half past five now, the days shrinking toward solstice, and by seven the corridors ran on lamplight and rumor. Jonah made his rounds, touched fourteen foreheads with the backs of his fingers, told Hollis he'd have his rye loaf yet, and then walked the long way to the dead house with the ledger flat against his belly under his coat, feeling it there like a second heartbeat.

The passage door to the annex stood ajar as he passed it. Through the gap: lamplight, and the scratch of a pen. Rosalind Ashe, awake, writing. Counting something. Recording someone. He could not make himself believe it was innocuous, and he could not make himself believe it was sinister, and he hated her a little for the uncertainty, and hated himself more for the warmth underneath the hate that refused, stubbornly, to die.

He kept walking.

In the dead house the air was stone-cold and sweet-rotten, candles guttering in jars against the walls. Six bodies under sheets on the biers, six men who had stopped being problems for anybody. He lifted the oilcloth bundle from his coat and stood a moment, holding it, and spoke aloud, very quietly, because the room seemed to require it.

"Keeping you safe," he said. "That's all this is. Keep you safe till I can get you counted."

No one answered, which was the proper way of the dead—silent, patient, trusting him with the only trust left in the world that asked nothing back.

He slid the bundle onto the belongings shelf, behind a dead sergeant's Bible and a dead drummer-boy's folded coat, and set the board back over it, and stepped out into the freezing yard.

Halfway across the yard, he stopped.

There was a lamp burning in Rosalind Ashe's window in the annex, and standing at that window—silhouetted, motionless—was Rosalind herself. Looking out. Looking, unless the night deceived him, directly at the dead house door he had just closed behind him.

She did not move. He did not move. Between them stretched thirty yards of frozen mud and every question either of them had failed to ask.

Then the lamp behind her went out, and the window became only a window, black and ordinary, and Jonah Vane stood alone in the cold with an empty place against his belly where the book had been, understanding suddenly and completely that whatever game had begun in his quarters last night had not paused—it had merely changed boards.

Tomorrow, he would have to decide whether to speak to her first, or wait to be spoken to.

He already knew which one frightened him more.
