## 6. A Bargain by Lamplight

### Rosalind

Rosalind Ashe had counted the lamps in Halven on her first evening in the town: eleven burning, four dark, one guttering in the wind off the river. She had counted the hospital's beds—thirty-one, though only twenty-six held men—and the orderlies, and the crates of lint and laudanum stacked against the east wall like a small city of suffering. Counting was what she did. It was how a woman alone moved through a war without being swept into it. Numbers were neutral. Numbers asked for nothing.

But numbers had led her here, to the door of Jonah Vane's quarters at half past nine, with a candle in one hand and her brother's name folded in her breast pocket like a knife.

She had watched him for three days. That was the census taker's gift, the thing that made clerks uneasy and colonels polite: she could watch without appearing to watch. She had seen him at the ward desk each night after the last lamp was trimmed, writing in a book he kept low against his chest, angled away from the orderlies, away from the wounded men who drifted between sleep and fever. She had seen him carry it back to his quarters pressed flat beneath his shirt, where a man might carry a wound of his own.

And tonight she had seen him leave for the dysentery tents across the yard, called away by a boy with a lantern, and she had seen the way his hand paused at the door of his quarters—not touching it, only pausing, the way a man pauses at a grave.

The lock was old brass, older than the war, and it turned to the thin blade of her letter opener as if it had been waiting years for someone to try.

Inside: a cot, a chair, a washstand, a trunk with a broken hasp. A soldier's room, spare as a psalm. But the floorboard beneath the washstand sat proud of its neighbors by the width of a fingernail, and the dust along its seam had been disturbed, smeared by hands returning again and again to the same place. Rosalind knelt. The board came up with a sound like a sigh.

The ledger was bound in grey canvas, unmarked, no title, no number. She lifted it out and set it on her knees and did not open it at once. Her heart was behaving badly—racing, stumbling, the arithmetic of it all wrong—and she made herself breathe until it steadied. Then she opened the cover.

Names. Columns of them, in a small, disciplined hand that slanted forward as if hurrying. *Private Anselm Duret, 4th Rifles, died of wounds 14 March, absent from dispatch.* *Corporal Tomas Beck, drowned at the weir, 2 April, recorded as transferred.* Page after page. Dates. Places. Causes. And beside some entries, in a different pressure of pen—a harder stroke, almost angry—single words: *omitted*, *struck*, *unreported*.

Her eyes went down the columns the way water goes downhill, seeking its level, and found him on the ninth page.

*Elias Ashe, private, 7th Line, wounded at Kessel's Field, 21 May. Removed from rolls 30 May. Recorded: deserted.*

Recorded: deserted.

The words did not shock her. She had carried them too long for shock; they had worn smooth as river stones from handling. What struck her was the date. Thirty May. Elias had been struck from the rolls nine days after Kessel's Field—nine days in which, according to every answer she had pried out of clerks and quartermasters, no one had seen him, no one had questioned him, no one had done anything at all except decide that he had never existed.

Except this man. This nurse who wrote in the dark and hid the dead beneath his floorboards like a miser hoarding souls.

She heard the boots on the gravel outside and had perhaps four seconds. Enough to close the ledger, enough to lift the board, enough to lower it flush—or nearly flush, she would think later, cursing herself, nearly—and not enough to reach the door before it opened.

Jonah Vane stood in the doorway with rain still beaded on his shoulders, and the look on his face went through three expressions in the space of a breath: the blankness of a man seeing a stranger, then the recognition, then something colder and more careful settling over both like frost forming on glass.

"Your tent is across the yard," he said. "You've taken a wrong turn."

"I don't take wrong turns, Mr. Vane. I take measurements." She rose, keeping her voice level, keeping her hands visible and empty, which was somehow worse than holding a weapon. "I know what's under your floor."

The lamplight caught the side of his face. He did not move toward her and did not move away, and she understood that stillness itself was his armor—he had learned it in wards full of dying men, where panic killed faster than bullets.

"You searched my quarters."

"You left them unlocked to a woman you've been lying to for three days." She let that sit. "I searched them because I've been searching for my brother since June, and every road ends either in a clerk's shrug or in you."

"Then you've wasted your time. I'm a nurse. I change dressings and count pulses."

"And write names." She took a step toward him, and now her voice came lower, and she hated the tremor in it and let it stay anyway, because some things should tremble. "*Elias Ashe. Private, Seventh Line. Wounded at Kessel's Field. Struck from the rolls thirty May. Recorded deserted.* Your handwriting, Mr. Vane. I would know it anywhere now. It's very neat. Neater than the army's."

Something moved behind his eyes—the first crack in the frost. Not guilt exactly. Recognition, perhaps, of how far past bargaining they both already were.

"You read my brother's name," she said, "in a book of dead men who were never allowed to be dead. So before you lie to me again—and you will, because you're kind, and kindness lies beautifully—I want you to understand that I have walked four hundred miles on this. I have outlasted three clerks and a major. You are not going to outlast me."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere across the yard a man coughed the wet, tearing cough of the dysentery tents, and Jonah's head turned toward it an inch—an instinct, she saw, older than any secret—before turning back.

"Sit down, Miss Ashe," he said quietly. "If we're going to do this, we'll do it properly. There's tea in the tin on the shelf. I find the truth goes easier with something warm in the hand."

"It isn't truth yet," she said. "It's a bargain. Truth comes after."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, he almost smiled—tired, wry, the smile of a man finding an opponent worth the losing. "Yes," he said. "I suppose it does."

### Jonah

Jonah had imagined this moment a dozen times since the ledger first went under the floorboard—imagined the knock at midnight, the provost's lantern, the officer reading his own careful script aloud in a voice gone flat with contempt. He had rehearsed his defenses the way other men rehearsed prayers. None of the rehearsals had included tea.

She sat across from him with the cup cradled in both hands, steam rising past a face that might have been carved for a courthouse frieze—composed, exacting, patient as arithmetic—and Jonah understood that he had lost the moment she'd spoken Elias's page aloud. Not because she knew. Because of *how* she knew: word for word, date for date. She had read that entry the way he had written it, with everything in her.

"The ledger stays hidden," he said. "That's the first condition, whatever else we agree."

"It's the only condition I care about, so we may yet agree." She set the cup down. "Tell me why. Not the noble version. The true one."

He turned his own cup slowly on its saucer, watching the ring of damp it left. No one had asked him that. The orderlies thought him eccentric; the chaplain thought him devout; the dead, who were the only ones who truly knew, said nothing.

"The first one was Duret," he said. "Anselm Duret. He died in my ward in March with his hand around my wrist, asking me to write to his mother, and I wrote, and the letter came back marked *addressee unknown*, because officially there was no Anselm Duret to have a mother. His company had been cut to pieces covering a retreat that Colonel Brandt's dispatch described as a *measured repositioning*. Forty men went into the orchard at Vell. Nine came out. The dispatch named none of them." He heard his own voice go flat and stopped, and started again. "I began writing them down because the alternative was to stand at their bedsides and know that when they closed their eyes, the world would simply... close over them. Like water. I couldn't make the world honest. I could make a book."

Rosalind was watching him with an expression he couldn't parse—something between assessment and grief.

"And Brandt knows?"

"Brandt signs the omissions. Whether he knows a record exists—" He shook his head. "I've assumed not. I've assumed that if he knew, I'd already be digging trenches somewhere south of nowhere."

"Then you've been lucky."

"I've been careful. Luck is what careless people call other people's caution." He met her eyes. "Now. Your half of the bargain. You said passage through the checkpoints. Say it plainly, because I've been lied to by professionals this year and I've developed an ear."

She didn't flinch at that, which he noted, and respected.

"I'm a census taker for the Interior Commission," she said. "Wartime enumeration—refugee counts, requisition audits, population returns. My credentials are genuine, my commission is genuine, and my route to the capital is genuine: Marrow Ford bridge, the tollhouse at Breck, the capital gate. Three checkpoints. Sanitary commissions and census parties pass with a signature and a glance, because no one wants to be the officer who delayed the government's paperwork." She paused. "I can take one companion listed as my assistant. Enumerators keep assistants. No one looks at assistants. Assistants are furniture."

"And in exchange?"

"Elias's page." Her voice stayed level but her hands, wrapped around the cooling cup, had gone white at the knuckles. "Not the whole ledger. His page. Torn out, given to me, mine. Whatever happens to the rest—to you, to Brandt, to the tribunal I know you're dreaming of carrying this to—that page belongs to his family before it belongs to history."

Jonah sat back. Outside, the rain had thickened, drumming on the canvas awning over the window, and the lamplight bent and shivered with the sound.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. "A tribunal doesn't weigh evidence by the ounce. It weighs it by the pound. One page proves one death misrecorded. The book proves a practice. Tear out Elias and you weaken the case that might clear his name entirely."

"I understand arithmetic, Mr. Vane. I do it for a living." For the first time something broke through the composure—not anger, something rawer. "My mother has received no body, no effects, no letter. She receives once a month an official form confirming that her son deserted, and she is expected to sign her name beneath the word *deserter* in acknowledgment, as though acknowledging it might make it bearable. Every month she sends the form back unsigned. Every month they send it again. You want to talk about weight?" She stood, and the cup rattled on its saucer. "Give her back one page, and she can stop signing."

The silence afterward had texture, like felt. Jonah looked at her—at the mud dried gray on the hem of her skirt, at the ink stain on her right middle finger, at the exhaustion she wore the way soldiers wore theirs, invisibly, having long since stopped noticing—and felt something shift in his chest that he distrusted immediately and completely.

"If Elias's page leaves the book," he said slowly, "it leaves with a copy. In my hand. Word for word. If anything happens to you between here and the capital—if you're robbed, detained, drowned in a ditch—the record survives."

"That's fair."

"And if we're caught, the book burns. All of it. I won't hand Brandt a list of forty families to visit in the night."

"Burned," she agreed. "Though I'd ask you to let me try to swallow it first. I've always wondered how far conviction extends past the throat."

Despite himself—despite everything, the fear and the fury and the three days of watching her watch him—Jonah laughed. It came out rough, unused, startling them both. Across the yard the coughing went on. The lamp steadied.

"There's one more thing," he said, and now he was the one who had to choose, standing at the edge of his own floorboard. "You should know what you'd be carrying. Not just paper. If the provost takes us, they don't hang couriers. They take couriers apart slowly, in rooms without windows, and they start with questions about their accomplices. Your mother's address. Your commission's seal. Everything you are becomes ammunition pointed at everyone you love. I've carried this book eight months knowing that. You'd be choosing it in an evening."

"I've been choosing it since June," Rosalind said. "I simply hadn't told anyone."

She extended her hand across the little table, palm down, the gesture of a merchant sealing terms rather than a lady taking a greeting, and Jonah found that he liked her enormously for it.

"One question," he said, before taking it. "At dinner the first night. I asked if you'd lost anyone in the war, and you said, *the war hasn't finished collecting*. Why not tell me then?"

"Because you lied to me first," she said, "about whether you'd ever heard the name Ashe. Your face did something complicated, and I count faces the way I count lamps." She shrugged, almost apologetic. "Besides—a bargain needs two people who need each other. Last week you needed nothing. Tonight you need me, and I need you. That's the only foundation I trust."

He took her hand. Her grip was dry and strong and cold from the walk across the yard, and it held for a moment longer than commerce required.

"We leave the day after tomorrow," he said. "There's a sanitary convoy forming for Marrow Ford—lint, chloroform, a surgeon who drinks. We attach ourselves to it. Until then, you sleep in the census billet, you ask me nothing in front of witnesses, and the ledger stays where it is."

"Under the floorboard that no longer sits quite flush," she said, "because I'm apparently better with numbers than with nails."

He crossed to the washstand, worked the board up, and settled the grey canvas book back into its dark. As he pressed the wood home he became aware of her standing behind him, watching—not the hiding place, he realized, but his hands. Learning them. Deciding, perhaps, whether they were hands that could be trusted with her brother's last testimony.

"They'll search again," she said quietly. "Whoever loosened that nail before me. You know that."

He did know. Someone had been through this room within the past fortnight—the board had been replaced by a different hand, hurriedly, one corner pinched where his own fit smooth. He had told himself it was a pilfering orderly. He had not believed himself.

"Then we move sooner," Jonah said. "Tomorrow, if the convoy will take us."

Outside, the rain was slackening, and far off toward the front a gun spoke once, twice, and fell silent, like a man clearing his throat before saying something terrible. Rosalind turned her face toward the sound, and Jonah watched her count the intervals between the reports—lips moving slightly, making her quiet arithmetic even of thunder.

"Daylight tomorrow," she said. "Before the convoy forms. I want to see the road while it's still empty."

He nodded, and blew out the lamp, and in the dark they stood a moment on opposite sides of the table, two people bound now by a grey book beneath a loose board, listening to the war count down the hours.
