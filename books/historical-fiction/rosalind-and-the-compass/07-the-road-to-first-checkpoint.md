## 7. The Road to First Checkpoint

They left before the birds had finished their argument with the dawn.

Rosalind had counted the hours in the dark—she counted most things now; it was how she kept the fear from pooling—and by her reckoning they had four hundred and eleven minutes of grey light between the hospital's last lamp and the moment the Marrow Ford bridge would grow eyes. Four hundred and eleven minutes to become someone else.

The sanitary commission had been Jonah's idea, or rather his memory of an idea: a supply wagon bound west for the river towns, carrying lime and bandages and a driver who asked no questions because he was paid not to. The commission's papers were real enough—requisitioned from a clerk at Halven who owed Jonah a debt and did not know it yet. Two names added to a manifest in careful copperplate: *A. Merrick, inspector. R. Voss, assistant.* Rosalind had watched him write them and felt something turn over in her chest, because she recognized the handwriting. She had seen it on Elias's last letter home, the one that arrived eight months late with its corner burned away.

She said nothing about that. There were things you carried and things you set down, and she was still learning which was which.

"You'll want the shawl higher," Jonah said now, not looking at her. He was checking the wagon's tailboard straps, tugging each one twice. "Women on commission papers draw attention unless they look like they'd rather be anywhere else. Miserable is convincing."

"I've been miserable since March," Rosalind said. "It shouldn't require effort."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might take root. "Good. Then we're halfway dressed."

* * *

The road west from Halven ran along the river's shoulder, a rutted seam of mud and old corduroy logging track, and by mid-morning it had begun to fill with the debris of the retreat. They passed a gun carriage sunk to its axles, horses cut loose and gone. They passed a company of stragglers who watched the sanitary wagon pass with expressions Rosalind catalogued without meaning to: envy, suspicion, and in one young man's face something worse—the particular blankness of a soldier deciding whether to beg or to steal. She counted them as they passed. Thirty-one men, two limping, one carrying another. She could not stop doing it. The counting had begun years ago in the census offices, when numbers were only numbers, and now every figure wore a face, and the faces followed her into sleep.

"Forty-two," she murmured, after a column of prisoners shuffled past under escort.

Jonah glanced up from the manifest he was pretending to study. "What?"

"Prisoners. Forty-two, going the other way." She caught herself, pressed her lips together. "Habit."

"It's not a bad habit," he said. "Out here it's the only honest arithmetic left." He turned back to the page, but she saw that his eyes weren't moving along the lines. He was watching the road ahead, the way a man watches weather. "Most people count what they're owed. You count everything."

"That's what made me good at my work," Rosalind said. "And what made everyone hate me for it."

"And what brought you to Halven."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it. But neither of them looked away, and the silence between them took on the quality of a held breath—two people agreeing, wordlessly, not to say the thing they were both thinking about, which was a name, and whose name, and what page of a grey book it might be written on.

The driver, a stooped man named Pell with a voice like gravel poured through a sieve, spat off the bench and said, "Bridge is two miles. You want to walk it from here. Wagons get searched proper at the Ford; foot traffic gets waved through if you walk like you're tired."

"We are tired," Rosalind said.

"Then you're dressed for it," Pell said, and reined in.

* * *

They walked. The morning had turned thin and bright, the kind of light that showed everything honestly, and Rosalind found herself cataloguing that too: the brown water running high and fast below the embankment, the willows stripped to whips, the smell of lime from the wagon bed clinging to their sleeves like a second identity. Jonah carried the document case against his ribs, strapped flat beneath his coat, and walked with the deliberate looseness of a man who has practiced not looking like what he is.

"How long have you been doing this?" she asked, when the bridge came into view—a stone arch, older than the war, with a timber guardhouse squatting beside it like a tick.

"Doing what?"

"Noticing. Watching rooms. Knowing where every exit is before you finish saying hello."

He considered it longer than the question deserved, which was itself an answer. "My father was a parish clerk," he said at last. "Burials, baptisms, marriages. Everything written down twice, once for God and once for the Crown. He used to say a name unrecorded was a soul unguarded." A pause. The wind came off the river and flattened the reeds. "I believed him when I was young. I stopped believing in the Crown part somewhere around the third winter of this war. But I never stopped believing in the writing down."

Ahead, the checkpoint resolved into detail: six soldiers, a sergeant with a ledger of his own—heavy, official, red-bound—and a barrier arm of peeled pine lying across the road. Beyond the bridge, the country opened toward the low hills and, three days' hard travel beyond them, the capital, where a tribunal sat—or was supposed to sit—waiting for truth to arrive on foot.

"The officer at the desk," Jonah said quietly, without turning his head. "Young one, spectacles. That's Lieutenant Corrane. He was stationed at Halven through the autumn. He has a memory like a photograph plate—you show him your face once and it develops forever. If he looks too long at either of us—"

"Then we don't give him long to look," Rosalind said. "Papers first, voices bored, eyes down. Inspectors are always slightly insulted to be inspected. We'll be insulted."

He looked at her then, really looked, and there was something new in it—not the wariness of the hospital, not the calculation of the lamplit bargain, but something closer to respect arriving reluctantly, like a guest who'd sworn he wouldn't stay. "You've done this before."

"No," she said. "But I've been counted before, Mr. Vane. By clerks, by soldiers, by men who thought a woman with a ledger was furniture with opinions. I know exactly what it feels like to be a number instead of a person." She lifted her chin toward the barrier. "So today we are the ones holding the pen. Try to enjoy it."

* * *

The sergeant at the barrier was broad and bored and wanted the war to be somewhere else. He waved the papers at arm's length, squinted, and might have waved them through entirely if the young officer hadn't come out of the guardhouse wiping ink from his fingers.

Lieutenant Corrane was perhaps twenty-four, pale from indoor work, with the spectacles and the stillness of a man who reads everything. He took the commission papers from the sergeant's hand and read them the way Rosalind imagined he read everything—completely.

"Sanitary commission, western route." His voice was soft. "You're a day behind schedule. The Ashford convoy went through yesterday."

"Roads were flooded above Tarn," Jonah said. "We lost half a morning digging out."

Corrane's eyes came up. Not accusing—cataloguing. Rosalind watched him do it and felt the strange vertigo of recognition: this boy counted too. Somewhere behind those spectacles was a mental ledger, and she understood with cold clarity that their survival depended on whether their names were already written in it.

"Inspector Merrick," Corrane said, reading aloud. "Assistant Voss." His gaze settled on Rosalind, polite and thorough, taking in the shawl, the travel-stiff coat, the ink-stained fingers she had thought to keep visible. "The commission usually sends men."

"The commission sends whoever can add," Rosalind said, letting a thread of weariness sharpen into something dry. "The casualty returns from this sector couldn't be reconciled with the requisitions. Someone sent me because I apparently annoy people until columns agree."

For one heartbeat—one—something flickered across the lieutenant's face. It might have been amusement. It might have been the memory of her face, or a face like hers, in some office in Halven. The river roared softly under the arch, filling the silence, and Rosalind counted her own heartbeats the way she counted everything: one, two, three—

"Sergeant," Corrane said, handing back the papers. "Lift the bar."

Then, as they stepped through: "Madam. The tribunal in the capital—if your reconciliations concern the Third Brigade's returns, you may find the wait longer than expected. There have been... postponements."

Rosalind kept walking. It cost her more than the bridge had cost anyone to build. "Then I'll reconcile faster," she said over her shoulder, bored to the bone, and heard behind her the pine barrier groan back down onto its cradle.

They did not speak for a quarter mile. Then the road bent, the checkpoint slid out of sight behind a stand of black poplars, and Jonah exhaled like a man surfacing.

"'Postponements,'" he repeated.

"He knows something," Rosalind said. "Or he suspects. Either way—" She stopped. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking, and she folded them into the shawl where he wouldn't see. "Either way the door we're walking toward is being nailed shut while we walk. We can't lose another hour."

Jonah was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, almost gentle, which frightened her more than any threat. "You counted his blinks, didn't you? Back there. You counted something."

"Seventeen seconds he held the papers," she admitted. "Nine seconds looking at me. Three at you. I don't know why I do it. I've done it since I was a girl. My mother said I'd count the stitches in a funeral veil." She laughed, short and without humor. "Perhaps I thought if I could count everything, nothing could be taken without my knowing the exact size of the loss."

"And did it work?"

Ahead of them the road ran straight and empty toward the hills, and somewhere on it, in a grave or a marsh or a column of erased dispatches, was a number that no amount of counting had ever brought back. Rosalind looked at the horizon and told the truth, because the morning was too bright and too honest for anything else.

"No," she said. "Elias was one man out of eleven thousand in the district rolls. One line. I counted him my whole life and I never once noticed the exact weight of him until he was missing." Her voice held steady; she was proud of that, later. "That's why the book matters, Mr. Vane. Not the paper. The weighing. Somebody has to know exactly what was lost, or the losing costs nothing at all."

Jonah walked beside her without answering for a long time. When he finally did, it was barely above the wind.

"There's something you should know about the book," he said. "Before the next checkpoint. Something about the last page."

Behind them, faint as a struck match, a bugle sounded from the direction of Marrow Ford—and it was not the signal for a wagon to pass.
