## 8. Checkpoint One: The Bridge

The Marrow Ford revealed itself the way wounds reveal themselves—gradually, then all at once. First the smell: river mud and tar and the char of burned pilings from some earlier engagement. Then the sound, water dragging itself over the shallows with the patience of something that had outlasted every army ever sent to cross it. And then the bridge itself, a hunched wooden span repaired so many times it looked less like engineering than like a scar that had learned to hold its shape.

Rosalind counted under her breath as we came down the last stretch of road. She didn't seem to know she was doing it. Steps, I thought. She counts steps.

"Four guards on the near end," she murmured. "Two at the far barricade. A sergeant by the brazier. An officer."

"I see him."

The officer stood apart from his men the way a blade stands apart from its scabbard—upright, unnecessary-looking until you remembered what he was for. He was young. Younger than me by years, though he carried himself like a man who had been issued his posture rather than grown into it. His cap was immaculate. That was the detail that frightened me most, in the end. Everyone else at that checkpoint was gray with mud and woodsmoke, and this boy stood in the middle of the war looking like he'd been ironed that morning.

"He'll want papers," Rosalind said. "Everyone wants papers. But watch—he won't read them the way the others do."

"How do you know?"

"Because he isn't looking at us yet." Her eyes stayed forward, steady. "He's watching the water. Men who memorize things look away while they think. It keeps the picture clean."

I had told her about the bugle the night before—or started to. The confession about the ledger's final page had died somewhere between my teeth and the wind, swallowed whole when the sound reached us across the dark fields. Now it sat in me like a stone I'd eaten. There would be time. There always seemed to be time right up until there wasn't.

Our disguise held up to a glance, which is all any disguise can ask. We were the Sanitary Commission out of Halven—two clerks of cleanliness, sent to inspect latrine trenches and boiling kettles along the retreat corridor. Rosalind had produced the credentials herself, inked in a fair administrative hand, stamped with a seal she assured me was genuine because it had come off genuine correspondence. "A forgery lies," she'd said. "A true seal merely tells a different truth." I had decided not to examine that philosophy too closely. My own ledger did the same work every day.

The satchel rode against my ribs. I had rewrapped the ledger that morning in oilcloth, then in bandages, then inside a folio marked *Dressing Returns, March*, because no officer alive wanted to open a folder about gangrene. It weighed nothing and everything. All the way down the road I had felt it against my side like a second heartbeat, slightly out of rhythm with mine.

"Halt," called the nearest guard—a private with a carbine slung wrong and frostbite eating the tip of one ear. He waved us into the queue beside a grain wagon whose driver was arguing with destiny over a manifest.

Then the young officer turned around.

I understood Rosalind's warning in the space of a breath. He didn't look at us the way men look at strangers. He looked at us the way a man looks at a page he intends to keep. His gaze went to my boots first—the correct boots, thank God, army issue, though mine were two sizes generous—and then to Rosalind's hands, then my hands, then our faces, and I felt each pass like a cold finger drawn down a ledger column.

"You're not from Halven station," he said. Not a question. A correction.

"We're attached to it," Rosalind answered before I could arrange my face into anything useful. "Commission authority runs regional, Captain. Halven is where our stores are; we are not where they are. That's rather the point of an inspection round."

Something flickered behind his eyes. He was weighing her sentence, I realized—not for sense but for structure. Testing whether it sounded composed or remembered.

"Captain Voss," he supplied. "Lieutenant, actually." He almost smiled. "Everyone promotes me. It saves them having to learn my name."

"Lieutenant Voss." Rosalind extended her credentials without being asked, and I marveled at her nerve even as terror climbed my spine like floodwater. "Rosalind Ashe, census commissioner seconded to sanitary inspection. This is Mr. Corben, my assistant."

*Corben.* She had given me a dead man's name—I recognized it, God help me, from page eleven of my own ledger, a corporal drowned in the spring thaw whose death had never made the dispatches. She had chosen a name only I would recognize, and the look she gave me over the offered papers dared me to find it funny.

Voss took the credentials. And then, instead of reading them, he recited them.

"Ashe, R., commission grade two, endorsed at the provincial registry, countersigned—" his eyes lifted, bright and terrible, "—countersigned by Undersecretary Hallow, whose signature has a distinctive flaw in the descender of the L. Yours matches." He handed the papers back. "You're genuine. That's rare enough to be interesting."

My mouth had gone dry as gunpowder. Beside me Rosalind said, "Truth usually is," with such perfect boredom that I could have kissed her.

"And your assistant." Voss turned those cataloguing eyes on me. "Corben. First name?"

"Thomas," I said. It was the first lie I'd told all day and it tasted of copper.

"Corben, T.," Voss repeated softly, filing it wherever boys like him filed things—in whatever flawless drawer his mind kept that never stuck, never smudged, never forgot. "There was a Thomas Corben listed with the Ninth Reserve. Drowned, wasn't it? Spring crossing." He tilted his head. "Common name. But I remember common names best. They hide better."

The river roared beneath us. Somewhere behind the grain wagon a mule complained about the century it had been born into.

"Clerks borrow dead men's names all the time," I said, and heard my own voice arrive from very far away. "It's a superstition of the trade. The dead don't object to paperwork."

Voss looked at me for three full seconds. I have stood in surgery watching a man die and known less fear than I knew in those three seconds. Because here was the thing I understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand ice underfoot: this boy wasn't suspicious. Suspicion requires a hypothesis. He simply collected people—faces, names, flaws in signatures—and stored them against a future need, the way a miser stores coins he doesn't yet know how to spend. If we were ever unmasked, it wouldn't be today's interrogation that damned us. It would be today's memory.

"Superstition," he repeated. "I like that. Most men's superstitions cost them buttons or salt. Yours costs you an identity." He stepped aside, gesturing toward the bridge with two fingers. "Pass. Commission business is above my pay, more's the pity. I'd enjoy a longer conversation."

"We're expected back by dark," Rosalind said.

"Everyone says that." For a moment—just a moment—something human moved through the lieutenant's face, some loneliness so well drilled it barely showed. "No one means it. The war eats returns like ours. Go on, then. Mind the third plank from the far end. It lies about being solid."

We walked onto the bridge.

The planks rang hollow under our boots. Below, between the gaps, the Marrow Ford ran brown and fast, carrying sticks and leaves and one abandoned forage cap toward whatever sea received this province's grief. Halfway across, Rosalind spoke without turning her head, her lips barely moving.

"He memorized us."

"Yes."

"Both names. Both faces. The seal."

"The seal most of all."

She was silent for four steps. Five. Six. Counting, always counting. Then: "If we're stopped again—if anyone compares notes—we cannot pass this way twice. Whatever happens at the tollhouse, whatever happens at the gate, we do not come back over this bridge."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stopped then, in the middle of the span, wind pulling loose strands of hair across her face, and looked at me with those gray eyes that totted up everything and forgave little. "Because you've been carrying that satchel like a man carries a guilty conscience, Mr. Vane, and a guilty conscience hesitates. At the far end there's a sergeant with a brazier and a manifest book, and if Lieutenant Voss has a counterpart anywhere in this province, it will be there."

"It won't be."

"You've seen the orders?"

"I've seen the pattern." I nodded back toward the checkpoint, where Voss had resumed his vigil, immaculate among the mud-caked. "They put the memorizers at the crossings. Names flow through bridges. Everything else flows everywhere." I started walking again; standing still invited questions. "The tollhouse will have different dogs. Toll men count cargo, not faces. Cargo is heavier to remember."

"And the gate at the capital?"

I didn't answer that. She noticed me not answering it. She filed it, the way her kind files everything.

At the far end, exactly as advertised, the third plank lied about being solid—it dipped a half-inch under my boot with a groan like a dying animal, and I flinched harder than the plank deserved, and Rosalind saw me flinch, and said nothing, and I loved her a little for saying nothing.

The sergeant at the barricade was everything Voss was not: sixty if a day, red-nosed, bored down to the marrow, warming both hands at a brazier that smoked more than it warmed. He glanced at Rosalind's credentials for perhaps a second and a half.

"Sanitary lot," he grunted. "Been through Tuesday already."

"Different pair," Rosalind said. "Same war."

That earned a laugh like gravel poured slowly. He waved us past the barricade, past the grain wagon now trundling onto the bridge behind us, past the two guards at the far piling who looked up with the universal indifference of soldiers watching anything that wasn't food or leave.

And then—because the world is built of small cruelties arranged with comic precision—the sergeant cleared his throat.

"Just the one formality," he said. "Manifest. Anything you're carrying needs declaring. Regulations since the Halven business." He slid a grimy ledger across the barrel-head toward us. His ledger. A real one, kept by a real clerk of the army, listing real cargo, and waiting for my hand to write upon it.

Rosalind's eyes touched mine for a quarter of a heartbeat. *Don't hesitate.*

"Bandages, dressings, boiled linens, tinctures," I recited, taking the pencil. My hand was steady. I made it steady the way you make a plank hold—by refusing, moment to moment, to acknowledge what stood on it. Against my ribs, separated by canvas and oilcloth and six inches of air, the other ledger pressed warm as a living thing. Two books, ten inches apart, one asked for and one forbidden, and the entire difference between hanging and passage reduced to a sergeant too cold to care. "*Dressing Returns, March,*" I wrote in the manifest line, and signed it *T. Corben*, and gave the dead corporal's name to the army one more time.

The sergeant squinted at the entry, grunted, and closed his book.

Behind us, faintly, I heard Lieutenant Voss's voice at the near checkpoint, pitched to carry: "Ashe, R. Corben, T. Commission grade two." Repeating us to himself, or to a runner, or to the clean white drawer where he kept us. Sealing us into memory the way amber seals a fly.

We walked. The road bent away from the river through bare birch scrub, and for a quarter mile neither of us spoke, and the only sounds were our boots and the wagon fading behind and my heart insisting on its own account of events.

Then Rosalind laughed.

It startled me badly—I actually stopped walking. It wasn't a big laugh. It was small and sharp and slightly unhinged, the laugh of a woman who had just passed a loaded musket through a nursery and knew it.

"Inches," she said. "Inches, Mr. Vane. He recited the seal flaw. The flaw. Who notices a flaw in a signature's descender?"

"A man who was built wrong," I said. "Or built too right. They exist. I've met three. They always end up either running archives or running executions, and the difference is only a matter of which war finds them first."

She sobered at that. We walked on. The birches thinned, the road straightened, and ahead the country opened into long gray fields stitched with retreating columns' leavings—broken cart wheels, a shoe, a hymnal gone to pulp in a ditch.

"There's a copy of the manifest in his book now," she said at last. "T. Corben. Dressing Returns. If anyone pulls that record—"

"No one pulls records in a retreat. Retreats burn their records. That's half of why I do what I do." I adjusted the satchel strap on my shoulder, and felt the ledger shift beneath the wrappings, and thought of the stone in my chest that was still the last page, still unsaid.

But she caught the motion. Of course she caught the motion. She was a woman who counted steps.

"That book weighs more every day," she observed quietly. "Not the paper. You."

I looked at her. Wind, birches, the long road east toward a tribunal that might or might not convene, and this woman beside me who had traded her brother's ghost for a stranger's cause on the strength of one lamplit bargain.

"When we're clear of the next checkpoint," I said, "I'll tell you what's written on the last page. All of it. I swear it."

Rosalind Ashe studied me the way Voss had studied our credentials—looking, I suspected, for the flaw in the descender.

"You'd better," she said. "Because I counted the pages once, while you slept. And Jonah—" it was the first time she had used my name aloud, and hearing it in her voice did something to my balance that no bridge had managed, "—the last page isn't blank. Someone's already begun writing on it."

Ahead, where the road dropped toward the marsh country, a rider in colonel's colors was coming up fast, trailing dust, waving us down.
