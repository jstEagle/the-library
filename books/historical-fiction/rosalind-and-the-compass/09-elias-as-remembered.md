## 9. Elias, As Remembered

The barn stood a half-mile off the Marrow Ford road, sunk in a fold of ground where the land forgot it had once been farmland. Its roof sagged like a tired back. Whoever had kept the hay last had done so before the war came north; now the loft held only dust, mouse skeletons, and the sweet rot of bales gone to nothing.

Jonah Vane built the fire small, as he did everything—economical, deliberate, a man who rationed even his movements. He laid three stones in a ring and fed it with broken slats from a crate stenciled with the army quartermaster's mark, and when the flame caught he crouched beside it with his hands out, warming them without looking at them, the way men do when they are thinking of something else entirely.

Rosalind sat on an overturned feed trough with her census satchel across her knees. The leather was scuffed at one corner where she'd dropped it on the bridge that morning, in the terrible moment when the young officer's eyes had moved from her face to Jonah's kit and back again like a needle finding its groove. She could still feel her own pulse in her teeth. They had passed. The ledger had passed, sewn flat against Jonah's ribs beneath his shirt, riding there like a second heartbeat. But passing through danger, she was learning, did not end the fear. It only banked it, like a coal under ash, waiting for the next wind.

She had waited all day for this. Through the jolting miles in the sanitary commission cart, through Jonah's silence and her own, through the checkpoint and the long gray afternoon beyond it—she had waited, because some things cannot be asked for on the road. Some things require darkness, and firelight, and the particular honesty that comes only when you are too tired to be anything else.

"Read it to me," she said.

Jonah didn't pretend not to understand. She watched him decide whether to feign ignorance anyway, and watched him abandon the idea. He was many things—a liar when lying served, a smuggler when smuggling served—but he did not insult her with cowardice dressed up as obtuseness.

"It's late," he said.

"I've been late for two years, Mr. Vane." She heard how her voice came out: level, clerical, the voice she used with farmers who resented being counted. "I crossed half this province filling out forms for the Bureau of Enumeration. I wrote down how many pigs a widow owns while my brother's name was being struck from the rolls of the living. I have earned the right to hear it read aloud."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a night bird made its single note and stopped.

Jonah reached into his shirt. He brought out the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and even now—even knowing what it was, having bargained for it by lamplight three nights past—she felt the sight of it move through her like cold water. It was smaller than she'd imagined. A book the size of a psalter, its cover stained dark, its spine split and mended with thread. So much weight in so little paper. She thought: *this is what my brother has been reduced to. One page. Perhaps less.*

Jonah unwrapped it slowly, and she understood that he was giving her time to change her mind, or giving himself time. His thumb moved along the page edges without opening them, the way a priest might touch a door he wasn't sure he should enter.

"You should know what you're asking for," he said. "It isn't always kind, what's written here."

"Neither is what's written in me."

He looked at her then—really looked, for the first time since the bridge—and whatever he found in her face seemed to settle something in him. He turned to the back of the ledger, where the pages were newer, the ink fresher, and began, carefully, to count backward through the entries with his fingertip. She watched his lips shape letters without sound. *Aldous. Merrick. Fenn.* And then his finger stopped.

He held the book out to her instead of reading.

"He's yours," Jonah said. "I only wrote it down. You should be the one who says him."

* * *

The page was ruled in a clerk's hand that was not quite a clerk's hand—the lines wavered slightly, as if written standing up, or kneeling, or by lamplight in a place where lamplight was stolen. She recognized the handwriting from the sample she had studied for two years without knowing what she studied: the distinctive way the crossbar of the *t* hooked leftward, like a man flinching.

She read it aloud. Her voice held, mostly.

"*Elias Ashe. Private, Third Border Regiment, Company D. Age twenty-three. Enlisted at Halven, spring muster. Wounded at the millworks engagement—fragment wound, left thigh and hip, treated at the forward station under my care. Fever broke on the fourth day. Lucid, recovering. Removed for questioning on the fifth day regarding events witnessed during the retreat from Kolvary Ridge. Not returned. Not transferred. No discharge recorded. No death recorded. Struck from company strength by order of—*"

Her voice went out like a snuffed lamp.

By order of what? By order of whom? The sentence simply ended, the ink stopping mid-line, and below it, added later in the same hooked-t hand but pressed harder, deeper, as though the pen had been gripped like a weapon, a single line:

"*I do not accept this omission. Entered against orders. Let the tribunal judge which of us lied.*"

Rosalind set the ledger down on her knees very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off.

"You wrote that," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I wrote it."

"The rest of the book—you said the names were omitted from official dispatches. I assumed clerical cowardice. Commanders trimming their casualty lists so the capital would keep sending them fresh men and favorable reports." She had assumed this because it was the assumption that let her sleep. Bureaucratic rot she could fight. She was a census taker; rot in ledgers was her native country. "But this is different. This is a man removed from a hospital bed in the middle of recovering, questioned about something he saw, and never seen again. And you—" her breath caught, steadied— "you were told to leave him blank. And you refused."

"I was told worse than to leave him blank." Jonah fed a slat to the fire. In the new light his face was all angles and shadow. "I was told there was no such patient. I had changed his dressings myself that morning. I knew the shape of the wound better than I know my own hands. And a captain with Brandt's crest on his collar informed me that Private Ashe had been reassigned, and that if I valued my posting I would adjust my records accordingly." A pause. The fire ate. "So I adjusted them. Into this book."

"Where is the forward station? Where they took him?"

"Gone. Burned in the autumn withdrawal—or burned *as* the autumn withdrawal, depending on who tells it. There were men in that ward who were well enough to walk." His jaw tightened. "There were men in that ward who were not."

Rosalind looked down at the page again. *Removed for questioning. Not returned.* Two years she had lived inside the official version: *deserted.* The word had come to her in a War Office envelope with a wax seal so casual it might have sealed an invoice. *Deserted in the vicinity of Halven. Any information regarding the whereabouts of the above to be directed to—* She had memorized the address. She had walked past that building every week for a year and hated it with the quiet, useless hatred of the powerless.

Desertion had a shape she knew how to hate: the shape of her brother choosing to leave, choosing the road over the regiment, choosing absence over family. It had humiliated her in a private way she'd never admitted to anyone—that some part of her grief was anger, and some part of the anger was shame, and the shame was the worst part because Elias had taught her to shoot and mend fences and stand up straight, and she had believed he would never run from anything.

But wounded men do not desert. Wounded men with fever barely broken, men who cannot walk unaided, do not slip away into the night past sentries. Someone carried him out. Or someone walked him out between two escorts. Or—

Or no one walked him anywhere at all, and the questioning was the last room he ever entered.

"I suspected," she said quietly. "Before tonight, I suspected. That's why I came to your hospital with my census questions. That's why I asked one too many." She almost smiled. "You saw it immediately. I could tell. You have the look of a man who knows what hunters look like, because you've spent two years being hunted by your own conscience."

"That's generous. Mostly I've spent two years being tired."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, and she let it stay sharp. "Don't make yourself small about this. I've read the whole page now, Mr. Vane. I know what refusing cost you. Every name in that book is a small treason. And mine—" she touched the entry, lightly, her fingertip resting just beside the words so as not to smudge them— "mine is the one that matters. Because I'm going to find out what happened in that questioning room. Even if the answer is the one I'm afraid of."

* * *

The fire needed feeding again. Neither of them moved to feed it.

In the silence Rosalind found herself doing what she always did when the world became too large: counting. Nine slats left in the crate. Forty-one steps to the barn door; she had counted them on the way in. Three days' travel to the capital if the roads stayed open, five if they didn't. Two checkpoints remaining. One brother, unaccounted for. She had counted her way through the funeral-that-wasn't, through the empty chair at the winter table, through the nights when she woke reaching for the sound of his whistling in the yard. Counting was how she kept the world from dissolving. Numbers held still when everything else ran.

"Elias used to say I counted because I was afraid of infinity," she said. The words surprised her by arriving. Grief does that—it opens doors you bricked over years ago. "He was the dreamer. Maps drawn in the dirt of places he'd never see. He joined up because he thought the war would be short and he'd come home with stories." She laughed, or something near it. "He wanted stories. Instead he became one. A bad one. *Deserted.* Do you know what that word does to a family in a town like Halven? People don't say anything. That's the cruelty of it. They simply stop meeting your eye, and you spend the rest of your life walking through your own village like a ghost, and your mother spends hers dying by inches in front of the window, watching the road."

"Is your mother living?"

"No." The word was flat as a stone on a grave. "She waited eighteen months and then she stopped. I don't say that to burden you. I say it because you should understand that this ledger—" she lifted it, felt its true weight at last, the weight no scale could measure— "this ledger is not paper to me. Every name in it is someone's window-watch. Someone's unanswered letter. Someone who died twice: once in the flesh, and once more when the record erased them, because the second death is the one that never ends. The first death takes the man. The second takes everyone who loved him."

Jonah was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was roughened, as if the words had to be dragged over gravel.

"That's why I started it. After the third name. Aldous—Corporal Aldous. He died of gangrene while his colonel wrote home that he'd deserted with regimental funds." The firelight showed something moving behind Jonah's eyes, old and banked and dangerous. "His mother wrote to the hospital asking where her son's effects had been sent. There were no effects. There was no son. There was nothing, officially. And I sat down that night with a blank book and I wrote his name in it because it was the only place left in the world where Corporal Aldous existed." He looked at her. "I couldn't bring the dead back. But I could refuse to let them be deleted."

"And now we carry them to the tribunal."

"Now we carry them to the tribunal." He hesitated, and she saw the hesitation cost him. "Rosalind. You should hear the rest of it, before you decide what this mission is. The page doesn't end with Elias. There are forty-one names in this book. Some of them are simple omissions—casualties trimmed for report. Some are worse. Men who died of wounds their officers' negligence caused. Men executed without trial. And three—" his voice dropped— "three are like your brother's. Removed for questioning. Not returned. Witnesses to something at Kolvary Ridge that Brandt's people did not want witnessed. If we deliver this book, we aren't correcting arithmetic. We're accusing a colonel of making men disappear, and the tribunal will either burn him or bury us."

"Then it's settled," Rosalind said.

She stood, and the ledger felt different in her hands now—not a burden, not contraband, but something with a pulse. All day it had been cargo. Now it was communion. Somewhere between the bridge and this fire, between the page and her own voice reading it aloud, her mission had changed its nature entirely. This morning she had been a woman transporting evidence. Tonight she was something older than bureaucracy. She was a keeper of names. Her brother had been reduced to a page, and she would carry that page to the capital the way relics were once carried—against the body, past the guards, through fire if it came to it—and if the tribunal judged truly, then Elias would exist again in the only way the dead can: in the record, in the telling, in the mouths of strangers saying *he was here, he suffered, he counted.*

"They'll ask at the next checkpoints why a census taker and a nurse are traveling toward a war tribunal," Jonah said. "We'll need a story better than the sanitary commission."

"We'll need more than a story." She folded the oilcloth over the ledger and held it out to him, and watched him take it back against his chest, against the scar she'd glimpsed once when his shirt gapped at the collar—a man marked, like her brother, like every soul in the book. "Because tomorrow night, if the map holds true, we reach the estate of Colonel Brandt, who has offered hospitality to travelers on the capital road. And you and I, Mr. Vane, are going to dine with the man who erased my brother."

Jonah went very still. The fire popped once in the silence.

"You knew," he said.

"I count everything," Rosalind said. "Steps. Rations. Lies. And the name at the bottom of Elias's page—the order that struck him from the rolls—it bears a signature I've seen before, on requisition documents in the Halven archive. Colonel A. Brandt. The same man whose invitation is pinned inside my coat." She met his eyes across the small fire, and felt the sacred thing inside her settle into its final shape, hard and bright as a nail. "Tomorrow we eat at his table, and we smile, and we watch him toast honest men. And the night after, God willing, we watch him fall."

Outside, the wind shifted through the bare trees, carrying from the far-off capital road the faint, regular sound of wheels—an army wagon, or a courier, or fate keeping its appointment. Jonah banked the fire with his boot heel until only coals remained, two red points in the dark like eyes deciding whether to trust her.

"One condition," he said quietly. "If it goes wrong in that house—if Brandt looks at you too long, asks one question too many—you take the ledger and you run, and you don't stop for me."

"No," said Rosalind Ashe.

And the coals burned on between them, neither yielding.
