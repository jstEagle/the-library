## 10. The Colonel's Dinner

The invitation came down like weather—inevitable and impossible to argue with.

A courier in Brandt's livery found us on the road north of Halven, two days out from the bridge checkpoint, riding a horse too fine for a war this far gone. He carried a card of heavy cream stock, edged in black, that read: *Colonel A. Brandt requests the pleasure of Nurse Vane and Companion at supper, Wrenholt Manor, Thursday evening.* The word *companion* had been added in different ink, later, by a hand that pressed too hard.

"Refusal would be noted," the courier said, not unkindly.

I looked at Rosalind. She was studying the card as though it were a page from one of her census rolls, cataloguing something I couldn't see. Her face did what it always did under pressure: nothing. But her thumb moved along the edge of the card once, twice—a counting motion. I had learned to read her counts. This one said *danger.*

"We'd be honored," she said.

We rode behind the courier through country that grew richer as it emptied. Burned farms gave way to standing ones, then to orchards heavy with fruit no one had picked because every hand that should have picked it was dead or conscripted or buried in a ledger no one kept but me. Wrenholt Manor rose out of a stand of elms in the last hour of light, all pale stone and tall windows, requisitioned as divisional headquarters three months ago and already wearing its occupation like a tailored coat.

Inside the ledger—the ledger itself, sewn now into the false bottom of Rosalind's medical satchel, riding against her hip as we walked through the doors—Colonel Aurel Brandt appeared eleven times. Not as a casualty. As an author.

*Omission ordered per Col. A.B., 14th March.* *Per A.B., roll corrected, 9 men struck.* *A.B. directs no dispatch re: Halven action, casualties absorbed into prior returns.*

I had written those entries myself, transcribing them from documents I'd been made to carry and burn. My handwriting sat beside his initials like a confession neither of us had signed.

And now the man himself stood at the head of a long table laid with real linen, raising a glass of amber wine toward us, smiling with the easy warmth of a host who has never once doubted his welcome.

"Nurse Vane," he said. "They speak well of you at Marrow Ford. Come. Sit. You look like a man who hasn't eaten a proper meal since the retreat."

He had no idea how right he was.

* * *

There were eight of us at table. Brandt; his adjutant, Captain Weiss, a young man with a scholar's stoop and a duelist's eyes; a quartermaster major who ate the way other men pray, with total devotion; two staff officers whose names slid off me; Rosalind, seated at Brandt's left with her spine straight and her hands quiet; myself at his right; and at the far end, half in shadow, a wounded orderly who served the wine because the manor's servants had fled and someone had to pour.

Private Kessler, his sleeve said. His left arm was bound in a sling, the bandage spotted brown at the elbow. He poured with his right hand only, and he poured carefully, the way men do when their whole body hurts and they've decided pain is none of anyone's business.

The soup was pheasant. The fish was trout from a stream I had crossed on foot two days earlier, wading through water the color of rusted iron. The meat came with plums. I ate everything put in front of me and hated myself a little for how good it tasted.

"You were at the river line," Brandt said to me, midway through the fish. It wasn't a question. "Third dressing station."

"I was, sir."

"Bad business." He turned his glass by its stem. "We lost more men there than the returns will ever show, if you believe the talk. Desertions, straggling, confusion. War makes poor bookkeepers of us all."

Beside me, Rosalind's spoon paused over her plate for exactly one second. One second. Then it resumed. If Brandt noticed, he gave no sign. But I felt the pause the way you feel a floorboard shift under your weight.

"The official figures seemed complete to me, Colonel," I said.

"Did they?" He smiled at me across the candlelight, and it was a genuinely warm smile, which made it worse. "That's either loyalty or naivety, Nurse Vane, and I find both are useful in a field hospital. Loyalty keeps men working. Naivety keeps them sleeping."

Laughter went around the table like a passed dish. I laughed too. I heard my own laugh come out of me, correct and timed, and thought: *this is what it costs, this is the price of the meal, and I am paying it bite by bite.*

"Miss Ashe," Brandt said, turning to Rosalind. "A census taker, my adjutant tells me. Counting heads in a province that's losing them faster than anyone can tally."

"Someone must keep the count, Colonel, or no one will know what was lost."

"And what do you do with the count? File it? Publish it?"

"Both, eventually. Records are promises, sir. Made to people who aren't here to collect."

The table went quiet—not shocked quiet, but the particular silence of officers watching a civilian say something true in a room where truth was a guest without credentials. Brandt studied her for a long moment, and I watched his eyes, and I saw there something I had seen before in other eyes, in the eyes of men who had ordered things erased: not guilt. Curiosity. The curiosity of a man wondering how much a person knows, and whether knowing is dangerous.

"A promise," he repeated pleasantly. "What an antique notion. Tell me, Miss Ashe—when your count comes up short, when the numbers don't agree, whom do you blame? The clerk, or the war?"

"Neither," Rosalind said. "I blame whoever decided the numbers should disagree."

Captain Weiss set down his fork. The quartermaster major suddenly found his wine fascinating. And Colonel Aurel Brandt laughed—a full, delighted, carrying laugh—and raised his glass.

"Well said! Well said indeed. Gentlemen, mark this woman. She'd have made a fine auditor and a terrifying wife." He lifted the glass higher. "To honest men. May we be counted among them, whatever the ledgers say."

We drank. Every one of us drank. I felt the wine go down like a coal, and beneath the table, my knee pressed against the satchel strap at Rosalind's hip, and I thought: *eleven entries. Eleven times your name is written in ink you are toasting tonight. If I opened this bag and turned these pages, you would drink to yourself.*

That was the thing about dramatic irony—I knew it even then, sitting in it, drowning slowly in it—that the audience at that table included only me. Everyone else was performing a play whose script they hadn't read.

* * *

After the meal, the officers withdrew to smoke on the terrace, and Brandt detained me with a hand on my sleeve.

"A moment, Nurse. Walk with me. The house smells of too many men."

We walked the gallery off the dining room, past portraits of some family that had owned Wrenholt for four centuries and now, presumably, counted themselves among the disappeared. Candles burned in sconces. Our shadows stretched ahead of us like men walking to execution.

"Marrow Ford," Brandt said. "The bridge officer—young Ferenc—he wrote to me about you. Says you crossed with a sanitary commission, papers immaculate. Says the woman with you had census credentials stamped in the capital, first impression, no hesitation."

"She's thorough," I said.

"He says she counted the garrison while she waited. Horses, sentries, boats at mooring. He found it charming." Brandt stopped before a portrait of a man in a powdered wig and turned to face me. In the candlelight his face was open, friendly, and absolutely unreadable, the way a closed ledger is unreadable. "I find it interesting."

"War teaches people to count," I said. "It's cheaper than teaching them to fight."

"It does." He resumed walking. "You know, Vane, I've been in this profession thirty years. Do you know what I've learned about records?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Records are weapons that fire backward. Every list is a future tribunal waiting to convene. That's why a good commander curates his paper the way a surgeon curates his instruments. Nothing extra. Nothing that doesn't serve."

"And the men who don't fit the record, sir?"

"The men who don't fit the record—" he considered it, honestly considering, as though I'd asked him about cavalry tactics, "—are usually the reason the record needs curating." He clapped me on the shoulder. His hand was heavy and dry and warm. "But you're a nurse. You mend. You don't count. That's why I trust you at my table. Good night, Nurse Vane. Miss Ashe sleeps in the blue room; you'll take the green. We ride at seven—I'll have my wagons take you as far as the crossroads. Consider it thanks for the service you've rendered."

"What service, Colonel?"

He smiled back over his shoulder, already receding into the candlelight.

"Why, keeping the books, of course."

* * *

I did not sleep. I lay on the green room's narrow bed in my clothes with my boots beside the door, listening to the house settle, and at some point after midnight there came the softest knock—two knuckles, twice—and I opened the door on Rosalind holding a candle and wearing the expression she wore when a number refused to add up.

"He searched our rooms," she whispered.

"I know. My kit's been repacked. Everything's back where it was, but the fold of the spare shirt is wrong. He folds like a soldier. I don't anymore."

"They took impressions of my credentials. Wax and paper—there's dust on the window sill of the blue room, a seal matrix's worth." She stepped inside and shut the door. "He knows who we claim to be. Now he wants to know what we actually are."

"Then why are we still alive?"

"Because suspicion isn't proof, and proof isn't leverage, and Brandt deals in leverage." She set the candle on the washstand. Its light climbed the wall. "Jonah. At dinner. When he toasted honest men—he was watching us when he said it. Both of us. He was measuring."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I spent the entire meal doing arithmetic, and the sum is bad. He mentioned the river line unprompted. He mentioned missing men unprompted. He quoted the rumors of omission to the very two people most likely to care. That's not conversation. That's bait on three hooks, and we swallowed all of them politely."

Downstairs, faintly, a door opened and closed. Boots in the hall, unhurried, passing. We stood silent until they faded.

"There's another thing," Rosalind said quietly. "The orderly. The one who poured."

"Kessler."

"You caught his name too." She almost smiled. "His brother was in the Halven action. He told me while the others were on the terrace. His brother's company was struck from the rolls—every man of them—and the family received nothing. No pension letter. No death notice. Just silence." Her voice tightened around the last word, the way rope tightens. "He wanted to know if I could find out why. He thought, because I'm a census taker, I might have access to—"

"To records," I finished.

"To records." She looked at me in the candlelight, and I understood that we were both thinking of the same object sewn into the same satchel, ten feet away, weighing perhaps a pound and a half and more than either of us could lift. "Jonah, his brother might be in there."

"I know," I said again, and heard how hollow the words had become, said so often in rooms where they couldn't mean anything.

She picked up her candle and moved to the door, then paused with her hand on the latch.

"One more piece of arithmetic," she said. "Brandt's wagons leave at seven. They'll take us to the crossroads—which is precisely where the road forks toward the capital, and precisely where a colonel who suspects his guests would send riders to watch them part ways. Or—" her thumb moved along the doorframe, once, twice, "—it's generosity. From a man who trusts nurses who don't count."

"Which is it?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She opened the door a crack, and the corridor beyond was dark and long. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we find out whether we're guests or evidence."

She slipped out. The candlelight went with her. I lay back down in my clothes and stared at the ceiling, where the plaster held a hairline crack running corner to corner like a fault line drawn on a map of somewhere about to break.

Somewhere below us, in a locked drawer in a study that smelled of brandy and pipe tobacco, Colonel Aurel Brandt kept his own ledger. I was certain of it now. Men like him always kept accounts—it was the rest of us they deleted from them.

I slept maybe an hour, and dreamed of the bridge at Marrow Ford, and woke before dawn to the sound of Private Kessler in the corridor outside my door, moving slowly on his bad arm, stopping just long enough—just long enough—to whisper through the wood:

"Nurse Vane. I saw your writing. On the dressings, at the second station, in autumn. I'd know it anywhere. Please. Before the wagons come. There's a name I need written down."
