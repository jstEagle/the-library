## 11. What Loyalty Costs

The Brandt house had been a merchant's pride once, before the war took the trade routes and left only the house. Its long dining table still gleamed under candlelight, polished by servants who had not yet fled, and around it sat officers whose boots had crossed rivers Jonah had helped them bleed into.

He sat at the far end, below the salt, in his borrowed dress uniform with its borrowed buttons. A nurse's place at a colonel's table was an honor, Colonel Brandt had said, clapping him on the shoulder with a hand that smelled of bay rum and horses. An honor. Jonah had smiled and said thank you, sir, and spent the whole exchange aware of the ledger lying beneath the floorboard of the room he'd been given upstairs, twenty feet above their heads, thinner than a hymn book and heavier than all of them.

Brandt held court from the head of the table. He was younger than Jonah had imagined—forty perhaps, with a boxer's broken knuckle on his right hand and a voice pitched for parade grounds even when the room asked for conversation.

"—and so I told the quartermaster, if you cannot feed my wounded, then you are feeding the enemy's morale instead, and I will have you shot for treason against my kitchen." Laughter moved down the table like wind through wheat. "To honest men," Brandt raised his glass, "who do what must be done."

Glasses rose. Jonah lifted his own and let the wine touch his lips without passing them. *Honest men.* The toast went down the table and came back again, and each time it passed over Jonah he felt it land somewhere between his shoulder blades, like a hand checking for a weapon.

Rosalind was not at dinner. She had presented herself as his clerical assistant—a census woman traveling under sanitary commission papers—and Brandt's adjutant had installed her in the counting room with the household ledgers, which was either carelessness or a test, and Jonah had not yet decided which frightened him more. So he ate alone among enemies, and watched, and memorized.

He memorized the major on Brandt's right who laughed a half-second late. The captain across from him who drank nothing at all and watched everyone else drink. The lieutenant, barely nineteen, whose hands shook whenever the artillery sounded in the distance—which it did now, faintly, a sound like a door closing in another part of a very large house.

And he memorized Private Kessler.

Kessler stood along the wall with the other orderlies, in a clean jacket two sizes too big, serving wine with his left hand because the right one was bound and splinted and strapped across his chest. Jonah had treated him three days ago at Marrow Ford—a shattered forearm, bone gone to gravel, the kind of wound that meant no more rifle work and probably no more anything. Kessler had been brave about it in the way young men are brave, joking through the splinting, asking twice whether he could still hold a spoon. He had a brother, he'd said, while Jonah worked. A twin. Also named to serve, posted to the 4th Column, last anyone had heard.

Jonah had forgotten him within the hour. There were always more wounds. That was the arithmetic of his life: names went in, names came out, and the ledger kept what the war discarded.

Now Kessler was moving down the table with the wine bottle, and he reached Jonah, and he poured, and then he stopped. His eyes had dropped to Jonah's hands—to the right one, where Jonah had turned his fork while cutting meat, and where the old splinter scar crossed the thumb joint in its familiar pale seam.

Kessler stared at the scar. Then at Jonah's face. Then back at the scar.

"You wrote the discharge card," he said quietly. Not a question. "At Marrow Ford. For the man in the next cot. The handwriting—" He stopped himself, glanced up the table. Brandt was deep in some story about a horse. "You write your sevens with the cross-stroke going backward. Nobody does that."

Jonah set down his fork. "You have a good memory for handwriting, Private."

"My mother taught penmanship." Kessler straightened the bottle in his grip as though it might steady something else. "Sir—I mean, Sister Vane, isn't that what they call you? The Englishman who nurses."

"They call me worse."

"I need to ask you something." The boy's voice had dropped to almost nothing, and there it was—the thing Jonah had been waiting three days for someone to say and dreading every hour of it. "Not here."

"There is nowhere else tonight."

"The water closet corridor. After the sweet. They'll smoke on the terrace. Ten minutes, no more." Kessler's splinted arm trembled against his chest, and his face had gone the color of tallow. "Please, sir. It's about my brother."

* * *

The corridor behind the water closet was stone-floored and cold and smelled of lamp oil and lye. Through the shuttered windows Jonah could see the officers on the terrace, small orange points of their cigars moving like slow fireflies, Brandt's laugh rolling out over the garden. Somewhere upstairs Rosalind was alone with the household accounts, and Jonah prayed she was reading them and not thinking about him, and knew she was doing both.

Kessler came to him at eleven minutes past, moving fast for a man with one working arm. He looked both ways down the corridor, which was foolish, since Jonah had already done it twice, and then he said:

"Matthias. Matthias Kessler, 4th Column, second company. He's not on any roll. Not dead, not discharged, not transferred—he's just *gone*, the way things go when nobody writes them down." The boy's breath was coming short. "I've written four letters to the muster office. Four. They sent back two saying no such soldier exists in the second company, and two saying they'd look into it, and then nothing. My mother is in Halven. She thinks he's alive because there's no death notice. She'd rather have a death notice, sir. Do you understand? She'd rather bury him than stand at the gate every morning wondering."

Jonah said nothing. In the silence, Kessler stepped closer, close enough that Jonah could smell the carbolic still on him from the ward, and his voice broke open.

"But you know. Don't you. You're the one they talk about—the sister-nurse who keeps his own book, who writes down the ones the dispatches lose. Orderlies talk, sir, we talk in the dark when the morphine's going round, and three different men from three different hospitals said the same thing: there's a nurse called Vane, and what Vane writes stays written." His good hand seized Jonah's sleeve, gripped the wool hard enough to shake. "Write him down. Please. Write Matthias down in your book so that somewhere, somewhere, he exists. Even if it's only ink. Even if it's only you."

"Private—"

"He was *real*." The word cracked in half. Kessler's face did something terrible then, all the discipline of the uniform failing at once, and he pressed his splinted arm against himself like a man holding a wound closed. "He was real and he was funny and he could whistle through his teeth, and if the army says he never was, then my mother will die standing at that gate, sir. She will literally die there. And I can't—" He steadied. Swallowed it. Became a soldier again, which was worse to watch. "I'm asking as one keeper of records to another. Add him."

The corridor was very quiet. On the terrace, Brandt laughed again, and the sound came through the shutters like weather.

"You don't understand what you're asking," Jonah said at last. "The book isn't here. And if it were—if I opened it in this house, at this table's reach, with that man upstairs from us drinking to honest men—"

"Then don't open it here."

"And where would you suggest?"

"Anywhere." Kessler's eyes were wet and furious. "Anywhere but nowhere. Sir, I watched you splint me. Your hands didn't shake. Everyone shakes, the surgeons told me, everyone's hands shake, but yours didn't. So yours are the hands. Yours are the hands it should be in." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a folded paper, thrust it against Jonah's chest. "His particulars. Company, birth date, our mother's name, the parish register number. Everything a proper record wants. I copied it fair. My own hand—you can check it against my letters if you doubt me."

Jonah took the paper. It was neat, schoolroom copperplate, the kind of writing that gets praised in childhood and breaks hearts later. Matthias Kessler, born the ninth of March, twin to Anton Private Anton Kessler, 2nd Provisional. Mother: Marta Kessler, née Vogel, of Halven.

Halven. The river town. Rosalind's town. The thread pulled through everything, the way threads do.

"I can't promise you the book survives the week," Jonah said. "I can't promise it survives the night. If it's found, it's found with everything in it, and everything in it becomes evidence, and evidence gets people hanged—not the men who gave the orders, Private, the men who *kept* the pages. Me. Whoever helps me. Maybe you, just for asking."

"Then hang me with my brother's name in the world instead of out of it." Kessler said it flatly, without drama, the way men state facts about weather. "That's the trade. I've already made it. I made it when I walked into this corridor."

Footsteps in the main hall—someone coming, laughing voices. Kessler stepped back, became furniture, became an orderly holding an empty bottle. Jonah slid the paper inside his jacket, against his ribs, where the ledger usually lived in his mind.

"One condition," Jonah said quickly, low. "If I add him, I add him properly. Date, circumstance, whatever I can find or reconstruct. No comfort lies. If your brother was killed in the retreat at Sorrel Field, that's what the page will say."

Kessler nodded. Then, as the footsteps neared: "Thank you, sir. Whatever happens. Thank you."

He turned and was walking away, shoulders square under the too-big jacket, when the doors to the hall opened and the adjutant came through with a lantern and two military policemen behind him.

* * *

They were looking for someone, that much was immediate—the MPs' eyes went from face to face down the corridor with the flat professional hunger of men hunting. The adjutant's lantern swung up and caught Kessler mid-stride.

"You. Orderly. Name?"

"Kessler, sir. Second Provisional, attached medical."

"Anton or the other one?" One of the MPs smirked. Twins; the army loved the confusion, used it like a toy.

"Anton, sir."

"Where were you at half past nine?"

"Serving at table, sir. Then fetching wine from the cellar."

The adjutant studied him. The lantern light made Kessler's face a mask of yellow wax, and Jonah, standing by the window with his hands folded, felt his own pulse tick in his throat like a clock in an empty room. *Walk away,* he willed the boy. *Look bored. Look stupid. Stupidity is armor.*

But Kessler was not stupid, and he was not bored, and he was terrified, and terrified men overcorrect. He answered one question too many, offered one detail too many, and Jonah watched the adjutant's attention snag on it—the cellar route, the timing, the ten missing minutes—and file it away somewhere behind his eyes.

"That will be all," the adjutant said finally.

Kessler saluted, turned, walked toward the service stair. At the corner he paused—half a heartbeat, no more—and looked back at Jonah. It was nothing. A glance. A servant checking whether the guest needed anything.

It was everything. It was a signature across the space between them, legible to anyone trained to read, and the adjutant was trained.

The MPs moved off down the corridor. The adjutant lingered, lifting the lantern as if to check the window shutters, and his gaze crossed Jonah's for one civil, empty second.

"Sister Vane. Enjoying the colonel's hospitality?"

"Deeply, Captain."

"We're fortunate in our guests. And in our staff." He smiled. "Everyone in this house is exactly where they should be. Isn't that so?"

"Every soul," Jonah said.

The lantern bobbed away. Jonah stood alone in the cold corridor with Matthias Kessler's particulars against his ribs and understood, with the clarity that comes only after the trap has finished closing, that he had just spent ten minutes buying a dead boy a name with a living boy's safety as the coin.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Once. Twice. Then silence—the particular silence of a door being opened carefully, and closed the same way.

Rosalind's room was at the end of the hall. Jonah took the stairs two at a time.
