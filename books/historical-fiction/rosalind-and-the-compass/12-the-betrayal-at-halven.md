## 12. The Betrayal at Halven

The news came to Rosalind the way bad news always did in wartime: sideways, wrapped in something ordinary.

She was in the market square of Halven, buying eggs she did not want, because a woman who buys eggs is a woman going home, and a woman going home is no one worth remembering. The egg-seller—a broad-faced woman with flour on her knuckles—leaned across her basket and dropped her voice beneath the noise of the square.

"You're the one from the sanitary commission. The one lodging above the chandler's."

"I'm a census taker," Rosalind said. It had become her truest lie.

"The soldier they took this morning—the young one with the bandaged arm—he was asking after you. Before they marched him off to the provost's tent." The woman glanced over her shoulder. "He gave them your face, miss. Your face and the nurse's. They're drawing it up now."

Rosalind counted—she always counted—to steady herself. Six steps to the corner. Three soldiers by the well, none looking at her. One bell tolling the half hour. She paid for the eggs with hands that did not shake, which surprised her, and walked out of the square at the pace of a woman with nothing in the world to fear but spoiled butter.

Behind her eyes, Private Kessler's face assembled itself: the fever-bright boy at Brandt's dinner table, gripping Jonah's sleeve, begging for his brother's name to be written down. *Write him where they'll find him. That's all. Write him where he counts.*

And now Kessler was in the provost's tent, and the counting had begun without her.

* * *

Jonah was packing when she reached the chandler's house—packing badly, the way men pack when their hands know what their minds refuse to admit. His medical kit lay open on the cot, and beside it the ledger, already wrapped in oilcloth, and beside *that*, laid out with terrible deliberateness, a scalpel, a bone saw, and the small folding knife he carried for cutting bandages.

He looked up when she came through the door, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop mid-motion, a roll of linen suspended in the air.

"They have Kessler," she said.

The linen fell. "When?"

"This morning. He asked after us before they took him. He gave them our faces." She set the eggs on the table, absurdly, an offering to no god she believed in. "They're drawing us now, Jonah. By nightfall there will be sketches at every checkpoint between here and the capital."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, quietly, in the voice he used for the dying: "Did he give them anything else?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters." He sat down heavily on the edge of the cot, as though the question weighed more than his body could bear. "If he gave them only faces, we can change faces. If he gave them the ledger—if he told them about the ledger—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I should never have opened it at that table. I knew better. A ledger lives or dies by its concealment, and I opened it like a man showing off a love letter."

"He begged you," Rosalind said. "You couldn't have refused him without becoming Brandt."

"Perhaps I'd rather be Brandt than be caught." It was a coward's sentence and he knew it; she watched him flinch from his own words as if they were splinters he'd coughed up. "No. No, I wouldn't. But God help me, Rosalind, there are four hundred names in that book, and every one of them dies a second death if it's seized here—filed away, stamped, buried in some quartermaster's strongbox until the war ends and everyone who could have been ashamed of it is comfortably dead."

"Then we run tonight."

"Yes."

"*I* run tonight." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice of the woman who had counted every ration and every mile since Marrow Ford. "One person moves faster than two. One person draws less notice. You're known at every hospital along this road, Jonah—you're *wanted* at them—and I am a woman with a forged pass and a talent for arithmetic. Give me the ledger. I'll take it to the tribunal myself."

"No."

The word landed between them like a dropped instrument. He didn't raise his voice. That was the worst of it—that he said it the way he said *this will hurt* before setting a bone.

"Give me the book," she said.

"No."

"You don't trust me with it."

"I trust you with it more than anyone alive," he said, and rose, and stood close enough that she could see the exhaustion ground into the creases around his mouth. "That isn't why. If you carry it alone and you're taken, you hang alone. If I carry it alone and I'm taken, I hang alone. But if we're taken together, they'll assume conspiracy, and conspiracy means interrogation, and interrogation means—" He stopped.

"Elias," she said. "You mean Elias."

"I mean that everything we've done, everything we've risked, unravels the moment either of us gives the other's name under pressure. Together we are one secret. Apart we are two." He turned away, began wrapping the scalpel with unnecessary care. "Besides. You've never crossed the marsh road alone. You'd be dead in the reeds by Thursday, and the ledger with you."

"And you'd be dead in a cell by Friday, and the ledger with you. At least my death would be *efficient*."

Something almost like a smile touched his mouth and died there. "You count even your own corpse's usefulness."

"Somebody has to. You spend yours like a drunkard spends wages."

The argument went on as arguments do between people who have shared too much danger to lie gracefully: circling, repeating, wearing grooves. She said *faster alone*. He said *safer together*. She said the tribunal might postpone again, that every day was a day Brandt spent tightening the noose around the evidence. He said a lone woman with a satchel full of treason was a lantern in a dark field. Neither of them yielded, because neither of them was wrong.

It ended—not resolved, ended—when she picked up the ledger and simply held it against her chest and said, "Then tell me how you'll stop me."

He looked at her for a long time. And she understood, watching his face, that he *could* stop her—that he was stronger, and that the door was behind her, and that some part of him was calculating exactly those distances and despising himself for calculating them.

"I won't stop you," he said finally. "I'll ask you. Stay. One more night. Kessler held out for six hours at least—they arrested him at dawn and it's not yet noon, and a boy with a broken arm doesn't break quickly. By morning we'll know how much he gave them. If it's only our faces, we walk out of Halven tomorrow as different people. If it's the ledger—" his jaw tightened "—then we burn the road behind us and take the marshes, both of us, and to hell with speed."

"And if it's Elias? If Kessler talked about Elias, about the page, about why I'm really here?"

Jonah was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had gone very gentle, which frightened her more than shouting would have.

"Then, Rosalind, you must decide whether your brother is served by your capture. Because they will use him. Whatever he is—wherever he is—they will hold him in front of you like a lamp, and you will walk wherever the light goes."

She had not let herself think of Elias as bait. She had kept him folded away in the safe part of her mind, the wounded brother, the name on the page, the grief that had become a mission. Now Jonah had unfolded him and held him up, and he looked back at her with the terrible plausibility of truth.

"You're cruel," she whispered.

"I'm a nurse," he said. "Cruelty is just honesty that arrived too early."

* * *

They did not sleep. They sat in the chandler's cold room with the shutters closed and the lamp turned low, and they listened to Halven.

Halven at night was a town holding its breath. Boots on cobbles came in rhythms, and they learned the rhythms—patrols passed the corner every quarter hour, two men, unhurried, bored. Bored patrols were good patrols. Somewhere toward the river a dog barked itself hoarse and was silenced. Once, much later, a wagon rolled through, heavy-wheeled, slow, and they both froze until it passed, because wagons at that hour carried either ammunition or prisoners, and neither bore thinking about.

Near midnight Rosalind found herself doing what she always did under strain: counting. Steps of the patrol. Seconds between the church bells. The names in the ledger—four hundred and eleven now, if Kessler's brother had been written in at Brandt's table, and he had, she'd seen Jonah do it, seen the pen hesitate and then commit.

Four hundred and eleven names. One of them her brother's. And possibly—one she had not yet dared look for—one that belonged to the man sitting three feet away, guarding the book as though it were his own pulse.

"Why did you start it?" she asked suddenly, into the silence. "The ledger. Before me, before any of it. What made a battlefield nurse begin writing down the men the army wanted forgotten?"

Jonah was quiet so long she thought he would not answer.

"The first one was a boy called Anselm," he said at last. "First winter of the war. He died of gangrene in my ward with a lieutenant's boot print on his chest—the officer had kicked him for crying during rounds. The dispatch listed Anselm as *died of natural causes, unnamed, effects unclaimed*. Unnamed. As if he'd never had a mother who named him." He turned the oilcloth bundle over in his hands, not looking at it. "I wrote his name down because the dispatch lied, and because I thought—I was young enough to think—that a lie corrected is a debt repaid. Then there was a second name, and a third. You know how debts go. They multiply."

"And now the debt has your name on it too."

His head came up sharply. In the lamplight his face was unreadable, and then readable, and then carefully blank again, and Rosalind felt the strange vertigo of having said something true that she did not fully understand herself.

"What makes you say that?" he asked.

"The way you guard it. Not like a weapon—like a confession." She leaned forward. "There's something in that book about you, Jonah Vane. I've watched you with it for two hundred miles. You touch certain pages the way a man touches an old scar."

For once, the liar who had lied badly to her over tea had nothing ready. He looked at the ledger, and then at the window, and then at her, and what he said was: "If we live to reach the tribunal, read it. All of it. Last page first, if you like. But not tonight. Tonight I need you to trust that whatever is written in it, my purpose hasn't changed since the first name."

"That's not trust," she said. "That's credit. And I'm running out."

Outside, boots on cobbles. Closer than the quarter-hour rhythm allowed. Two sets—or three. They stopped below the shuttered window.

A voice, low and official: "This one. Chandler's house. She lodges above it."

Rosalind's blood went to ice water. Jonah moved without seeming to move—lamp pinched out, ledger into its hiding place beneath his coat, her hand seized and drawn down behind the cot into the black corner where the floor met the wall. Through the gap beneath the shutter came a blade of lantern light, sweeping, pausing.

Knuckles on the door. "Open in the name of the Provost Marshal!"

They did not open it. There was no reason to open it, and every reason, and the contradiction lasted four heartbeats—then the voice outside spoke again, and the words rearranged Rosalind's whole night:

"Not this house. The other chandler. *Kessler's sister* lodges there—move on."

Boots receding. The lantern light slid off the shutter and away.

In the darkness, Rosalind realized she had been holding Jonah's hand hard enough to bruise it, and that he had not let go.

"Kessler has a sister," she whispered.

"Everyone has someone," Jonah breathed back. "That's the whole trouble with names."

But she was already past him, working the shutter open a finger's width, watching the lanterns converge on a house two streets over—watching a girl of perhaps seventeen, barefoot in the doorway, being questioned by men with paper. Watching the girl point, twice, once toward the market square and once toward the river road.

Toward them.

"She's giving them the route," Rosalind said. "By morning they'll have the marsh road watched, the ford, the weir—all of it. Jonah—" she turned, and the words came out stripped of argument, of leverage, of everything except the plain arithmetic of survival. "We leave *now*. Tonight. Both of us or neither, I don't care anymore which way you win the debate—we cannot be here when the sun comes up."

Jonah was already strapping the kit closed, already moving, and when he handed her the ledger to carry while he buckled the straps, their fingers touched on the oilcloth, and neither of them pulled away.

"Marsh country, then," he said quietly. "Both of us."

"There's a smuggler works the flooded ground past the weir," she said, dragging the map of Halven's underworld up from memory—the census office kept files on everyone. "Name of Corvus. He owes me nothing, but he hates the army, and hatred is a currency."

"Hatred and four hundred and eleven names." Jonah blew out the last ember of the lamp and opened the door onto the freezing dark. "Let's hope it's enough."
