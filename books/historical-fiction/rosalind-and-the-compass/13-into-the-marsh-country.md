## 13. Into the Marsh Country

The road ended where the drowned trees began.

Jonah stood at the edge of it and looked out at a country that did not seem to have decided whether it was land or water. Black trunks rose from grey shallows in ranks, their bark peeling like old paper, and between them the water lay flat as poured tin. There was no wind. There were no birds. Somewhere far off, a frog made one sound and stopped, as if it had thought better of it.

"That," said the man called Ferenc, "is your road now."

He was a small, wiry creature with a fisherman's shoulders and a face like a knot of driftwood, and he had appeared at dusk outside a ruined mill with the unbothered air of a man who had been expected for years. Rosalind had found him—or he had found her; with Ferenc it was never clear which way these things ran—and had paid him something out of the purse she kept sewn inside her coat, and now here they stood at the lip of the marsh country while the last light drained out of the sky behind them.

"How deep?" Jonah asked.

"Knee. Sometimes chest. Sometimes to here—" Ferenc touched his chin— "if you step where I don't tell you to step. The water is cold enough to stop your heart if you stay in past an hour. There are holes that go down forever and things living in them that eat whatever falls. And there are patrols on the causeway roads, which is why you will not be taking any causeway roads." He smiled without warmth. "You wanted off the roads."

"We wanted *past* the checkpoints," Rosalind said.

"Same thing, out here."

She looked tired. That was what struck Jonah, watching her in the failing light—not afraid, not desperate, though she had earned both, but simply worn down to a finer grain than she had been two weeks ago. The woman who had walked into his hospital with a census book and a hunter's eyes had been polished all over. This woman had marsh mud to mid-calf already, from the walk down from the mill, and her hair had come loose from its pins, and she was counting again—he could see it, the small movement of her lips—as though numbers were the last thing in the world that still behaved.

"How long to cross?" Jonah asked.

"Two days, if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't." Ferenc shouldered a coil of rope and a lantern he had not yet lit. "And payment first. Half now, half at the far shore. You gave me half."

Rosalind glanced at Jonah. It was a small thing, that glance, and quick—but he caught what lived underneath it. She had given Ferenc nearly everything she carried. What remained would not cover the second half, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so in front of him.

"You'll have the rest at the far shore," she said evenly.

"I'll have the rest or I'll leave you standing in the middle of nowhere, which is worse than the middle of somewhere, because at least the middle of somewhere has people who might help you." Ferenc spat neatly into the water. "Sleep tonight. We go at first light. The marsh is kinder in daylight, and so am I."

* * *

They slept in the mill, on a floor of rotted boards, beneath a roof that let the stars through in places. Jonah could not sleep. He sat with his back against the wall where the plaster had fallen away and turned the ledger over in his hands—not opening it, just holding it, feeling its weight change as the pages swelled slightly with the damp.

It was heavier than paper ought to be. He had thought that once before, in the hospital, writing by candlelight: that names must weigh something, because this book weighed more every week. Forty-one names now. Forty-two, if Kessler's brother had died in the night, which the word passed along the prisoner line suggested he had. He had not yet written it. His pen had hovered over the page three times and three times he had put the book away, because to write it meant deciding something about himself that he was not ready to decide—that he was no longer recording omissions but making entries, that he had become the kind of man who kept accounts against the army that fed him.

Across the dark room, Rosalind spoke.

"You're holding it like a child holds a doll."

"I'm holding it like a man holds a thing that can hang him."

"That too." A pause. In the starlight through the broken roof he could see her sitting up, knees drawn in, arms wrapped around them—the posture of a much younger person, someone's sister rather than someone's spy. "I used to hold Elias's letters that way. After they stopped coming. I'd read the old ones until the folds wore through, and then I'd just sit holding them, because paper remembers hands even when ink forgets words."

"Elias wrote you often?"

"Every week. Terrible spelling. He'd describe the food, mostly, and complain about the sergeant, and ask after our mother's garden as if the war were a holiday he'd taken up out of boredom." Her voice did something complicated on the word *mother*. "The last letter came eight days before the date on his page. Your page." She corrected herself softly. "The page you keep."

"The page the ledger keeps," Jonah said. "I only write it down."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "That's the lie we've agreed to tell ourselves, isn't it? You write nothing down. You just happen to be holding the pen."

He almost laughed. It startled him—that anything in him could still laugh—and what came out instead was a rough exhale, halfway between a cough and surrender. "You're sharper than you look, census taker."

"I look exactly as sharp as I am. That's my whole method. Nobody suspects a woman who looks like arithmetic." She unfolded herself and came across the room and sat beside him against the wall, close enough that he could feel the thin warmth of her through the cold. She did not ask to see the ledger. She never asked anymore; that bargain had been struck weeks ago, and its terms held. But she laid her hand flat on the cover, over his fingers, and left it there.

"Forty-one names," she said quietly.

"Forty-two soon. Perhaps tonight."

"Then forty-two men will have existed. Whatever else happens. Whoever burns whatever rolls." Her hand pressed down, briefly. "That's yours, Jonah. No tribunal can take it back once it's written. That's the whole terrible power of ink."

Outside, somewhere in the black water, something large moved without hurry, displacing a soft weight of sound. Neither of them mentioned it. Her hand stayed where it was until she slept, sitting up, her head tipping gradually sideways until it found his shoulder, and Jonah Vane, who had spent three years learning how to be invisible, sat perfectly still for four hours so as not to disturb the one person in the province who had counted him.

* * *

First light came up grey-green through fog, and the marsh took them.

Ferenc went ahead with his lantern unlit, reading the water the way Rosalind read ledgers—by marks invisible to anyone else. He carried a long pole and probed each step before committing his weight, and behind him came Rosalind, and behind her Jonah with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth inside his shirt, against his skin, where its dampness had begun to feel less like wetness and more like breath.

The first hour was knee-deep and tolerable. The second hour was chest-deep and not. They waded a channel where the bottom dropped away and the cold closed around Jonah's ribs like a fist, and Rosalind made a sound—a single bitten-off syllable—and then locked it away and kept moving, chin high, lips blue, hands above the waterline gripping the pack. When they reached the shallow shelf beyond, she stood shaking so hard her teeth chattered, and Jonah put his arm across her back without thinking about it, and she leaned into him without thinking about it either, and for a full minute the two of them stood dripping in a drowned forest, holding each other upright, saying nothing, because there was nothing that needed saying that the body had not already said better.

"Move," Ferenc called back, not unkindly. "Standing still is dying slowly. Walking is dying slower. Choose."

They chose walking.

By noon the fog burned off and the sun came down hard and useless, and the marsh showed them its other face: clouds of biting insects rising from the warm shallows, mud that sucked at boots with a wet kissing sound, and the endless flat light that made distance impossible to judge. They ate hard bread and river water boiled over a smokeless fire of dry reeds. Rosalind counted the bread into thirds with her knife, and when Jonah tried to give her his third piece she cut it in half again and put half on his tin plate and said, "Don't argue with a census taker about fair division. It's insulting to us both."

In the afternoon Ferenc stopped so abruptly that Rosalind walked into his back.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"Patrol." He pointed with his chin. Far off, maybe half a mile, a line of soldiers moved along a raised track through the reeds—six men, a mounted officer, the sun glinting on a bayonet. "Scouring party. They're looking for deserters, smugglers, and lately a nurse and a lady clerk, if the notices at the tollhouse were worth the paper." He turned and grinned at them both. "So we go under."

"Under?" Rosalind said.

"There's a peat cutting. Old works, flooded. You can walk through it bent double under the bank, breathing through the reed stems, for about four hundred paces. It smells like death and tastes worse." Ferenc was already moving. "Or we can wait here for those gentlemen to finish their sweep and hope they're blind. Choose fast."

They went under.

It was the worst hour of Jonah's life, and he had buried men with his own hands in frozen ground. They crouched in a tunnel of black water beneath a shelf of peat, bent at the waist, faces inches from the surface, breathing through hollow reeds that Ferenc cut and handed back one by one. The water smelled of rot and iron. Things touched his legs—fish, roots, he told himself, roots—and once something large and unhurried slid past his thigh and he bit down on the reed so hard he nearly crushed it. Ahead of him Rosalind's hands gripped the hem of his coat, and he realized she was holding onto him the way a drowning sailor holds a spar: not for comfort, but because letting go was unthinkable. So he became the spar. He set his feet wide on the treacherous bottom and moved forward one slow step at a time, and whenever the current tugged or the footing shifted he felt her grip tighten, and answered it by going more carefully, and in this manner—her fear measured out through his coat, his steadiness measured back through her hands—they crossed four hundred paces of underworld together.

When they surfaced at last into clean air and honest daylight, Rosalind vomited river water and then laughed—a wild, gasping, undignified laugh, bent over with her hands on her knees—and Jonah found himself laughing too, helplessly, two fools cackling in a swamp while a smuggler watched them with something close to amusement.

"You're mad," she managed. "Both of us. Completely—"

"Counted," Jonah said. "We've been counted. Everything else is decoration."

She straightened, wiping her mouth, and looked at him with an expression he had not seen before—open, unarmored, gone in the next instant but unmistakable while it lasted—and he understood that some doors, once seen open, can never quite be believed shut again.

* * *

They camped that night on a hummock of high ground, a dry island no bigger than a barn floor, ringed by whispering reeds. Ferenc built a fire low and screened it with his oilcloth. The food was gone except for a heel of bread, which they split three ways without discussion, and the water was warm and tasted of peat, and Jonah had never been so exhausted in his life. Every muscle he owned had filed a formal complaint. His feet had gone past pain into a numb white silence.

Rosalind sat across the fire with her boots off, rubbing feeling back into her toes, and her hair hung loose and damp and she looked, in the firelight, like a painting of somebody's wife waiting out a siege—except that her eyes were still counting, always counting, the flames, the reeds, the bread, him.

"Ferenc," she said suddenly. "About the rest of your fee."

The smuggler looked up from mending a sandal with sinew and a bone awl. "What of it?"

"You've seen what we carry. Not the ledger itself—I mean the weight of it. Two days more, and the tollhouse notices, and patrols on the far shore. You know what you're escorting. So let's speak plainly about what happens at the far shore, when I can't pay."

Jonah's stomach dropped. He had been circling the same fear all day like a bird over a corpse, and here she was, landing on it.

Ferenc threaded his needle and did not look up. "Plainly, then. You owe me twelve crowns. You have perhaps three, counting the lady's hidden sewing money, which she thinks I don't know about." Rosalind's jaw tightened. "Three crowns buys nine crowns of goodwill. Nine crowns short. Out here, short is short."

"And what does short cost?" Jonah asked.

Ferenc set down the sandal. For the first time since the mill, he looked at them both directly, and there was nothing knotted or driftwood about his face now; it was smooth and patient and entirely awake.

"Out here," he said, "a man takes payment in whatever has value. Crowns. Credentials. Information. Or a thing so rare that even a smuggler stops and stares at it." His eyes moved, deliberate and slow, to the oilcloth bundle beneath Jonah's shirt. "A book of names, for instance. Names of dead men the army says never lived. Do you know what such a book is worth, nurse? Not in crowns—in power. To the right buyer in the capital, or the wrong one, that book is worth more than everything floating in this marsh, everything sunk in it, and the marsh besides."

"No," Jonah said.

"I haven't named a price."

"You named it just now, with your eyes."

Ferenc smiled and picked up his sandal again, and the firelight made his shadow enormous on the reeds behind him. "Then sleep on it," he said pleasantly. "We reach the far shore tomorrow evening. And tomorrow evening, nurse, one way or another—we settle accounts."

The fire popped. Out in the darkness, the marsh breathed, vast and indifferent. Rosalind's hand found Jonah's wrist beneath the blanket line and held on, and he held back, and neither of them slept.
