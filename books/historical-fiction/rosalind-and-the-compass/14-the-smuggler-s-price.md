## 14. The Smuggler's Price

The smuggler's name was Ferenc, though I doubted it was the one his mother gave him. He sat across from us in a hollowed-out boathouse on the marsh edge, a lantern turned low between us so that his face was mostly shadow and scar, and he said, "The book."

I had known it would come to this. From the moment Jonah had counted out our coins on the wet boards—eleven crowns, three of them clipped, one foreign—and from the moment Ferenc had looked at that small pile and then at the oilcloth bundle beneath Jonah's coat and smiled with all the warmth of a pike surfacing, I had known.

"Not the book," Jonah said. His voice was steady. I had learned by now that steadiness in Jonah Vane was not calm; it was effort, like a man holding a door shut against water.

Ferenc turned his lantern up a notch, unhurried, letting the light do what threats could not. It found the ledger's corner where it bulged under Jonah's coat, found my face, found the mud dried gray along my hem. "Eleven crowns buys you two hours of poling through the drowned willows," he said. "It does not buy you past the tollhouse at Vasker. The tollhouse is where they shoot first and read papers after. You want me to take you there, you pay me something worth the shooting."

"The commission pays—" I began.

"Your commission." He laughed once, a dry bark that scattered something small and feathered in the rafters. "By spring your commission will be paper for pipe-lighting, if the tribunal ever sits, which it won't, or if it sits, which means Brandt has decided to allow it, which means he has already bought it. No, census woman. Not coin. Coin is what men carry when they intend to survive. Give me something a desperate man carries instead."

Jonah's hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, toward the ledger. I saw it—the small involuntary motion of a man reaching for his own heart to offer it out. And something in me went cold and then hot, because I understood that he meant to give it. That he had been ready to give it since the coins touched the boards. Eleven crowns against four hundred names, and he was already doing the arithmetic of sacrifice, and the sum always came out the same way with him: him on the short side.

"No," I said.

Both men looked at me. Ferenc with interest; Jonah with something worse than anger—hurt, quickly buried, the look of a man who had offered his life and been told it wasn't wanted. But that was exactly the trouble, I thought. That was exactly, precisely the trouble. Everything this man owned he was prepared to spend, and he did not seem to know that some things were not his alone to spend anymore.

* * *

We had come into the marsh country three days ago, off the roads since Halven, since Kessler's arrest, since the description of us—*a tall nurse with a lame left arm, a woman with a census satchel who counts under her breath*—went into every garrison book between the river and the capital. Three days of flooded meadow and black water, of sleeping in drained peat cuttings with our boots tied around our necks so we wouldn't lose them in the mire, of Jonah carrying the ledger inside his shirt against his skin so the damp wouldn't take the ink. Three days, and I had learned things about him that no interrogation would have yielded: that he talked to wounded birds; that he could not sleep unless he knew where I was, and checked twice; that he counted too, in his way—not steps or rations but faces, going back over the dead each night like a miser over coin, making sure none had gone missing in his keeping.

And he had learned things about me. He'd learned I count under my breath, yes—that much had made the wanted notices—but he'd also learned why: my mother counting stitches as she died, telling me numbers were the only prayer God never interrupted. He'd learned I could not pass a graveyard without reading every stone. He'd learned, I think, that I was not hunting Elias only. I was hunting the habit of erasure itself, wherever it lived, whatever shape it wore.

Now the shape it wore was a smuggler in a boathouse, wanting the book.

"Then what?" Jonah asked Ferenc, but his eyes came to me, and I understood the question was really mine to answer.

I opened my satchel.

Everything I owned of value was in it: the commission papers with their wax seals, the letters of introduction signed by men whose names still meant something in the capital, the little brass stamp that certified me an agent of the Census Office of the Interior—empowered to enter any dwelling, to demand any register, to compel any answer. Papers that had gotten us past the bridge at Marrow Ford. Papers that had gotten me out of a guardroom at the tollhouse with nothing but a headache and a lie well told. My credentials were not sentiment. They were the only currency I had ever had, earned over six years of trudging provinces and counting the uncountable, and without them I was simply a woman in a muddy coat with a price on her head.

"I'll give you these," I said, and laid them on the boards between the lantern and the coins. "All of it. Commission, seals, letters. You can sell them—there are men who'd pay well for a working identity, and mine is a good one. Or keep them. Walk into the capital as Rosalind Ashe, census taker, and no gate in the province will question you."

Ferenc's fingers hovered above the papers without touching them, the way a man hovers over cards he suspects are marked. "And then what are you?"

"A woman walking into the tribunal with a book sewn into her coat and no name to protect her," I said. "Which is, if you think about it, a fairer condition than most of the people in that book ever got."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the marsh breathed—wind moving through drowned willows, water finding its level somewhere in the dark. Somewhere far off, a gun spoke twice and was answered by nothing.

"You'd walk into that city naked," he said. "No office behind you. No seal. If they take you at the gate, there'll be no one to say *she's ours*. They'll hang you as a spy and file you under *unknown female*, and no one will count you, census woman. Isn't that the thing you can't abide? Being uncounted?"

"Yes," I said. My voice held. I was proud of that, later. "Yes, it is. Which is why you should understand that I'm not offering these papers lightly. I'm offering the only self I have that anyone official recognizes. Take it. It's worth more than eleven clipped crowns and it's worth more to me than you can imagine, and I'm giving it anyway, and that should tell you how much I need those pages to reach the tribunal."

"And the soldier," Ferenc said softly. "Elias Ashe. Your brother. He gets his name read aloud, and you stand in the gallery with no papers, no office, no name anyone honors, watching. Is that the trade?"

"He gets his name read aloud," I said. "That's the whole of the trade. The rest of it is mine to carry."

For a moment—I want to record this honestly, because everything else in that chapter of my life has been disputed—I believed Ferenc might take it. His hand came down flat over the papers, possessive, final. And then his eyes lifted to Jonah, and narrowed, and he said:

"But you're not the one paying, are you? She pays her name, and you keep your book, and you both feel very noble, and I am supposed to pole you forty miles of marsh for a stack of paper I'll have to fence at a loss because half the offices it names will be burned or bought by summer. No." He pushed the papers back across the boards with one finger, gently, insultingly. "Not enough. The woman's papers buy her passage. Yours costs more, ledger-keeper. I want the book itself."

* * *

"There are copies," Jonah said.

It was the worst lie I had ever heard him tell, and I had heard him lie about Elias Ashe over tea in a hospital tent, badly, guiltily, with his hands betraying him. This was worse. This was a lie told with his whole body squared and his chin level, a lie he intended to be caught in, because he wanted Ferenc to call it, wanted the excuse, wanted the fight rather than the surrender.

Ferenc called it. "Show me the copy, then."

Silence. The lantern hissed. A drop of marsh water fell from the roof beam into a puddle somewhere behind us, precise as a clock.

"There are no copies," Jonah said quietly.

"No," Ferenc agreed. "There are no copies. There is one book, and it is the only reason either of you is alive, and it is the only thing you have that I cannot get elsewhere, and so it is the price. The book, or you swim the Vasker channel yourselves and let the tollhouse marksmen decide your accounts. Those are the ledgers tonight, nurse. Choose."

I watched Jonah's hand go again to his chest. I watched him begin, actually begin, to draw the oilcloth bundle out—to unbuckle the strap with those careful field-hospital fingers that could tie a tourniquet in the dark—and I did the thing I had sworn I would never do, the thing that had kept me traveling alone for six years, sleeping in different towns, trusting arithmetic and never people.

I put my hand over his.

"Bargain with him," I said, low, just for Jonah, though Ferenc heard every word; I intended Ferenc to hear every word. "But not with the book. There's something else he wants more. Look at him. He hasn't stopped looking at it. He doesn't want to sell the book—he wants to be *in* it."

* * *

You must understand what I saw in that moment, because it changed what happened, and what happened changed everything after.

Ferenc's face did something small and catastrophic when I said it—a flicker, a flinch, the mask slipping not off but sideways, showing the face beneath. And beneath the smuggler's scar and the pike's smile was a man perhaps thirty years old, exhausted in a way that sleep doesn't touch, sitting in a leaking boathouse in a war that had eaten his province, holding a lantern over other people's treasures because he had nothing of his own that anyone would trouble to steal.

His brother, I would learn much later, had drowned at the weir below Marrow Ford in the second autumn of the war, in a retreat no dispatch ever mentioned, in a company whose muster roll was lost—or burned, or edited—and whose colonel was decorated that winter for a victory in which the drowned company had done the dying. Ferenc had spent two years asking questions in river towns. He had gotten answers the way I got answers: slowly, in fragments, from people afraid to be seen giving them. He had gotten, at last, a rumor. A rumor of a hospital nurse who kept a book.

That is why the price had been the book. Not greed. Not leverage. He didn't want to sell it. He wanted, I think, in the secret place where even smugglers keep their uncounted dead, to open it and find a name.

"Ask him," I said to Jonah. "Ask him whose name."

* * *

Jonah is a slow man to move and a fast man to understand; it took him perhaps ten seconds, and I watched understanding arrive on his face like weather. Then he turned to Ferenc, and he did not ask whose name. He asked a better question, a nurse's question, a keeper-of-ledgers' question:

"What was he called?"

"Ferenc," the smuggler said. His voice had gone rough. "My brother. Ferenc Orosz. Company C, Fourth Provincial Rifles. Drowned at Marrow Ford, October of the second year, with thirty-one others, and the dispatch says the company was never stationed at the ford." He laughed again, that dry bark, but it broke in the middle this time. "Thirty-two men, and officially they were never there. My mother went to her grave writing letters to the War Office about a son who, according to the province, never existed. So. Now you know what I want with your book, nurse. Read me the page or don't. But you're not leaving this boathouse with it."

"It isn't in there," Jonah said.

The words fell into the boathouse like a stone into still water, and the ripples went outward through all three of us. Ferenc came forward off his crate with the lantern shaking in his fist, and I put myself half between them without deciding to, and Jonah raised both hands—palm out, the gesture he used with dying men, *easy now, easy*—and said:

"He isn't in it because the book holds only the omitted dead of the border hospitals—the ones I buried or watched buried, the ones erased from rolls I personally saw. Ferenc Orosz drowned at Marrow Ford before I was posted there. I've heard the story—Kessler spoke of it, the night before they took him. Thirty-two men. But I never saw their bodies. I never entered their names. It isn't omission I can mend, because it isn't my omission." He paused, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "But the tribunal doesn't distinguish. Every name read into that record counts, however it got there. And there is room on the last page. There has always been room on the last page."

"For a fee," Ferenc said, but the pike-smile was gone, and what remained was younger and more frightened than anything I had seen in three days of marshes.

"For a fee," Jonah agreed.

* * *

I thought the fee would be money we didn't have, or the papers Ferenc had refused, or the compass that hung at Jonah's belt—the one thing, besides the book, I had seen him touch like a talisman. I was wrong. I have been wrong about Jonah Vane perhaps five times in all, and this was the greatest of them.

He set the ledger on the boards between us. He untied the oilcloth. And in the lantern light, with the marsh breathing outside and the war grumbling somewhere beyond the willows like a dog in its sleep, Jonah Vane opened his forbidden book to the final page—the blank one, the one I had glimpsed and never understood—and he uncapped his pen, and he wrote.

I read it upside down, as I read everything, out of six years of habit. It was a single line, in that careful upright hand I would know anywhere now, the hand Kessler had recognized across a dinner table at the cost of his freedom:

*Vane, Jonah. Field nurse, Third Border Hospital. Recorded by his own hand, that someone may find him.*

"That's not a name," Ferenc whispered. "That's a confession. That page convicts you. If the tribunal reads that book, the man who kept it is a criminal under military law—harboring evidence, falsifying records, treason by their arithmetic. You've written yourself into your own indictment."

"Yes," Jonah said. He capped the pen. His hands were perfectly steady, and for once I knew it wasn't effort. "That's the fee. You want your brother counted? Then count me with him. The book leaves my possession tonight, Ferenc, but it goes to the tribunal, not to you—and in exchange, my name stays in it forever, next to the last of the entries, as the man who carried it. Whatever they do to the book's keeper afterward, that's the price of the thirty-second name at Marrow Ford. Write your brother under mine. Do it yourself. It should be family ink."

I have stood in many rooms while terrible bargains were struck. I have heard men sell land they loved and women sell years they couldn't spare. But I had never watched a man purchase another man's grief with his own future until that night in the boathouse, and I confess—I, who count everything—I lost the number of my own breaths.

Ferenc took the pen. His hand shook so badly that Jonah steadied it, the way you steady a wounded man's wrist before dressing a wound, and the smuggler wrote his brother's name beneath Jonah's line in a scrawl gone soft and childish, the handwriting of a boy writing home. Then he closed the book as if it were a coffin lid, and tied the oilcloth, and pushed it back across the boards to Jonah.

"The passage stands," he said hoarsely. "Dawn tomorrow. We go up the drowned channel to within sight of Vasker tollhouse, and from there you walk the last mile in the fog, and God and the sentries do what they like. The papers she offered—keep them. She'll need them more than I will, if she lives." He stood, swaying slightly, a man who had set down something heavy. Then, at the door, with the marsh wind coming in cold around him, he turned back.

"Nurse. That page. When they read the names aloud in the capital—they do read them aloud, don't they? All of them?"

"All of them," Jonah said. "In order. To the end."

"Then I'll be in the gallery," Ferenc said, "listening for two," and he stepped out into the dark, and the marsh closed behind him like water over a stone.

* * *

Jonah rewrapped the ledger and stowed it, and I busied myself with the satchel, with the papers I had nearly given away, with anything that kept my face turned from him, because what was in my face was not safe to show. Six years of traveling alone. Six years of trusting numbers because numbers don't leave. And here was a man who had just written his own death warrant into the one document that mattered, casually, the way other men sign for parcels, because a stranger's brother needed counting.

"You shouldn't have done it," I said finally. It came out harder than I meant.

"No," he agreed mildly. He was checking the lamp wick, the buckle on his kit, the thousand small preparations of a man who expected to die useful. "Probably not."

"They'll hang the keeper of that book. You know that. Tribunal or no tribunal, Brandt's people will make certain the man who compiled it answers for it, whatever happens to the evidence."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*" I rounded on him. "That's all? Yes?"

He looked at me then, and the lamplight was kind to neither of us, and he said, "Rosalind. Every page in that book is a man who answered 'yes' to something like this. Someone stayed behind so the others could run. Someone signed a form that got him shot. Someone wrote a letter the censors killed him for. I have spent three years recording what it costs to be counted. It seemed dishonest to flinch from paying it myself." A pause. The lantern flame leaned in the draft from the door. "Besides. You gave up your papers tonight. You were going to walk into the capital with no name at all. I merely matched your stake."

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same. That's what frightens you."

I had no answer to that, because it was true, and because the truth of it sat in my chest like swallowed river water—cold and expanding. We stood there in the failing light, two people who had each just sold a version of themselves, and the silence between us was no longer the silence of strangers or even allies. It was the silence of co-conspirators in each other's souls, which is a far more dangerous condition.

"We should sleep," I said at last. "Dawn comes early on the marsh."

"It does." He banked the lamp. In the near-darkness I heard him settle onto the boards, heard the ledger's faint crackle of oilcloth as he drew it inside his coat, against his heart, where it slept every night. And then, just before sleep took me, just at the edge where listening blurs into dreaming, I heard him speak once more, so quietly I might have invented it:

"Goodnight, Rosalind Ashe. Counted, at last."

I woke before dawn to the smell of peat smoke and the sound of a pole driving into shallow water, and Jonah standing at the boathouse door looking east, where the fog was lifting off the channel in long gray banners—and beyond the fog, faint as a pencil line on the horizon, the black silhouette of the Vasker tollhouse against a sky the color of ash.

"Two miles," Ferenc said, already in the boat. "And the fog burns off by seven. Pray your God of numbers keeps it low."

We stepped down into the boat. The marsh took us. Ahead, through the thinning mist, I could see the tollhouse plainly now—and I could see something else, something that stopped my breath and started my heart hammering like a fist on a door.

On the tollhouse wall, fresh-pasted, white as bone in the gray morning light, hung a new notice. And even at two hundred yards, even through fog, I could read the large printed word at its head.
