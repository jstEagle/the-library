## 15. Checkpoint Two: The Tollhouse

The notice was nailed to the tollhouse door with four nails, and I counted them the way Rosalind would have—because counting had become a kind of prayer for both of us, and because I needed my hands to be doing something other than shaking.

*WANTED FOR SEDITION AND THEFT OF MILITARY PROPERTY.* Below it, a description in a clerk's careful hand: *A woman of middle height, brown hair worn pinned, carries herself upright and counts aloud when nervous. Answers questions precisely. May travel under census credentials. Companion believed to be a male nurse of the field hospitals, name unknown.*

They had her exactly. Not the shape of her face—that they'd gotten wrong, or rather they hadn't tried—but the *way* of her. The counting. The precision. Whoever had written that notice had stood close enough to Rosalind Ashe to hear her breathe arithmetic.

Kessler's description. It could only have been Kessler. And if he'd given them this much about her, he might already have given them more about me—the handwriting, the ledger, the floorboard at Halven that no longer kept anything but dust.

I turned away from the door before one of the two soldiers lounging beneath the eaves could mark how long I'd looked. I became instead what I had been trained to be across three years of war: a man whose face announced nothing. I walked back down the toll road toward the stand of alders where Rosalind waited with our single satchel and the pages sewn flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She saw my expression before I said a word. She was getting good at reading me, which should have frightened me more than it did.

"Well," she said.

"They know you. They know how you count."

Something moved behind her eyes—not fear, not quite. Calculation. I had watched her do it a hundred times since the marshes: take a fact, turn it over, find its weight and its edges. "Then walking through together is finished," she said. "If they stop us as a pair, they stop us entirely."

"There'll be another road."

"There isn't. You know there isn't." She nodded up the valley, where the river narrowed into white water and the old weir crossed it in a broken stair of stones, built when the province had been at peace with itself and had nothing better to do with its masons. "The tollhouse guards the bridge. Nobody watches the weir in winter except drowned men."

"The weir is guarded by ice."

"Everything is guarded by something." She said it without drama, the way she said everything, and I understood suddenly that she had already decided. She had probably decided while I was still walking back down the road, reading my face from forty paces.

* * *

We argued for an hour in the alder shade, which is to say she dismantled my objections one at a time with the patience of a woman auditing a dishonest account, and I kept raising new ones because raising them was the only way I knew to say *don't do this, don't put yourself between me and the guns again, I can't carry what it would cost.*

"You're the one they want," I said. "The notice describes you. Me it barely knows—a name unknown, a male nurse. I walk through the bridge. You take the weir."

"And hand the ledger to the man who lies badly?" She almost smiled. Almost. "Jonah. At Halven, when I asked about Elias, you lied to me so poorly I felt embarrassed for you. If they question you even lightly—if they ask your regiment, your posting, the name of your commanding officer—you will hesitate, and your hesitation will be louder than any confession."

"That was weeks ago. I've improved."

"You've improved at lying to me. That's different. Lying to me required only that you betray yourself. Lying to a checkpoint guard requires you to betray the dead, and you've never once managed that." She crouched, opened the satchel, and began dividing our things with quick, economical movements: bread to one side, the tin of lamp oil to the other, the flint, the spare stockings. Everything counted. Everything assigned. "Besides. Think what the notice actually gives us. They expect a pair. A woman with census papers traveling alone is unremarkable—I did it for six years before the war taught everyone to be suspicious of paper. But a man crossing alone, at a forewarned checkpoint, on the same day a wanted couple was reported heading south? Every eye goes to him. No—" she shook her head, closing the satchel, "—it has to be me on the bridge, walking straight through their suspicion, giving them a target so solid and so *expected* that they never think to look at the water."

"And if they arrest you?"

"Then I talk my way out. I'm good at talking my way out; you've seen it. And while I'm being good at it, you'll be on the far bank with the ledger, and none of what happens to me will matter."

"It will matter to me."

The words were out before I could weigh them, which was becoming a habit around her—one I neither examined nor corrected. Rosalind went very still. For a moment the whole argument hung between us, suspended, and I thought: *she counts everything, steps and rations and lies, but she cannot count this,* and I didn't know whether that was a comfort or a wound.

"We split at the bend above the mill race," she said quietly, declining the sentence I'd offered. "You go down through the birches to the weir. I come along the road, alone, with my credentials and my honest face. When I'm through—if I'm through—I make for the shrine at Saint Aldhelm's, the one the smuggler marked on the map. Three hours' walk beyond the checkpoint. You wait until dark. If I don't come by full dark, you go on without me."

"I won't go on without you."

"You will. Because going on without me is the entire point of me." She stood, brushed the leaf-mold from her skirt, and looked at me with those gray eyes that took inventory of everything and surrendered nothing. "Jonah. Do you trust me?"

It was such a small question for such an enormous thing. I thought of all the trust we'd spent already—her trusting me not to run when she first showed me she knew about the ledger; me trusting her with Elias's page, the one page I'd sworn belonged to no living soul; both of us trusting the smuggler who'd taken Rosalind's credentials and my dignity and left us with a map drawn in charcoal and a warning about the tollhouse. Trust, I had learned, was not a feeling. It was a decision made repeatedly, in the dark, against all evidence, like keeping a ledger nobody ordered you to keep.

"Yes," I said. "God help me. Yes."

"Then let me be bait. It's what I'm best at—I've been bait for the truth my whole life. Everyone talks freely around a woman with a clipboard." She held out her hand, and I took it, and her grip was dry and steady and colder than the morning air warranted. "Three hours past the checkpoint. Saint Aldhelm's. Count on me."

* * *

The birches came down to the river in a pale, shivering wall, and I went through them bent double with the satchel strapped tight across my chest, the way I'd once carried wounded men. The ground sloped steeply, slick with rotted leaves, and twice I fell and lay still afterward, listening, certain the noise must have carried to the bridge. It hadn't. War teaches you that sound dies fast in country like this—or perhaps it teaches you that no one is listening, which is worse.

The weir revealed itself in pieces: first the sound, a long white hiss beneath the birdsong; then the smell, cold stone and churned water; then the thing itself, stepping down the river in eleven broken stages, each stage a slab of moss-slick granite with black water sliding over it thin as glass. In summer, smugglers crossed here with salt and untaxed brandy. In November, it was a staircase built for ghosts.

I stood at the top step and did what Rosalind would have done. I counted. Eleven slabs. Perhaps thirty feet of river. On my left, upstream, the bridge and the tollhouse sat a quarter mile off, hidden by the bend, but I could hear the traffic—cart wheels, a mule complaining, the bored call-and-answer of soldiers inspecting goods. Somewhere in that boredom, Rosalind was walking toward them with the dead men's names pressed against her ribs.

I stepped onto the first slab.

The water took my boot to the ankle immediately, of course; there was no dry line anywhere, and hoping for one was a child's hope. What mattered was the grip. I tested each stone the way you test a wounded man's ribs before moving him—firmly, quickly, without mercy—and found most of them sound and two of them liars. On the fifth slab my leading foot shot sideways on a skin of green moss and I dropped to one knee in the shallows, arms out, heart slamming, the satchel held high and dry above the flood like an offering. The cold climbed my leg to the hip in seconds. I stayed kneeling longer than I needed to, letting the water decide to spare me, and when it did I rose and went on.

Halfway across, I heard the shouting.

It came faintly, flattened by distance and the river's noise—a voice raised at the bridge, sharp, official, followed by a second voice answering it. A woman's voice. Even through the water-hiss I knew the cadence: precise, unhurried, each word placed like a coin on a counter. She was counting for them. Or reciting. Or simply being Rosalind Ashe at her most unbearably reasonable, and every soldier on that bridge would find himself half convinced and half furious about it, unable to say why.

Every instinct I owned screamed at me to go back. To climb the bank, walk down the road, put myself between her and whatever was happening, because that was the arrangement my body had silently made somewhere in the marshes, in the dark, without consulting me. Instead I knelt on the seventh slab, freezing water pulling at me, and made myself listen.

The voices continued. No whistle. No running feet. The mule still complained. Whatever was happening at the tollhouse, it was happening slowly, in the manner of inspections and arguments and paperwork—which meant Rosalind was winning, or losing so gradually that winning and losing had become indistinguishable.

Trust, I told myself. A deliberate act. Do it again.

I crossed the last four slabs on my hands and knees, because pride drowns faster than fools and I had no intention of drowning within sight of the finish. My knees found the final bank, the mud of the far side closed over my wrists, and I hauled myself up into the reeds and lay there gasping, one side of me soaked and steaming, the satchel pressed under my chest, the ledger safe inside it—safe, always safe, that word that had stopped meaning anything except *not yet lost.*

From the reeds I could see the road on the southern bank, curving away toward Saint Aldhelm's through bare fields. I could see, distantly, the bridge. Traffic still moved over it. No alarm bells—there were no alarm bells; this province fought its wars with paperwork—but no knots of running men either.

I waited in the reeds while the sun crawled a hand's width across the sky. I counted the minutes because I couldn't help it now; she'd infected me. At the hour mark I allowed myself despair. At the second hour I rationed it. At the third hour, with the light going amber and my soaked side aching like a bruised rib, I saw a figure come around the bend of the southern road.

Small. Upright. Walking at a pace that measured itself, that counted the ground as it covered it.

I stayed flat in the reeds until she was near enough that I could see the mud on her hem and the set of her jaw, and then I stood up out of the rushes like something risen, and she stopped dead, hand flying to her coat—then she saw it was me, and the hand dropped, and for one naked moment her face did something I had never seen it do. It came apart. Just slightly. Just at the edges. The look of a woman who had counted on herself all day and was briefly, privately astonished to have added up.

"You're wet," she said.

"You're late."

"I was detained." She fell into step beside me, and we walked, and only after a hundred paces did she add, in the tone of someone reporting a successful audit: "A sergeant with excellent handwriting wanted to copy my credentials. I let him. He'll report a census taker named Ashe passed southbound, cooperative, dull." A pause. "He also mentioned the tribunal has been postponed."

I stopped walking.

"Postponed," I repeated.

"Indefinitely. Until after the campaign, the sergeant said. Which means after the war, which means—" She didn't finish it. She didn't need to. We had carried these pages through marshes and checkpoints and the bottom of a frozen weir, and now the door we were carrying them toward had quietly swung shut, and somewhere behind us Private Kessler sat in a cell having traded us for nothing.

Rosalind looked north, toward the capital, toward the postponed truth, and then at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her reach out and take the satchel strap from my shoulder—an intimacy so sudden and so practical that I nearly wept.

"You're bleeding," she said. "Your knee. From the weir."

"It's nothing."

"Everything is something. I count it all." And she walked on beside me, lighter than she should have been, heavier than she knew, while behind us the tollhouse sank into the dusk with its sergeant and his excellent handwriting, and ahead the road ran dark toward Saint Aldhelm's, where a smuggler's map promised shelter—and where, though neither of us could know it yet, someone was waiting who had been dead for eight months on the official rolls of Colonel Brandt's command.
