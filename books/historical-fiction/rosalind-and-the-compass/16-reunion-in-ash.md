## 16. Reunion in Ash

They took her at the tollhouse gate with a courtesy that frightened her more than violence would have.

"Madam Ashe? Rosalind Ashe, of the Provincial Census Commission?" The sergeant who asked it had kind eyes and a hand resting on his holster all the same. "There's a matter of paperwork. Won't take a moment."

Paperwork. She had learned by now that in wartime the word was a drawer where they kept knives.

Behind him, two soldiers flanked her as though escorting rather than detaining, and the tollhouse door closed behind her with the soft click of a lock that pretended to be a latch.

* * *

The room they gave her had once been an office—she could tell from the ghost of a map still pinned to the wall, its borders faded to rumor—and now held a table, two chairs, and a stove that smoked. A lamp burned low. On the table lay a single sheet of paper, face down.

She was left alone for an hour. That too was technique; she knew interrogation the way she knew arithmetic, from manuals read in better years, from the census office's thin pamphlet on *Conduct Toward Officials of Other Departments*. Let them wait. Let them imagine what you know. The empty hour is the cheapest instrument ever devised, and it works on nearly everyone.

Rosalind Ashe sat with her hands folded and counted instead.

Four hundred and eleven seconds before the stove settled into a rhythm. Sixty-two cracks in the ceiling plaster. One sheet of paper, face down. One window, barred, looking east toward the capital she could not see but could feel the way one feels weather coming—a pressure behind the ribs.

And somewhere across the weir, upstream in the grey water, Jonah Vane was moving with a satchel against his chest, trusting her to be loud enough, ordinary enough, memorable enough to be remembered by the wrong people for the right length of time.

*Bait*, he had called it, and hated himself for the word. She had corrected him: *decoy*. A decoy at least implied skill.

The door opened at last, and the man who entered was not a sergeant and not a soldier. He wore civilian clothes cut well enough to be expensive and badly enough to be deliberate—a man dressed to seem harmless, which in her experience meant the opposite. He carried no papers. He carried a teapot.

"Census work," he said, pouring without asking. "What a profession. You spend your life counting people, and nobody counts you back." He smiled. "Major Lenz. Provost administration. Sit, please."

"I'm standing comfortably," Rosalind said, and remained standing.

"As you like." He set the second cup across from himself anyway—an invitation shaped like furniture. "You were detained at the Marrow Ford bridge nine days ago. Sanitary commission, traveling credentials signed by Dr. Hessel. Then nothing. No arrival logged in Halven. No departure. And now here you are, walking the toll road alone, no commission, no companion, toward a capital you have no business entering under present regulations."

"Regulations change," Rosalind said. "I was recalled. My orders came by courier through the garrison at Brandt's headquarters."

"Mm." He turned the face-down paper over with one finger, unhurried, the way a man turns a card he already knows. It was a wanted notice. Her own description looked back at her—*female, thirties, methodical speech, carries documents in oilcloth*—and beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand: **accompanying male, army nurse, name uncertain, may give Vane.**

Her heart did something complicated and quiet. She kept her face doing something simple.

"That isn't me," she said.

"No," Major Lenz agreed pleasantly. "It isn't anyone. Descriptions are like that—half the province could be guilty of being a woman of thirties who speaks precisely." He sat back, studying her with the frank interest of a man examining an insect he suspected of being new to science. "But here is what interests me. Nine days ago a sanitary commission crossed Marrow Ford. Two days later, Private Kessler of Colonel Brandt's own staff was arrested for asking questions about a ledger. Do you know what a ledger is, Madam Ashe?"

"A book of accounts."

"So it is. So it is." He poured tea he did not drink. "Kessler described a handwriting. A particular hand—small, slanted, economical, the hand of a man who writes fast because he writes often and doesn't want to be seen doing it. Kessler saw that hand in a hospital book at Halven. He said the man who owned it kept lists of names." Lenz's voice never rose. It lowered, if anything, gently, drawing her toward it like deep water. "Names of dead men, Madam Ashe. Names that appear nowhere else. Now—you are a counter of people. Professionally. Tell me: when a person disappears from your rolls, what does it mean?"

Rosalind thought of Elias. Of the dispatch that said *deserted* in ink she could almost smell from here. Of a page sewn against her ribs beneath her coat, riding over her heartbeat like a second skeleton.

"It means someone made an error," she said. "Or chose to."

"And which do you believe?"

"Which do *you* believe?" She met his eyes. It cost her; her whole body wanted to look anywhere else, toward the window, toward the door, toward the east where hope had gone to die. "You've shown me a notice describing two travelers. You've mentioned a private who asked questions. You've poured tea you won't drink. You're not interrogating me, Major. You're fishing. Which means you don't know what I know—which means either there's nothing to know, or your information is worse than you'd like your superiors to think." She let that sit. "I was recalled to the capital. My commission expires in six weeks. If I'm not there to certify the provincial returns, the entire census year for this district is void, and the tribunal sitting in session will want to know why a provost major detained the one woman who could have saved it."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a sentry coughed.

Then Lenz laughed—a short, genuine bark that seemed to surprise even him. "Oh, they told me you'd be difficult. They said, *the census woman counts everything.*" He stood, buttoning his coat. "Here is what happens now, Madam Ashe. You'll be escorted to the capital tomorrow under military pass. Legitimate, stamped, unimpeachable. Your census credentials will carry you through every gate between here and the tribunal hall. You'll arrive exactly when and where you're supposed to."

"Generous."

"Not generosity. Arithmetic." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The tribunal has been postponed. Indefinitely. By order of the War Ministry. There is no longer any session for you to testify before, no record for you to amend, no clerk in that hall with authority to hear a word you say. You can walk into the capital carrying whatever you like, Madam Ashe." His reflection watched her from the dark pane of the window glass. "You'll be speaking truth into a room where no one is required to listen. That is not detention. That is simply the country we live in now."

The door closed. The lamp guttered.

Rosalind stood a long time in the smoke-smelling room, one hand pressed flat against her coat, over the small stiff rectangle of pages that had cost three checkpoints and a growing catalogue of the dead.

*Postponed indefinitely.* Four words, and the ground moved beneath her like marsh.

* * *

They gave her a cot in the tollhouse loft and, absurdly, a blanket that smelled of horses. Sleep would not come, so she conducted inventory instead—it was how she prayed, though she'd stopped calling it prayer years ago.

One brother, alive until proven otherwise. One ledger, twenty-nine names, thirty with Kessler's brother if Jonah had dared—and she knew him well enough now to know he had. One nurse somewhere north of the weir with wet boots and a conscience too heavy for his kit. Zero tribunals currently in session. Indefinite: a number she could not count, and therefore the only number that truly frightened her.

Through the loft's small window she watched the road east, silvered by a rising moon, empty in both directions. Somewhere out there Jonah was watching the same road, waiting for her the way they'd agreed—at the shrine of Saint Aldric where the toll road forked, the one with the leaning stone figure whose face the weather had erased. *If you're not there by dawn,* he'd said, *don't come looking. Go east alone.*

*Alone.* She turned the word over and found it had changed weight since Halven. Once it had been her native country. Now it felt like exile.

Dawn came up grey and cold. They processed her out with stamps and signatures, a military pass warm from the press, an escort of two bored soldiers who walked her the first mile and then lost interest at the crossroads, as Lenz had promised. The bureaucracy released her the way a trap releases—not out of mercy, but because it had calculated she was worth more walking free than lying in a cell. That was the part that chilled her: she had talked her way out, yes, but only into a larger room with no walls.

At midmorning she reached the fork and the shrine.

Saint Aldric leaned over his eroded face, hands raised in a blessing or a surrender—the stone could no longer say—and at his feet, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, sat Jonah Vane's field kit.

No note. Of course no note. A note was evidence. But tucked beneath the strap, folded twice, a page torn from a hospital register, and she knew the small slanted economical hand before she'd finished the first line:

*Ledger safe. Am going ahead via the asylum depot at Kirchmoor—there is a ward there requisitioned by the medical corps, and a name on their intake rolls I have to see with my own eyes before I can believe it. If I am wrong, I lose a day. If I am right, we lose nothing and gain everything. Tribunal or no tribunal, Rosalind—as long as one breath in this province remembers these men, postponed is not the same as cancelled. Count on that. —J.V.*

She read it three times. The third time her eyes snagged on the last line and stayed there, and the cold morning seemed briefly less cold, and she hated herself a little for how much that helped.

*Count on that.*

Below the signature, smaller, added after the ink had dried, almost shy:

*P.S. — Your brother's page travels next to my heart. It has for nine days. I thought you should know where it lives.*

Rosalind folded the letter along its creases, precisely, the way she did everything. Then she sat down on the wet grass at the saint's stone feet, put her face in her hands, and let herself shake for exactly sixty seconds—she counted, because counting was the only solid thing left—before standing, brushing off her coat, and turning south toward Kirchmoor.

The tribunal might be postponed. Hope might gutter like a lamp in wind. But a name waited in an intake roll at a requisitioned asylum, and a nurse she trusted more than any institution left standing was walking toward it, and somewhere in her coat, against her ribs, twenty-nine dead men were still insisting on being counted.

She began to walk. Behind her, the road to the capital stretched on, patient and closed.

Ahead, the marshes opened like a held breath.
