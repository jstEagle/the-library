## 17. The Man in the Mirror Ward

The depot had been an asylum once. You could still see it in the architecture—in the long corridors built to be walked in endless circles by men whose minds had turned on them like dogs; in the high, barred windows set too close to the ceiling for anyone inside to reach; in the doors with their little iron viewing slots, each one fitted with a plate of polished tin where a name card might go. The soldiers who garrisoned it now had torn the cards out and used the tin plates for mirrors, shaving at dawn, checking their collars before leave. A hundred small mirrors down a hundred corridors, reflecting a hundred men who did not want to look at themselves too closely.

Jonah understood the impulse.

He came to the Mirror Ward, as the garrison called it, on the third evening after the marsh, carrying a requisition chit he had forged himself—his old skill, learned in the first winter of the war when paper was scarcer than morphine—and a story he had rehearsed until it sat in his mouth like a smooth stone. He was a medical officer attached to no regiment, traveling under sanitary commission papers that were half genuine and wholly expired, seeking stores: bandages, quinine, anything the tribunal road would demand of them before the capital gate. The quartermaster's clerk, a boy of perhaps seventeen with chilblains on his ears, barely looked up from his tea.

"Mirror Ward's got stores," the boy said, jerking a thumb down the corridor. "Mind the patients, though. Some of them bite."

*Some of them,* Jonah thought, *are not patients at all.*

He had known something was wrong since Halven—or rather, since before Halven, since the ledger itself, if he were honest, which he tried not to be. A man who keeps a book of the erased learns to read erasure the way a banker reads counterfeit notes. And there had been pages in that ledger that never sat right with him. Names entered in his own hand at Rosalind's insistence, men listed as deserted or dead-on-records-never-filed, whose omissions had always struck him as strange—not the ordinary strangeness of command covering its shame, but a different strangeness entirely. Commanders erased the dead because dead men could not contradict them. But why erase a man so thoroughly that even the manner of his death went unrecorded? Why burn a page clean when a tidy lie—a fever, a stray shell—would serve just as well?

Because the lie would be checked. Because someone, someday, might come looking for a body.

Because the man was not dead.

Jonah had carried that suspicion folded up small in the back of his mind for weeks, the way a soldier carries a letter he is afraid to open. He had told himself it was nothing. He had told himself a great many things were nothing—the way Brandt's staff had flinched when Rosalind said her surname aloud; the way Kessler, before they took him, had whispered *they keep some of them, Vane, they keep some of them somewhere* and then refused, weeping, to say more. Kessler's brother had vanished after questioning. So had Elias Ashe.

So had eleven others whose names Jonah could recite in the dark like a litany.

He walked the corridor slowly, boots loud on the flagstones, past the little tin mirrors catching the lamplight in slivers. The ward smelled of carbolic and wet wool and, beneath both, the sweetish institutional stink of despair long marinated. Men sat along the walls in the niches between barred windows, wrapped in gray blankets, some of them rocking, some of them staring at nothing with the particular fixed attention of men watching something that wasn't there. Wounded men, mostly—concussed, shell-shocked, broken in the ways the army had only recently invented words for. The army sent such men here to be warehoused until the war decided what to do with them. It was cheaper than treatment. Everything was cheaper than treatment.

A sergeant with a clipboard looked up as Jonah passed. "Medical?"

"Surgical stores." He showed the forged chit without showing it—held it at an angle, let the wax seal blur in the bad light. Forgery, he had learned, was ninety percent confidence and ten percent ink.

"Third door. Don't let them follow you out. Last week one of them got into the yard and they found him in the well."

"I'll bear it in mind."

The third door opened onto a storeroom that had been a day-room in the asylum's former life. Shelves of lint and splints, crates marked with the quartermaster's chalk, a stack of stretchers against the wall. Jonah took a roll of bandages and held it, feeling its weight, performing the part of a man taking inventory while his eyes did other work. There was a second door in the far wall, half-open, leading deeper—a corridor of small rooms, the old cells, and from behind those doors came sounds. Not screaming. Screaming he knew how to ignore. These were quieter sounds. Murmuring. A rhythmic tapping, patient as a clock. Somewhere, a man humming a lullaby in a language Jonah didn't recognize, over and over, getting every note right and every meaning wrong.

He should have taken his bandages and gone. Every instinct honed by two years of surviving this war told him to take the cotton and walk away, back through the tin mirrors, back to Rosalind waiting in the ruined chapel outside the walls, back to the plan they had agreed upon with the smuggler's price still bitter between them. Curiosity, in Jonah's experience, was the luxury of people who had not yet been shot for it.

But Kessler's whisper was in his ears. *They keep some of them.*

And beneath that, older, heavier: Rosalind's voice by the firelight after the first checkpoint, reading her brother's page aloud, her voice level as a surveyor's line until the last sentence, where it cracked like ice on a pond. *Wounded at the Kessel action, removed for questioning. No further record.* She had closed the ledger then with a care that frightened him more than any grief could have. Grief screams. That care had been silent, and silence, Jonah knew, was what a person made when they had decided to do something irreversible.

If Elias Ashe was anywhere in this building—if there was even a chance—then walking away was not caution. It was betrayal. And Jonah had spent enough of his life keeping accounts to know exactly which column betrayal belonged in.

He went through the half-open door.

* * *

The inner corridor was colder. The lamps here were fewer and farther between, and the tin name-plates on the cell doors had not been stripped—perhaps because these rooms held no one the garrison bothered to look at. Most of the plates were blank, the cards long gone. But near the end of the corridor, one plate caught the light differently, and Jonah stopped.

Someone had written on it. Not printed, not typed—written, in a hurried pencil hand, and then half-scrubbed away with a wet thumb, so that only ghosts of letters remained. Jonah angled the plate toward the lamp and read the shadow of what had been there:

*A—*

Just that. A single initial, scrubbed but not scrubbed hard enough. As if whoever wrote it had been interrupted, or had lost their nerve, or had wanted the name remembered even while obeying orders to forget it.

The cell behind the plate was dark. Jonah stood with his hand on the iron latch for the length of three heartbeats, listening. Inside: breathing. Slow, deep, deliberate—the breathing of a man who was awake and pretending otherwise, which was its own kind of eloquence. Patients slept like the drugged, because they often were. Soldiers slept in snatches, ready to wake fighting. This breathing was neither. This was the breathing of a man lying very still and counting the footsteps outside his door.

Jonah lifted the latch. The door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently—he noted that, filed it away, another wrongness in a building full of them—and lamplight spilled across a narrow room with a cot, a bucket, a stool, and a man sitting upright against the wall with his hands folded in his lap like a man posing for a portrait.

He was thin. He was gray. His hair had been cropped short with shears rather than cut, and there was a scar across his forehead, old and badly stitched, running up into the hairline like a fault line. His left arm ended at the wrist, the stump wrapped in clean linen—cleaner, Jonah noticed, than anything else in this place. Someone was tending him. Someone was keeping him alive with a deliberateness that sat ill beside the scrubbed name on the door.

But none of that mattered. None of it. Because Jonah had spent an evening by a fire listening to Rosalind describe her brother—the set of his jaw, the way he'd laughed through his nose, the crooked finger broken falling out of a pear tree at fourteen—and the man on the cot had all of it. The jaw. The nose. The crooked finger on his remaining hand, healed slightly askew, unmistakable.

"Elias Ashe," Jonah said quietly.

The man did not move. But his eyes—Rosalind's eyes, gray-green, watchful—came up and fixed on Jonah with an expression Jonah had seen before on the faces of prisoners of long standing: hope and terror occupying the same features at the same time, each making the other worse.

"You've got the wrong room," Elias said. His voice was rust and gravel. "There's no one here by that name."

"No," Jonah agreed. "I don't expect there is. I expect the records say you died at Kessel. Deserted, actually—that's what the official rolls carry. Listed as deserted, then amended to killed in the retreat, though no burial detail was ever logged and no effects were ever returned." He stepped inside and let the door swing shut behind him, softly. "I know because I copied the omission myself. Into my own book. At the request of your sister."

Something moved behind Elias's face then, quick as a fish under ice. "Rosalind." Just the name. He said it the way starving men say bread.

"She's outside the walls, in the chapel ruin, waiting for me to come back with bandages and a straight face. She believes you're dead, Elias. She has organized her entire life around your being dead. She crossed a war zone, lied to military police, and sewed stolen documents into her coat lining—all to carry your name to a tribunal, because she thinks the worst thing that happened to you is that you died unwitnessed." Jonah crouched down, bringing himself level with the man on the cot, and kept his voice low and steady, the voice he used for men coming out of anesthesia, men who needed something true to hold. "So you're going to have to help me understand why the army is keeping you alive in an oiled-hinge cell behind a scrubbed nameplate in an asylum full of mirrors. Because from where I'm standing, it looks like somebody went to a great deal of trouble to make sure you'd never be counted. And I have made a career of counting."

For a long moment Elias said nothing. His folded hands tightened, the crooked finger whitening. Then, in the corridor beyond the door, the humming started again—the man with the lullaby, passing on his endless circuit—and Elias flinched at it, a whole-body flinch, and Jonah saw that the sound meant something to him, that everything in this place meant something to him, that he had been listening to these walls for months the way Jonah listened to dispatches: reading the silences, mapping the omissions.

"They told me if I ever spoke to anyone," Elias said at last, "they'd move me. And moving means—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again from a different angle, the way men do when the direct road is mined. "You're a nurse? Medical?"

"Battlefield nurse. Two years. Before that I kept my parish's registers—births, deaths, marriages. I'm good with names."

"The man who questioned me—" Elias's good hand rose halfway and fell, as if the gesture weighed too much. "He wore a colonel's insignia. He was very polite. He apologized for the conditions. He said the army had a problem, and the problem was arithmetic. Do you understand? Arithmetic. He said that at Kessel, certain things had happened that could not appear in any report, and that the men who saw them presented a difficulty, because dead witnesses invite questions but missing witnesses can be made into deserters, and deserters are shameful, and shame closes mouths better than bullets." The words were coming faster now, tumbling, a dam going in stages. "He gave us a choice. Sign a statement swearing to what we were told to swear, disappear into the system, live. Or refuse, and be entered in the rolls as fallen in action—with honors, with a letter home, with everything proper. Dead men get letters. Missing men get silence." His laugh was terrible, dry, a sound with no humor anywhere in it. "I signed. God forgive me, I signed, because I thought—I thought if I lived, I could tell it later. I thought living was the brave choice." He looked down at his wrapped wrist. "Then I learned what living here means. You're not alive, you're withheld. Like supplies. Like evidence."

Jonah felt the floor tilt under everything. Eleven names. He had written eleven names in the ledger of the omitted, believing each one a corpse denied its grave. If even a few of them—

"How many?" he asked. "How many are here? In this building, or places like it?"

Elias's eyes came up, and in them Jonah read the answer before it was spoken, and the answer was a number large enough to make the ledger in Jonah's memory feel like a child's slate beside a cathedral wall.

"I stopped counting," Elias whispered. "Counting was the first thing they took from us. They don't beat us, they don't starve us—they just make sure no one ever says our names. You'd be amazed how fast a man comes apart when no one will say his name. It's the one thing—" His voice cracked, and he pressed the back of his good hand against his mouth, and for a moment the portrait-stillness broke and he was just a young man with a broken face sitting in a cold room, shaking. "It's the one thing a man is. And they put it away in a drawer."

Outside, somewhere across the yard, a bugle sounded the change of watch. The light in the corridor shifted. And Jonah, kneeling on the flagstones of a madhouse that had become a warehouse for inconvenient truths, understood with total clarity that the ledger in Rosalind's coat lining was no longer what either of them had thought it was. They had carried it toward the tribunal as a book of the dead. It was a book of the buried-alive. Every name they had mourned was a door that might still open. Every page was not an epitaph but an address.

And Colonel Brandt—who toasted honest men over candlelight, whose name ran through the ledger like a stain—had not been hiding corpses.

He had been hiding witnesses. Dozens of them. A whole congregation of the silenced, kept breathing precisely so that they could never testify.

"We can get you out," Jonah heard himself say. "Tonight. There's a chapel outside the walls, there's a woman out there who has crossed half a province on the belief that your name deserved to exist again, and I am telling you, Elias Ashe—she will burn the tribunal down herself before she leaves this country without you."

Elias stared at him. And then he shook his head, slow and final, and leaned forward, and gripped Jonah's sleeve with his crooked finger, hard, pulling him closer, close enough that Jonah could smell the carbolic on him and see, in the lamplight, the fine tremor in the jaw.

"Listen to me," Elias said, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, urgent as a wound bleeding out. "You cannot take me out the front of this place, because the front gate is watched by a captain who reports directly to Brandt's adjutant, and if I vanish tonight, every record in this building burns by morning—every file, every register, every scrap of paper that proves any of us ever existed. You'd lose them all. All of them." His grip tightened. "You want me out of here? Then listen to what happened at Kessel. Listen to all of it. Because I am the only witness they missed, and I have been rehearsing what I know for eight months in this room, alone, to the walls—and I swear to God, nurse, if I don't say it soon to someone real, I'm going to start saying it to the walls forever."

Through the little barred window, high above, the first gray suggestion of dawn was beginning to gather. Down the corridor, the lullaby hummed on. And Jonah Vane sat down on the edge of the stool, and folded his hands, and prepared to listen the way he had once prepared wounds: carefully, completely, knowing that what came next would change the shape of everything they carried to the gate.
