## 21. The Tribunal Reads

The tribunal chamber had once been a courthouse, and before that, Rosalind was told, a granary, and before that perhaps nothing at all but the ambition of men who wanted to build something tall in a provincial town. Whatever it had been, it was now a room built for judgment: high windows that let in the gray winter light at an angle designed to make every face look guilty, three long tables raised on a dais, and benches for the public that were filled by seven o'clock on the morning of the reading.

Rosalind arrived at six.

She came alone, as she had insisted on coming alone, though Major Ferren of the tribunal staff had offered her an escort twice and Jonah's name sat in her mouth like a coin she could not spend. He was in the capital—she knew that much. She had seen the notice pinned outside the garrison gate three days after the third checkpoint: *Jonah Vane, field nurse, detained under suspicion of forgery of military documents. Held pending tribunal review.* Suspicion. As if suspicion were not precisely what he had carried across two provinces in his coat lining, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into seams. As if he had ever been anything but suspected, from the moment he first dipped his pen.

She took a seat on the second bench, third from the aisle—the position of a woman who intended to be called upon and did not intend to be overlooked—and set her satchel between her boots. The pages rode inside it now, loose, no longer hidden. There was no point hiding them anymore. Hiding was finished. What remained was weight: one hundred and forty-one names, and she had counted them four times on the journey, and then a fifth time last night by candlelight in her rented room, because counting was the only prayer she knew.

The chamber filled slowly. Clerks with ink-stained cuffs. A colonel's widow in black crepe who wept quietly through the entire session, Rosalind would later learn, for reasons that had nothing to do with any of this. Reporters—two of them, from papers whose names she didn't recognize, which meant the war had grown large enough that strangers cared. And soldiers. Soldiers in twos and threes, sitting where they could see the dais, saying nothing, their caps held in their hands like men at a funeral.

At half past eight the tribunal entered: three officers and a civilian magistrate, a thin man named Osterling with spectacles that caught the window light so that when he looked down at you, his eyes vanished entirely, and you were left speaking to a blank shine of glass. He was the one who read the agenda aloud. He was the one who called her name.

"Rosalind Ashe. Census commissioner, provincial registry." He said it without inflection, a line item among line items. "You are here to give testimony concerning the matter of omitted personnel records. You may proceed."

She stood. Her legs held. That surprised her; she had spent the night imagining them failing.

She had rehearsed testimony the way other people rehearsed sermons, and she abandoned all of it in the first minute. The chamber was too cold, the light too flat, and somewhere behind her a soldier coughed—a small human sound that undid every formal sentence she'd prepared. So instead she began the way she had begun everything since Halven: with arithmetic.

"Honorable magistrates. In the spring campaign along the Marrow, official dispatches recorded four hundred and six dead. I have walked the burial grounds. I have read the parish registers at Halven, Dolev, and the ferry villages. I have interviewed gravediggers, chaplains' assistants, and one man who dug wells for a living and was conscripted to dig graves instead. By my count, the true number is five hundred and forty-seven."

A murmur moved along the benches like wind over water.

"One hundred and forty-one men," she said, "were killed in the service of this province and do not exist on any roll. Not dead. Not wounded. Not deserted. Absent. Erased between the field and the ministry, as if paper could unmake flesh."

Magistrate Osterling removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with great deliberation, and replaced them. "Commissioner Ashe. You are a census taker. You count people. It is your profession to find discrepancies. How is it that you come to possess a ledger of these discrepancies compiled in a field hospital, in a nurse's hand, against regulations?"

"Because I asked one question too many," Rosalind said, "and the man who kept the ledger decided I might be trusted with it. Or perhaps he simply decided the truth deserved better than a floorboard."

"And where is this ledger now?"

"It is in this room," Rosalind said. "It has been in this room since nine o'clock yesterday evening, when I delivered it to your clerk with a full inventory of its contents. One hundred and forty-one entries. Each with a name, a unit, a date, a cause of death or disappearance where known, and—" she paused, and her voice did not quite hold steady, and she let it waver, because the wavering was also testimony— "and where possible, a sentence about who the man was. What he carried. What he said. Because a name alone is a stone; the rest is the hand that sets it down."

Osterling consulted a paper. "We have the document," he said. "Its provenance will be examined. You understand that a private ledger compiled against orders constitutes evidence of insubordination as well as evidence of omission. Both matters must be weighed."

"Then weigh them both," Rosalind said. "I have never pretended the ledger-keeper was innocent. I have only pretended that innocence was the thing that mattered."

* * *

They weighed him first, as it turned out—not the ledger but the man. They brought Jonah in at eleven, escorted between two guards, and Rosalind saw him for the first time since the coffin at the gate.

He was thinner. His hands, always careful hands, had been bandaged at the knuckles, and he walked with the particular stiffness of a man who had spent weeks on a plank bed. But when his eyes found hers across the chamber—he found them immediately, as if he had counted the benches too, as if he had calculated exactly where she would sit—something in his face settled, like a compass needle finding north after a long spinning.

The tribunal questioned him for two hours. She listened to all of it.

Why had he kept the record? Because the dispatches lied. Who instructed him to omit names? No one instructed him to keep them; the omissions came down from above, signed in some cases by Colonel Brandt himself, and he had merely refused to let the subtractions stand. Had he falsified official documents? Yes. Did he regret it? A pause here, long enough that the magistrate looked up. No, Jonah said. He regretted only that there had needed to be a ledger at all.

"You understand," Osterling said, "that by your own admission you acted outside the law."

"I understand," Jonah said, "that inside the law, one hundred and forty-one men stopped existing. I chose the outside. I would choose it again tomorrow. I am prepared to be punished for the method if the tribunal finds it necessary. But I ask the tribunal to notice that the crime being punished today—the only crime anyone in this room disputes—is mine. The erasure itself remains officially legal. That seems to me the wrong arrangement of guilt, and I have brought you the arithmetic to prove it."

Rosalind closed her eyes briefly. She had crossed marshes with this man, split a river crossing with him, watched him walk into a checkpoint as bait knowing what bait was for. And still, hearing him speak, she felt she had never properly heard him until now: quiet, exact, unbending as a surveyor's chain.

At noon the tribunal recessed. Through the lunch hour she sat on the cold bench with bread she could not eat, and a reporter from the far-off paper leaned over and asked her, gently, whether the nurse in the bandages was someone to her.

"He is the reason the numbers exist," she said, which was true, and insufficient, and she let it be both.

* * *

The afternoon session opened with a surprise that Osterling announced in his dry, unhurried way, as though surprising tribunals were a routine part of jurisprudence.

"The tribunal will hear additional testimony. A witness has been produced whose presence bears directly on the matter of the death rolls. Bring him forward."

The door at the back opened, and Elias Ashe walked into the chamber.

Rosalind had known he was alive for weeks now. She had found him in the mirror ward of the requisitioned asylum, hollow-eyed and half-starved, a man who flinched at footsteps and spoke of the massacre at the Marrow ford in a voice flattened by horror. She had nursed him through the worst nights. She had learned again how to be a sister to someone who had forgotten he had family left. None of it—none of it—had prepared her for seeing him upright, in a borrowed uniform two sizes too large, walking the length of that terrible room toward the dais while every head turned and every whisper died.

She stood up. She couldn't help it. The formality of the chamber demanded sitting, and she stood anyway, and for one suspended moment her brother met her eyes across the benches, and something passed between them that had no words in any language either of them spoke fluently anymore. Then he nodded—once, small, the nod of a man conserving everything—and continued to the witness chair, and Rosalind sat back down with her hands pressed flat against her knees so they would not shake.

"State your name and former unit," Osterling said.

"Elias Ashe. Seventh Rifles. Recorded as deceased on the fourteenth of June." He paused. "I am not deceased."

A sound went through the chamber—not a murmur this time but a single collective intake of breath, as if the whole room had been holding it since morning.

"Explain."

Elias explained. Rosalind had heard the account once, in the asylum, in fragments, and hearing it whole in front of the tribunal she understood why he had given it in fragments. The retreat at the ford. The company cut off. The order—relayed verbally, never written—that the wounded who could not march were to be left, and that any man who spoke of what happened next would answer to the colonel personally. What happened next was that the rear guard fired on their own stragglers rather than let them fall into enemy hands alive, because live prisoners would tell stories, and stories travel. Elias had survived because he fell into the reeds with a bullet through his shoulder and enough blood on him to be mistaken for dead by everyone, including, for a while, himself.

"I crawled out two days later," he said. "And I learned very quickly that the fastest way to die properly was to say I hadn't. My own death notice saved my life. A dead man asks no questions. So I stayed dead. Until my sister started counting."

Osterling's spectacles flashed as he turned toward the dais. "Colonel Brandt is named in the ledger before us in connection with the order of June the fourteenth. Colonel Brandt, you are present. Do you wish to respond?"

Brandt had attended the entire tribunal in full dress, silver at his collar, immaculate as a monument. He rose now, and Rosalind watched a man attempt to be granite.

"The tribunal will forgive me," Brandt said, "if I decline to validate the testimony of a deserter and a woman whose professional reputation rests on manufacturing discrepancies."

"My brother's grave is empty," Rosalind said, before she could stop herself, before any protocol could catch her. Her voice carried further than she intended; heads turned; the widow in crepe lifted her tear-streaked face. "You may inspect it. I have. There is no one in it, Colonel. Would you like to manufacture an explanation for that as well?"

Brandt looked at her. For a moment—one long, glittering moment—Rosalind saw the thing beneath the granite: not rage but calculation, the rapid silent arithmetic of a man discovering that his ledgers had finally been audited. Then he addressed the dais. "I ordered a tactical withdrawal. Losses were regrettably incurred. The confusion of retreat produces irregular paperwork. If errors were made, they were errors of the fog of war, and I will answer for them as such."

"Then answer for this," Osterling said mildly, and nodded to his clerk, who rose and opened the ledger—the actual ledger, Jonah's ledger, its boards swollen with damp, its stitching repaired with waxed thread—and began to read.

* * *

They read until dark.

That was not the plan; Osterling had intended, Rosalind later learned, to admit the ledger as evidence and summarize its contents. But the public benches would not permit summary. When the clerk read the first name—*Havel Brenner, private, seventh rifles, killed at the ford, carried a harmonica*—a soldier in the third row stood up and said, loudly, "Brenner taught me that harmonica. Say the rest of the entry."

So the clerk said the rest of the entry. And the next name, and the next. And when he faltered over the handwriting, Jonah was permitted—this was Osterling's one act of grace, delivered without expression—to come forward and read in his own hand's voice, and he read steadily, entry after entry, while the light failed and lamps were brought, and no one moved to adjourn.

Men stood up out of the benches as their brothers' names were called. Some saluted. Some wept openly. One old sergeant repeated each name after the reader, softly, like an echo learning to exist. The widow in black crepe wept throughout, and near dusk Rosalind realized she had been wrong that morning: the woman was crying for a name, some name, one of the hundred and forty-one, and had come not knowing which page held him, and stayed for all of them.

Rosalind sat through all of it. She counted, because counting was her prayer. Forty names. Eighty. A hundred. At entry one hundred and twelve, Kessler's brother—added by lamplight at Brandt's own table, at Kessler's desperate asking—was read into the record, and Rosalind thought of the orderly who had paid for that addition with his liberty, and hoped wherever he was held, someone was reading him the news.

At entry one hundred and thirty-nine, the clerk paused. Jonah did not.

Because Jonah read the final two himself.

"*Entry one hundred and forty,*" he said, and his voice, which had held level through the whole catalogue, changed—barely, a hairline fracture, but Rosalind heard it, and she saw that Osterling heard it, and half the room heard it. "*Ashe, Elias, private, seventh rifles. Reported dead at the ford. Alive as of this writing.*"

The silence that followed had a texture to it, dense as wool. A man officially dead, read aloud as alive, in the chamber of a tribunal—in front of the colonel who had erased him. Elias, seated at the witness chair, lifted his chin.

"*Entry one hundred and forty-one,*" Jonah read. "*Vane, Jonah. Nurse, field hospital, Marrow sector. Keeper of this record. Let my name stand beside theirs. If I am to be punished, let it be on a page where I can be counted.*"

He closed the ledger and set it down on the table with a soft final knock, like a door shutting on a house whose rooms were finally all occupied.

No one spoke. Then the old sergeant began it, low, and others took it up—not a cheer, nothing so vulgar as a cheer, but a murmured recitation, name after name spilling back through the benches, the soldiers repeating what they'd heard, passing the names hand to hand like a bucket line fighting a fire, until the whole chamber was quietly alight with them.

Osterling rapped once for order. He looked at Brandt for a long moment over the top of his flashing spectacles. "Colonel," he said, "the tribunal will convene privately regarding your conduct. You are dismissed from this chamber." And Brandt—granite to the last, or almost—gathered his gloves and his dignity and walked out past the benches, past the soldiers who did not rise for him, past Rosalind, who did not look away.

* * *

Later—it was fully night, and the clerks were stacking papers, and the reporters were gone to their telegraphs—Jonah found her on the steps outside, where the cold air tasted of coal smoke and snow that hadn't fallen yet.

"They'll want statements," he said. "From both of us. Days of them, probably. Weeks." He flexed his bandaged hands. "And the tribunal hasn't formally ruled on me. Forgery of documents isn't forgiven just because the forgery was true."

"I know," Rosalind said.

"You should go home, Rosalind. Back to the registry. Back to counting things that behave themselves."

"I've been offered a commission," she said, and watched him turn. "Provincial records reform. Someone has to rebuild the rolls—all of them, every parish, every regiment. Someone methodical." She smiled, and it cost her something to smile, and she paid it gladly. "Someone who asks one question too many."

"And you'd want—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way he handled instruments. "What would you want from me? Exactly."

Rosalind looked at him—at the bandaged knuckles, the tired eyes, the man who had written his own name last so it could be read aloud first among the pardoned, someday, if the tribunal found its courage. She thought about the marsh road, and the tollhouse, and a coffin that had carried truth instead of a body, and a brass compass she had already bought from a pawnbroker that morning, engraved with initials, waiting in her pocket for the right season.

"I want you to recover," she said. "Fully. Somewhere quiet. And then I want you to tell me every story behind every one of those one hundred and forty-one entries, because the record has the names, Jonah, but you have the men, and the province is going to need both." She drew her coat tighter against the cold. "There's a road east of the city where the marsh country begins. I'll be walking it in the spring. Counting on meeting someone there."

Jonah Vane, keeper of the ledger, looked at her for a long time. Then he nodded—once, small, the nod of a man conserving everything for the road ahead.

"In the spring, then," he said.
