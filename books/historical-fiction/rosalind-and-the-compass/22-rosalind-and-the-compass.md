## 22. Rosalind and the Compass

The marsh road began where the cobbles ended, and it began the way most honest things did—without ceremony.

Rosalind Ashe stood at that border between stone and mud on a morning in late spring, seven months after the tribunal, counting what there was to count: forty-one steps from the tollhouse wall to the first willow; three carts on the road since dawn, none of them military; one man approaching on foot with a soldier's stride he had never quite managed to shed.

She had known him at two hundred paces. She would have known him at a thousand. There was a way Jonah Vane carried himself that no pardon could alter—the weight distributed as if he were still holding something precious against his chest, some ledger of the world that only he could feel.

He stopped ten feet away. Old habit, she thought. Ten feet was far enough to be refused politely.

"You came," he said.

"You doubted it?"

"I doubted the road." He looked past her, down the long gray ribbon of causeway unspooling into the marsh country, where the water lay flat as poured tin and the reeds stood in ranks like men waiting to be counted. "I doubted whether you'd want to see this place again. Most people don't come back to the places where they nearly died."

"I'm not most people," Rosalind said. "I'm a census taker."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, but the ground a smile might grow from. Seven months ago, in the tribunal chamber, when the last name had been read aloud and the president of the commission had asked if anyone else wished to speak, Rosalind had risen and said, *Yes. One more entry.* And she had read out the name Jonah Vane, nurse, field hospital at Halven, keeper of the true rolls—and the room had gone quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when a thing long hidden is finally set down in the light.

His own name. On the final page all along. She still did not know when he had written it, or how long he had carried that knowledge alone, walking beside her for weeks with his death already recorded in ink an inch from his heart. She suspected she would ask him someday. She suspected he would answer honestly, which frightened her more than any lie would have.

"The tribunal sends its regards," she said now. "And its paperwork. You're formally reinstated to the medical service, effective the first of the month. There are men who want you running the veterans' hospital in the capital. There's a colonel's chair with your name stenciled on it, if you can believe such a thing exists."

"I can't."

"Neither can I. Which is why I'm telling you while we're standing somewhere with mud under our feet. Stenciled chairs belong to cities. This—" she nudged the marsh road with her boot— "this belongs to people who count."

Jonah set down the canvas bag he'd carried from the coach stop. He looked older than the war had made him, or rather, differently old—the hollowness in his cheeks filled in somewhat, the tremor in his hands gone quiet except when he spoke of Halven. The pardon had done that, she supposed. Or the reading of the names. For three days in the tribunal hall they had read aloud every entry in the ledger, all four hundred and eleven of them, and each time a name fell into that great vaulted silence, Rosalind had watched something leave Jonah's shoulders by grains.

Four hundred and eleven names. Elias Ashe had been the ninth. Private Kessler's brother the fifty-third. And the last—the four hundred and eleventh—had been spoken in Jonah's own voice, because Rosalind had asked him to be the one to say it.

*Vane, Jonah. Nurse. Present at the writing of these pages.*

"They're going to put up a stone," he said. "At Marrow Ford, by the bridge. Four hundred and eleven names cut into granite. They asked me to approve the lettering."

"What did you tell them?"

"That granite weathers." He turned his hat in his hands—a new hat, a civilian's soft-brimmed thing he wore like a borrowed coat. "That I'd rather they taught the children the names than carved them for the rain."

"And what did they say?"

"They said I was a difficult man." Now the smile came, small and crooked and entirely his. "Colonel Brandt used to say the same thing. From him it was an accusation. From them it sounded almost like a medal."

* * *

They walked. That was all the morning required of them—to walk, side by side, down the causeway into the marsh country where everything had changed and nothing had. The water had receded since autumn; the flooded fields were green now, stitched with cattle, and a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of an accountant.

Rosalind counted her steps without meaning to. She always would. It was the shape of her mind, the same shape that had once made a career of doors and hearths and heads of households, and later had made her dangerous to men who preferred that certain persons go unnumbered. She had learned, over seven months of testimony and grief and slow mending, that the counting was not a wound. It was a compass. It only ever hurt when she tried to make it point at something it couldn't find.

Elias was in the capital. That was the sentence she still tested each morning against her teeth, the way one tests a tooth that has been mended, half expecting it to ache. Elias was alive, and in the capital, learning to walk again in the asylum ward that no longer hid living men but healed them. He had stood before the tribunal in a borrowed uniform, gray-faced and halting, and told them about the massacre at the quarry in a voice that never rose above a murmur—and had brought four hundred men back from the erasure with that murmur. When it was done he had found her in the corridor and held her the way a man holds the last solid thing in a flood.

*You went looking for me,* he'd said into her hair.

*I went looking for your name,* she'd said. *You were a bonus.*

He laughed at that still, sometimes, in the evenings when she visited. A thin laugh, but real.

"He asks about you," she said aloud now, to Jonah. "Every week. *Has Vane decided yet? Has Vane taken the post?*"

"Does he."

"He says a man who writes the truth down ought to be given a bigger book to write it in."

Jonah was silent for a dozen paces. A rail skylark went up off the causeway, singing the way larks do—as if ascending were the whole of the argument.

"There was a time," he said at last, "when I would have wanted that. A bigger book. A department, clerks, authority. I used to dream about it in the hospital tent—some office where no dispatch could be altered without my countersignature." He shook his head slowly. "But I've seen what becomes of keepers. The ledger kept me, Rosalind, more than I ever kept it. Four years I served those names, and the price was—I couldn't serve the living ones. I let a boy die on the third night of the retreat because I was copying out a dead man's spelling. I've never told anyone that."

"You're telling me."

"I'm telling you." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw that this was the true gift he had brought to the marsh road—not the news, not the paperwork rolled in oilcloth in his bag, but this: the habit of confession, learned too late and practiced anyway. "So no. No stenciled chair. I'll take a ward bed instead, or a village practice, or whatever small thing lets me look at faces instead of pages. I spent enough of my life making the invisible count. I'd like to spend what's left counting the visible."

Rosalind nodded, and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were hot. She had not wept at the tribunal. She had not wept over Elias's page by firelight, though she remembered the night she had read it aloud and how Jonah had sat very still, letting her grief have the whole fire. But this—this ordinary sentence, *counting the visible*, spoken by a man walking in spring air with no soldiers within thirty miles—that undid her a little.

"Well," she said briskly, because feeling was a country she entered only with provisions, "then I have something for you, and it isn't paperwork."

* * *

She took it from inside her coat—an absurd place to carry anything in warm weather, but she had carried it that way since winter, against her ribs, the way one carries letters that matter. It was wrapped in a square of the gray cloth she'd once used for census records, tied with string.

Jonah unwrapped it carefully, the way he handled everything now, as if the world were a document that could be torn.

A brass compass.

It was old—the case worn to a dull gold at the edges, the hinge working on a whisper of oil—and the lid was engraved with two initials in a clerk's careful hand:

**E.A.**

"Elias sent it," Rosalind said. "It was Father's. Elias carried it through the whole war—it was in his pocket at the quarry, in the water, in the asylum, all of it. He says brass survives what flesh doesn't." She watched Jonah's thumb move over the engraving, and went on, quietly, because this part needed saying plainly: "He wanted you to have it. He said—you'll want to sit down for this, but there's nowhere to sit, so stand—he said, *Tell Vane he counted me back among the living before anyone else did. Tell him the ledger wasn't a book of the dead. It was the only map anybody drew of where we actually were.*"

Jonah did not speak. He opened the compass, and the needle swung, wavered, settled—north, steady north, indifferent and faithful as arithmetic.

"He also said," Rosalind continued, "that you'd try to refuse it. And that if you did, I was to remind you that in this family we do not accept refusals of objects that have survived worse than you have."

That startled a laugh out of him—a real one, brief and rough, gone almost before it arrived but leaving the air changed behind it. He closed the compass and held it a moment in both hands, and Rosalind saw his lips move around words he didn't send out loud. A thank-you, perhaps. Or a name.

"It points north," he said finally. "I'm told the marsh road doesn't."

"The marsh road points wherever you're brave enough to walk," Rosalind said. "That's what makes it a good road."

They walked on. Somewhere ahead the causeway curved out of sight behind a stand of alders, and beyond the alders, she knew from her own maps—she had drawn maps of everything now; the tribunal had commissioned her to rebuild the provincial registers, parish by parish, name by name, and she had accepted before the president finished asking—lay the village of Halven, rebuilt at its river crossing, its church re-roofed, its census lists corrected in her own hand. Forty-one survivors' households. Two hundred and six returned souls. Every one of them written down.

"Do you remember," Jonah said, "the first time you came to the hospital? With your little notebook and your terrible tea?"

"My tea was excellent. You were simply too busy falsifying government records to appreciate it."

"I was keeping *accurate* records. There's a difference, and history agrees with me." He glanced sideways at her, and there was the crooked smile again, easier now. "You asked one question too many that day. I've been grateful ever since."

"One question too many is how every true thing gets found," Rosalind said. "One question too few is how men get erased. I've made my choice about which mistake I can live with."

The alders came up on their left, and beneath them the ground firmed, and the road—no longer causeway now but common dirt, rutted by cart wheels, bordered by cow parsley gone white as paper—ran on toward Halven. Toward everything. Rosalind felt the day open in front of them the way a ledger opens: blank pages, ruled and waiting, patient with possibility.

Jonah stopped. He took out the compass again, opened it, held it flat on his palm between them.

"If it's pointing north," he said, "and Halven is west—"

"Then it's pointing at the capital, and the capital can wait."

"Can it?"

"For one afternoon?" She looked at him—this stubborn, careful, impossible man who had written his own death so that others might live on paper, and who had walked out of that sacrifice into a pardon he still hadn't learned how to wear. "Jonah Vane. I spent two years learning that the whole world is made of people someone decided not to count. I am not spending this afternoon being one of them."

The needle trembled once between them—north, north, faithful—and Jonah closed the lid over it with a click like a full stop, or like a door opening, which are often the same sound.

"Together, then," he said.

"Together," Rosalind agreed. "Counted."

And they walked west, away from the compass's sure north, down the road toward Halven—two names, entered at last in the record of the living, setting out to see what else the world had failed to write down.
