## 2. Fifteen Years of Silence

The fog had not lifted by nine o'clock, and Elodie Galloway was grateful for it. Fog was a kindness in a city like Bristol — it blurred faces, softened the hard geometry of the docks, and gave a man in a driver's coat license to look at nothing and no one. He sat at the reins of the Vane motorcar with his collar up and his cap low, waiting outside the Harrow shipping offices on Prince Street, and he let the fog do its work.

The parcel on the seat beside him was wrapped in brown paper and tied with string, addressed to Mr. Cedric Harrow in Rosalind Vane's upright, unyielding hand. A petition, most likely. Names gathered for names' sake. Miss Vane sent such things into the enemy's territory the way other women sent flowers to the sick — a gesture of civility that everyone understood to be something else entirely.

He was early. The Harrow office did not open its doors to tradesmen until half past, and a driver delivering for the Vane household was neither guest nor quite trade. So he waited, hands loose on the wheel, and watched the street assemble itself out of the mist: a coal cart grinding past with its driver hunched like a gargoyle; two clerks hurrying under one umbrella, their conversation a hiss of numbers; a boy of perhaps eight sweeping the pavement before the Harrow door with a broom worn down to a whisker.

Elodie knew that boy's walk. He had worn it himself once — the particular careful gait of a child who has learned that pavements are not his, that brooms can be taken away, that existence itself is a thing requiring permission.

*Fifteen years,* he thought. *Fifteen years since I was that boy.*

And because the fog made the street feel like a held breath, and because the parcel could wait twenty minutes without the world ending, he let the memory come. He had learned long ago that memory was like weather: you could not stop it arriving, but you could choose where to stand when it broke over you.

* * *

He had been born — if born was the right word for a thing that happened without witnesses — somewhere near the mudflats below the New Cut, in the winter of 1889 or thereabouts. His mother had been a laundress whose name he learned only secondhand, from a woman who remembered her at the washhouse: *Galloway, she was called. Pretty once. Coughed herself into the churchyard when your lad was six.* No first name survived. No photograph. Only the surname, which Elodie kept the way a man keeps a coat too thin for the season — not because it served him well, but because it was what he had.

From six onward, he had belonged to the docks the way rats and gulls belonged to them: usefully, invisibly, on sufferance. There were dozens like him. The harbor swallowed ships and spat out work, and work drew families, and families sometimes failed, and the children of the failed learned to live in the spaces between warehouses, in the lee of bollards, in the warm dark behind the rope-walks where tar barrels kept the frost off.

They had a society among themselves, the dock children. Rules. You did not steal from a man who might feed you later. You slept in shifts so someone always watched for the police. You never, ever told an adult anything true about another child. And above all — above every other law, written nowhere and enforced everywhere —

You did not learn to read.

Not because reading was forbidden. Nothing so dramatic as that. But because reading marked you. A child who read was a child who had been near books, and being near books meant being near the counting houses, the shipping manifests, the letters that passed between men who wore coats worth more than a dock family earned in a year. The other children would look at you differently. The grown men would notice you. And noticed was the one condition a dock orphan could not afford to be.

Elodie had broken that law at eight years old, and it had saved his life, and it had ended the only belonging he ever had, and both those facts remained true simultaneously forever, the way two timbers can be nailed together into a shape that should not stand but does.

It happened because of a pilot named Mr. Quill — no relation to his profession, though Elodie had appreciated the coincidence even then. Old Quill kept the tide tables and the harbor ledgers, and he had a habit of sitting on a crate by the Custom House steps at midday with bread and cheese and a newspaper, reading aloud to nobody in particular because his eyes were going and he liked the sound of the words anyway. The dock children orbited him for the crumbs. Elodie orbited him for the sounds.

There was music in it. That was the truth he had never managed to explain to anyone since. Not the meanings — meanings came later, slowly, painfully, like learning to see through water — but the *sounds*. The way *harbor* rolled and *ledger* clipped short. The way a sentence rose and fell like the tide itself, coming in and going out, carrying things.

Old Quill caught him mouthing along one afternoon — caught the shape of the boy's lips forming words the boy could not yet properly read — and instead of cuffing him or turning him in, the old man had simply moved over on the crate and said, "Well? Sit down, then, if you're going to listen. You may as well listen proper."

For two winters, that was Elodie's education: a crate by the Custom House steps, half a sandwich, and an old man's voice working through newspapers, almanacs, tide tables, and once — memorably, mysteriously — a novel about a man shipwrecked alone on an island, which had struck Elodie as less fiction than documentary.

"You've a head for it," Quill said once, tapping the newspaper against the boy's knee. "Better than mine at your age, I'd wager. Reading's a trade same as caulking or ropemaking. Only difference is, nobody pays for it direct."

"What's it pay, then?" Elodie had asked.

Quill considered this seriously, as he considered everything. "It pays in seeing," he said. "Most folk go through the world blind, boy. They see what's put in front of their noses and nothing else. A reader sees what's *between*. Sees the letter that isn't signed. Sees the sum that doesn't add. Sees what a man means instead of what he says." He had chewed his cheese thoughtfully. "Course, seeing's a dangerous trade too. World's built by folk who'd rather you didn't."

Elodie had been nine and had understood none of it except the danger, which he understood completely, because danger was the first language of the docks.

He was eleven when the silence saved his life.

It happened in the spring of 1900, the year of the great strike talk, when the harbor seethed like a pot with the lid rattling. Elodie had fallen into casual employment carrying messages for the tally clerks — small sums, small notes, nothing that required a signature — because a boy who read and wrote neatly was useful, and usefulness was the closest thing to security the docks offered. He carried a folded paper from a clerk in the grain office to a house on Queen Square, and he was quick about it, and he was quiet about it, and he read it.

He hadn't meant to. That was true, and it was also irrelevant; the fold was loose and the name at the top was visible and his eyes went to print the way his feet went to solid ground, without consultation. It was a note about shipments being diverted — about cargo listed as damaged that was not damaged — about certain men paid to look away. Small corruption, the ordinary kind, the kind that greased every port in England. But it named names, and one of the names was a man named Dreever, and Dreever was a tally clerk with broad shoulders and a temper, and three days later Dreever stopped Elodie in the shadow of a warehouse arch and asked him, pleasantly enough, whether he'd seen the note he'd carried. Whether he'd *read* it.

Every instinct Elodie owned screamed the answer. Eleven years old, cornered by a grown man with fists like mooring knots, asked a question with only one survivable answer, and the terrible joke of it was that the honest answer — *yes, sir, every word* — was the one that got boys thrown in the Floating Harbour with their pockets filled with stones.

"I can't read, sir," Elodie said. "Me ma never had me taught. She said book-learning was for them as could eat regular."

Dreever looked at him a long time. Long enough for the fog of fear to condense on the back of Elodie's neck. Then the man laughed — a short, satisfied bark — and cuffed him affectionately on the ear, and said, "Good lad. Can't be having readers about. Come here Saturday, I'll find ye a job shifting grain."

He shifted grain. He took the job. And he never again let a soul in the harbor know that he could read — not the tally clerks, not the other orphans, not Old Quill himself, whom he continued to visit until the old man died in the winter of 1903, still believing his pupil had learned nothing but sounds. At the funeral — such as it was; a curate, two dockhands, a boy — Elodie stood at the edge and listened to the vicar mispronounce half the service, and he grieved in silence, in the silence that was now his native country, and he thought: *This is the price. This is what it costs. You get to see everything, and you belong to nothing.*

He was fifteen when he left the docks entirely — walked up through Redcliffe with a bundle of clothes and a forged character reference he had written himself in the handwriting of a foreman who had died the year before (the dead made excellent referees; they could not contradict) — and he became, successively, a boot-boy in Clifton, a messenger for a solicitor who never suspected that the silent boy filing correspondence absorbed it all, and finally, at nineteen, a driver, because driving paid better than filing and offered the one commodity he valued above wages: a position from which a man could watch the whole city pass beneath him and remain, himself, unseen.

Then, in the autumn of 1908, he had answered an advertisement in the *Bristol Times*: *Lady requires steady, discreet man for motorcar. References essential.*

Discreet. The word had drawn him like a hook. He had manufactured references — three of them now, each belonging to a dead man — and presented himself at a tall narrow house in Clifton with a view over the suspension bridge, and a woman with grey-streaked hair and eyes like a clear cold morning had interviewed him herself, because Miss Rosalind Vane interviewed her own servants, which was unusual, and asked him questions no other employer had asked.

"Can you keep confidences?" she said.

"Yes, ma'am." True.

"Do you have opinions?"

He had hesitated. "I keep them private, ma'am."

Something had flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might anchor itself. "That," she said, "is the correct answer in this house, Mr. Galloway. Though I will warn you: I intend to make keeping private opinions very difficult. I hold that everyone has them, and that a household which pretends otherwise is lying to itself."

She had hired him. She had never once lied to herself, in all the years since. And he had driven her through four years of meetings and marches and midnight strategy sessions, reading every letter that crossed her desk when the chance allowed, memorizing and burning and forgetting, telling himself that this — this proximity without participation, this knowledge without ownership — was a life. Was enough of a life.

* * *

The fog thinned. The Harrow offices emerged from the white like a ship from a bank of sea-smoke: four storeys of honey-coloured stone, brass lettering polished by a man whose entire wage depended on that polish, a doorway wide enough to admit ambition. ELIAS HARROW & SONS, SHIPPING AND GENERAL CARRIAGE. The *and Sons* always struck Elodie as optimistic, since Elias Harrow had precisely one son, and the son was, by all accounts, a young man in a hurry.

Elodie stepped down, took up the parcel, and joined the current of clerks and messengers flowing through the doors.

Inside was warmth and varnish and money. The entrance hall smelled of beeswax and pipe tobacco and, beneath it, faintly, of the river — because no building on Prince Street ever entirely escaped the river, whatever the brass plates implied. A reception counter. A board listing arrivals and departures of vessels. Men moving with the unhurried certainty of people who had never once wondered whether they'd eat.

Elodie knew them. That was the strange, corrosive gift of his fifteen years: he knew everyone. He knew the shipping clerk Mr. Pellow drank at the Anchor on Fridays and wept after his third pint, quietly, facing the wall. He knew the second Harrow accountant kept a mistress in Bedminster and a second set of books, and that the two arrangements fed each other. He knew which junior partner beat his horse and which senior partner beat his wife, and which of the messengers was saving every shilling to bring his brother out of the Rhondda, and which of the office boys sold gossip to anyone who bought.

He knew all of it, the way Old Quill had promised he would — saw the between, the underneath, the unsaid — and none of it was his. Knowledge without belonging. Sight without standing. He was the best-read invisible man in Bristol, and if he vanished tomorrow, the sum total of his absence would be a note in some ledger: *driver, discharged, cause unknown.*

"Name and business?" The reception clerk — a new one, Elodie noted; the old one, Mr. Snell, had retired to Weston-super-Mare in September — looked up with the flat courtesy of a man guarding a door he did not own.

"Delivering for Miss Vane, Clifton. For Mr. Cedric Harrow, personal." Elodie set the parcel on the counter.

The clerk's pen paused. Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion exactly, but the wariness of a man newly employed in a house where he had already sensed currents. "Wait there, if you please."

Elodie waited. He was good at waiting; it was practically his profession. And while he waited he did what he always did, helplessly, involuntarily: he read. The board of vessel movements — the *Maid of Avon*, in from Cork; the *Cormorant* overdue, ten days overdue, and someone in the back offices would be chewing on that. A memorandum pinned by the counter, typed, unsigned: *Reminder — all correspondence re: harbor premises to be routed through the private office. NOT the general file.*

His eyes snagged on it and would not let go.

*Harbor premises.* The phrase was bland as porridge, and yet it had been typed with a care that suggested someone had chosen the blandness deliberately. Correspondence routed away from general files was correspondence somebody wanted unread. Elodie had spent fifteen years learning what unread correspondence meant, and it never meant anything innocent. Innocent letters travelled openly, like honest coin. Only secrets needed routing.

The clerk returned. "Mr. Harrow will take it himself," he said, and there was a slight emphasis on *himself* that told Elodie the instruction had come from above — that the heir of the house wanted his hands personally on whatever Miss Rosalind Vane was sending into his father's fortress.

And there he was, descending the stairs: Cedric Harrow, twenty-six or thereabouts, fair-haired, clean-featured, dressed in the kind of grey that cost more discreetly than black ever could. Handsome in the way of well-kept buildings. His eyes found the parcel first, then Elodie's face, and lingered — a half-second longer than courtesy required, a ledger-man's glance taking inventory.

"You're Vane's driver," he said. Not unkindly. Perfectly, precisely not unkindly.

"Yes, sir. Mr. Galloway."

"Galloway." Cedric Harrow repeated it the way a man repeats a figure he intends to check later. He untied the string with two economical pulls, unfolded the covering paper just far enough to confirm the contents, and retied it without reading further — a performance of discretion performed for an audience of one. "Give my regards to Miss Vane. Tell her I'll consider it." A pause, thin as ice. "Tell her I consider everything."

"I'll pass that on, sir."

"Good man." And Cedric Harrow climbed back up his staircase with the parcel under his arm, and the hall resumed its hum, and Elodie walked out into the thinning fog feeling the way he always felt leaving that building: as though he had brushed against machinery much larger than himself, still turning, still fed by things he could hear but not yet see.

At the motorcar he paused, hand on the wheel, and looked back once at the honey-coloured stone and the brass letters shining above the door.

*All correspondence re: harbor premises,* he thought. *Routed through the private office.*

Fifteen years of silence had taught him one thing above all others: when powerful men begin rerouting their paper, it is because the paper is about to become dangerous. And when the son of the house takes delivery of a suffragist's petition with his own hands and says *I consider everything* — that, too, is a sentence with something hidden between its lines.

He turned the crank, took up his seat, and drove back toward Clifton through the lifting mist, already composing in his mind the bland report he would give Miss Vane — *delivered, sir, receipt acknowledged* — while the rest of what he had seen filed itself away in the deep, orderly, lonely archive of his head, beside fifteen years of other things no one knew he carried.

He did not yet know why the phrase *harbor premises* would matter. He would know soon.
