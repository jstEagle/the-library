## 3. The Lady in the Motorcar

Rosalind Vane had learned to rehearse her speeches in moving motorcars for the same reason she had learned to sleep on trains and think through headaches: because the world of 1912 did not grant a woman a quiet room simply because she required one. The motorcar was hers—the only room in England that was entirely hers, in fact, if one did not count the driver's seat, which belonged to Elodie Galloway, and the road, which belonged to everyone, and the morning fog off the Avon, which belonged to no one at all.

She sat in the back with her notes on her knee and the little brass lamp clipped to the window frame throwing its small yellow circle over them. The car moved along the quayside road at a decorous pace, wheels hissing on wet cobbles, and beyond the glass Bristol assembled itself out of the mist in layers: first the black ribs of cranes, then the masts, then the warehouses stacked like grey books, then the gulls hanging motionless in the air as though pinned there by someone patient.

"The question before us," she said, "is not whether women are capable of citizenship. The question is whether this country can afford to go on pretending we are not."

She heard it come out slightly wrong. Too much like a lecture, not enough like a fire. She crossed out *capable* and wrote *fit* above it, then crossed that out too—*fit* sounded eugenic, and Mrs. Pankhurst's people were touchy about vocabulary just now—and settled on *qualified*, which was dull but safe.

Through the open window between herself and the front seat, she could see Elodie's hands on the wheel.

She had made a small study of those hands over two years, in the way that a woman who is watched constantly learns to do her watching sideways, through mirrors and window-glass and the reflection in a teapot. They were broad hands, dockworker's hands, scarred across the knuckles in a pattern she had once privately catalogued—a rope burn, she'd decided, and something older, something from before he came to her household with three references that all turned out, when she'd written to inquire, to be dead men. She had hired him anyway. She had never told him she knew about the references. It was one of the many things they did not say to each other, and the catalogue of unsaid things had grown so long over the years that it had begun to feel less like an omission and more like a language—one they alone spoke, fluently, wordlessly, every day.

His thumbs rested along the wheel's rim. He changed gear without looking down. When a coal cart swung out from the tobacco warehouse without signalling, his right hand went to the horn and his left to the brake in a single motion so economical it looked almost lazy, and the cart driver never knew how close he'd come, and Rosalind's pen had paused mid-word without her permission.

"Qualified," she tried again. "The question before us is not whether women are qualified for citizenship."

Better. Duller, but better. Miss Hartree would approve; Miss Hartree approved of everything dull. It was one of the reasons Rosalind kept her, and one of the reasons Rosalind sometimes wanted to put her in the river.

The fog thickened near the drawbridge. Elodie slowed without being asked—he always slowed there, always, rain or shine or committee member in the back seat tapping her watch—and she found herself watching the line of his shoulders instead of her notes. He wore his uniform cap pulled low and his collar buttoned to the throat, and he sat the way he did everything: contained, watchful, occupying exactly as much space as the job required and not one inch more. She had known generals who took up more room breathing.

There was a particular quality to being driven by him that she had never managed to articulate even to herself, and she was a woman who articulated things for a living. Other drivers drove. Elodie *read* the road. She had caught herself thinking of it in those terms and had been quietly alarmed by the thought, because reading was her trade and she resented sharing it.

"Turn up past the Exchange, please," she said. "I want to see how the crowd sets itself before I have to stand in front of it."

"Yes, miss." His voice was low and even, pitched precisely for obedience and nothing else. Two years, and she had never heard him use more words than a task required.

Except once.

*Once*, eighteen months ago, driving her home from a meeting that had gone badly—a brick through the window, a policeman who laughed—she had been sitting rigid in the back with broken glass dusting her shoulder like sugar, and he had said, without turning around, "You didn't flinch."

And she had said, "Of course I flinched. I'm not a fool."

And he had said, "No. But you finished your sentence first."

She had sat with that the whole way home. *You finished your sentence first.* No one else had noticed. Her own mother hadn't noticed. And she had understood, in the way one understands a trap closing gently, that the man in front of her saw everything—that her performances, so convincing to audiences and allies and enemies alike, were transparent to him, and had perhaps been transparent since the day he arrived—and that he had said nothing about it, ever, except that once, and only to tell her that what she feared looked like weakness had read, to at least one observer, as strength.

It should have frightened her. It did frighten her. And she had kept him anyway, raised his wages twice without being asked, and told herself it was because he was the best driver in Bristol, which happened also to be true.

* * *

The crowd was gathering in the square outside the Exchange, as crowds did for her now, and Rosalind watched them form with the professional interest of a woman who had spent fifteen years learning how human beings arrange themselves when they are undecided. Shop girls in twos and threes, arms linked. A knot of dockers at the edge who might be hostile or might be curious—you couldn't tell until you started speaking, and sometimes not even then. Clerks drifting past with newspapers folded to look casual. Two constables, bored. A woman selling chestnuts who had, Rosalind happened to know, walked eleven miles last month to hear her speak in Bath and pretended in Bristol not to know her at all.

"They'll fill in from the north side once the church lets out," she murmured, mostly to herself.

"Aye," said Elodie, and she realized he'd been doing the same arithmetic.

That was the other thing. That was the thing she could not put into any speech and would not put into any diary. She had spent her life in rooms where men explained things to her slowly, condescendingly, redundantly, and she had built her whole public self out of patience with that. And here was a man who had never once explained anything to her, because he had never once needed to. She would say six words about crowd flow and he would complete the sentence, the paragraph, the argument. She had tested him once—idly, cruelly, the way one tests anything one is afraid of—by wondering aloud whether the Harrow shipping family would dare interfere with the harbor meetings, and he had answered, after a silence of exactly the length a careful man allows himself, "They wouldn't need to interfere. They own half the ground you'd stand on."

He had said it flatly, like a weather report. But she had seen his eyes in the rearview mirror, and they had stayed on the road while saying it, and something in the set of his mouth had suggested a man choosing his words the way other men chose their footing on ice.

She had thought about that answer for a week. She had thought about it again during the Harrow subscription dinner, watching Cedric Harrow laugh at his own jokes across the table, and again when the harbor meetings began drawing larger crowds, and again, lately, with an uneasiness she could not source, like smoke smelled two rooms away.

"You've been quiet," she said now, as the car looped back toward the square. "Quieter than usual."

"I'm always quiet, miss."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," he agreed, and said nothing further, and the silence between them was so perfectly weighted—not sullen, not evasive, just *full*—that she nearly laughed aloud at the sheer craftsmanship of it. He could make silence do work that most men needed a full paragraph for.

She looked down at her notes. *The question before us is not whether women are qualified for citizenship.* She thought of the shop girls linking arms out there, of the chestnut woman's eleven miles, of Thomas Idris's workshop full of printers working by lamplight on pamphlets that bore her name without her knowledge until Thomas had sheepishly shown her one and she had read her own sentences reproduced in cheap ink by men who could lose their livelihoods for it, and she felt the old vertigo of responsibility, the weight of other people's courage banked against her own.

"Elodie," she said.

"Miss."

"When I'm speaking today. If anything goes wrong—if there's trouble—" She stopped. She had rehearsed this instruction in her head a dozen times and it still came out wrong, still came out sounding like fear, which she refused to perform in front of him of all people.

"If there's trouble," he said evenly, "you'll finish your sentence."

She stared at the back of his head. Through the gap between cap brim and collar she could see the beginning of a scar she had never asked about, running down into shadow.

"That's not what I was going to say."

"It's what you meant."

It was, infuriatingly, exactly what she meant. She wanted to say: *if there's trouble, drive. Don't wait for me, don't be a hero, get yourself clear, because you are worth more to me behind that wheel than any dozen speeches*—and she could not say it, because it was true, and because truth spoken plainly between mistress and servant curdles into something neither of them can carry, and because some part of her, some part she kept locked below the waterline like contraband, knew that he already knew that too.

Instead she said, "Very well," in the voice she used for cabinet ministers, and saw his shoulders move—once, briefly—in what she was almost certain was suppressed amusement.

* * *

He parked at the corner where she'd asked, engine ticking as it cooled, and got out to open her door, and for a moment they stood close enough that she could smell engine oil and coal smoke and, beneath both, something clean like soap, and the crowd's murmur rose around them like water coming to a boil, and someone shouted her name, and the performance resumed—her chin up, her smile calibrated, her hand brief and cool on his sleeve as she stepped down, a touch that meant *thank you* and *nothing else* and *everything else*, all at the volume of a fingertip.

She spoke for forty minutes. The crowd filled in from the north side as predicted; the dockers stayed curious rather than hostile; the constables remained bored; the chestnut woman sold chestnuts and did not look at her once, which was its own kind of salute. Somewhere in the middle of the speech—*the question is not whether we are qualified, but whether this nation can survive the waste of us*—she lost her place for half a second because she glanced toward the motorcar and saw Elodie standing beside it, arms folded, watching her, and his face wore an expression she had never seen him wear indoors or on duty or anywhere a face could be accused of meaning anything.

It was pride. Plain, unguarded, unmistakable pride, worn as openly as a man wears a Sunday coat, and gone the instant he noticed her noticing, folded away and buttoned under, and she finished her speech with her heart beating strangely and her voice steadier than it had been all morning.

Afterward, walking back to the car through the thinning crowd, she made herself compose her face into its usual administrative calm, because whatever lived between the two of them could not survive daylight or acknowledgment or a single honest sentence, and she was a woman who had chosen her cause over every comfort, and she meant to keep choosing it.

But as she settled onto the seat and gathered her notes, she saw, tucked into the fold of her papers where no one could have put it but him, a folded slip of paper. She opened it with her thumb, casually, the way one opens a bus ticket.

In a clerk's careful hand, five words:

*Watchers on the quay tonight.*

She looked up. Elodie was already in his seat, facing forward, hands on the wheel, posture perfect, expression empty, a man who was nobody at all.

The engine turned over. Rosalind slid the note into her glove, and said nothing, and wondered—with the first true coldness of the season settling into her chest—who in Bristol had watchers, and why her driver thought she ought to know about them, and what else he knew that he was choosing, for reasons of his own, to hand her one folded slip at a time.
