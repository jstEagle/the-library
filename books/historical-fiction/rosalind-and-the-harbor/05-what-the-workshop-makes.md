## 5. What the Workshop Makes

The tide came in at six, and with it the smell of tar and wet rope that Thomas Idris had breathed for thirty-one years, since the morning his father first set him to scraping barnacles off a punt no bigger than a coffin. He stood now in the doorway of the harbor workshops with his tea going cold in one hand and watched the water climb the pilings, and he thought—not for the first time—that there was no honester hour in Bristol than this one. The hour before men arrived. Before the day's small deceits began: the prices quoted too high, the hours logged too low, the smiles given to gentlemen whose fathers had broken strikes. Just him and the harbor and the gulls arguing over something dead on the mudflats.

Behind him, under the tarpaulins at the back of the long shed, sat four hundred pounds of printing press.

It was a Heidelberg, secondhand, bought off a bankrupt jobber in Cardiff three winters past, and by daylight it slept beneath sailcloth and coils of rope as innocent as any other stored gear. By night it woke. By night the workshop's two back rooms filled with men who smelled of engine oil instead of brine, and the Heidelberg clattered out its argument in ten-point type until two in the morning—*The Harbor Voice*, eight pages, five hundred copies, sometimes eight hundred when the docks were restless. Thomas had learned the machine the way he'd learned boats: by breaking it twice and being ashamed enough never to break it again.

He drank his tea and looked at the water and did not think about the press, because thinking about it in daylight was how men got careless. But the thought was always there, the way a splinter is always there under the skin. You didn't look at it. You just knew which motions hurt.

The first of his crew came up the slipway at half past—Dai Llewellyn, young, bow-legged from years in a boat too small for him, carrying a bag of hot rolls from the woman on Prince Street who asked no questions about anything except whether you wanted butter.

"Morning, Mr. Idris."

"Tom," Thomas said, as he said every morning. "How many times."

"Force of habit." Dai handed him a roll. "Butter?"

"Go on then."

They ate standing up, watching the fog lift off the water in rags. It was March still, though it couldn't make up its mind, and the light over the harbor was the color of pewter gone soft. Down the row, the other workshops were opening—the chandlery, the spar-maker, old Hobbes's sail loft with its windows like yellow teeth in the grey. The whole street smelled of pitch and canvas and frying onions from somewhere unseen, and Thomas felt the familiar tightening in his chest that was either pride or dread or both braided together, because every one of these buildings lived on Harrow shipping contracts, and every one of them would starve inside a season if Cedric Harrow decided they should.

"Press needs a new roller," Dai said, mouth full. "Or it will soon. There's a ridge coming up on the near side."

"You'd know that how? You're not supposed to be near it."

"I'm not supposed to be near anything, Mr.—Tom. I'm not supposed to be anywhere. And yet here we all are."

Thomas snorted. The boy was twenty-three and already had the union man's disease: an answer for everything and a hunger for the argument itself. He'd be a good one, in time, if the work or the police or the Harrows didn't eat him first. Thomas had buried enough good ones to know the odds ran long.

* * *

By seven the shed was full and loud. The *Maid of Frome*, a forty-foot ketch, was hauled out on the ways with her planks opened like a surgeon's patient, and half the crew swarmed her while the other half took the repair queue: a pilot cutter with sprung frames, a barge's windlass, eleven fenders to recork, and a dinghy belonging to a gentleman from Clifton that Thomas had accepted only because the gentleman paid triple and never asked what else went on behind the tarps.

The work was good work. That was the thing people outside the harbor never understood—the pride of it, the plain arithmetic of a broken thing made whole. When the *Maid of Frome*'s owner, a fruit merchant with hands soft as bread, came down to inspect her and found her seams caulked tight and her spars re-tarred, he'd gripped Thomas's hand and called it a miracle, and Thomas had wanted to tell him: no miracle, mister, just nine men and their fathers' knowledge, passed down hand to hand like a flame carried through wind. Nobody wrote songs about caulking. But caulking kept Bristol floating.

And the pamphlets—well. The pamphlets kept something else floating.

At midmorning the gentleman's driver arrived to collect the dinghy, and this was where Thomas's day tilted, as it did every few weeks, toward the thing he could not say aloud to anyone but the men behind the tarps.

The Vane motorcar stood at the top of the slipway, gleaming black even under harbor grime, absurd among the carts and barrows like a swan moored among trawlers. And beside it, waiting with his cap in his hands and his eyes doing none of the waiting, stood Miss Vane's driver.

Elodie, the household called him. Though Thomas had heard the name spoken once and thought: *that's a name someone gave him, not one anyone asked him for.*

He came down the slipway now, unhurried, and Thomas met him at the door of the shed, and the two men shook hands—which was itself the strange part, the part Thomas had noticed the first time and never stopped noticing. Servants didn't shake hands. Servants looked at your boots or past your shoulder or through you, whichever was furthest away. This one looked you in the eye. Had done from the very first visit, two years ago, when he'd come to arrange the collection schedule and Thomas had caught him studying the workshop the way a man studies a room he might someday live in.

"Morning, Tom."

"Morning. She's ready. Paid for her own repairs, that dinghy—more than I can say for some of my customers."

"She's a fine little boat."

"Her owner's a fool, but the boat's honest." Thomas jerked his head toward the office. "Come in a minute. Tea's fresh."

"It's all right—"

"It's all right nothing. Get in here before Dai starts asking you questions. He's been reading too many pamphlets and thinks everyone wants to hear his opinions."

The driver's mouth twitched—the almost-smile Thomas had catalogued along with everything else about him—and he stepped inside, into the smell of oakum and paint, and took the proffered mug without fuss.

They talked boat talk for ten minutes. Caulking compound prices, the quality of Swedish pine versus American, the rumor that the council meant to raise the mooring fees again come April. Ordinary talk. Safe talk. And underneath it, the way current runs under calm water, the other conversation continued: the glance Elodie let fall toward the tarped corner, brief as a blink; the pause before he answered when Thomas mentioned the festival committee; the careful nothing in his face when a Harrow lighter passed the window on the tide.

Two years of these visits. Thomas knew the pattern better than the man probably knew himself. He came, he collected, he drank tea standing, he left. He never stayed longer than propriety allowed. He never asked about anything he wasn't supposed to ask about. And he never—not once, not ever—looked at the harbor men the way the Vane house's last driver had looked at them, which was the way a man looks at furniture.

"Heard you had trouble up the road," Thomas said casually, refilling the mug. "Watchman at the coal yard says there's been gentlemen walking the lane after dark. Taking notes."

Something moved behind the driver's eyes—quick, controlled, gone. "There's often gentlemen walking after dark. It's a free city."

"Mm. Free for some." Thomas set down the kettle and studied him openly now, because he'd earned that much and more. "You know what we do here. Daylight side of it."

"I repair boats, sir, and mind my own affairs."

"That's exactly what I mean." Thomas leaned against the bench, arms folded. "Two years you've been coming here, and you've seen the tarps, you've seen the ink stains on Dai's cuffs, you've heard the press if you've come late enough—and you have come late enough, twice, don't think I didn't notice—and you have said *nothing*. Not to me, not to your mistress, not to anyone. Now I'll ask you plain, and you can lie to me and I'll believe the lie because it's well made: are you a friend to this place, or are you just a man who keeps his head down?"

The question hung between them. Outside, a gull screamed and the tide turned with its soft vast sigh, and somewhere down the row Hobbes's sail loft clattered open its doors.

The driver looked into his tea. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and very level.

"When I was a boy," he said, "I slept under the north quay stairs. Winter of 'ninety-seven. I know what happens to places like this when the owners decide they want them gone. So I suppose I keep my head down the way a man keeps his head down in weather—because he means to still be standing after it passes."

Thomas felt something in his chest loosen and settle, the way rigging settles after a storm. "Under the quay stairs," he repeated. "Christ. How old?"

"Seven, maybe. Nobody counted."

"And now you drive a motorcar for a suffragist lady and drink tea in a union workshop, and nobody knows which side of the wall you belong to."

"No, sir," the driver said, and for just a moment the mask slipped entirely, and underneath it was something so young and so tired that Thomas had to look away. "Neither side. That's rather the point of me."

"Well." Thomas cleared his throat roughly. "Well. For what it's worth—and I know what it's worth, I've been worth nothing to powerful men my whole life—for what it's worth, you've got a standing invitation here. Not to the night work. I wouldn't ask that of you; it'd be a death warrant wearing a nice coat. But the tea. The rolls. A seat by the stove when it's cold. Any time your mistress's errands bring you down, you're not a servant in this building. You're a guest."

The driver was silent a moment. Then: "Thank you, Tom."

"Don't thank me. Thank yourself for looking men in the eye. There's not five men in this city above stairs who do it."

* * *

He loaded the dinghy onto the car's rack with Dai and two others, and the driver helped—actually helped, sleeves rolled, hauling with the best of them, and Thomas watched Dai's face change from curiosity to something like respect in the space of one shared weight.

When it was lashed fast, the driver pulled on his gloves and paused, one foot on the running board.

"Tom. The watchman at the coal yard—the gentlemen taking notes. Which end of the lane?"

"Why?" Thomas asked, too quickly.

"No reason. Habit of mind. I like to know where things are." And he smiled the polite smile, the carriage smile, and got in, and the motorcar coughed and rolled away up the road, leaving diesel-adjacent smoke and a silence behind it.

Dai appeared at Thomas's shoulder. "What did he say to you in there?"

"Boat talk."

"What did he *say*, though?"

Thomas watched the car shrink up the hill toward Clifton, and he thought about the boy under the quay stairs, and the eyes that read a room like a ledger, and the way the man had said *neither side* as though it cost him blood to say it.

"He said," Thomas answered slowly, "that he means to still be standing when the weather passes."

"And do you believe him?"

Thomas picked up his mug and found it cold, and drank it anyway, the way his father had taught him—waste nothing, trust the harbor, and hold your friends close enough to feel their pulse.

"I believe," he said, "that man knows more than he tells. And I believe he told me the truest thing he knows." He turned back to the shed, to the tarps, to the sleeping press and the waking day. "Now come on. The *Maid of Frome* won't caulk herself, and neither will the truth. We've got a run to print Thursday, and I want that roller changed tonight."

They went in together, and behind them the tide rose over the pilings, patient and certain, carrying on its surface the whole floating city—every hull, every secret, every name—toward whatever the evening would bring.
