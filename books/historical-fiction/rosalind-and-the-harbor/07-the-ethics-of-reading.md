## 7. The Ethics of Reading

The fire in Elodie Galloway's grate had burned down to a red eye by the time he finally sat down to think about what he had done.

Not what he knew. What he had *done*. The distinction mattered, and he was a man who had learned that distinctions were the difference between sleeping at night and lying awake listening for boots on stairs. Knowing something, in his experience, was weightless. It sat in the skull like a stone in a pocket—you felt it there, you could take it out and turn it over, but it committed you to nothing. Doing was different. Doing left marks. Doing was the thing the world could see.

And tonight, for the first time in fifteen years, Elodie had *done* something. He had taken a sheet of Harrow notepaper, laid it flat on his narrow bed beside a guttering candle, and copied every word of it onto a page torn from the back of an old delivery ledger. His hand had shaken so badly on the third line that he'd had to stop and press his palm flat against the mattress until it steadied. Then he had finished. Then he had folded his copy into a square no bigger than a stamp and sewn it into the lining of his second-best coat, the one he wore on Sundays and to funerals.

Now the original lay on the hearthstone in front of him, and the fire's red eye waited to take it back into nothing.

He did not pick it up immediately. He sat instead with his elbows on his knees and his chin in his hands, and he let himself look honestly—at last, without flinching—at the arithmetic of his own life.

Fifteen years ago, a boy crouched behind barrels on Prince Street wharf had learned the first lesson of survival: *what you don't know can't be wrung out of you.* He had watched men who knew things die of knowing them. Old Cobb the tally-clerk, who saw a crate of rifles going aboard a ship bound for a war the government swore didn't exist—dead within the month, drowned in six inches of water, and everyone nodding as though drowning in six inches were the most natural thing a man could do. The woman in the lodging house who read other people's post because she was quick and curious—turned out of Bristol with a week's warning and never heard of again. Knowledge, in the harbor's economy, was currency, and the poor who held it were robbed rather than enriched. So the boy had made himself a vow: learn everything, hold everything, *say* nothing. Be the water that carries the boat but is never seen to move.

It had worked. God help him, it had worked beautifully. He was alive. He was fed. He slept under a roof that belonged to the Vane household and drove one of the finest motorcars in the city, and not one soul in Bristol—not Rosalind herself, not Thomas Idris with his warm handshake, not the solicitors whose letters passed through his hands like blood through a heart—knew that the quiet driver with the careful face could read a letter through the paper, almost, just from the way it was sealed.

*Almost* was the word that undid him now. Almost. Because three days ago a letter had arrived already opened and resealed, and he had read it, and the reading had been the easy part. Anyone could have read it. A child could have read it. The words were right there on the page in a lawyer's tidy copperplate: *clearance of the harbor workshops*, *discretion assured*, and then—the word that had crawled up his arm like cold water when he found it—*fire*.

Fire. In a business letter. As though buildings burned themselves, as though the harbor festival came with a season of conflagrations built into the calendar. And beneath the fire, in the same tidy copperplate, the phrase that had turned his hands to ice: *the Vane woman will require handling.*

He picked up the letter now. The paper was heavy, cream-colored, warm from his pocket where he'd carried it around all day like a coal he couldn't put down. He held it over the hearth and watched the corner begin to brown, and he thought: *this is the moment. Burn it and I'm still invisible. Keep it and I am a participant.*

That was the lie he told himself, anyway, and he was honest enough to know it was a lie even as he thought it. Because invisibility had ended three days ago. It had ended the moment he understood what the words meant. A man who knows a house will burn, and says nothing, is not a bystander to the burning. A man who knows a woman will be destroyed, and holds his tongue to keep his own skin whole, has already chosen a side—he has chosen the side of the fire. Neutrality, he understood suddenly, with the clarity of a man seeing his own face in dark glass, was a fiction. It had always been a fiction. The harbor taught that lesson a hundred times and he had refused to learn it: the man who stands in the crowd and watches the press-gang take a sailor is not neutral. The crowd is part of the machinery. Silence is oil in the gears.

The corner caught. Flame ran up the edge of the cream paper, and the copperplate curled and blackened, and Elodie watched *the Vane woman will require handling* become smoke, and he felt—nothing. That was the terrible part. No relief, no absolution. The words were gone from the paper but they lived in him now, printed deeper than ink, and burning the original changed nothing except the evidence.

But that, of course, was exactly why he had copied it first.

He stood abruptly, restless, and went to the window. Below, the lane was quiet; the fog had come off the river in the evening and hung between the houses like gauze over a wound. Somewhere down toward the water a ship's bell rang the hour, muffled and patient. And beyond the warehouses, past the cranes standing against the sky like gallows, the workshops hunched along the quay with their lamps lit—Thomas Idris's kingdom, where boats were mended by day and truth was printed by night, where children did their homework on upturned crates among the wood shavings and nobody thought anything of it.

Elodie had been watching that place for two years. Watching, cataloguing, admiring—from the safe remove of the driver's seat, from the doorway, from the far side of the counter where a man's eyes might meet yours but his life never would. He knew the foreman's laugh and the names of his daughters. He knew which press was the old one that jammed and which apprentice had the gift for type. He knew all of it the way he knew everything: thoroughly, silently, from outside.

And the Harrows meant to burn it. And the Harrows meant to destroy Rosalind Vane while the embers were still warm, so that the movement would lose its voice and its money and its spine all in one season, and the shipping interest could go on clearing the harbor of everyone who asked inconvenient questions about wages and hours and whether human beings were cargo.

*Rosalind.*

He said her name inside his head the way he permitted himself nothing else—not aloud, never aloud, only in the sealed room behind his eyes where he kept the things that were his. Miss Vane to the committee. Madam to the servants. Rosalind only here, in the dark, where it cost nothing and bought less. She paid him a wage and trusted him with her schedule and her correspondence and her safety on the road, and she did not know—and must never know—that her driver memorized her letters, or that he had loved her since the wet Tuesday in November when she had climbed out of the motorcar in the rain, given her umbrella away to a dockworker's wife, and walked into the meeting hall soaked to the skin with her chin up as though rain were a kind of applause.

If he told her—if he broke fifteen years of silence and laid the Harrows' letter before her—what then?

He tried to imagine the scene honestly, without dressing it in hope. Her face going carefully blank, the way clever faces do when they are calculating. The questions: *How do you know this? Why were you reading my correspondence? Who gave you leave?* And beneath the questions, unspoken but louder than any of them: *my driver reads my letters.* The intimacy he treasured would curdle into exposure. She would see him, yes—but she would see him as a violation, a spy in the house, a servant who had presumed. And worse: she would be *obliged*. Obliged to act, obliged to choose, obliged to weigh the workshop families against the bill against her committee's timetable, and whatever she chose, she would have to choose it knowing her own servant had forced the moment upon her. He would not be delivering her information. He would be handing her a burden wrapped in his own guilt.

And if he said nothing—

If he said nothing, the workshops would burn. Thomas Idris would perhaps die at his presses, or live scarred, and the membership rolls and the pamphlets and the children's homework crates would go up in the same orange light, and the harbor would watch, and the inquiry would pronounce some gentle fiction about electrical wiring, and the Harrows would pour their tea the next morning in a dining room that smelled of nothing worse than toast. And Rosalind would be handled. Disgraced by forged letters, ruined in the papers, broken publicly enough that her cause would stagger—and Elodie would stand in the gallery of history with his hands clean and his knowledge intact, the most complicit innocent in Bristol.

He pressed his forehead against the cold glass. Behind him the fire ticked and settled. On the hearthstone the last corner of the Harrow letter glowed, curled, and went out, leaving a scrap of ash shaped like nothing at all.

There was, he realized, a middle road. There always was, for men like him. Not telling Rosalind—never telling Rosalind—but telling *someone*. Anonymously. A note. A warning slipped to Thomas Idris, unsigned, untraceable, deniable. *Check your premises at night.* Four words. Enough to put a man on his guard, not enough to hang anyone—including himself.

It was cowardice dressed as prudence, and he knew that too. But it was also the maximum a man could risk and still remain in the world, and Elodie had spent fifteen years learning the exact width of that margin. A full confession might save everything or lose everything, and the odds were unknowable. A note was small, and small things survived.

Yet even as he composed the note in his mind—even as he weighed the phrasing, the handwriting (not his own; he would print it, block letters, the way a child writes, the way no one writes)—he felt the floor of his old life give slightly beneath him, the way ice gives before it goes. Because the rule had been *know everything, say nothing*, and tomorrow morning he intended to break half of it, and rules once broken do not mend. The note would make him a participant as surely as the copy in his coat lining did. Anonymous mercy was still mercy; anonymous warning was still warning; and somewhere downstream of this night, someone would act because of him, and the current would run backward eventually, the way currents in harbors always do.

He took the copy from his coat and looked at it one more time under the lamp. His own handwriting, hurried but legible, every damning word preserved: the clearance, the discretion, the fire, the handling of the Vane woman. Evidence. Ammunition. A debt he had no idea yet how he would pay.

Where to hide it? Not the house—houses were searched, and the Vane house had visitors of a kind he did not trust. Not on his person—a body could be stopped, turned out, gone through. It wanted a place that belonged to everyone and therefore to no one, a place so official, so dull, so permanent that no conspirator would ever think to look there. He turned the problem over as he blew out the candle, and the harbor answered him the way it had answered all his life, murmuring in the dark below the window: *the ledgers, boy. Nobody burns a ledger.*

Tomorrow. Tomorrow there was a delivery to make, and a route to drive, and somewhere along the quay a foreman who deserved four words in block capitals. Tonight there was only the fire dying in the grate and the stone in his pocket that had become a keel, a rudder, a whole submerged structure bearing his weight.

He had believed himself a neutral man for fifteen years. Lying in the dark, listening to the fog thicken against the windows, Elodie Galloway finally admitted the truth he had outrun since childhood: there was no such thing as a man who merely watched. The harbor kept its accounts in full, and it had just opened a new page, and his name was written on it whether he spoke it or not.

Down the hill, in the lamplit workshops, Thomas Idris banked his presses for the night and checked the door bolt twice, for no reason he could name—only a driver's eyes on him across the yard that afternoon, holding something the driver hadn't said.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of coal smoke. Elodie drove Rosalind to her committee meeting with the letter-copy sewn against his ribs and the anonymous note in his breast pocket, and he performed the role of driver so well that he frightened himself with how easily it fit—hands steady on the wheel, face arranged into its usual attentive blankness, answering her good-morning with the two syllables that were expected and no more.

She looked tired, he thought. There were shadows under her eyes that powder couldn't entirely lift, and when she reviewed her speech notes against her knee, her lips moved faintly, rehearsing. Once, at a junction, she glanced up at him in the driving mirror, and for a moment neither of them looked away, and Elodie felt the note in his pocket grow hot as a brand.

"You've been quiet lately," she said at last, lightly. "Quieter than usual, I mean."

"The roads want quiet, madam. Fog on the cut, ice in the shade."

"Mm." She returned to her notes, but he felt her attention linger on him a moment longer than habit allowed, and he understood with a lurch that he was not, perhaps, quite as invisible as he had spent a lifetime believing. Women like Rosalind Vane noticed things for a living. It was possible—God, it was possible—that he had never been hidden from her at all, only tolerated, only *awaited*.

He dropped her at the hall on Queen Square, watched her go up the steps with her papers clutched like weapons, and then drove alone down toward the harbor with the engine ticking and his heart going harder than the engine.

The workshops were loud with morning work when he reached the quay—the ring of hammers, the hiss of the caulker's iron, someone singing low. Thomas Idris stood in the yard directing a crane swing, sleeves rolled, forearms black to the elbow. He saw the motorcar and raised a hand, easy and open, the only man in the Vane orbit who greeted Elodie like a person rather than a function.

"Morning! You'll want to mind the north approach, we've got planking down."

"Aye." Elodie cut the engine and sat a moment. Then, before courage could fail him, he stepped down, crossed the yard, and shook the offered hand—warm, grease-marked, alive. "Mind your premises at night," he said, quietly, under the hammer-noise, looking Idris full in the face. "All of them. Trust me on it."

Idris blinked. "Trouble?"

"Trust me," Elodie said again, and it cost him everything he had, and it was nowhere near enough.

Then he walked back to the car, and drove away, and told himself—as the workshops shrank in the mirror, lamps burning, children shouting, presses waiting for the dark—that a warning given was a duty discharged, that anonymity was armor, that four words were enough.

The harbor, keeping its silent accounts behind him, declined to agree.
