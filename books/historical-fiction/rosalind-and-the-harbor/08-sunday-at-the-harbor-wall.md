## 8. Sunday at the Harbor Wall

Rosalind Vane had learned long ago that the best way to see a thing was to stop being seen while looking at it.

It was a lesson her mother had taught her without ever meaning to. *A lady is invisible when she wishes to be, darling,* Eleanor Vane used to say, adjusting Rosalind's collar before church. *That is our one great power, and we waste it on modesty.* Her mother had meant it as a joke about mourning dress and lowered parasols. Rosalind had made it into a discipline.

So on Sunday mornings, when the city was at its most honest—when the churches emptied their congregations into the streets like pockets turned out, when the harbor smelled of roasting chestnuts and bilge water and the particular sweetness of fried fish that no respectable kitchen would admit to—Rosalind walked out of Clifton in plain clothes and went down to the water.

She wore a gray coat that had belonged to her late aunt, a hat with no feathers, and boots with a scuff on the left toe that she had never bothered to have repaired because the scuff was the point. She left the motorcar at home. She left the house by the garden door. And she left behind, every week, the woman whose name was spoken in drawing rooms and committee meetings and, lately, in the columns of the *Bristol Times and Mirror* with a mixture of alarm and fascination that she had come to think of as her second income.

Down here she was nobody. Down here she was just a tall woman with a firm walk who looked at things too closely.

The fog had lifted overnight, replaced by a thin, high brightness that made the harbor look almost kind. The masts stood up against the pale sky like a forest that had been drowned and had decided, stubbornly, to go on growing. Gulls argued over something near the coal hoists. A barge named *Providence*—the harbor's idea of a joke, since nothing arrived here by providence but everything by labor—was being unloaded by men who moved with the slow economy of people who had learned that speed only bought them more work.

Rosalind walked the quay wall slowly, her hands in her pockets, and let the harbor do what it always did: remind her why.

There were those in the movement—the comfortable ones, the ones with husbands who tolerated their committees as they tolerated gout—who spoke of the Cause as though it were an argument to be won in Parliament, a matter of clauses and majorities and the patient wearing-down of men like her uncle, who had represented Bristol for twenty years and believed, sincerely, that women already had all the votes they needed because they had their fathers' ears and their husbands' arms. Rosalind did not argue with these women anymore. Arguing wasted breath that could be spent walking.

Because here was the argument. Here, every Sunday, laid out along the harbor wall like goods on a stall.

The children came first, always. They came down to the water before their parents could catch them, boys in shirts that had been shirts for two generations, girls with their hair tied back in string, and they ran the length of the quay like small weather systems, shrieking, dodging between crates, daring each other toward the edge where the green water slapped the stone. One of them—a girl of perhaps seven, with a smudge of soot across her cheekbone like a beauty mark applied by accident—had climbed onto the bollard nearest the ferry steps and was standing on it with her arms out, riding the wind, and Rosalind stopped to watch because there was a lesson in it. The child had no fear of falling. Not because she was brave, but because she had calculated, with the cold arithmetic of poverty, that the water below would not kill her and the fall would not break anything that mattered more than pride.

*That*, Rosalind thought. *That is who I am working for.* Not the abstraction called Woman, capital W, sitting in state in the national imagination, waiting to be enfranchised like some dowager granted a pension. But this child, this exact child, who at seven years old already knew the price of everything and the worth of herself, and who would spend her whole life being told by men who had never met her that she was too delicate to be trusted with a ballot.

The girl's mother appeared—a big woman in a Sunday shawl, calling a name that was swallowed by the wind—and the child leapt down without hesitation and was gathered up under one arm like a parcel, protesting, laughing, and carried off toward the smell of frying fish. The whole transaction took eleven seconds and contained, Rosalind thought, more genuine governance than Parliament had managed in a decade.

She walked on.

The workshops came next along her route—or rather, she came to the stretch of wall opposite them, the low brick buildings wedged between a rope-walk and a cooperage, their doors open to the Sunday quiet because half the harbor worked even on the Sabbath, or wanted the appearance of it. Smoke rose from their chimneys in a color that was almost respectable. She could hear, faintly, the sound of hammering—not the urgent hammering of the weekday rush but the unhurried tapping of men doing work they loved on a day they were supposed to rest.

She knew what happened in those buildings after dark. Of course she did. Thomas Idris, the foreman—a mountain of a man with a voice like a landslide and hands that could cradle a moth—had never told her outright, because he was too careful and too kind, but he had looked at her once, over the head of his youngest daughter at last spring's benefit, with an expression that said everything his mouth would not. *We know what you are, miss. We hope you know what we are.*

She did. She knew that by day the workshops repaired the lighters and the pilot boats and the small craft that kept the harbor's blood moving; and she knew that by night, in the back room behind the steam-bent timber, a press ran until two in the morning turning out pamphlets that were passed hand to hand through the docks like contraband scripture. She knew that the money that kept her movement fed—the subscriptions, the collection plates at the harbor meetings, the shillings pressed into her palm by women with rope-burned fingers—came from wages earned in those very buildings. The harbor funded her cause. The harbor sheltered her cause. And the harbor, if it ever came to it, would be asked to bleed for her cause, though no one had yet said so aloud, and she lay awake some nights hoping no one ever would.

She owed these buildings. That was the truth of it, and she did not flinch from debts. Every pamphlet with her name in the byline, every meeting at the Colston Hall, every inch of ground the franchise question had gained in this city in three years—it rested on foundations she had not built and could not see, and the least she owed it was to look at it plainly, on foot, in a scuffed boot, once a week.

Which was why the stillness bothered her.

She noticed it the way you notice a missing sound. Along the workshop wall, where on any other Sunday there would be children playing pitch-and-toss against the bricks—she had watched them a dozen times, had once lost sixpence to a boy of nine with a dead-eye throw and been unable to be angry about it—there was nobody. The stretch of wall was empty. Empty, and swept. Someone had swept it, recently; the grit lay in neat windrows against the kerb, and there were fresh chalk marks on the doorpost of the middle building, small marks, tally-like, that she did not remember seeing before.

And there was a man leaning against the corner of the rope-walk who had been leaning there, she realized with a slow coldness climbing her spine, since before she had passed him ten minutes ago. He had a newspaper folded under one arm. He had not opened it. He was watching the street with the studied blankness of a man paid to watch nothing, and when her gaze crossed his he examined the rooftops instead, then his boots, then the harbor, in the manner of a man performing innocence for an audience he hoped wasn't there.

Rosalind did not change her pace. Fifteen years of public life had taught her that the worst thing you could do with fear was show it a reflection.

But her mind, which had been wandering pleasantly among children and chimney smoke, snapped suddenly into focus, and began, unbidden, to count. Counting was what she did. It was how she had built the movement out of tea parties and fury—counting signatures, counting shillings, counting allies in the third row and enemies in the second. Now she counted watchers. One at the rope-walk corner. Another, she saw now, standing at the far end by the cooperage gate, wearing the particular stillness of a man who has been told to stand somewhere and is enduring it. Two watchers on a Sunday morning outside workshops that repaired boats.

Boat repairs did not need guarding. Secrets did.

Her first thought was for the press. *They've found the press.* The word *they* arrived fully formed, without a face attached, and she hated that—hated that her mind supplied the category before the culprit. Harrow? The Harrows had opposed the harbor improvement scheme, had fought the dockers' wage talks tooth and claw through their solicitors, had the money and the temperament for exactly this sort of quiet muscle. But surely even the Harrows—

Her second thought arrived a moment later, and it was worse, because it was closer to home.

*Who knows my route?*

She had never told anyone about the Sunday walks. Not the committee, not her sister, not her diary—she kept no diary, diaries were evidence. The walks were the one unguarded hour in a carefully guarded life. And yet here she was, standing at the exact stretch of wall where she always stopped, being observed by men who were themselves observing something else, and a small cold voice in her head, a voice she distrusted immediately and could not silence, asked: *How does a stranger know where to find the thing I've never told anyone about?*

The voice answered itself, because voices like that always do. *Someone watches you, Rosalind. Someone close enough to learn your habits without asking. Someone in your own house.*

She rejected it. She rejected it firmly, the way she rejected the tremor that sometimes got into her voice before a speech. Her household was four people and a cat. Mrs. Pym, who had buried two husbands and could not be bribed because she considered bribery beneath the dignity of both parties. The girl who came in for the rough work. Old Mr. Tredinnick, who did the garden and was mostly deaf and entirely loyal to the roses. And Elodie.

Elodie Galloway. Her driver. Her reader of letters, though neither of them had ever used that phrase aloud, because using it aloud would have made it a confession of something neither of them had agreed to commit. He had been with her five years now, which was longer than most servants lasted anywhere and longer than any man had stayed in her orbit by choice. He was quiet in the way that furniture is quiet—present, useful, unremarked upon—and he read letters the way she imagined a surgeon reads an X-ray: completely, dispassionately, and without leaving fingerprints.

She had chosen him precisely because he seemed to want nothing. In a world where everyone wanted something from her—votes, money, her name on a letterhead, her presence at a rally, her silence on a platform—Elodie Galloway's perfect neutrality had felt like a luxury. Like clean air.

Standing now at the harbor wall with two hired watchers pretending not to watch, that luxury curdled in her stomach and became a question. *Neutral men are also unreadable men. What else haven't I asked him?*

She thought of his hands on the wheel—long, careful, scarred at the knuckles in a way no coachman's hands ought to be, scars that spoke of rope or machinery or worse, of a history he had never offered and she had never requested, because requesting would have broken the elegant fiction that he had sprung fully formed from the seat of a motorcar. She thought of the way he looked at her in the rearview mirror—not often, but when he thought the road needed watching, which was nearly always, which meant nearly always he was looking at her, and she had filed that away for five years under a heading she refused to write down.

She thought of how he always knew. Knew when the mail had come early. Knew which deliveries mattered before she'd said. Knew the harbor the way she knew the committee rooms—every turn, every tide, every door that opened and when.

*He reads everything,* she thought. *I pay him to. I have never once asked what he does with it.*

The gull cried overhead, ugly and imperious, and broke the spell. Rosalind became aware that she had been standing still for too long—that the watcher by the rope-walk had shifted his weight, that stillness was itself becoming conspicuous. She resumed walking, unhurried, past the workshops, past the swept and empty wall, past the chalk marks she could not read from here but resolved to examine another day. At the end of the quay she turned, as she always turned, and looked back once—the habitual glance of a woman checking her exit, or her losses.

The man with the newspaper had gone. So had the other. The wall stood empty in the sun, innocent as a lie.

She walked home the long way, up through the steep lanes where the houses leaned together like conspirators, and by the time she reached the garden door her mind had done what her mind always did: it had organized the dread into questions, and the questions into a list, and the list into a plan. Tomorrow she would find out who owned the rope-walk lease. Tomorrow she would ask Thomas Idris, casually, whether anything was troubling him—casually, because if trouble had found the workshops, the last thing they needed was her worry arriving like a searchlight. Tomorrow she would begin, quietly and without accusation, to map the geography of her own household: who came, who went, who saw her letters before she did.

And tomorrow, she thought, pausing with her hand on the garden gate, she would look at Elodie Galloway properly. Not the mirror-glance, not the convenient furniture. Properly. She would set him some errand—something harmless, something with an obvious right answer—and she would watch what he did with it, the way she had watched witnesses at inquiries, the way she had watched men lie about wages and hours and the condition of their factories. She was good at watching. It was, along with the invisibility, her other inherited power, and she had wasted far less of it on modesty.

It was not suspicion, she told herself, letting herself into the garden, where Mr. Tredinnick was attacking the roses with the tenderness of a man defusing bombs. It was prudence. There was a difference, and she intended to keep hold of it.

But the difference felt thinner than it had that morning. Because underneath the prudence, in the place where she kept the things she refused to write down, another thought had taken root and was quietly refusing to die:

*If he is hiding something, he has had five years to hide it well. And if he is not hiding anything—if he is exactly what he appears—then someone taught a dockside orphan to drive a motorcar and read a solicitor's hand, and put him in my house, and that is a story, and stories like that have authors.*

Either way, she thought, closing the door on the smell of roses and cut grass, Monday would require a false errand.

She did not yet know that the false errand was already unnecessary—that the man she meant to test had spent the previous night copying, by lamplight, a letter that named her—and that while she sharpened her suspicions against the wrong stone, the real danger to her name was drying in a hidden copy, page by page, in a hand she would recognize anywhere, because she had been reading it for five years in the margins of her own correspondence, patient and precise, and had never once wondered what else it might be writing.
