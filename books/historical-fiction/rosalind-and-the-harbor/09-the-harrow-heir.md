## 9. The Harrow Heir

Cedric Harrow had learned early that the most dangerous men in Bristol were not the ones who shouted in the streets but the ones who poured the tea.

He stood at the window of his father's counting house on Prince Street, watching the fog come off the Floating Harbour in grey ribbons, and adjusted his cuffs. The room behind him smelled of leather, pipe smoke, and the particular staleness of money that had been counted too many times — his father's smell, though his father was not present. His father was never present for these meetings. That was the arrangement: Ambrose Harrow set the policy and the budget, and Cedric executed it, and neither of them ever said aloud what *executed* meant. It was a word that did its work best unexamined, like a clause in a charter party.

The three men arrived separately, as instructed. First came Mr. Quill — no one believed it was his name, which was rather the point of it — a small, dry man with the complexion of old newsprint and eyes that moved like a man perpetually reading a room for errors. Then the brothers, Fitch and Fitch, who were not actually brothers but had been hired as a pair so often that the fiction had hardened into fact. They were big without being conspicuous, quiet without being subtle, and they had the great virtue of their trade: they understood that violence was a language of grammar, not vocabulary. One did not need many words if one placed them correctly.

"Gentlemen." Cedric turned from the window and gestured to the chairs arranged before the cold hearth. "Please. Sit."

They sat. Quill folded his hands like a clerk awaiting dictation. The Fitches sat the way furniture sits.

"You'll want to know why I've asked you here before the season begins," Cedric said, taking his own chair opposite them, angling it so the light from the window fell across his face and left theirs in half-shadow. He had read enough about stagecraft to know that light was a form of authority, and he intended, in all things, to hold the authority. "I'll be direct, because I value your time and I expect you to value mine."

Quill's mouth twitched at *value*. He knew what he was being paid. But he inclined his head, granting the courtesy of the fiction.

"The harbor workshops," Cedric continued. "Idris's yard, and the two smaller premises adjoining. My father's office has been patient with them for — how long would you say, Mr. Quill?"

"Four years of patience, sir. By my count."

"Four years." Cedric let the number sit in the room like a bad debt. "In four years those premises have gone from repairing hulks to printing pamphlets. Do you know what they printed last month? Eleven thousand leaflets calling for a general strike of the dock labor. Eleven thousand. My father's clerks counted them." He smiled, and made sure the smile reached his eyes, because a smile that didn't reach the eyes was a weakness, and he had trained himself out of weaknesses the way other young men trained themselves out of regional accents. "A cancer, gentlemen. That is the medical term I've settled on. And one does not negotiate with a cancer. One excises it."

"And the method of excision?" Quill asked mildly.

"Encouragement first." Cedric rose and crossed to the sideboard, where a decanter and glasses waited — he poured nothing, but the gesture gave his hands something elegant to do while he spoke the uglier parts. "There are men in that workshop with leases, with debts, with wives who shop on credit at shops that extend credit because the Harrow name is good in this city. Those men can be encouraged to find other premises. Other trades. Other cities, ideally. Australia has a climate very restorative to men of restless politics." He set down the empty glass. "You will begin with the debts. Quietly. Through intermediaries. If a man's landlord raises his rent by a sum he cannot pay, that is misfortune. If a man's creditors call in notes early, that is commerce. Neither is arson, gentlemen, and neither is conspiracy, and neither will ever appear in a courtroom with our name attached."

"And if encouragement fails?" This from the elder Fitch, who spoke perhaps six sentences a week and spent them well.

Cedric returned to his chair. He let the pause stretch — another lesson learned from his father, who could make silence do the work of a foreman with a whip.

"Then the premises become uninsurable," he said. "Fires happen in harbors. Tar, pitch, oakum, lamp oil, men smoking where they shouldn't. The insurance assessors will find what assessors always find, which is whatever is cheapest to write down. And a burned workshop is a cleared workshop, and a cleared workshop is land, and land on the harbor is worth more than any ten printers." He laced his fingers over his knee. "But we are nowhere near that conversation. We are at the conversation about rents and credit. Let us hope we never leave it."

"We're paid either way," said the younger Fitch, with the flat honesty of his profession.

"You are paid for results," Cedric corrected pleasantly. "The method is your affair within the limits I've described. What is *my* affair — the only thing that is my affair — is that nothing traces back to this room. Not a note, not a coin, not a glance between you in a public house. You will be paid through Mr. Quill, who will be paid through a solicitor in Cardiff who has never heard of this family, and if any thread of it is ever pulled, it will unravel into a knot with no names in it. Am I understood?"

"Perfectly, sir," said Quill, and there was something almost admiring in it, which Cedric noted and filed away. Admiration from such men was useful; it was also a warning. Men admired what they might later blackmail. He would have Quill watched, eventually. Everything was eventually.

* * *

The meeting concluded with brandy none of them drank and handshakes none of them trusted, and the three men left by separate doors into separate fogs. Cedric remained at the window, watching the street below, and it was then — because he had made a habit of watching, of noticing what other men's eyes slid past — that he saw the motorcar.

It stood at the corner of Prince Street and the quay, engine off, driver at the wheel, in the patient way of vehicles waiting on their principals. A Vane motorcar. He knew it on sight; everyone in Bristol who moved in anything above the working classes knew Rosalind Vane's motorcar, that grey Darracq she drove about the city like a small insurrection, delivering speeches and collecting subscriptions and generally behaving as though the nineteenth century had been a regrettable misunderstanding.

What interested Cedric was not the car. It was the driver.

The man had been there, by Cedric's count, for the better part of forty minutes. Miss Vane was not in the car. She was, according to Cedric's information — and his information was excellent, purchased the way one purchases everything, quietly and through intermediaries — at a committee meeting two streets away, a meeting scheduled to run past noon. A driver with forty minutes to wait had a hundred places to be: a public house, a tea shop, the cab rank gossip, the warm seat of his own thoughts. This one sat at the wheel like a man keeping an appointment.

And twice — Cedric was nearly certain it was twice, though the fog made certainty expensive — the man's head had turned toward the door of the counting house. Toward the door through which Quill had entered and departed.

Cedric did not move from the window. He watched with the unhurried attention of a man pricing a horse, noting the set of the shoulders under the driver's coat, the stillness of the hands, the way the man's gaze worked the street in sections, methodically, like a clerk checking columns. Not a loiterer. Not a servant killing time. Something else. Something with a system.

Interesting.

He went down himself — he did not send a clerk, because clerks remembered being sent — and stepped out into the fog with his hat at the correct angle and his expression arranged into the mild, open pleasantness that opened more doors than keys did.

"Good morning," he said, approaching the motorcar. Up close the driver was younger than he'd looked from above, with a face that was easy to stop seeing the moment one looked away — a forgettable face, which was itself memorable, in Cedric's experience. Very few men had forgettable faces naturally. "That's the Vane motorcar, isn't it? I admire it. My father won't have one in the yard — says they frighten the horses and the shareholders equally."

The driver looked at him. Just looked, for a moment longer than politeness required, and Cedric felt — faintly, absurdly, and with a displeasure he examined later at length — *read*. As though the pleasant speech had been taken up and unfolded and its stitching inspected.

"Miss Vane is at her meeting, sir," the driver said. His voice was level, West Country underneath the polish, careful as a man walking on ice he'd crossed a thousand times. "She won't be long now."

"No hurry at all. I only wondered —" Cedric gestured vaguely up the street, toward the counting house, casual as weather talk. "Whether you'd been waiting long. Fog like this plays havoc with the traffic. I hope nobody's kept you standing about."

"Not long, sir."

*Not long.* Forty minutes was not long, then. Cedric smiled his open smile and received back a face that was politely blank as fresh paper, and the exchange ended the way such exchanges end, with nods and the small courtesies of class, and Cedric walked away along the quay with his hands clasped behind him and his mind working like a dynamo.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he turned over and over as the fog thickened around his shoulders and the harbor water slapped its slow applause against the pilings. Drivers did not watch doors. Servants did not count minutes. Unless they had been told to watch, or unless they were watching for reasons of their own.

If the Vane woman had set a man on his father's offices, that was politics, ordinary and manageable — she spied, they spied, the whole city spied, and the ledger balanced. But if the driver had been watching on his own account, unprompted, out of some private alertness — then somewhere in the machinery of the operation there was a gear turning that Quill hadn't reported, and gears that turned unreported were how empires caught fire.

He thought of the workshops. He thought of Idris, that granite foreman with his union roll and his Sunday speeches. He thought of the arithmetic his father had drilled into him since boyhood: every enterprise has a point of failure, and the point of failure is never the front door.

And he thought — with a pleasure so keen it startled him, a collector's pleasure, the pleasure of a rare stamp discovered in a common album — that he might have found the Vane household's point of failure, sitting in plain sight at the wheel of a grey Darracq, wearing a chauffeur's coat and a forgettable face.

"Find out who he is," Cedric told Quill that afternoon, when the little dry man came to submit his first expense account. "The Vane driver. Name, wages, lodgings, where he goes on his half-day, what he drinks, whether he has family, whether he has debts. Everything. Discreetly."

Quill's pen paused over the figures. "Trouble, sir?"

"Opportunity," Cedric said. "There's a difference, and the difference is timing." He looked out the window again, at the fog-bound harbor, at the distant black shapes of the workshop roofs huddled along the water like patients waiting for a diagnosis he had already written. "Every household has a servant who knows too much. When you find ours, Mr. Quill, tell me at once. Before anyone else thinks to look."

Quill wrote it down. Outside, the fog closed over the harbor like a lid, and somewhere in it, Cedric was certain, the grey motorcar was carrying its quiet driver home through streets that did not yet know how thoroughly they were being mapped — by the Harrows, by the watchers, by the man at the wheel himself.

Three maps of the same city. Only one of them, Cedric reflected, would be allowed to survive the year.
