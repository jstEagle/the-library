## 10. First Warning

The note took four drafts.

Elodie sat in the loft above the Vane stables—his room, though *room* was generous for a space that held a cot, a washstand, and a trunk he kept locked even when he slept—and wrote by the light of a candle stub trimmed so low it barely cleared its own tin plate. The first draft said too much. *Your workshop is to be fired by men paid by Cedric Harrow; I have read the letter myself.* He read it back and felt his stomach turn over, not at the danger of it but at the handwriting: neat, educated, unmistakably the hand of someone who had been taught with care. Nobody in Thomas Idris's world wrote like that. Nobody in the workshops had ever had the luxury of copperplate.

The second draft he printed in block capitals, ugly as a child's slate work. Better—but the letters leaned wrong, all of them tilting east like frightened trees, and he caught himself pressing harder on certain strokes, the way he always did when his hands wanted to shake and he wouldn't let them. A man's fear lives in his penmanship whether he wills it or not. Elodie had read enough letters over fifteen years to know that the paper remembered everything the writer tried not to say. He would not send a note that confessed him on every line.

The third draft he burned without reading twice. It had begun *I know* — two words, and already too many words, because *I know* implied an *I*, and an *I* implied a face, and a face could be found, and a found face could be made into a name, and names were the one currency Elodie Galloway had spent fifteen years refusing to hold.

The fourth draft said:

**CHECK YOUR PREMISES AT NIGHT. ALL OF THEM. THE CELLAR MOST OF ALL.**

Eleven words. No signature, no date, no address. He copied it out twice more until the capitals stopped leaning and sat square and dumb as type, then tore the sheet into three pieces along lines he'd ruled faintly in pencil first, so the tears looked accidental, the way paper tears when it's been through pockets. One piece went into his left boot. One into the lining of his driving coat. One he held a long moment over the candle flame and watched curl into a black comma of ash that drifted up and died against the rafters.

He told himself, sitting there in the smell of horse and hay and tallow, that this was enough. That a warning given was a warning delivered. That Thomas Idris was a foreman with thirty men under him and eyes in the back of his calloused hands, and if a stranger's note told him to watch his cellar at night, Thomas would watch his cellar, and nail his shutters, and set a lad on the roof with a bucket, and that would be that. The plot would find nothing but padlocks and patience. The fire would go hungry. And Elodie would go back to being nobody, which was the only country he had ever held citizenship in.

He blew out the candle and lay down in the dark and did not sleep, and the lie he had told himself sat on his chest like a cat, warm and heavy and utterly indifferent to him, until the grey came up over the harbor and it was time to be nobody again.

* * *

Morning fog off the Avon, thick as milk gone wrong. Rosalind Vane's motorcar coughed itself awake in the cobbled yard behind the house on Berkeley Square, and Elodie drove her through streets where the gas lamps still burned at nine o'clock, each one wrapped in its own private halo of yellow haze.

She was reading in the passenger seat—a pamphlet proof, pencil marks bleeding through the cheap paper—and she did not speak, which was usual, which was the arrangement, which was the whole architecture of their mornings: the lady and her driver enclosed together in a glass-and-leather box, six feet apart and a world apart, both of them silent, both of them watching. He watched the road. He knew, from the corner of his eye, that she watched him. Neither of them ever acknowledged the second watching. It was, he sometimes thought, the most honest part of their relationship—the part they never performed.

"Queen's Road," she said, "and then the Clifton end. Mrs. Pargeter's drawing room wants me by half past."

"Yes, miss."

The fog swallowed them. Somewhere behind them, three streets back—he'd counted the turns, he always counted now—came the sound of another engine, patient, keeping distance. He did not mention it. She did not ask. The watchers had learned the Vane car's rhythm days ago, and Elodie had learned theirs, and between the two learnings there had grown something almost like manners: everyone pretending the stalking was weather.

At the top of Park Street he braked for a dray, and in that held moment she spoke without looking up from her proofs.

"You've taken the same route to Wapping four times this week, Mr.—" a pause, deliberate, the pause she always left where a name should have been, "—to Wapping. The Harrow offices are in Queen Square."

"The pilot station wants collecting from," he said. "Charts and tide tables. Miss Vane asked for them herself, Tuesday last."

"I know I did." Her pencil tapped once against the paper. "I also know you come back from those collections with coal dust on your cuffs and the look of a man who has been somewhere he wasn't sent."

Elodie kept his eyes on the dray, on the fog, on the wet gold shine of the lamps. His heart performed its small treachery and he let it, privately, while his face did nothing at all.

"The harbor is dirty, miss. Cuffs follow."

"Mm," she said, and returned to her proofs, and the conversation closed like water over a dropped stone. But he felt the stone falling all the way down the rest of the drive. She was counting his routes. She had been counting for some time. There was a version of this morning, available any day she chose it, in which she asked him plainly what he was doing in Wapping and he either lied to her face or handed her the truth like a live coal—and he did not yet know, God help him, which of those things he would do.

He dropped her at Mrs. Pargeter's among the wet plane trees of Clifton, watched her go up the steps with her proofs rolled under one arm and her chin set at the angle that meant *the world will move or I will*, and then turned the car back down toward the water, toward Queen Street, toward the one errand he had assigned himself.

* * *

Thomas Idris was recaulking a dinghy when Elodie found him, kneeling in the workshop doorway with a mallet and a cold chisel and a line of oakum, working with the slow economy of a man who has done one thing ten thousand times and intends to do it ten thousand more. Around him the great shed breathed its daytime breath: sawdust and pitch and pipe smoke, hammers going somewhere deep inside, the smell of hot iron from the forge, boys' voices raised over some dispute about football. Light came down through the glazed roof in dusty shafts and lay across the floorboards like planks themselves.

"Miss Vane's driver," Thomas said, not looking up. "Come to have us look at your magneto again? Third time this month. Either your motor's dying or your excuses are."

"It's the magneto," Elodie said. "It's always the magneto."

"Aye. Well." Thomas set the mallet down and stood, knees cracking like small gunfire, and gave Elodie the full weight of his attention—grey beard, forearms like mooring posts, eyes the color of the harbor in February. This was what Elodie had never got used to about him, in all the years of oil changes and brake adjustments and magneto fictions: that Thomas looked at him. Straight on, unhurried, the way you'd look at a man rather than a function. Everyone else in Elodie's life looked *through* him—at the road, at the letters, at the use of him. Thomas looked *at*. It was worse than suspicion. Suspicion you could manage.

"Walk with me, then, since we're pretending about magnetos." Thomas led him down the shed's length, past the steam box and the timber racks, talking loudly about valve clearances, and stopped at the far bench where no one stood near, and lowered his voice to match the quiet there. "Now. What is it?"

Elodie had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it in the fog on the drive down, three sentences, casual, a man mentioning a thing the way you mention weather. What came out instead, before he could stop it, was nothing—just his hand, moving with a will of its own, pressing the folded scrap of paper into the gap between Thomas's apron and his waistband, quick as a pickpocket working in reverse.

Thomas didn't flinch. Didn't look down. His face stayed exactly where it was, open and mild, a foreman listening to nonsense about spark plugs, while his hand came up and covered the paper's hiding place as naturally as scratching.

"Valve clearances," Elodie said. His own voice sounded distant to him, borrowed. "You'll want them checked. And—" he made himself breathe, made himself finish, made himself give the eleven words their escort, "—your premises. At night. All of them. The cellar most of all. Don't ask me how I know. Don't ask me anything. Just check."

For a long moment Thomas said nothing. The hammers went on somewhere behind them. A boy ran past with an armful of batten ends, shouting something about the tide. The dust moved in its shafts of light, indifferent, ancient, the same dust that had moved through shipyards when this harbor built the ships that carried sugar and slaves and empire and never once paused to think about it.

"You've read something," Thomas said quietly. Not a question.

"I drive a motorcar," Elodie said. "That's all I am. Check your cellar, Mr. Idris. Please."

"And if I find something in the cellar worth finding"—Thomas's voice was very steady, the steadiness of a man holding a heavy load at arm's length—"what am I meant to do about who told me?"

"Nothing. I was never here."

"You're here now."

"I'm fixing a magneto," Elodie said. "I've always been fixing a magneto."

Thomas looked at him for a long time, and whatever he saw—the coat, the cap, the gloves, the careful nothing of the face—he must have seen under it too, because something in his expression shifted from assessment to something older and sadder, the look of a man recognizing a particular kind of animal: one that has lived so long among hunters it has forgotten it isn't one of them.

"All right," Thomas said. "Magneto." He clapped Elodie once on the shoulder, and the weight of that hand went through Elodie like a bell being struck. "You'll take tea before you go? There's a pot on. My missus sends gingerbread down Thursdays and it wants eating or it'll outlive us all."

"I can't. Miss Vane—"

"Aye." Thomas stepped back. "Miss Vane." He said the name without mockery and without reverence, the way you say a fact of geography. Then, as Elodie turned to go: "Driver."

Elodie stopped.

"Whatever you read." Thomas's voice was low, and for the first time there was iron under it, the voice that thirty men obeyed without knowing why. "Whatever it says is coming for us. You read it right, and you read it whole, didn't you. Not the summary. The whole of it."

The workshop noise filled the silence Elodie couldn't.

"Because if there's a thing in that letter that tells us *when*," Thomas said, "or *how many*, or *which night*—and you've kept it back, kept it for your own skin—I'll understand. I'll even forgive it. But I won't thank you, and my dead won't either. So think on which man you mean to be before the smoke starts, because after it starts there's no choosing."

"I told you what I know," Elodie said. It came out barely above a whisper.

"No," Thomas said gently. "You told me to check my cellar. That's not knowledge, lad. That's a riddle." He picked his mallet back up. "But it's more than anyone else has given me, so I'll say this once: whoever you are, whatever name you're hiding behind—you've got the look of a man who knows exactly what paper can do. When you're ready to tell the truth in daylight, there's a bench in this shed with your weight on it."

Elodie walked out through the sawdust light with his ears ringing. Behind him the mallet resumed, stroke after patient stroke, caulking a boat against water, which was the oldest work in the world and suddenly seemed to him the most heartbreaking.

* * *

He told himself it was enough.

He told himself all the way back up through the fogged streets, past the docks where cranes hung like gallows in the murk, past the Custom House, past the tram wires dripping, that eleven words in a foreman's waistband constituted action—that he had *done something*, that the ledger of his conscience now showed credit where before there had only been blank space, and blank space, in a man like him, passed for virtue.

Check your premises at night. All of them. The cellar most of all.

A good warning. A real warning. Thomas would act on it—of course he would; the man had shoulders built for acting on things. He'd post a watcher. He'd move the pamphlet press, maybe, or the membership rolls, or whatever it was the cellar actually held besides boat stores and turpentine. The Harrows' men would come with their cans and their matches and find a locked, dark, empty shell, and the whole plot would starve politely to death, and no one would ever know how close it had come, and Elodie Galloway would remain precisely what he had engineered himself to be: a man the history of 1912 could lose without noticing the loss.

He parked the car in the mews and sat in it after the engine died, hands on the wheel, fog beading the windscreen like condensation on a held breath.

And underneath the telling-himself, in the place where he kept the truths he read in other people's letters and never his own, another voice was speaking, and it was not gentle, and it did not accept eleven words as payment.

*A riddle,* Thomas had called it. *Not knowledge. A riddle.*

Because here was the arithmetic Elodie could not stop performing, the sum that always came out the same no matter how he rearranged the figures: the letter had named a method. Paraffin through the cellar grate. A night when the watchman would be bought. Two men, hired, one of them known to the public houses around Narrow Quay. Elodie knew these things the way Thomas knew caulk—not as facts but as craft, in his fingers. And he had written down none of it. He had withheld the paraffin and the grate and the bought watchman and the public-house man, withheld them in the name of safety, in the name of *never here*, and what he had handed over instead was a fortune-teller's card, a shilling warning, a thing a man could read and still die from, because *check your premises at night* does not stop a fire that comes through a wall.

They would come through a wall. He was nearly sure of it. The letter had said *premises being stubborn*, which in solicitor's language meant the front door was expected to fail.

He put his forehead briefly against the cold rim of the steering wheel.

Enough. It had to be enough. Because the alternative to enough was the rest of it—the names, the dates, the whole architecture of the plot laid out in daylight, signed, sourced, *his*—and the rest of it was not a note in a waistband. The rest of it was a man standing in a workshop saying *I read your mail, all of it, for years, including yours, and here is what I am*, and that man had a face, and the face had a name, and the name, once spoken, could be written down by anyone, and what was written down could be found, and what could be found could be ended. Fifteen years of survival said: eleven words. Eleven words and no more.

The house was quiet when he went in through the kitchen. Cook had gone marketing. The range ticked. On the dresser, squared neatly beside the salt, lay the afternoon's post—and on top of it, addressed to Miss Vane in a hand Elodie knew better than his own heartbeat, a letter from the Harrow shipping offices.

His feet carried him toward it before his mind had voted. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would remember afterward with shame: there was no decision at all. His hand simply rose, the way it had risen ten thousand times, and lifted the envelope, and turned it, and weighed it—and found it already opened. Resealed. The flap gummed down over a fold that had been broken and pressed flat again by someone in a hurry.

Someone in this house was reading Rosalind Vane's post before she did.

Elodie set the letter back exactly where it had lain, squaring its edges to the salt, and stood in the ticking kitchen with his heart going like the engine he had just switched off, and understood that the fog outside the windows was nothing—nothing—to the fog that had just come indoors.

Eleven words, he thought. He had given Thomas Idris eleven words and told himself it was courage.

Upstairs, a door opened. Footsteps on the landing—light, quick, hers.

He took off his cap and went to meet them.
