## 12. A Driver's Education

Elodie Galloway had spent fifteen years learning what not to look at. Now he taught himself the opposite discipline: to see everything.

It began the morning after the copying, when he woke with the lamplight still printed on the inside of his eyelids and found that his hands had memorized the letter better than his mind had. He lay in his narrow room above the Vane coach house—his for eleven years, narrow as a coffin lid stood upright—and watched his fingers move against the blanket, tracing shapes. *Clearance of the harbor workshops.* *Discredit by association.* The words had a weight now, a physical gravity, like ballast shifted wrong in a hull. He got up before the gulls woke and stood at his window looking down at the motorcar, gleaming dully under its canvas, and understood that something in him had changed overnight and could not be changed back.

He had always been a man who knew things and did nothing with them. That was the whole architecture of his survival: knowledge went in, was weighed, was burned or buried, and never came out through his hands or mouth. It had kept him alive through the workhouse winters, through the years of sleeping in sail lofts and coal sheds, through every close call since. But knowing about a fire—a fire meant to happen, scheduled like a delivery, with Rosalind Vane's name written into the same ledger as the flames—that kind of knowledge would not sit still. It moved in him like water finding a level, seeping into cracks he hadn't known were there.

So he made himself a promise that felt, in the grey half-light, almost like a prayer: *I will not stop it with my mouth. I am not built for mouths. I will stop it the way I do everything—by watching.*

* * *

The watchers came in three kinds, and within a week he could tell them apart the way a fishmonger tells herring from sprat.

There were the professional ones, two men who worked shifts out of a doorway opposite the Harrow shipping offices on Prince Street—broad-shouldered, soft-shoed, with the particular stillness of men paid to stand. They wore their caps low and read newspapers they never turned the pages of. One had a habit of cracking his knuckles when he grew bored; the other smoked only halfway down each cigarette, as if time itself were being rationed. Elodie learned them from the driver's seat, from behind the windscreen's glare, from the reflection in shop glass while pretending to study his own face—which he had not truly looked at in years, because looking at yourself is the first step toward being seen.

Then there were the casual watchers: dockers hired for an evening here, a porter there, men whose loyalties were bought by the pint and whose reports traveled by rumor up the chain to whoever paid. These were easier. They looked where they were told and nowhere else. A man paid three shillings to watch a door will watch the door and miss everything passing behind him.

And then there was Cedric Harrow himself, which was neither professional nor casual but something worse: personal. The heir drove his own motorcar now—a gleaming thing, silver-grey, too fast for Bristol's cobbles—and twice in one week Elodie saw it pass the workshop lane at Redcliffe without slowing, without stopping, the way a man walks past a dog he intends to have put down once he's decided on the day.

Elodie began to keep the schedule in his head.

Not on paper—he was done with paper that could be found—but in the place where he kept everything else, the vast dark warehouse behind his eyes where fifteen years of other people's letters lay shelved and dusted and ordered. He built it the way he'd learned to build routes: start at the fixed points, hang the variables between them.

The fixed points first. The Harrow offices opened at eight; the professional watchers took over the doorway shift at seven-forty, meaning they were briefed earlier, meaning there was a briefing—somewhere near, somewhere warm enough for winter mornings, probably a public house. He noted the King's Arms on Welsh Back without letting himself think about why. The second shift began at two in the afternoon, and the men changed over at the corner by the tobacco warehouse, always the same corner, always the same ten-minute overlap during which both pairs stood together and talked. Ten minutes. He filed that away the way another man might file a key.

Delivery schedules next. This part came to him easily, almost sweetly, because it was his native language. He had driven Rosalind's letters to every counting-house in the city; he knew which firms sent their post down to the harbor by van and which trusted it to boys on bicycles. The Harrows received two deliveries daily—the morning post at nine, and a private courier at four o'clock sharp, a lean man in a bowler who never used the front steps but went round to the yard entrance, where a side door opened before he knocked. Nobody opens a door before the knock unless they know the knock. The courier came Tuesdays and Fridays. On Fridays he stayed forty minutes. On Tuesdays, twenty.

*Instructions come Friday,* Elodie thought, sitting in the motorcar outside the chemist's while Rosalind attended her committee, watching the harbor water go gold and then bruise-colored with evening. *Money or orders. Twenty minutes is a report; forty is a discussion.*

He surprised himself with the thought. It sounded like strategy. It sounded like something a different sort of man would think—a man with plans instead of habits.

* * *

Thursday, he drove Rosalind to Clifton, and she talked the whole way, which she often did—not to him, precisely, but aloud, in his presence, the way one talks to a horse or a hearth, trusting the silence to hold the words safely. She was rehearsing again. He could hear the seams in it, the places she would cut.

"—and if they say the franchise is a door that must open slowly, we must ask them: slow for whom? For the woman standing outside, every year of slowness is—" She stopped. Started again. "No. Too much like a sermon. Mr. Asquith's people will simply smile."

"Perhaps," Elodie said, because she sometimes left gaps like that, little openings a servant could fill without trespassing, "they'll smile less if you give them the arithmetic instead of the sermon. Numbers are hard to smile at."

Her head turned. In the driving mirror he caught the edge of her attention, quick and bright as a dabchick diving. "Arithmetic," she repeated.

"How many women work in Bristol's mills and warehouses. What they're paid against what a man's paid for the same hours. If the door is opening slowly, ask how many years of wages the slowness costs them. Let them do the sum themselves." He heard his own voice and was briefly alarmed by it—steady, unhurried, the voice of a man who had opinions worth hearing. He fixed his eyes on the road. "That's all, madam. Forgive me."

"Don't forgive yourself," Rosalind said, and there was something in her tone he couldn't read, something between amusement and appetite. "Do it again."

He didn't. He let the silence come back down over the carriage like a tarpaulin, and she let him, and the rest of the drive passed in the comfortable quiet of two people agreeing, wordlessly, on the terms of their arrangement. But his heart was going like a piston, and not from her praise. From the arithmetic itself—from the sudden, vertiginous realization that the skill he was sharpening against the Harrows, this cold new talent for patterns and sums and schedules, was the same skill she'd been trying to coax out of him for years without ever naming it. She wanted him visible in small ways. The Harrows had forced him to become visible in large ones, to himself at least. And he could not yet tell whether those two visibilities were pointing in the same direction, or whether he was becoming a weapon that would eventually have to choose which hand held it.

* * *

Friday, he followed the courier.

Not obviously—God, no, nothing obvious; invisibility was a craft and he was its journeyman. He timed it so that a delivery of Rosalind's correspondence to a solicitor on Queen Square brought him past Welsh Back at four o'clock, motor idling, ostensibly waiting for a signature. The courier came down the alley from Queen Street on foot, briefcase chained to his wrist—actually chained, Elodie noticed, a thin steel leash running under the coat sleeve, the kind of precaution taken with bank notes or with orders that must not fall into the wrong hands. He watched the man pause at the King's Arms door, check the street both ways with the flat sweep of a professional, and go in.

Ten minutes. Fifteen. At twenty-two minutes the courier emerged, unchained, walking faster now, and turned toward Prince Street and the Harrow offices, and Elodie sat very still in the motorcar and felt the whole shape of the thing assemble itself in his head like a route drawn in ink.

Briefing at the King's Arms. Orders couriered Fridays from—someone. Delivered to the Harrow yard. Watchers briefed Saturday morning, positioned by Monday. And overlaid across all of it, the date that throbbed beneath the city's surface like a bad tooth: the harbor festival. Three weeks off. Bunting already going up along the quays; the committee meeting in the guildhall; fireworks ordered from a firm in Bath, he'd read the invoice in a batch of civic correspondence last month without thinking anything of it.

A city full of noise and light and every face turned upward.

His hands had gone cold on the wheel. Not fear, exactly—or yes, fear, but fear of a new flavor, fear mixed with something appallingly like competence. Because he could see it now, whole, the way you can finally see the pattern in a knot once someone shows you the loose end. The watchers weren't watching the workshops to protect them. They were mapping them. Learning when Thomas Idris locked up, which windows were barred, where the night soil carts passed, how long the lane stayed empty after the public houses closed. Every idle man in a doorway was a line in a drawing, and the drawing was nearly finished, and the frame around it was the fourth of November.

*You know this,* he told himself, staring at the black water of the Floating Harbour where it lay beneath the crane arms like a sheet of hammered lead. *Knowing isn't enough anymore. Knowing is just watching with your hands tied.*

What then?

He ran the routes in his head that night, lying in the dark above the coach house, the way he'd once run escape routes as a boy—over the roofs of the bonded warehouses, down the water stairs, out along the mudflats at low tide. Options. He was good at options; options had kept him breathing when breathing was a skill. Option one: go to the police. He examined it and set it aside gently, like handling something broken. Whose police? The constables drank in the same houses the watchers drank in; the Harrow name sat on half the charitable boards in the city. Option two: go to Rosalind. His chest tightened around that one the way a fist tightens. He turned it over, feeling its edges. She would believe him—she would, he thought she would—but then what? Her committee was already arguing, he'd heard the fragments through doors, that the workshops were a liability, that the Harrow connection was poison to the bill. She would act, and acting would cost her, and the cost would land on her, not on him, because that was how the world apportioned things. Or she would hesitate, weighing duty against danger the way she weighed everything, and hesitation had a price too, and the price would be paid in smoke.

Option three. He didn't let himself finish thinking it. Not yet. It involved Thomas Idris and the smell of tar and woodshavings, and it involved becoming, permanently and irreversibly, a man who did things.

Below his window, the motorcar stood under its canvas like a sleeping animal, and beyond it the harbor lay in darkness, and somewhere out in that darkness a man was chaining orders to his wrist and a woman was drafting speeches and a foreman was oiling presses that printed the truth in pamphlets nobody official would read. Everyone moving along their routes. Everyone certain of their schedules.

Everyone except the invisible man in the coach house, who had just finished drawing the map, and who understood—with a calm that frightened him more than any panic could have—that a map is a promise. Once you can see the whole route, you can no longer claim you didn't know where it led.

Three weeks until the festival. Two days until Tuesday's courier, and whatever shorter, sharper instructions he carried.

Elodie closed his eyes and began, methodically, to plan.
