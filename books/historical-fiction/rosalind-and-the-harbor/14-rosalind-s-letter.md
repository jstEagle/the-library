## 14. Rosalind's Letter

The letter arrived on a Thursday, which was in itself an anomaly. Rosalind Vane had built her correspondence into a fortress of predictability: the morning post came with the milk, the afternoon post came with the second delivery from Queen Street, and anything arriving outside those hours was either urgent or dangerous. This letter was neither marked urgent nor marked at all—no stamp, no return address, no franking mark of any kind. It had simply appeared in the brass tray by the door between half past four and half past five, when Elodie had been out with the motorcar collecting her from the committee rooms, and when the only other person in the house had been Mrs. Trewin, who was deaf as a post and honest as Sunday.

Rosalind stood in the hall holding it for a long moment before she opened it. The envelope was cheap—grey, machine-made, the sort sold in threepenny packets to shopgirls and clerks—and it was addressed to her in a hand that had been deliberately disguised. She could see the disguise the way one sees the seams in a stage set: the letters were printed rather than written, block capitals drawn slowly and carefully, each one constructed rather than flowed. Whoever had written it did not normally write in capitals. Whoever had written it was hiding something specific.

She carried it upstairs to her study before she broke the seal—or rather, broke the fold, because there was no seal, only the flap tucked in like an envelope that expected to be thrown away unread.

*Madam,* it began, *I write to you anonymously because I cannot do otherwise. You should know that certain interests connected with the Harrow shipping concern have taken an interest in your public work that is not friendly. There are documents circulating that concern you personally. I am not at liberty to say more. Burn this letter.*

She read it three times. The first time she read the words. The second time she read the warning beneath them—the vague menace of "documents circulating," the phrase "not friendly" which was so carefully bloodless it might have been lifted from a bank notice. The third time she read the hand itself, because Rosalind Vane had spent eleven years reading other people's letters—petitions, threats, appeals, confessions—and a hand told her things that words tried to conceal.

The capitals were well formed but not natural. The writer's true hand showed through in the small betrayals: the crossbar of the T slanted slightly upward, left to right, like a man hurrying uphill. The R had a leg that kicked out too far at the bottom—a flourish the writer had suppressed everywhere else but couldn't quite kill in his own initial letter. And there was this: every time the word *letter* appeared, the double-t was crossed with a single stroke, one continuous line, economical, the habit of someone who wrote quickly and often and had trained himself to save motion.

She had seen that single-stroke crossing recently. She had seen it on the backs of envelopes in her own hall tray, on the notes Elodie left for her about motorcar maintenance, on the little list he made each Monday of errands and distances and petrol consumed, folded and placed beside her breakfast plate with the discretion of a man who understood that his presence should be felt as little as possible.

She set the letter down on her desk, flat, and pressed her palm across it as though she could hold the words still by weight alone.

It was not proof. That was what she told herself first, and she noticed, with a cold and clinical displeasure, that she was telling herself things first now—arranging her own conclusions the way she arranged evidence for the committee, choosing which facts to lead with. A single-stroke crossing was not unique. Half the efficient men in Bristol wrote that way. The kicked leg of the R might be coincidence; the upward-slanting crossbar might be nothing. She had no more than a suspicion dressed in graphology, and suspicions about one's own household were a corrosive thing, a thing that ate its container.

And yet.

And yet the letter had come through her own door, into her own tray, in the one window of time when the house held exactly two people—one of them deaf, and one of them the most observant man she had ever employed.

Rosalind went to the window. Below, in the cobbled yard, Elodie was washing the motorcar, though the motorcar did not need washing; he washed it the way other men smoked, as something to do with his hands while his mind went elsewhere. He worked with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and even at this distance she could read him the way she'd learned to read crowds from platforms: the economy of his movements, the way he never wasted a gesture or looked directly at anything he cared about. Fifteen months he had driven for her. In fifteen months he had never once asked her for anything, never once volunteered an opinion, never once given her cause to think of him as anything other than what he appeared—which was, she understood now, precisely the problem. Nobody was actually that invisible. Invisibility was a discipline. It was practised. And people practised it for reasons.

*Burn this letter,* the anonymous hand had said.

She did not burn it. She locked it in the drawer of her desk where she kept the threatening correspondence—the suffragists' enemies were generous with such things—and then she sat for a long while with her hands laced in front of her mouth, thinking about the shape of a trap.

Because that was the other possibility, and she was too honest with herself to refuse it a hearing. The letter might be bait. If the Harrows meant to discredit her—if, as the letter itself implied, documents were being prepared—then what better instrument than a warning delivered from within? Let the lady discover her own driver was feeding her secrets; let her dismiss him in anger or keep him in complicity; either way, they would have a thread to pull. Anonymous letters were the cheapest weapons in the arsenal, and she had been cut by them before. Two years ago, after the Bath speech, someone had sent the newspapers an accusation so precisely calibrated to her character—her vanity, her ambition—that she had spent a fortnight wondering which friend had written it. The answer had turned out to be no friend at all, but the fortnight had taught her the lesson: *the cruelest letters arrive looking like gifts.*

So she would not ask him. She would not confront, not accuse, not even glance at the drawer. What she would do—the idea arrived fully formed, and she disliked how easily it arrived, how naturally deception sat upon her—was test him.

* * *

The false errand took her until Saturday to design, and she designed it the way she designed a speech: backwards from the effect she wanted.

If Elodie was merely innocent—if the letter had come from some well-meaning stranger—then any errand would be run cleanly and forgotten. But if he knew something, if the warning had come from his hand, then he was carrying a secret the way a cracked jar carries water, and the right pressure applied at the right seam would tell her everything.

The errand must be meaningless but important-seeming. It must take him past a place where he could be observed. And it must contain, threaded through it like wire through a cable, one small falsehood—a detail that could be checked, that *would* be checked, if anyone along the route was curious enough to check.

On Saturday morning she gave him the instructions over the breakfast table, watching him over the rim of her teacup with the pleasant, absent expression she wore for the benefit of journalists.

"I need a parcel taken to the offices of Wrenfield and Sons, on Marsh Street," she said. "You'll find the address on the label. Ask at the counter for Mr. Wrenfield himself—he'll be expecting it." She paused, delicately. "Oh—and you may find the office closed. They keep short hours since the season ended. If so, leave the parcel with the ironmonger next door, a Mrs. Castellan. She holds things for them."

Every element of it was invented except the street. Wrenfield and Sons existed—she had verified that much—but they kept full hours, there was no Mr. Wrenfield expecting a parcel, and the ironmonger next door was run by a Mr. Castellan, a widower, who had never in his life held parcels for anyone. If Elodie returned and reported that he had delivered the parcel successfully to a waiting Mr. Wrenfield, he was lying—and lying smoothly, without checking, which would mean he hadn't gone at all, which would mean the errand had confirmed nothing except her own paranoia. But if he reported the truth—the office open, no Wrenfield expecting him, an ironmonger named Castellan and male—then he had gone, had looked, had paid attention, and had come back and told her plainly what he found, including the parts that made her look foolish.

That was the real test. Not whether he followed the errand. Whether he told her the truth about its failure.

Elodie received the instructions with his usual stillness. He took the parcel—wrapped, on her instruction, around a brick—and weighed it once in his hands, and for a fraction of a second his eyes came up and met hers, and something moved behind them, quick and deep, like a fish turning under ice.

"The label says Wrenfield and Sons, Marsh Street," he repeated. Not a question. A confirmation. And then, mildly, in that low voice he used for speaking to horses and employers: "Very good, ma'am."

He went out. She heard the motorcar start in the yard, heard it diminish toward the city, and stood at the window watching the space where it had been, feeling like a woman who had just done something irreversible and couldn't yet say what.

Mrs. Trewin brought the second pot of tea and announced, apropos of nothing, that the gulls had been fighting again over by the lock gates, and Rosalind said, "Yes, thank you, Mrs. Trewin," and thought: *this is what it costs.* Not the danger—the danger she had been living inside for eleven years, the threat letters, the hired hecklers, the newspaper men bought by her opponents. None of that had ever reached past the walls of the house. But this—sitting in her own kitchen measuring out lies for her own driver, watching her own words land on a man who waited on her daily, who warmed the motorcar for her before dawn in February, who had once, unprompted, begun teaching himself the routes to the hospital because she fainted twice that spring and he had decided, silently and without permission, that she should never wait longer than necessary for a doctor—

This was the cost. Distrust was a debt that compounded.

* * *

He was gone ninety minutes. The round trip to Marsh Street and back was forty minutes at most.

She spent the surplus fifty minutes at her desk pretending to work on the franchise petition, reading the same paragraph eleven times, and listening for the motorcar the way one listens for footsteps outside a sickroom. When she finally heard it—later than it should have been, later than any honest explanation allowed—she did not go to the window. She made herself stay seated. She made herself pick up her pen.

His knock came at the study door. Two knocks, always two, soft, apologetic, as though the door itself were owed an apology.

"Come in."

He entered with the empty wrapping paper folded under his arm, and he stood where he always stood, just inside the door, at the boundary between servant and occupant, and he said: "The office was shut, ma'am."

A silence, one heartbeat long.

"There was a sign up—half day, staff illness, something of that order. So I left the parcel next door, as you instructed. With the ironmonger." Another fractional pause, so small that anyone who didn't know him would have missed it entirely. "Castellan. He was—surprised. To be asked. I don't believe it's an arrangement they usually make use of. I've the parcel back here—" and he produced it, the same brown paper, the same string, the brick inside it shifting with a dull private sound, "—as it seems Mr. Wrenfield won't be wanting it today. Or perhaps at all. I can try again Monday, if you like. Or you might wish to send it by the parcel post instead. It's—it's not urgent, I'd judge."

He finished, and stood there, and waited, and Rosalind looked at him and understood two things simultaneously, and the understanding arrived with the sensation of a floor tilting.

The first thing: he had lied to her. Not crudely—he had done it beautifully, had woven the false errand's own traps into his report like a man returning borrowed goods with the tags removed. The office shut, the parcel left, the surprise of Mr. Castellan—all of it matched her inventions perfectly, which meant he had either gone to Marsh Street and found everything exactly as she'd fabricated it (impossible; she had fabricated it), or he had never needed to go at all, because he had known, somehow, instantly, standing in her kitchen with the teapot still warm, that the errand was a test, and he had answered it the way one answers an examination: by giving the examiner what she wanted to hear.

Or—the third possibility, the one she saved for last because it hurt most—he had gone, found the office open and no trap waiting, understood completely what she had tried to do, and then chosen to lie anyway. Chosen to let her believe her test had caught him. Chosen, in effect, to confess to a crime she'd invented, in order to conceal a real one.

The second thing, arriving underneath the first like the undertow beneath a wave: whatever he was concealing, he was concealing it *for* her. She knew it the way she knew the weight of a crowd before she saw it—some instinct older than reasoning. A man lying for gain lies loosely, comfortably, with his body open. Elodie stood like a man holding something fragile with both hands, terrified of dropping it, offering her the lie the way one offers up a wound: *here, cut me instead of asking questions.*

"You went to considerable trouble," she said at last, and her voice came out level, warm even, and she marveled distantly at her own performance, at how easily the mask fitted her too. "For a parcel that wasn't wanted. I'm sorry for the wasted journey."

"Not wasted, ma'am. It was a fine morning for a drive."

"Yes," she said. "Fine."

He nodded, turned, and went, closing the door behind him with the same apology of a click, and Rosalind sat alone in her study with the brick wrapped in brown paper sitting on her desk like a headstone, and she thought: *he knew. He knew it was false, and he lied to protect me from knowing that he knew, and now we are both liars in the same house, each protecting the other from a truth neither will speak.*

She unlocked the drawer. She took out the grey envelope and looked again at the single-stroked crossings, the kicked leg of the R, the upward-slanted crossbar, and this time she did not look away from what the hand told her.

Then she did the hardest thing she had done in eleven years of hard things. She put the letter back, locked the drawer, and resolved to say nothing—to watch, and wait, and let him carry his secret a little longer, because she had suddenly understood that pressing him now, with the bill so close, with the committee so exposed, would shatter something she could not reassemble.

But as she turned back to her petition, her eyes fell on the brick-padded parcel, and a cold thought slid beneath her ribs like a blade finding the gap in armor:

If Elodie had known the errand was false before he left the house—had known it from her voice, her face, the way she'd held the teacup—then he read her more fluently than she had ever permitted herself to imagine.

And a man who could read her that well had certainly already guessed why she'd sent him.

Which meant that somewhere out on the roads of Bristol, in the fog and the traffic, her driver was driving—and deciding, minute by minute, how much of what he knew she could bear to learn.
