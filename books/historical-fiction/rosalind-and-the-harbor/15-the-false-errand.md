## 15. The False Errand

The envelope came down to him at half past nine, still warm from her hand.

"Clifton," Rosalind said from the top of the steps, not looking up from the paper in her own hands. "For Mrs. Prewitt, the milliner. Queen's Road, number forty-one. Wait for an answer."

Elodie took it. Cream stock, good weight, sealed with plain wax—the kind of envelope that went to friends, not causes. Mrs. Prewitt of Queen's Road was a real woman; he had driven past her shop a dozen times and seen her arranging hats in the window like a general positioning troops. There was nothing strange about the errand itself.

What was strange was everything around it.

Rosalind did not give him errands on Thursdays. Thursdays were the committee letters, the hours he spent at his own small desk in the back hall copying correspondence while she argued with Miss Fairweather about the wording of resolutions. Thursday mornings she went nowhere and sent him nowhere. Thursday mornings were fixed as scripture.

And she had not looked at him when she gave it.

Fifteen years of reading people from the edge of rooms had taught him that a person's eyes told you less than their hands. Rosalind's hands had been very still when she held out the envelope—too still, the way a hand is still when it is pretending not to be nervous. And she had said *wait for an answer*, which she almost never said, because answers could be trusted to the post.

He carried the envelope out to the motorcar and sat with it on his knee for a full minute before starting the engine.

*She knows.*

The thought arrived without drama, the way the worst thoughts always did. Not *she suspects*. Not *she wonders*. She knew something, or believed she knew something, and this errand was a trap laid with the same neat precision she brought to a committee agenda. He turned the envelope over. The seal was unbroken. His name was written on it in her hand—*Elodie*, spelled correctly, which only three people in Bristol bothered to do—and there was no address on the front, because he did not need one. That too was wrong. Every letter she gave him bore its address. This one assumed he already knew where he was going.

Or assumed he would read it first.

He sat in the cold of the garage and let the two possibilities stand side by side like rival witnesses. Possibility the first: she had learned of the anonymous note he'd passed to Thomas Idris—impossible, surely; Thomas would sooner swallow a press-galley than betray a confidence—and this was her way of catching him in the act of carrying secrets. Possibility the second: she suspected nothing at all, and the morning's oddness was only the bill, and the timing, and the weight she carried these days that made her hands go still.

He wanted the second possibility so badly it frightened him. Wanting was the thing he had trained out of himself for fifteen years, because wanting made a man predictable, and predictable men ended up in the river or the workhouse or worse. But wanting had crept back into him over these months like damp through plaster, and now it was here, softening every wall he'd built.

There was only one way through a trap, if it was a trap: walk straight down the middle of it, exactly as an innocent man would.

He broke the seal.

* * *

*Dear Mrs. Prewitt,—I find I shall want the grey hat altered after all; the brim must come down half an inch on the left. Might I call tomorrow at eleven? Forgive the short notice.—R.V.*

It was, on its face, the most harmless letter ever committed to paper. A hat. Half an inch of brim. He read it twice looking for the cipher in it, the second meaning folded inside the first, and found none—or found none he could prove. That was the elegance of it, if it was a test. A letter about a hat could be delivered by anyone. It contained nothing to steal, nothing to copy, nothing worth the risk of opening.

Which meant the letter wasn't the test at all.

The test was whether he opened it.

His hands were steady. That was the horror of it—he could feel them being steady, deliberately steady, the steadiness arriving like a servant answering a bell. Fifteen years of practice lived in those fingers. They had learned their trade in a dockside loft above a chandler's store, from a one-eyed ex-teacher named Mr. Calloway who fed orphan boys bread and the alphabet in equal measure and said, *Reading is the only theft nobody punishes.* Elodie had stolen whole libraries that way, silently, page by page, and had never once been caught, because nobody watches a boy they believe cannot see.

But Rosalind Vane watched him. Had watched him for months, perhaps longer. He thought of chapter four of her book on the franchise—*the governed are never so well informed about their governors as the governors are about themselves, and this asymmetry is called order*—and felt the sentence turn and look at him across the garage.

She had given him a letter about a hat, sealed, on a Thursday, and asked him to wait for an answer.

If he delivered it sealed, he passed. If he steamed it open in some alley and resealed it with a candle stub, he failed—not because she would know for certain, but because he would know, and she had built a life on understanding that people become what they believe is expected of them.

Unless the test ran the other way. Unless she was thinking, right now, in the morning room with Miss Fairweather: *If he delivers it untouched, he has stopped reading my post—which means either he never read it, or he has something to hide and is being careful. If he opens it, he is what I have always half-believed him to be, and I will finally know.*

There was no move that won. That was the mark of a well-made trap, and God help him, she was better at this than the Harrows' men, because she had built hers out of affection instead of fear.

Elodie put the letter in his breast pocket, over his heart, and drove to Clifton.

* * *

Queen's Road climbed away from the water in long terraces, and number forty-one wore its respectability like fresh paint. He parked where the shade of a plane tree fell across the bonnet and sat with the engine ticking and watched the milliner's window. Hats. Dozens of them, arranged on stands like a congregation of headless ladies waiting patiently for their heads.

Mrs. Prewitt herself came to the door before he knocked. She was sixty-odd, sharp-faced, with the forearms of a woman who had pinned fabric for forty years, and she looked at Elodie the way Clifton always looked at him—a flicker of assessment, driver's cap, working coat, dismissed.

"You're the Vane girl's man." Not a question. "She said you'd wait."

"She did, ma'am."

"Well, there's no answer yet. She said—" Mrs. Prewitt paused, and in pausing confirmed everything. "She said to tell you the alteration might take some time. That you should wait as long as it takes. She was very particular about it." The old woman's eyes narrowed slightly, not unkindly. "Very particular indeed. Odd, for a hat."

"I'll wait," Elodie said.

He waited ninety minutes. He stood by the motorcar in the plane tree's shade while Clifton's respectable traffic flowed past, and he understood, with each minute, more precisely what he was standing inside. Rosalind had telephoned ahead—that was how Mrs. Prewitt knew to say *wait as long as it takes*. She had constructed an hour and a half of empty time and handed it to him like a loaded pistol, to see what he would do with it. An innocent driver would grow bored, smoke, stamp his feet against the cold. A guilty man would use the time.

He did both, because both were true.

He smoked one cigarette down to the filter and hated himself for the performance of boredom even as he performed it. Then, when Mrs. Prewitt had gone back inside, he took out the letter—not to open it, but to hold it, and found that holding it was worse than opening it ever was. Because the letter was nothing. The letter was a hat. The whole apparatus, the seal, the waiting, the particular instructions, was built on nothing, and yet he could feel the question underneath it as clearly as heat under a door:

*What are you, Elodie Galloway? What are you, when you think I'm not looking?*

Not Galloway. Even in his own mind he flinched off the name. Galloway was the foundling-name the parish had stamped on him at seven years old, one of a batch of twelve issued that winter like ration cards. His real name lived somewhere deeper, wrapped in oilcloth below the waterline of memory, next to his mother's face and the smell of the stokehold of the ship that had taken her. He had not spoken it aloud since he was eleven years old, the night silence saved his life, and he was not going to take it out now on a pavement in Clifton because a woman had set a riddle in front of him.

Except that she had. That was the unbearable part. She was asking *who are you*, and he could not answer, because the honest answer—*I am a man who reads your post and burns it and loves you in a language neither of us has agreed to speak*—was the one answer that would end everything.

At eleven o'clock by the church clock, Mrs. Prewitt returned to the door with an envelope in her hand.

"No alteration needed after all," she said, and there was something almost apologetic in her briskness. "Miss Vane rang again not ten minutes ago. Said the grey would do as it was. Said—" the old woman hesitated, reading from memory, and her voice slowed as though she heard the strangeness only now, saying it back, "—said to tell you that a careful man is worth ten bold ones, and that she hopes the morning hasn't been dull."

She watched him take the envelope. He felt the watchfulness like weather.

"Thank you, ma'am."

"Mm." Mrs. Prewitt did not go inside. She stayed in her doorway with her arms folded, an old woman who had spent forty years fitting things to people and knowing when something didn't fit, and she looked at him with something that was nearly gentleness. "Whatever it is she's testing," she said quietly, "I'd pass it quick, if I were you. Life's short, and that one doesn't ask twice."

Then the door closed, and Elodie stood alone on the pavement with a second sealed envelope and the certain knowledge that the morning had gone exactly as badly as it could have gone without anything actually happening.

* * *

He did not open the reply. He drove back down through the terraces with it in his pocket, unbroken, and the not-opening cost him more than any opening would have. That was the discovery of the day, arriving cold: deception done cleanly was still deception, and it weighed. Each mile down Queen's Road the weight grew. By the Floating Harbour he was carrying it in his shoulders; by the Vane house gate it had settled behind his sternum like swallowed lead.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape. If the morning had been a test, he had passed it—but passing it meant proving he was the man who *didn't* read her letters, which was a lie, and now that lie sat between them wearing his face. And if the morning had not been a test—if she truly had wavered over a hat, rung twice, changed her mind—then he had spent ninety minutes constructing counter-moves against a woman who had merely been thinking about millinery, and the absurdity of that was somehow worse.

Either way, he had lied today. Not with words—he hadn't spoken a false syllable—but with the whole architecture of his silence, the careful blankness he had shown Mrs. Prewitt, the innocent man he had performed in the plane tree's shade. He had lied the way he had lied for fifteen years, fluently, expertly, and for the first time the fluency disgusted him. It had kept him alive when alive was all there was to keep. Now there was something else, someone else, and the skill that preserved him was the same skill that made him unknowable to her—and she was trying to know him. Today proved it. You do not build traps out of affection for a man you consider furniture.

The gates stood open. He brought the motorcar round to the coach house and cut the engine, and sat in the sudden quiet listening to the metal tick.

Through the kitchen window he could see Mrs. Tarrant at her sink, arms in grey water. Through the morning room's long window, half-drawn, he could see Rosalind standing at the far end of the room with her back to the glass, speaking to Miss Fairweather, one hand pressed flat against the wall as she talked—her gesture when an argument mattered. He could not hear the words. He could see, plainly, the shape of her conviction, and beyond it the smaller truth that had ruined his whole careful morning: that he loved her. Not the cause, not the movement, not the legend the pamphlets were building out of her name. Her. The woman who misspelled nothing, who rehearsed speeches to empty chairs, who had set a hat-letter like a snare because she could not bring herself to ask him directly what he was hiding.

And she was hiding something too. He saw it now, or believed he saw it, which came to the same thing. The stillness of her hands. The errand on the wrong day. The message that praised careful men—praise shaped exactly like a warning. She suspected her household. Chapter fourteen of her own book: *a movement is betrayed most often not from without but from within, by the loyal hand that trembles.*

He got out of the car and stood in the cobbles of the yard, and made himself look at the thing squarely, because he had promised himself, burning the Harrow solicitor's letter weeks ago, that he would never again mistake comfort for safety.

She was watching him. Perhaps she had been watching him for months. And the letter hidden in his coat—the copy he should not have made, the copy that named her among the Harrows' targets—was six feet away from her through two walls, and every hour he did not tell her, the distance between *protecting her* and *deceiving her* narrowed by a width no instrument could measure until suddenly it was gone.

Mrs. Tarrant appeared at the kitchen door, wiping her hands. "She wants you," she said. "In the morning room. Soon as you're back."

"Did she say why?"

Mrs. Tarrant's face did something complicated. "No," she said. "But she asked me what time you left. And she asked me again just now what time you got back. To the minute, love." She pulled the door to behind her, then opened it a crack. "To the minute."

Elodie stood in the yard with the engine cooling behind him and the morning room window glinting ahead, and understood that the false errand had not been the errand at all. It had been the return. Anyone can leave innocently. Coming home is where a man's face tells everything.

He straightened his cap, put his hands in his pockets to stop them doing anything honest, and walked toward the door she was standing behind—carrying the unopened letter, the copied letter, and fifteen years of silence, all of it suddenly too much for one coat to hold.
