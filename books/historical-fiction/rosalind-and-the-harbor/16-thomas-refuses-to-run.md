## 16. Thomas Refuses to Run

The bell over the workshop door had not rung in an hour, and Thomas Idris liked the silence that followed it. Silence in the harbor workshops was never empty. It was full of the small honest sounds of work: the hiss of the kettle on the iron stove, the tick of cooling metal from the day's last repair, the creak of the loft beams above where the tarred canvas lay stacked like sleeping animals. He stood at the long bench with a mallet in one hand and a half-sewn sail patch under the other, and he let the quiet settle into his shoulders the way a man lets warm water settle into an old wound.

He was fifty-one years old and he had been mending boats since he was seven, when his father first put an awl into his fist and told him the water forgives nothing and neither should you. Forty-four years of salt and pine tar and canvas. His hands were maps of every one of those years—scars across the knuckles, a right thumb that bent wrong at the joint where a block and tackle had taken its toll, calluses so thick he could pick up a stove lid barehanded. His father had died in these workshops, or very nearly; they'd carried him as far as the door before his heart gave out, and Thomas sometimes thought the old man had timed it deliberately, so he wouldn't have to be buried from anywhere else.

Which was why, when the knock came at the side door—the one that didn't face the street, the one only certain people used—Thomas knew before he opened it that trouble had found him again.

* * *

The man on the step was thin as a gaff hook and twice as sharp-looking, wrapped in a coat too good for the harbor and a hat pulled down against the drizzle. Thomas had seen him twice before, once outside the Pilot's Rest and once standing at the top of the slipway like a man pricing up real estate. Both times the fellow had looked at the workshop the way a butcher looks at a beast.

"Evening," the man said. "Mr. Idris."

"It's past evening. It's nearly dark."

"Dark suits me fine." The man smiled without warmth. "I'll be quick, since you're a busy man. My employers are reasonable men. They've given this matter a great deal of patience. But patience has a season, Mr. Idris, same as everything else on the water. And the season's turning."

Thomas leaned against the doorframe and said nothing. He'd learned young that silence makes talkers talk.

"The printing," the man went on, when the silence had stretched to its full length. "It stops. The men who use your premises after hours find other premises. You keep your lease. You keep your trade. Everybody stays dry." He reached inside his coat and produced a card, white and heavy, and laid it on the bench just inside the doorway, the way a man lays down money he expects to be picked up. "There's a number on there. A week. That's generous. That's more than generous. Men less reasonable than my employers would have stopped asking weeks ago."

"And if I don't ring?"

The smile again. "Then my employers will conclude you've made your choice, and they'll make theirs. And I'll tell you something for free, Mr. Idris, since you're a straight-talking man: their choice won't be made of paper."

He touched his hat brim, turned, and walked off down the wet cobbles without hurrying, because men who deliver threats never hurry. Thomas watched him go until the fog took him, then looked at the card lying on his bench among the needles and the sailcloth and the little brass fittings that had come out of boats whose owners were dead and gone.

He left it there all night. In the morning he used it to scrape pitch off the bench, and then he fed it to the stove.

* * *

Elodie Galloway came two days later, and he came through the front door like a customer, which was the way he always came, and Thomas respected him for it even as it broke his heart a little. Here was a man—a young man, twenty-five at most, with careful eyes and a driver's cap and a way of holding his shoulders like a man carrying something breakable—who could have been one of them. Who should have been one of them. The harbor made men like Thomas out of boys like Elodie every generation: it took them off the mudflats and put awls in their fists and taught them that a thing worth having is worth defending. But somewhere along the line somebody had taught this boy a different lesson, a harder one, and now he moved through the world like smoke through a keyhole, belonging everywhere and nowhere, and he watched people the way a man watches weather he can't control.

"Morning," Elodie said. "Miss Vane's compliments. There's a delivery coming Thursday, canvas for the ferry refit, and she wanted me to confirm you've room for it."

"We've always room for canvas." Thomas wiped his hands on his apron. "And how is Miss Vane?"

"Well."

"Just well?"

A pause, small as a coin but real. "Busy. There's a meeting Saturday."

"There's always a meeting Saturday." Thomas grunted and jerked his head toward the back room, and Elodie followed him through, past the presses under their tarpaulin, past the racks of type in their wooden cases like trays of jewelry, into the little office where the stove burned hotter and the window overlooked the slipway and no one passing on the street could see a man's mouth move.

They stood either side of the stove like two men warming themselves, though the morning wasn't cold enough for it, and Elodie spoke low and fast, and what he said turned Thomas's blood to harbor water in February.

"They've set a time. After the festival. The fireworks—everyone looking up, nobody watching the water. There'll be watchers on both lanes, and the watch will be paid to be elsewhere. I've seen it in writing. I can't tell you how I've seen it, but I've seen it." Elodie's hands were flat on the bench, pressing down, as if the truth might otherwise lift off and fly away. "You have to move the presses, Mr. Idris. Tonight. Tomorrow at the latest. Get them out, get the type out, get the rolls out, scatter it across six cellars so there's nothing here to burn."

Thomas looked at him for a long moment. Then he crossed to the window and looked out at the workshop floor, at the benches his father had built, at the press itself—a Columbian, iron eagle on the crown, forty years old and still true—and at the loft stairs where the union's membership rolls sat in their oilcloth wrapping, four hundred names in a strongbox, four hundred men and women who trusted this roof over their heads.

"You know what this place is?" Thomas said, not turning round.

"A workshop."

"This is the only roof in Bristol where a dockhand can say what he thinks out loud and not lose his bread for it. You know what happens if we run? If the presses vanish in the night like thieves' goods?" He turned then, and his voice was steady, which cost him more than shouting would have. "First question everyone asks: what were they hiding? Why did they run? A workshop that runs is a workshop that's guilty, boy. We'd hand them the story. They'd print it themselves—*radicals flee harbor*, *union prints seditious filth and slinks off when questioned*. Every man on those rolls becomes a conspirator instead of a workman asking for fair pay. No. Let them come. Let the whole harbor see what Harrow men do to a boatyard that asks for an eight-hour day. Let them burn it with witnesses."

"There won't be witnesses. That's the whole point of the timing. Everyone will be watching the fireworks."

"Then the fire will speak for itself. Ash doesn't lie about how it got hot." Thomas came back from the window and put his hand flat on the Columbian's frame, the way another man might touch a horse's neck or a child's head. "Besides. You think I haven't thought about running? Three nights I've thought of nothing else. I could have the press on a barge by midnight. Old Morgan would take her down-channel and moor her off Pill and no one the wiser." His thumb traced the eagle's wing, worn smooth by decades of ink and oil. "But my father built these benches. And the men who meet here Tuesdays, they don't have anywhere else. Half of them sleep in rooms where a landlord listens at the door. This is the one place they're men. I don't get to take that away because some Harrow pup is frightened of pamphlets."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing. "You'd risk four hundred names for a principle."

"I'd risk one old man and a shed for four hundred people's right to exist in daylight." Thomas almost smiled. "That's not principle, boy. That's arithmetic. You do the sums different when you've buried friends."

"That's exactly what I'm afraid of." Elodie pulled his cap off, turned it in his hands once, put it back on. "Then at least let me do something. The night of the festival—I want to be here, or near here. I can't stop it, maybe, but I can—"

"You can stay away," Thomas said, gently, and meant it kindly. "You're a driver. Whatever else you are—and I think you're a good deal else—you're not a fireman, and you're not a fighter, and if they catch a Vane man sniffing round my yard the night it burns, they'll hang Miss Vane with the same rope. You've done your part. More than your part. Now let an old fool stand in his own doorway."

For a moment something moved behind Elodie's eyes—not fear exactly, and not anger, but the look of a man watching a wall he cannot climb and cannot knock down. Then he nodded, once, and shook Thomas's hand, and his grip was stronger than his frame promised, and he left through the front door like a customer.

Thomas stood alone in the warm room and listened to the stove tick and thought about his father dying at the threshold with his boots still on.

* * *

That evening he told the Tuesday men.

There were nine of them, crowded into the back room with the shutters closed and the lamp turned low, and when Thomas finished speaking—flatly, plainly, the way he told a crew a storm was coming—no one said anything for the space of ten breaths. Then Dai Prosser, who stevedored and stammered unless he was reading aloud, said, "We could take the rolls out. Just the rolls."

"And leave the presses? Then they burn the yard anyway, out of spite, and we've saved nothing but paper."

"Morgan's barge—"

"No." Thomas heard his own voice hit the table and was surprised at himself. He softened it. "No, Dai. Listen to me. If we scatter, this dies. Not pauses—dies. They win without striking a blow. But if they strike us, in our own yard, in cold blood—" he looked around the lamplit faces, men he'd known since they were boys hauling rope, "—then the harbor knows what we've known all along. That this isn't about pamphlets. It never was. It's about whether working folk are allowed to have a voice at all. And a burned workshop makes more noise than a thousand printed pages ever could."

"It makes ash," said old Rees, the caulker, who had survived the *Great Britain*'s engine room and three wives and trusted nothing that burned. "Ash and dead men, if you're standing in it."

"I'm not asking anyone to stand in it." Thomas met the old man's eye. "I'm telling you what I'm going to do, so you can't say you weren't told. Festival night, I'll be here. Somebody has to mind the house."

Rees spat into the stove, which was his way of voting. Dai looked at his hands. And young Owen, the cooper's son who did the typesetting and had ink permanently under his nails like a stain of grace, said quietly: "Then I'll be here too. Someone has to carry the rolls out if it comes to it, and I know the loft better than anyone."

Thomas wanted to refuse him. He opened his mouth to refuse him. But he remembered being nineteen and furious and not believed, and he closed his mouth again, and nodded, and hated himself for nodding, because nodding meant accepting that the boy might burn.

"Oilcloths on the strongbox," he said instead. "Wet blankets in the loft, by the stair. And Owen—if it catches, you drop the box and you go out the water door, and you don't come back for anything, not for me, not for the type, not for anything. Swear it."

Owen swore it, bright-eyed and lying, and Thomas knew he was lying, and let it lie, because there are prayers you accept knowing God hasn't signed them.

* * *

Later, alone, he climbed to the loft and stood among the tarred canvas and the hanging spars and looked down at the dark workshop floor, silvered faintly by the harbor light through the high windows. The press waited under its sheet like a covered body. Four hundred names slept in their strongbox three feet from his boot.

He thought about the thin man's card feeding the stove flames. He thought about Elodie's careful face, and the way the young man's hands had pressed the bench as though trying to hold the world still. *You'd risk four hundred names for a principle.* The boy had meant it as a rebuke. Thomas took it as a compliment.

His father had told him once, mending net by lamplight, that a man's reputation is the only boat he ever truly owns—everything else leaks, sinks, gets repossessed—but the reputation you build by standing still when standing still costs most, that's the hull that carries you across. Thomas hadn't understood it at nineteen. He understood it now, standing in the loft of a building he already knew was condemned, and the understanding felt like ballast: heavy, and steadying, and taking on water slowly enough that he could pretend otherwise.

Below, in the town, someone was letting off a single early rocket—festival practice, probably, a green star bursting over the rooftops and fading. Two days. Two days and the sky would be full of light and nobody would see the fire.

Thomas put his palm flat against the loft beam, wood his grandfather had adzed, and made the only promise left to make: that whatever came, the rolls would not burn while he drew breath. Then he went down, banked the stove, checked the water door, and walked home through the fog past the Pilot's Rest, where through the window he saw a familiar slim figure in a driver's cap sitting alone with an untouched glass, staring at the middle distance like a man rehearsing a speech he hoped he'd never give.

Their eyes met through the glass. Neither raised a hand. But each knew the other had seen, and both understood that between them now lay two days, a promise, and a fire that only one of them intended to survive.
