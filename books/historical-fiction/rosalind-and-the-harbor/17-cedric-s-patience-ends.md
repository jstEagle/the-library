## 17. Cedric Patience Ends

The letter from his father arrived on a Tuesday morning, and Cedric Harrow knew before he broke the seal that it would not be kind. His father's letters never were. They came on heavy cream stock from the Queen Square office, written in the hand of Mr. Pellow the secretary because Edmund Harrow considered his own time worth four guineas an hour and his son's worth rather less, and they always began with the same salutation—*Cedric*—no *my dear*, no *dear boy*, nothing so warm as an adjective between the name and the comma. Just the name, dropped onto the page like a coin onto a counter.

*Cedric:*

*I have received the quarterly figures for the harbor frontage and I do not like them. The workshops continue to occupy ground we have wanted for eleven years. The tenants show no inclination to leave. You assured me in March that your arrangements were progressing.*

*The shipping season opens the first week of May. I will have the frontage cleared, or I will have an explanation of why it is not. I am too old to be patient with explanations that are really apologies.*

*Cedric.*

He read it twice at his desk in the Clifton house, with the morning light coming grey through windows that looked out over the Suspension Bridge and the gorge, and he felt the familiar sensation of being twelve years old again, standing in this same room while his father explained to him, without anger and therefore without mercy, exactly how much of his allowance he had forfeited by some failure or other. His father did not shout. Shouting was for men who lacked leverage. Edmund Harrow simply stated what was true, arranged matters so that the truth became expensive, and waited.

Cedric set the letter down and pressed his palms flat against the blotter until the leather creaked.

Eleven years the workshops had squatted on Harrow ground. Eleven years of leases let at rates so low they amounted to charity, back when his grandfather had been soft-hearted about the boatwrights and chandlers who'd served the family's own ships since before anyone could remember. His grandfather had called it loyalty. His father called it sunk capital. And now the whole strip of harbor wall—from the dry dock to the old rope walk—was worth more than everything it produced, if only the people in it could be made to go away.

They would not go away. That was the trouble with the lower sort; they mistook endurance for entitlement. Forty years a man patched hulls on the same slipway and he began to think the slipway belonged to him, and then along came someone like Thomas Idris, foreman and self-appointed philosopher, telling him that indeed it did, that labor owned what labor built, and printing pamphlets by lamplight to prove it. The union had crept into the workshops like damp into timber. You couldn't see it and you couldn't reason with it, but you could feel the structure going soft wherever it reached.

Cedric had been patient. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in some fair ledger of his own keeping: he had been patient for eight months. He had hired the right men—not bruisers off the quay but quiet professionals out of London and Newport, men who understood that the object was pressure, not violence. Pressure applied correctly looked like coincidence: a workshop burgled and its tools spoiled; a chandler's credit withdrawn by three suppliers in the same fortnight; rumors seeded in the public houses that the leases were ending, that the land was sold, that the writing was on the wall whether one could read it or not. Two families had already gone. A cooper and a sailmaker, both older men, both tired. It had felt, in the autumn, like progress.

But the core held. Idris held. And the printers—the printers were the cancer proper, the thing that metastasized outward into every yard and warehouse along the Frome, and the printers were not frightened of anything so genteel as pressure.

His father wanted the frontage cleared by May. The shipping season opened the first week of May. Which meant, when you did the arithmetic honestly—and Cedric prided himself on doing arithmetic honestly, it was the one discipline his father had ever praised in him—that the workmen would have to be gone by late April at the outside, to allow time for surveyors and clearance crews and the first piles of the new transit shed.

It was the third week of February.

He sat for a long while looking at the letter, and then he took out a sheet of paper and began, in pencil, to make lists. Lists steadied him. Lists turned a problem from a fog into a route.

*What has failed:* intimidation, economic strangulation, rumor. Each method had been deniable and each had been insufficient. The tenants dug in deeper with every turn of the screw, because Idris told them the digging-in was itself the victory, and because the union paid out strike funds and hardship money like a church paying out alms, and there was always another subscription list circulating somewhere among the sympathizers in Redcliffe and Bedminster. Among them, if the intelligence was correct, the Vane woman's people. Money moved through that network the way blood moves through a body, invisibly and everywhere.

He underlined the last thought twice. The Vane woman. Rosalind Vane, whose name appeared in the newspapers monthly, whose speeches drew women in feathered hats from Clifton down to the docks to hear about the franchise, whose committees had lately taken up the cause of the harbor workers with an enthusiasm that made the papers call her *the friend of Bristol labour*. She was, in herself, nearly harmless—a speech could not stop a wrecking crew—but she was a lens. She focused attention. Attention was the one commodity the operation could not afford.

And she had a motorcar now, and a driver, and the driver had a habit of lingering near things he ought not linger near. Cedric had noticed him twice in January, once outside the shipping office on a day when no Vane business could plausibly have brought him there, and once on Prince Street, idling opposite the counting-house window with the stillness of a man memorizing something. Servants who watched were either curious or bought, and curiosity in a servant was merely purchase waiting for a price. He had given instructions that the man be identified. The report had come back thin: name unverified, engaged within the past year, references impeccable in the way that cost money to fabricate. *Impeccable references* meant somebody had wanted him placed, or wanted himself placed, and neither possibility comforted Cedric.

But that was a thread to pull later. First, the arithmetic.

He turned to a fresh page.

*What remains:* force. Not the deniable kind—he had exhausted the market for deniable force, and besides, deniable force had been tried and found too slow. What remained was the undisguisable kind, the kind that announced itself in smoke and could afterward be attributed to accident, negligence, or the natural carelessness of men who kept oil and pitch and open flame in timber buildings by a working waterfront. A fire. The word sat on the page in his own handwriting and he regarded it the way a surgeon regards the site of an incision: without squeamishness, without pleasure, with a professional attention to where the cut must fall and what it must sever.

A fire solved everything at once. It cleared the frontage. It destroyed the presses—Idris's precious membership rolls, his type, his distribution lists, fifteen years of the union's institutional memory, all of it combustible. It scattered the tenants, because men whose livelihoods burned tended not to rebuild on the same spot, especially when the landlord declined, with every appearance of regret, to renew their leases. Insurance? There would be questions, but the workshops carried their own insurance, not his; Harrow & Sons had no policy on property it did not insure, and the inquiry—if it came to an inquiry—would find a building old, ill-wired, lit by gas and lamp, crowded with tar and turpentine and the resinous offcuts of shipwrighting. An electrical fault, even. The papers loved *electrical fault*. It had a modern, blameless sound, like weather.

The risk was witnesses. And here, turning the problem over, Cedric found the shape of the answer arriving with the smooth inevitability of a tide.

Bristol Harbor Festival. The second Saturday of April. Every year the quays filled from dawn to midnight—bunting, brass bands, the rowing regatta, the fireworks over the Basin at nine o'clock sharp, ten thousand people packed shoulder to shoulder with their faces turned up toward the sky. Ten thousand faces turned upward, all at once, at the same hour, by design and by tradition, as reliable as the tide tables.

No one watched a warehouse during fireworks. No one watched anything. And afterward, in the confusion, the smoke rising over the harbor would be one spectacle mistaken for another, a rocket gone astray, a squib fallen short, and by the time anyone understood what they were seeing the building would be past saving and the night would already be writing its own account of events.

He wrote the date at the top of the page. April the twelfth. Seven weeks. It was sooner than he had planned—his plan had run through spring, a slow tightening through March, the last families squeezed out by Easter—but his father's letter had ended patience, and Cedric discovered, examining himself with the same cool attention he gave everything else, that he did not much mourn it. Eight months of pressure had taught him something about himself: that he was good at this. Not the squalid parts—the hired men and their tavern conferences bored him—but the architecture of it. The sequencing. The way a situation, properly mapped, contained its own solution the way a lock contained the shape of its key. He had spent his life being measured against a father who built ships and fortunes and had no talent left over for sons, and here at last was a field in which the measuring instrument was Cedric alone.

There was a version of this where he felt shame. He was aware of that version the way one is aware of a room through a wall—knowing it existed, hearing the muffled sounds of it, declining to enter. The men in those workshops were, in the aggregate, decent enough. Idris himself was even admirable, in the way a stubborn mule is admirable. But admiration was not jurisdiction, and sentiment was not strategy, and the world did not arrange itself around the feelings of boatwrights. His grandfather had built the family's position by buying cheap hulls off men desperate after wrecks. His father had built it by breaking the 1891 strike with blacklegs shipped up from Cardiff. Every fortune in England sat on ground cleared by someone, of someone. The workshops' fire would simply be this generation's installment on the same old mortgage. History was not a moral enterprise. It was a clearing enterprise.

He rang for Pellow—or rather, he composed a reply to his father and sent it down to Queen Square by hand, three lines only, because brevity in correspondence was another inheritance: *Your figures are received. The matter will be resolved before the season opens. Expect no further correspondence on the subject.* He wondered if his father would hear, in the flat finality of it, an echo of his own voice. He hoped so. It was the nearest thing to affection available between them.

Then he went down into the city to make the arrangements himself, because the one lesson of eight months' patience was that arrangements delegated were arrangements diluted.

* * *

The man from Newport was called Llewellyn, which was almost certainly not his name, and he drank his beer in a private parlor of the Anchor on Welsh Back with the unhurried air of a tradesman pricing a job. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the forearms of a man who had once done honest violence for a living and had since graduated to arranging it. He listened without interrupting, which Cedric had learned to value above intelligence or courage.

"The festival," Llewellyn said, when Cedric had finished. "You want it done while the town's watching rockets."

"I want it done while the town is watching anything else."

"Understood." Llewellyn turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table. "It's a different animal, though. The other work—the frighteners, the break-ins—you can stand off from that. Distance. This is close work. Close work means close men, and close men mean witnesses I can't screen, because half of Bristol will be on the quay that night."

"Which is precisely the point of choosing it."

"Aye." A pause. "And the point of my saying so is that the price changes."

"Name it."

Llewellyn named it. Cedric did not blink, though the figure was steep enough to sting even Harrow money. What he heard underneath the number was the real quotation: Llewellyn was charging him for the difference between mischief and arson, between a job a magistrate might wave through and a job that, gone wrong, ended at the end of a rope. The price was honest. Honesty in that trade, as in any other, was worth paying for.

"One condition," Llewellyn said. "My men don't know the source. They know a client. The client has a name and the name isn't yours. When it's done, the account closes and we haven't met."

"You've done this before."

"I've done work before." The grey eyes were level. "Some kinds once."

They settled it. The twelfth of April. Llewellyn would put two men inside the workshops' orbit over the following weeks—casuals taken on for festival repairs, a carter, whoever the traffic allowed—to learn the premises, the watchman's habits, the state of the locks and the cellars. The workshops stored pitch, oakum, linseed oil, and spirit varnish in quantities that would make the rest a question of placement and timing. The fire would begin low and aft, in the old boat shed where the night printing was done, so that the flames would find the paper stores first and the evidence with them.

"And the Vane woman's driver," Cedric said, as Llewellyn stood to go. "The one who lingers. Keep my people's eyes on him. If he's watching the workshops, I want to know what he sees and who he tells. Quietly. Nothing rough—not yet. A servant who watches is either a spy or a seller, and either way he'll show his hand if we give him rope."

Llewellyn nodded once and went out into the rain-smell of Welsh Back, and Cedric sat alone in the parlor with the dregs of his own untouched drink and looked out the window at the harbor.

* * *

The harbor in February was iron-grey and working, cranes swinging their slow cargoes, steam tugs shouldering upriver against the ebb, gulls hanging motionless over the refuse barges like scraps of torn paper caught on wind. Along the eastern wall the workshops showed their long low roofs and their forest of mast-timbers and spars, smoke standing up straight from the forge chimney in the still air. Even from here he could pick out the details his reports had made familiar: the boat shed with its big double doors facing the water, the loft above it where the type was kept, the drying lines where sails hung like the shrouds of hanged men.

Seven weeks.

He walked the length of the frontage slowly, hands in his coat pockets, hat brim low, a gentleman slumming it on a wet afternoon and drawing no more notice than any other gentleman doing the same. At the dry dock, boys were caulking a barge, singing something tuneless. Outside the chandlery, a woman scolded a child in Welsh. On the workshop steps, a man he recognized from photographs and descriptions—Idris, broad and grey-bearded, sleeves rolled despite the cold—stood talking with two others, gesturing with a piece of chalk as though the air itself were a blackboard.

Cedric stopped across the lane and looked at him for a long moment.

Here was the whole difficulty of the age in one standing figure: a competent man, an honest man by his lights, holding ground that history had priced out from under him and calling the holding a principle. In twenty years Idris would be a footnote or a martyr, and either way the sheds would be a transit warehouse, and the ships his father would build on the cleared ground would carry cargo Idris's grandchildren would load. The machine did not require Idris's hatred. It did not even require his understanding. It required only his absence.

Idris laughed at something—one of the men had said something funny—and the laugh carried across the lane, easy and unguarded, the laugh of a man who believed the future was a place where people like him got to keep what they had built.

Cedric turned up his collar and walked on. He did not hate the man. That was the strange thing he would never be able to explain to anyone who asked, if anyone ever asked: there was no heat in it at all. The workshops were an obstruction; obstructions were removed; the removing had a schedule, and the schedule now had a date, and the date was chosen not for cruelty but for cover, because the festival's fireworks would give ten thousand witnesses somewhere else to look.

Behind him the forge smoke stood straight up into the grey sky, a dark thread stitching the workshops to the weather, and Cedric Harrow walked back toward Clifton already composing, in the orderly ledger of his mind, the sequence of the seven weeks to come: the men placed by the middle of March, the premises mapped by the end of it, the last pressure applied to the remaining tenants in the festival week itself so that the fire would arrive as the final blow rather than the first. And threaded through all of it, quietly, the matter of the Vane motorcar's driver, who watched, and who would need watching, and who might yet prove to be either the loosest thread in Rosalind Vane's respectable weave or the sharpest knife in it.

He did not yet know which. But he had seven weeks to find out, and Cedric Harrow had discovered that he was very good at finding out.
