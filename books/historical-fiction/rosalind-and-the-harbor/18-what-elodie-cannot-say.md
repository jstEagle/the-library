## 18. What Elodie Cannot Say

The house on Berkeley Square had a particular silence after eleven o'clock, and Elodie Galloway had learned its grammar the way other men learned French. There was the settling of pipes in the walls—a low complaint, like an old man shifting in his sleep. There was Mrs. Quill's last circuit of the ground floor, her keys chiming against her hip in the pattern of a woman locking away not just silver but certainty. And there was, most nights, the single lamp that burned in Rosalind Vane's study until one or two in the morning, its light leaking under the door into the corridor like water finding the lowest seam.

He told himself it was habit that took him past her door. He told himself a great many things.

The truth was that the letter—the second letter, the one with *fire* written in a solicitor's careful hand—had been living inside him for nine days now, and it was beginning to digest him from within. He had copied it by lamplight in the coach house, his hand so unsteady that two words required rewriting. He had hidden the copy in the lining of his coat, then moved it, because a coat could be taken from a man's back; then moved it again to the false bottom of his bootblack box; then, finally, begun carrying pages of it down to the harbor in installments, folded small as communion wafers, slipped between the ruled columns of the pilot ledgers where old Mr. Tredinnick recorded tides and wind and the comings and goings of every vessel in the roadstead. The ledgers were copied quarterly, archived, never destroyed. Official paper was the one thing in Bristol no one dared burn.

But evidence hidden is not a warning delivered. That distinction had been gnawing at him for days, wearing through his sleep like damp through plaster.

Tonight, the lamp was still burning.

* * *

He knocked. It was a liberty—servants did not knock on their mistress's study door at half past midnight—but fifteen years had taught Elodie which liberties were invisible and which were load-bearing, and this one, tonight, would bear weight.

"Come."

She was at her desk with her collar open and her hair coming loose from its pins, surrounded by papers the way a general is surrounded by maps. A half-finished speech lay before her, its margins dense with her own corrections. She looked up at him and something in her face rearranged itself—not softening exactly, but a kind of attention she gave no one else in the household, the attention of a woman who knew precisely what she was looking at.

"Galloway. At this hour?"

"The motorcar wants looking at," he said. "I heard something in the differential today. On the Clifton road."

It was even true. He had heard it. He had also spent forty minutes that afternoon lying on the cobbles beneath the chassis finding nothing wrong, because his hands needed occupation while his mind turned the same wheel.

"And you've come to tell me about a differential." She set down her pen. "At midnight."

"I've come because I couldn't sleep," he said, which was also true, and truer.

She studied him. He felt the study of it the way one feels weather change—pressure first, then wind. Rosalind Vane missed nothing; that was the engine of her whole life. She read rooms the way he read letters, quickly and completely, and she had never once pretended otherwise in front of him, which was perhaps why he had loved her for six years without ever once saying a word about it aloud, even to himself, except in the dark behind his eyes where all his true accounts were kept.

"Sit down, Galloway."

"There's a chair—"

"You're standing like a man at a dockside inquiry. Sit."

He sat. The fire in the grate had burned down to a red memory. Somewhere above them a floorboard ticked.

"I want to ask you something," she said, "and I want you to answer me as though we were two people who had known each other longer than we have admitted to knowing each other." She folded her hands on the desk. "Is there something happening in this house? In my house. Something I should know."

The question landed in the room like a coin dropped down a well. He listened for the bottom.

"What makes you ask, ma'am?"

"Don't *ma'am* me. Not now." But there was no anger in it—there was something worse, something patient. "Three weeks ago you started taking the long way to the harbor when I sent you on errands that didn't need the long way. You've stopped humming. You hummed for six years, badly, the same four songs, and now you don't. You read my correspondence faster than ever and your face has gone more blank than ever, and Elodie Galloway, blankness is a face too. I've learned to read it." She leaned forward slightly. "Something is wrong. Something connected to the harbor. And you are carrying it around this house like a stone in your shoe, and it is making you limp, and I am tired of watching you limp."

He looked at his hands. They were steady now—they had learned steadiness the way other boys learned courage, out of necessity—and he made himself look up at her instead, because if he was going to do what he had come to do, he owed her his eyes at least.

"If a man knew something," he said carefully, "something that wasn't his to know. Through his work. Through—" he gestured vaguely, hating the vagueness, "—through paper. Letters. If what he knew touched people he—" *loved*, said the word silently, and swallowed it whole. "Touched people. People who were working toward something larger than themselves. And if speaking would protect them but might damage the work—might cost the work everything, right at the moment it could least afford a cost—what should that man do?"

Rosalind went very still.

It was a beautiful stillness. He had seen her deploy versions of it in drawing rooms full of hostile MPs' wives, in committee meetings where men spoke over her, on platforms in towns where the stones thrown preceded the invitations. Stillness was her armor and her weapon both. But this stillness was different. This one was aimed at him.

"You tell me," she said quietly. "You're the one who knows how stories end. You read enough of them."

"That isn't fair."

"No," she agreed. "But neither is asking me that question without telling me why you're asking it." She rose and came around the desk—not close, but closer, near enough that the firelight found the lines of her face that daylight was too polite to show. "So let me make it easier for you. Hypothetically. If a man in your position learned of a threat—to persons, to property—in connection with my work, and he brought it to me, what do I imagine would happen?" She held up a hand before he could speak. "I'll answer. I would act. Immediately, possibly rashly, certainly publicly, because that is who I am and I have never once managed to be anyone else. And my committee would spend a fortnight explaining to me, with great love and greater condescension, that I had handed the Harrows exactly the crisis they wanted. That the franchise bill comes back to the Commons in"—she glanced at the calendar on her wall, and her voice changed, flattened, became the voice of the woman on the platform rather than the woman at midnight—"in five weeks. Five weeks, Galloway. Fifteen years of organizing, three hundred branches, women jailed and force-fed in cells not forty miles from this room, all of it balanced on a bill that will pass or fail in five weeks. So when you ask me what a man should do with dangerous knowledge, understand what you're really asking me. You're asking whether I would trade the largest thing any of us will ever be part of for the safety of—"

She stopped.

The sentence hung there, unfinished, and they both looked at the place where it had been. For the safety of whom? For the safety of what? Her eyes moved across his face and he watched her arrive somewhere she hadn't planned to go, some country beyond the question, and he saw—clearly, helplessly—that she understood more than he had ever wanted her to understand, and less than he desperately needed her to.

"For the safety of the people involved," she finished, and her voice was perfectly level, and it cost her something visible to keep it so.

"Yes," he said. "That."

They stood in the ruined quiet. The fire shifted. Down in the street a late cab rattled past, its horse's hooves marking time like a metronome counting down something.

"Tell me plainly," she said. "Whatever it is. Tell me plainly and let me decide what it costs. That is all I have ever asked of anyone, Galloway—all I have ever demanded of the men who claim to speak for me. Plain speech and the right to weigh my own risks. Will you deny me what I demand of Parliament?"

And God help him, he almost did it. The words were right there, loaded, his finger already on the trigger of them—*the Harrows mean to burn the workshops, there is a list, your name is on it, they intend to destroy you with forged letters and I have read every word of the plan in their solicitor's hand.* Nine days of stone in the shoe. He opened his mouth—

"In five weeks," he said instead, and hated himself with a completeness that surprised him.

Her expression didn't change, but something behind it closed, the way a shopkeeper closes a till.

"In five weeks," she repeated. "Yes. Which is why whatever it is must be handled properly, not shouted from a rooftop. Which is why I need to know *now*, while there is still time to be clever about it."

"Cleverness takes time," he said. "Sometimes the clever thing and the safe thing are the same thing, ma'am, and sometimes a man has to trust—"

"*Trust.*" She laughed, short and without humor. "You come to my door at midnight with a hypothetical wrapped in seven layers of cotton wool, and you ask me to trust you. Do you know what I do all day, Galloway? I trust. I trust women I've known for a decade not to break under prison feeding tubes. I trust printers not to sell my membership lists to the police for drink money. I trust my own committee not to sell *me*. Trust is my profession." She stepped nearer, and now her voice dropped, and the platform-woman was gone entirely, and what remained was only Rosalind, tired, at midnight, in a cold room. "And you—I trust you more than any of them, which should frighten us both, because you are the one person in my life whose entire existence is built on not being trusted with anything. You read what you read. You forget what you forget. You burn what you burn. I have watched you do it for six years and I have never once asked what passed through your hands, because asking would make me complicit in something I'm not certain I want named." Her jaw tightened. "So don't stand in my study lecturing me about trust. You of all people."

The words went in clean and deep, the way true things do.

He stood up. His knees felt strange, borrowed.

"You're right," he said. "All of it. Every word." He moved toward the door, and then stopped, with his hand on the frame, not turning around, because he couldn't say the next part to her face. "There's one more thing. A piece of advice, free of charge, from a man whose opinion costs nothing. If you walk the harbor wall on Sunday—if you take your walks along the water the way you do—you might consider walking them elsewhere for a while. Just for a few weeks. The air down there is changing. Some airs carry sparks."

Behind him, the silence stretched so long he thought she wouldn't answer.

"Elodie." She almost never used his name. When she did, it landed on him like a hand on the shoulder. "Look at me."

He looked at her.

"Whatever you cannot say," she said slowly, "I want you to understand that I can see its shape. I have been able to see its shape for days. And I am choosing—for reasons I will examine very closely tomorrow, when I am less tired and more honest—not to demand it of you tonight. But understand this." She came toward him, and stopped just outside the distance at which either of them could pretend the closeness meant nothing. "If harm comes to those people because information sat unspoken in this house—in *my* house, in *your* mouth—it will not be the movement that fails you, or the bill, or the timing. It will be me. I will fail myself. Do you understand the difference? You are protecting me from a choice. I would rather have the choice and lose than be protected and win."

"Yes, ma'am," he said, which was cowardice dressed in deference, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so.

She held his eyes a moment longer—holding, holding, the way one holds a match after the flame catches, deciding whether to shake it out—and then she turned back to her desk and picked up her pen, and the interview was over in the way of things that are never actually over.

"Good night, Galloway. Mind the differential."

"Good night, ma'am."

* * *

The coach house was dark and smelled of oil and leather and the ghost of burnt letters. He lit no lamp. He sat on the step of the motorcar's running board in the dark with his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands, and he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for nine days.

She had given him the choice. She had said, in her iron midnight voice, *I would rather have the choice and lose than be protected and win,* and he had walked out anyway, because she had also said *five weeks*, and *three hundred branches*, and *force-fed in cells not forty miles from this room*, and every one of those phrases was a wall between him and the door marked PLAIN SPEECH. Love against duty, she'd have called it, if anyone wrote novels about the likes of him. But it was worse than that. It was love *as* duty, both directions, each of them sacrificing the other to something larger and calling the sacrifice respect.

A warning delivered softly is a warning not delivered. Thomas Idris had his note—*check your premises at night*—and Thomas had read it, nodded once across the workshop bench, and kept printing. A note was not enough. A hint was not enough. Tonight proved it: he had stood three feet from her, with the whole plot in his mouth like a hot coal, and he had blown on it gently and put it back.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow I find another way. Not the front door—she watches the front door—but some way to make the danger undeniable without making himself the message. Because a messenger could be intercepted, discredited, disappeared. A messenger was a name on someone's list, and he had spent fifteen years ensuring that no list anywhere contained his name.

He got up, checked the motorcar's differential out of pure superstition, and let himself out the side gate onto the mews.

The fog had come in off the river again, thick and yellowish under the gas lamps. He walked fast, collar up, thinking of pilot ledgers and tide tables and the page still folded in his boot, and it was not until he reached the corner of Queen Street that he understood the footsteps behind him were not echoes.

They had matched his pace for two streets. They stopped when he stopped, under a lamp, in the fog, and did not start again.

Elodie stood very still in the yellow light, listening, and heard—one street over, closing slowly, unhurried as a tide—the sound of a second set of boots.
