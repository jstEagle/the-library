## 20. Midpoint: The Festival Eve

### I. Rosalind

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Rosalind Vane finally set down her pen.

She read the speech again from the beginning, the way she always did—aloud, quietly, her voice pitched for an audience of one empty chair. The words were good. She had made them good through three drafts and one small act of theft, borrowing the rhythm of a hymn she'd heard sung at a suffrage meeting in Manchester years ago, before Bristol, before the motorcar, before any of it. Rhythm was half of persuasion. The other half was knowing what the crowd already wanted to say and giving them the courage to say it first.

*We are told that patience is a woman's virtue.* She let the sentence sit on her tongue. Tomorrow, from the festival platform, with the harbor spread out behind her and two thousand people packed along the quays, it would do its work.

Tomorrow. The harbor festival. Lamps strung from the cranes like fruit, the bandstand draped in bunting, the whole waterfront given over to music and fried fish and children with paper windmills—and her, standing at the center of it, delivering the most important speech of her career to an audience who had come for fireworks and would stay, if she was any judge of crowds, for something larger.

She should have been asleep hours ago. Instead she sat at her desk in the blue hour before dawn, listening to the house settle around her, and found her mind circling the same unease that had dogged her all week.

Elodie.

He had been strange lately—or rather, he had been exactly as he always was, and she had only now begun to notice how much strangeness that constancy required. A man who never asked questions. A man whose eyes went soft and careful whenever she spoke of the workshops, as though he were handling something breakable. A man who drove her past the harbor wall at precisely the hour the workshop lamps came on, and whose hands—she had watched those hands for two years now, the way one watches weather—had gone still on the wheel last Tuesday in a way that meant he was thinking hard about something he could not say.

There was more in him than driving. She had known it since the first month, when she'd left a letter open on the carriage seat and watched his eyes move across it once, briefly, involuntarily—the flicker of a man reading by instinct, the way some men breathe. She had never confronted him with it. Naming a thing gave it weight, and weight, in her experience, either anchored a person or dragged them under. Elodie Galloway was the steadiest creature in her household. She would not test the anchor.

But tonight, drafting and redrafting sentences about courage, she kept thinking of his face in the lamplight, and of the anonymous note she'd received ten days ago in handwriting she couldn't place but somehow almost could, and of the false errand she'd sent him on—the decoy delivery to Clifton, the address that didn't exist—and how cleanly he had passed the test, and how much the passing had cost him. She'd seen it in his shoulders as he'd walked back to the motorcar. Relief and grief wearing the same coat.

*You know something,* she thought at the empty chair, at the dark window, at the harbor she could not see but could hear, the water working against the pilings even now. *And you have decided I am safer not knowing.*

It was, she admitted, the same arithmetic her committee performed every month. Silence as protection. Omission as strategy. She had defended that arithmetic in smoke-filled rooms full of intelligent, decent women. It was strange how much colder it felt when it was practiced upon herself.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, the speech waited on the desk, finished and true, and somewhere below her window the motorcar stood in the coach house with its tank full and its tires checked twice, because Elodie checked everything twice, because Elodie missed nothing—

—and she fell asleep on that thought, or near it, without following it to the end.

### II. Cedric

Cedric Harrow had learned early that the secret to doing terrible things well was to schedule them like everything else.

His father's study smelled of leather and pipe tobacco and the particular dust of ledgers going back ninety years, and it was here, at half past eleven on festival eve, that the plan received its final blessing. Not a blessing—his father did not bless things; he approved invoices. "The season opens Monday," the old man said, not looking up from the shipping manifest. "If there's a delay, it comes off your portion."

"There won't be a delay." Cedric stood with his hands behind his back, a posture he'd practiced until it looked natural rather than defensive. "The works are scheduled for eleven o'clock. The fireworks begin at ten-thirty and run a full forty minutes. Every soul on that quay will be looking at the sky."

"And the insurance?"

"Filed last week. Electrical fault, aging wiring, the usual story. The surveyor owes me a favor and will find what he's told to find." Cedric permitted himself the smallest smile. "By the time the smoke clears, the workshop will be a matter for the coroner's office, not the papers. And whatever pamphlet-printing went on in there dies with the building."

His father turned a page. "The Vane woman."

"Handled separately." This was the part Cedric enjoyed describing least, because it was the part he understood least well. The letters were being forged by a man in London whose hand could imitate anyone's, and they would surface at the inquiry—*if* there was an inquiry, which after a simple fire there need not be—proving that Rosalind Vane had taken union subscriptions into her private account. Elegant. Deniable. And yet Cedric had lain awake two nights this week with the sensation of a loose thread somewhere in the weave.

It was the driver. He kept coming back to the driver.

Twice now he had seen the Vane motorcar idling too long within sight of the workshops. Twice the driver had been watching—not gawking the way festival visitors gawked, but watching with the flat attention of a man counting windows. Today, near the coal merchant's yard, their eyes had met across twenty feet of cobbles, and the driver had not looked away fast enough. Then he had looked away very fast indeed, which was worse.

"Father," Cedric said. "The Vane household. How much do we actually know about their staff?"

"Nothing. Why would we?"

"Their driver has been loitering near the workshops."

"Drivers loiter. It's what they do while their betters shop." His father finally looked up, and his eyes held the weary contempt he reserved for his son's occasional flashes of imagination. "One man in a cap is not a conspiracy. Finish the job tomorrow and stop inventing enemies. Inventing enemies is how a family loses what it's built."

*Built,* Cedric thought, walking home afterward through streets already sprouting bunting. As though the Harrow fortune had been constructed rather than extracted—as though his great-grandfather had laid bricks instead of buying sailors' wages cheap and drowning ships for the insurance. But his father was right about one thing: tomorrow would finish it. Eleven o'clock. Forty minutes of fireworks. A city watching the sky.

He paused at his door and looked back toward the harbor, where the workshop windows glowed with late light, and for one unaccountable moment he imagined someone inside them looking back at him, pen in hand, writing everything down.

Then he laughed at himself, went inside, and poured a drink.

### III. Thomas

Thomas Idris worked the grease rag along the big press's iron spine with the tenderness other men saved for horses and daughters.

"Forty years," he told the press, because at this hour there was no one else to tell. "Forty years you've been throwing type, and not once have you jammed on me the night before I needed you. Don't start tomorrow."

Around him the workshop breathed its night sounds—the tick of cooling boilers, the harbor lapping at the slipway, old Prosser snoring in the loft where he slept three nights a week since his wife died. Tomorrow the presses would be silent. Tomorrow was the festival, and Thomas had promised the committee no printing until Monday, because a man could not run contraband pamphlets on the one night the entire harbor establishment strolled past his door eating roast chestnuts. So tonight was maintenance. Greasing, cleaning, the rollers wrapped in oilcloth, the type locked in its cases and the cases hidden behind the hull timbers where they'd sat undetected through two previous searches.

They would search again. Thomas could feel it the way he felt weather in his knee—a pressure, a wrongness in the air. Elodie's second note had come yesterday, pushed through the gap under the door in that neat, careful hand: *Check your premises at night. Trust nothing that arrives by water.* No signature, of course. There never was. But Thomas knew the handwriting belonged to the Vane driver, the quiet young man who met your eye when he handed over Miss Vane's letters, and who had once lingered a full minute to ask Thomas whether the apprentices were fed properly, as though the answer mattered to him personally.

Two warnings now. And Thomas had done—what? Checked the locks. Moved the type. Posted Prosser's nephew as a watcher on festival night, a boy of sixteen with a whistle in his pocket.

He had not moved the presses. He had not closed the shop. When the union delegation had come round Tuesday, white-faced, saying *Tom, take the machines upriver until this blows over*, he had heard himself say the thing he'd been building toward for weeks: "A workshop that runs is a workshop that's guilty. They burn us out, we're criminals hiding. We're here in the morning, sweeping up, we're honest tradesmen who suffered a misfortune. The building stays."

He knew how it sounded. He knew, in the part of himself that lay awake after everyone else slept, that pride was a poor substitute for prudence and that sixteen families' roofs hung on his stubbornness. But he also knew this: the pamphlets printed in this room had put bread on tables and spine in men who'd spent their lives apologizing for existing. If he ran—if he folded the presses onto a barge and hid in the reeds like a smuggler—then everything the words had said became a lie the moment the ink dried.

So the presses stayed, greased and ready, and Thomas wiped his hands on the rag and climbed the stairs to check on Prosser, and paused at the window.

The harbor lay black and glittering below. Out past the moored barges, a lantern bobbed—the pilots' boat, making its rounds. And farther off, barely distinguishable, a motorcar stood parked on the far quay with its lamps unlit, and even at this distance Thomas knew the shape of it, because a man knows the shape of anything he's seen enough times.

He watched it for a long moment. It did not move.

"Whatever you're doing, son," Thomas murmured to the dark, "I hope you're doing it carefully."

He banked the boiler, hung the rag on its nail, and went up to sleep the sleep of a man who had made his choice and would live inside it, one way or another, by this time tomorrow night.

### IV. Elodie

The pilot station smelled of tar, tea, and fifty years of pipe smoke soaked into the beams, and old Morgan ap Rhys kept the ledger room locked against everyone except God and the Admiralty—which was why Elodie was copying his final pages by the light of a shielded lamp at one o'clock in the morning, having let himself in with the key Morgan believed was lost.

Three months of work, ending tonight. He turned to the fresh ledger page, ruled and waiting, and wrote in the pilot's own cramped notation the entry he had composed and recomposed a dozen times in his head:

*Tide high, 11:40. Wind SW light. Festival traffic heavy. Note—*

And then the note, buried in the middle of a dozen legitimate observations about moorings and fog signals, in the shorthand of abbreviations only a pilot would read closely:

*—workshop premises (Harbor St.) wired w/ electrical fault per survey 8/11; owner notified 9/11; surveyor's report altered; original in possession of C.H. Insurance filed 12/11. Fire expected. See also p. 114, p. 131.*

Page 114 held the solicitor's letter about "clearance of the harbor workshops." Page 131 held the word *fire*. Both copied out in full, dated, attributed to the sending office, tucked between tide tables where no arsonist would ever think to look and no clerk would ever dare destroy, because the pilot ledgers were law—the harbor's memory, archived and bound, consulted by admiralty men and coroners and historians, immune to burning by sheer institutional boredom.

No one sees the whole, Elodie thought, dipping the pen. That is the whole secret. Everyone sees their piece.

Rosalind saw her speech. He had driven her past the festival grounds at dusk and watched her lips moving through her rehearsal in the passenger seat, her gloved hand keeping time against her knee, and he had fallen in love with her all over again and hated himself for it, because tomorrow night she would stand on that platform and give the speech of her life to a crowd that did not yet know the harbor was about to catch fire behind her.

Cedric Harrow saw his schedule. Elodie had mapped it the way he mapped delivery routes: the hired men lodging above the Anchor Inn, three of them, drinking modestly, tipping badly; the coal cart hired for tomorrow morning with its bed lined in fresh timber; the surveyor's altered report sitting in the Harrow offices like a loaded gun. Eleven o'clock, during the fireworks. He had known it for six days and had done—what? A note to Thomas, softly worded. A boy with a whistle. The small coward's compromise, and now it was too late for anything larger, because warning Thomas harder meant explaining how he knew, and explaining how he knew meant confessing fifteen years of opened letters, and confessing that meant handing the Harrows a weapon to use against Rosalind that was sharper than any forged cheque.

So he copied. It was all he had: paper, patience, and a hiding place no one would ever suspect. If the workshop burned, these pages would outlive the ashes. If Rosalind was disgraced, these pages could prove the disgrace was manufactured. If he himself was caught—well. He wrote the last line, blotted it, and closed the ledger with a care that surprised him, as though shutting a coffin lid gently.

Morgan ap Rhys appeared in the doorway in his nightshirt, holding a candle, unsurprised. He had known about the key for weeks; Elodie had understood this the night the old man had begun leaving the lamp oil filled.

"Finished?" Morgan asked.

"Finished."

The old pilot came forward and rested a knuckle on the ledger's cover, the way another man might touch a Bible. "Fifty years I've written down what the water does," he said. "First time anyone's used my book to write down what men do. About time somebody did." He looked at Elodie then, straight on, with eyes gone pale as sea glass. "There'll be a reckoning over these pages, boy. Books like this don't come out of hiding without someone standing up and saying whose hand is in them. You understand that."

"I understand it."

"Do you?" Morgan set down his candle. "Because I've watched you three months, slipping in here like smoke. You're the best invisible man I ever saw. And the day that ledger goes before an inquiry, invisible stops being available to you. Forever. Make sure you can pay that price before you spend it."

Elodie gathered his tools—the pen, the spare nibs, the scrap of blotting paper—and stood in the doorway a moment, looking back at the shelf of bound volumes, fifty years of tides, his three months of secrets sleeping among them like seeds.

Somewhere across the black harbor, faint as breath, the first strings of the festival band began tuning up.

"I'll pay it," he said, and stepped out into the night that stood between him and tomorrow.
