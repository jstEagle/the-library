## 21. Fireworks and Fire

The grease smelled like victory, Thomas Idris thought, though he knew better than to say so aloud. He worked it into the press screws with a rag gone black as tar, the way he had every festival eve for nine years, because a press that ran smooth was a press that could print five hundred pamphlets before dawn if the mood took it. And tonight the mood would take it. The harbor was already filling with lanterns, with bunting strung between the crane arms like the rigging of some gaudy circus ship, and by midnight every soul in Bristol who wasn't watching the fireworks would be watching the fireworks—which meant nobody at all would be watching the workshops.

That was the whole arithmetic of it. He'd learned his sums in these rooms.

The workshop sat where it had always sat, wedged between the chandler's store and the old rope-walk, its windows fogged with salt and its floor worn into a shine by forty years of boots. By day they repaired boats here—caulked seams, planed keels, did honest work that any harbormaster could inspect and approve of. By night the boats gave way to something else: two hand presses, a cabinet of type, and a ream of paper hidden inside crates marked SPAR OIL. The union's voice came out of this room, page by page, and for nine years no one had stopped it, partly because no one had looked, and partly because men like Thomas made sure looking was difficult.

He stood back from the press and wiped his hands on his apron, taking stock the way he did every night. The type was distributed, each letter sleeping in its compartment. The paper was stacked and covered. The membership rolls—God help him, the rolls—lay in their iron box under the floorboards by the north wall, four hundred names in a ledger bound in sailcloth, every man and woman who'd paid their penny a week and signed their name or made their mark since the winter of '03. Four hundred people who trusted this room with the only proof that they existed and wanted better than they were given.

"You're greasing that screw like it insulted your mother," said Denny Cole from the doorway, and Thomas turned to find half the evening crew behind him, come back after hours in their good coats, faces bright with the festival. Denny was twenty-three and built like a capstan, with a laugh you could hear across the cut, and tonight he'd slicked his hair down for whatever girl was waiting at the fair.

"Press wants respect," Thomas said. "It gives what it gets."

"It gives pamphlets." Denny grinned. "Come on, Tom. The rockets go up at ten. There's beer on the *Bessie* and Mrs. Calloway's saved you a spot by her brazier. Even you're allowed one night."

Thomas looked at him—young, certain, alive in the particular way young men are alive when nothing has ever burned down around them—and felt the old warning move through his chest again, the one that had been stirring all week like weather coming off the channel. The note had come three weeks ago, unsigned, slipped under the door in a neat hand: *Check your premises at night.* Then another, more urgent, left with the boy who swept the chandler's step. *They mean fire. Move what cannot be replaced.*

He'd read both a dozen times. He'd shown them to no one but the committee, and the committee—God forgive them, decent men all—had weighed the risk against the season's printing schedule and decided a warning without a name attached was probably a rival union's mischief, or a crank, or the Harrows testing their nerve. *A workshop that runs is a workshop that's guilty,* Thomas had told them, and meant it. If they emptied the presses and hid the type on account of an anonymous scare, then the next threat, and the next, would win without a match ever being struck. You didn't run from a bully's shadow. Every man on the docks knew what happened to the ones who ran: the bullying followed them home.

But he'd be lying—and he'd promised himself he wouldn't lie to himself, not tonight—if he said the notes hadn't changed how he looked at the room. All week he'd been seeing it as a man sees a house he loves when he knows he may be leaving it. The scarred oak bench where apprentices had carved initials going back past anyone's memory. The window with the cracked pane that whistled in east winds. The smell of ink and oil and hemp that was, as near as anything, the smell of his own life.

"Go on, then," he told Denny. "All of you. Take the night. But someone should mind the premises on a night like this—strangers about, lanterns everywhere."

"I'll stay," offered old Prosser, who was seventy and deaf in one ear.

"You'll go and drink Mrs. Calloway's beer," Thomas said. "I'll mind it. It's my turn anyway."

It was not his turn. They all knew it was never his turn, because he never took one. Denny looked at him a moment too long, and something flickered across the young man's face—not suspicion exactly, but the animal unease of the young in the presence of the old when the old know something they don't. "You'll come down after?" Denny said. "Ten minutes? The first rockets are supposed to be something special this year. Chinese ones, from the new importer."

"Aye," Thomas lied, gently. "Ten minutes."

They went out in a knot of noise and good coats, and the door closed behind them, and the workshop settled into the particular silence it kept at night—the tick of cooling iron, the whisper of water against pilings underneath, the far-off roar of the fair assembling itself along the quay. Thomas locked the door, put the key in his waistcoat pocket where it always lived, and got down on his knees on the cold boards.

* * *

The iron box came up easier than usual; he'd oiled the hinges Tuesday. He lifted out the ledger and set it on the bench, and for a while he just looked at it.

Four hundred names. Some written in copperplate by the clerks who'd helped them early on; more in the blunt laboring hands of the men themselves; and some not names at all but marks, crosses and circles, witnessed and dated, because a third of the harbor couldn't write and everyone pretended otherwise. His own father's mark was in there, from '04, a cross so determined it had torn the page. His mother had signed hers properly—she'd been a schoolmaster's daughter before the sea took the schoolmaster. And there were names of men now dead, women now married away or widowed, boys who'd signed at sixteen and were foremen now. It wasn't a book. It was the harbor's spine.

If the Harrows burned this room, they'd burn the presses—that was loss, but presses could be bought again within the year, and everyone knew it, which was precisely why burning them was only half a plan. What the Harrows needed dead was the rolls. Because a union without its rolls was just a crowd, and a crowd could be dispersed, ignored, hired away. But a union with four hundred signatures in a book could prove it existed, could sue, could strike legally, could stand at an inquiry and say *these are our members, these are our grievances, and here is the proof in our own hands.* The rolls weren't sentiment. The rolls were ammunition.

Which was why, three nights ago, he had finally done the thing the anonymous notes had been begging him to do—not everything, but enough. He'd taken the ledger home wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, sat up till three in the morning in his kitchen with a candle stub and a steady hand, and copied the whole of it. Names, marks, dates, subscriptions owed and paid. Two hundred and six pages of other people's courage, reproduced in his own. The copy lay now in a false bottom of his sea chest at home, under his spare shirts, and tomorrow—no, today, Sunday—he'd carry it to his sister in Clifton, and after that it would live somewhere even the Harrows' imagination couldn't reach.

He'd told no one. Not Denny, not the committee. If he was wrong, he'd have made a fool of himself quietly. If he was right—

The thought hadn't finished when he heard it.

Not a sound, exactly. A smell. Coal oil, sweetish and wrong, threading in under the door from the yard side, where there should have been only salt and tar and the chandler's hemp. Thomas went still, the way he'd gone still once as a boy when a shark's fin had cut the water fifty yards off the stern of his father's trawler. Then he crossed the floor without a light and pressed his eye to the gap beside the door hinge.

Two figures in the yard, by the coal-store wall. One held a lantern shuttered to a blade of light; the other was doing something low against the timbers of the old boat shed that abutted the workshop's north wall—the shed nobody used, full of dry laths and thirty years of shavings. Even at this distance, even in that knife of light, Thomas knew what the man's hands were doing. He'd seen boys do it with straw and a taper behind the net-lofts. Kindling, laid with care. A craftsman's kindling.

The fireworks began.

The first rocket went up over the harbor with a sound like the world tearing its seam, and the sky beyond the high window filled with gold, and the whole room turned briefly the color of brass. Beautiful. God, it was beautiful—burst after burst, willows of green and red falling slow as seaweed, reflected doubled in the black water, and somewhere along the quay thousands of people making one long sound of pleasure. Thomas stood in the middle of his workshop lit by enemy fireworks, and understood with perfect clarity that he had perhaps ninety seconds and that every soul who might have helped him was watching the sky.

*A workshop that runs is a workshop that's guilty.* He heard his own words come back to him, and they sounded, tonight, like a sermon preached by a fool. Because the truth—the truth he'd known since the second note and refused—was that principle doesn't put out fires. Principle is what you say afterward, over the ashes, to make the losing look chosen.

He moved.

The ledger first—it was already out, thank God, already on the bench. He wrapped it in the oilcloth that lay folded over the type cabinet, tied it with the cord he used for bundling pamphlets, and slung it across his chest under his apron, where it rode flat and heavy as a second heart. That took seconds only because he'd rehearsed it in his head all week without admitting he was rehearsing. Then the papers—the loose stock, the printed pamphlets waiting for distribution, the committee's draft of the harbor-wage demand, three months of argument in ink. He crammed what he could into the canvas sack by the door. The type he could not save. The presses he could not save. He had known since Tuesday which thing in this room could not be remade, and he had chosen it, and the choosing sat in him now like swallowed stone.

Behind him, through the wall, came a sound like a long indrawn breath.

Then the light.

It didn't roar, at first. That was the terrible thing, the thing he'd try to explain later to men who hadn't been there: it *began*, quietly, the way dawn begins—a glow through the shed wall, a crackle like a large fire in a small grate, a heat on the back of his neck that arrived before any flame he could see. Then the old shavings caught with a rush and the breath became a roar, and the north wall went from timber to torch in the time it took him to cross the room, and smoke dropped from the rafters like a curtain coming down.

He got the sack to the door and found the handle hot. Wrapped his hand in the apron's skirt, turned it, shouldered out into the yard—into air, into the gold-and-crimson rain of the rockets overhead, into the smell of burning pitch. The yard gate was thirty feet away. Behind him the windows blew out inward with the change of pressure, glass across the cobbles, and the fire found the draft and stood up out of the roof like a thing summoned.

Someone screamed from the direction of the quay—but it was a scream of delight, a rocket scream; the whole harbor was screaming with joy. Nobody was coming. Nobody would come until the fire climbed high enough to be seen above the fireworks, and by then—

The sack was too heavy. He saw it plainly, the way a drowning man sees plainly: the papers dragging at his shoulder, the gate thirty feet off, the heat climbing his back like a hand. He could drop the sack and run clean. Or he could keep the papers and lose the seconds.

He kept the papers.

He'd made his peace with that arithmetic years ago, in a hundred small ways, and he didn't quarrel with it now. But the fire had opinions of its own. As he reached the midpoint of the yard, the shed wall leaned, groaned, and came apart in a column of sparks, and a brand the size of a ship's knee swung out of the dark and caught him across the shoulder and arm. He heard his own flesh hiss. The pain arrived a heartbeat later, vast and white, and his legs went from under him, and the last thing he understood clearly was the weight on his chest—four hundred names riding him down onto the cobbles, keeping him company, keeping him honest—as the sky above him bloomed and bloomed with colors he was too proud, even now, to admit were beautiful.

* * *

Elodie Galloway came out of the crowd like a man swimming against a river, and Thomas, drifting in and out of the dark, would remember fragments only: hands under his shoulders, a voice saying *hold on, hold on, I've got you*—a voice he half-knew, the quiet one, the Vane driver who looked men in the eye—and the strange fact of the fireworks still going, gold and green and indifferent, while the workshop that had printed the harbor's conscience for nine years folded itself into ash and ember behind them. Someone was shouting for water. The sack had landed nearby, scorched but whole. And the weight across his chest, when the hands lifted him, was lighter by nothing at all.

The ledger was safe. He clung to that the way a drowning man clings, because it was the only floating thing left. *Tell them,* he tried to say. *Tell them it was set.* But the words came out as smoke, and the sky went on blooming, and the last he saw before the dark took him proper was a face above his own—young, terrified, familiar in the way of a man seen often and never once spoken to freely—lit gold by the fireworks, wearing an expression Thomas Idris would spend weeks in a hospital ward trying to name.

It looked like guilt. It looked like love. He was too burned to know the difference, and the dark came down like a curtain, and the harbor cheered on.
