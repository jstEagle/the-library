## 22. Ash on the Water

The first rocket went up at half past nine, and Elodie was still a mile away when he saw its bloom reflected in the windows of the terraced houses along the Cut — gold and green, shivering on glass like something drowning.

He had been fighting the crowd since eight o'clock. The whole of Bristol had turned out for the harbor festival, families four deep along the quays, paper lanterns strung from the crane arms, a brass band somewhere behind him mangling a waltz into something martial. Children ran between his legs with sparklers. A woman pressed a cup of hot elderflower wine into his hand at the corner of Welsh Back and he took it without thinking, drank it without tasting, because his mind was not in the crowd at all. His mind was across the water, where the workshops stood dark against the festival lights, where Thomas Idris had greased his presses that afternoon and told Elodie, with a stubbornness like bedrock, *A workshop that runs is a workshop that's guilty.*

*Check your premises tonight,* Elodie had written to him twice now. Twice he had softened the warning until it said almost nothing, because saying more meant becoming someone who said things, and being no one had kept him alive for fifteen years.

The second rocket went up. He heard the crowd's roar roll down the harbor like weather.

And beneath it — faint, so faint he might have invented it — a sound that did not belong to any festival. A low, wet crackle. The smell of pitch smoke threading through the gunpowder haze.

He stopped walking. A man behind him cursed and shouldered past. Elodie stood very still in the press of bodies and let the harbor speak to him the way it had spoken to him as a child, before he had learned to make himself quiet: by smell. Coal tar. Rope. River mud. And under all of it, sharp and new and wrong —

Oil. Burning oil.

He dropped the wine cup and ran.

* * *

The lane behind the workshops was empty. Of course it was empty. Every soul in Bristol with legs and a fancy had gone to the quay wall to watch the sky burn in colors, and the one fire that mattered was starting here, unwatched, in the dark.

Smoke was already curling from the eaves of the long shed, thin as ribbon at first, then thickening, going from grey to black as it found the tarred sacking and the paint stores and the drums of linseed oil Thomas kept against the winter. Elodie hit the side door with his shoulder. It opened onto heat.

Inside, the workshop glowed like the inside of a banked furnace. The fire had started at the far end — the print room, he understood at once, the print room where the presses stood, and whoever had set it had known exactly what they were doing, because the flames were eating toward the membership rolls and the pamphlet stock and everything the harbor movement had written in its own hand for six years. The roof timbers above the print room were already catching, shedding embers that drifted down through the smoke like slow red snow.

"Thomas!" His voice cracked apart in the heat. "THOMAS!"

Something moved by the presses. A shape, low, coughing, dragging itself upright out of the smoke with an armful of ledgers clutched to its chest like a child.

Elodie went in.

The heat was a wall you walked through rather than around; there was no around. It took the air out of his lungs and replaced it with something that seared on the way down. He got his sleeve over his mouth and nose and stumbled forward, following the shape, and the floor gave off a tacky warmth through his boots where the oil had run and caught, little rivers of flame moving along the boards with terrible patience, finding every seam.

Thomas Idris turned, and even through the smoke Elodie could see what the fire had already done to him. The left side of his face was blackened, the skin split and weeping. His shirt had burned away from his forearm to the elbow. But his arms were full of the rolls — the great bound registers, every member of the harbor unions, every name and subscription, fifteen hundred men and women who had signed themselves into existence — and when he saw Elodie he did not reach out for help. He thrust the books at him.

"Take them," Thomas rasped. "Out. Take them out."

"I'm not leaving without you."

"The back door's blocked — I tried—" A fit of coughing bent him double. "The window. Print room window's over the cut. Take the rolls and go, boy, don't argue, this is the only thing that can't be rewritten—"

"I will carry them," Elodie said, "and then I am coming back for you, and if you're not here when I come back I swear on every letter I've ever read that I will come in after you anyway."

For one second, through the smoke and the roar, Thomas looked at him — really looked, the way he always did, the only man in the Vane household's orbit who met Elodie's eyes as though they belonged to a person — and nodded once.

Elodie took the registers. They were heavier than they had any right to be, soaked with heat, their leather covers hot enough to brand. He turned for the main doors and the light through them had gone from orange to white, and he understood with a lurch that he might not get out the way he'd come in. So he didn't try. He went left instead, through the fitting shop, past the lathe he had watched men lean over a hundred mornings, and put his shoulder to the loading door on the water side, the one they used for hauling timber up from the barges.

It gave. Cold air hit his face like a slap and he fell through it, rolled, came up on the cobbles with the rolls clutched to his chest, and behind him the workshop exhaled a column of fire through the broken roof that lit the whole eastern harbor in flickering orange.

Across the water, the third rocket burst. Green stars scattered over the masts. Somewhere a thousand people cheered.

They hadn't seen it. They couldn't see it — the quay wall curved, the warehouses stood between, the fireworks owned the sky. The greatest crime committed on these docks in a generation was happening forty yards from a festival, and the festival was applauding.

Elodie shoved the rolls under the coal cart standing by the yard gate, buried them under sacking, and went back through the loading door.

* * *

Finding Thomas in the smoke was worse than finding him the first time, because now the building was talking to itself — timbers groaning, glass bursting in its frames, the fire moving overhead in the roof space with a sound like surf. Elodie crawled, because the air at knee height was merely scalding instead of lethal, and called Thomas's name into the dark until his voice was ash.

He found him by touch. A boot. Then a leg. Then the whole weight of him, collapsed against the base of the print-room press, one arm flung over the machine as though he meant to hold it down by force if the fire came for it.

"Up," Elodie said. "Get up. You're not dying next to a printing press, Thomas, I won't allow it, it's a stupid place to die."

Thomas laughed, and the laugh became a cough that brought blood-flecked spittle to his lips. "Stubborn," he managed. "Knew you were... underneath it all."

Between them they got him vertical, one arm across Elodie's shoulders, and the journey back through the workshop was the longest walk of Elodie's life. The heat had doubled. Something in the roof let go and came down in a sheet of sparks ahead of them, and Elodie turned them sideways through the fitting shop, feeling the fire's breath on the back of his neck like a pursuer's hand. Thomas's boots dragged. At the threshold Elodie simply lifted him — the man weighed nothing, nothing, burned men weigh nothing, they are already halfway to smoke — and carried him over the sill into the night.

They went down together on the cobbles by the coal cart, and the cold river air poured over them both, and Elodie lay on his back gasping while the workshop burned above them and the fourth rocket went up, silver this time, raining down over the harbor like a blessing neither of them asked for.

"You got them out," Thomas whispered. His ruined face turned toward the coal cart. "Tell me you got them out."

"Under the sacking. All of them."

Thomas closed his eyes. What crossed his face then was not relief exactly. It was closer to the look of a man who has paid a debt. "Fifteen years of names," he said. "Every hand that ever... joined us. They can burn the shed. They can't burn the signing."

Then he was sick, and then he shivered violently, and Elodie pulled off his own coat and wrapped it around him and shouted, finally, at the top of a voice he had spent fifteen years training to be small:

"FIRE! FIRE ON THE EAST QUAY! FIRE!"

* * *

They came slowly at first, faces turning from the sky, annoyed at the interruption — and then the curve of the quay brought them round the warehouse corner and they saw it, the whole roof alight now, a banner of flame standing over the water, and the crowd noise changed key. The cheering broke apart into screaming and running and the terrible animal sound of two thousand people discovering at once that the spectacle was not entertainment.

Later, Elodie would remember the next hour in fragments, the way a man remembers a storm: buckets passed hand to hand and mostly empty, the harbor too shallow at the east wall for the fire float to swing her bows in; Mrs. Pargeter from the rope-walk standing barefoot in her good dress, screaming her children's names because her family slept in the loft rooms above the sail loft adjoining, and the fire jumping the gap between the roofs like something alive; three families' belongings coming out of windows in armfuls — a birdcage, a mattress, a sewing machine carried down the ladder by two men with their sleeves smoking; the sail loft saved by a bucket chain and pure spite; the long shed lost entire, its walls glowing and folding inward sometime after eleven with a sigh that everyone on the quay felt in their chests.

Three families homeless. That was the arithmetic by morning. The Pargeters, the Okafors, and old Widow Merrick with her lodger. Their rooms above the sail loft were scorched but standing; the storerooms behind the shed, where the Okafors kept their beds, were ash and nails.

Thomas they laid on a stretcher improvised from a hatch cover, and Elodie rode with him to the infirmary in a borrowed cart, holding the man's unburned hand while he moaned and surfaced and sank again. At the doors, a nurse tried to bar Elodie from the ward and Thomas gripped Elodie's sleeve with what strength remained and said, slurred but distinct: "Him stays. He pulled me out. Him stays."

They let him stay. He sat in the corridor outside the burns ward until nearly dawn, smelling of smoke, his hands blistered where the register covers had branded them, listening to Thomas breathe.

And all the while, in the pocket of his ruined jacket, folded flat against his heart, Elodie carried the page he had torn from his notebook in the yard while the bucket chains formed — the page on which, hands steady now, steadier than they had ever been, he had written in his careful clerk's hand:

*Workshop of the harbor unions set alight during festival fireworks, 14th September. Oil poured at the print-room end. Doors secured from outside — bolt marks in the frame, painted over fresh. Thomas Idris injured saving the membership rolls. Three families homeless. I read the letters that planned this. I know whose solicitor wrote them. I know the word 'fire' was used twice, in ink that cost less than the lives it spent.*

*I was a coward twice. There will not be a third time.*

* * *

He went back to the Vane house at six in the morning, gray light on the water, gulls working the ashes along the east quay. Rosalind was standing in the morning room in her dressing gown, still dressed for sleep, and the look on her face told him she had known since midnight — a note from a committee man, perhaps, or the fire bell heard even through closed windows — and had spent the hours since in a stillness he recognized, because it was the same stillness he had taught himself.

"Elodie." Her voice was careful. "You're hurt."

"It's nothing. Blistering. The rolls are safe — the union rolls, ma'am. Thomas saved them. He's in the infirmary. He'll live." He heard his own voice reporting facts like a telegraph clerk's, flat and complete, and hated how much it sounded like the voice of a man reading someone else's mail.

"They're saying an electrical fault," Rosalind said. "Already. Before the ashes are cold. Mr. Calloway sent round at five o'clock to say the committee considers it regrettable but contained, and that no statement should be made linking the fire to — to anyone. That an inquiry will find what inquiries find." Her hands were folded very tight at her waist. "Contained. As though it were a spill."

Elodie stood in the doorway with the smell of smoke still in his clothes and the page against his chest, and he understood that he had arrived at the exact point he had been walking toward since the night he copied that sealed letter by lamplight. Everything he knew was now worth something, and everything he was — invisible, unread, safe — was about to be spent buying it.

"Ma'am," he said, and his voice did not shake, which surprised him, "when the inquiry is called — and it will be called, whatever Mr. Calloway hopes — there will be evidence. Real evidence. Written down, dated, and hidden where no one thought to look. And when it comes into the open, there will be people in this house, people on your committee, who will wish it had stayed hidden. I need you to know, before that day comes, that I am the one who hid it."

Rosalind turned from the window. For a long moment she studied him — his burned hands, his smoke-black collar, his face — with the expression of a woman rereading a familiar letter and finding, in the margin, handwriting she had never noticed before.

"Evidence," she repeated softly. "What evidence, Elodie? Whose?"

"Mine," he said. "I've been reading the Harrow correspondence for a month. And last night I watched their words come true."

Outside, the tide was turning, and the first light caught the ash drifting on the water — gray flakes settling on the swell, on the pilings, on the hulls of the festival boats, falling all across the harbor like the slowest snow, like pages, like names.
