## 23. The Inquest Opens

The coroner's court at Bristol had been a magistrates' room once, and it had not troubled to change its character for its new duties. The same oak panelling, dark with a century of spilled porter and pipe smoke; the same high windows that let in a light the color of dishwater; the same air of men deciding things about other men's lives without ever having to look them in the face. Rosalind Vane sat in the public gallery on the second morning of the inquest into the fire at the harbor workshops, and she thought: *this is where verdicts are manufactured, not found.*

She had told herself she came as an observer. A citizen with an interest in civic probity. Her committee had advised against it—Mildred Hargreaves had been quite firm about it over tea, her spoon clicking against the saucer like a small metronome of disapproval. *Rosalind, dear, you cannot be seen attaching yourself to every misfortune in this city. The bill is three months from committee stage. Let the coroner do his dull work and let us do ours.* And so Rosalind had come alone, hat plain, coat plain, seated at the back beside a fishwife and a boy selling chestnuts he was not permitted to sell inside, and she had told herself the plainness was discretion rather than shame.

Two days in, she no longer believed it.

* * *

The first morning had been procedure. The coroner, Mr. Ambrose Quill—a dry, precise man with the complexion of a man who took his exercise indoors—had established the facts as though laying bricks. The workshop premises at Nine Staves Row, occupied by Idris & Sons boatwrights and, latterly, by certain printing operations whose nature the court did not press too hard upon. The fire had begun shortly after nine o'clock in the evening of the festival, while the city's attention hung in the sky above the harbor in gold and green and falling silver. First discovered by a watchman two streets away who saw the glow through a yard gate. By the time the brigade's engine arrived—and here Mr. Quill permitted himself a small, tired digression about the difficulty of moving engines through festival crowds—the roof was already failing.

Three families homeless. One man badly burned: Thomas Idris, foreman, forty-six years of age, presently at the General Infirmary, too ill to attend.

And then the expert evidence. An inspector from the Fire Brigade, a Mr. Callow, who testified with the flat competence of a man reciting his own handwriting: the point of origin was the wiring loft above the main bench room, where the premises' electrical supply—installed some eighteen months prior, against all advice, by an outfit from Bath whose name he produced with relish—had run through joists packed with oakum, cotton waste, and varnished rags.

*Electrical fault,* said the coroner's summary, and the stenographer's pen scratched it down, and the word went around the gallery like a coin passed hand to hand until everyone held it and no one remembered receiving it.

*Electrical fault.*

Rosalind had sat very still through all of it, her gloved hands folded in her lap, and she had watched the room accept the phrase the way water accepts oil—not mixing with it, merely closing over it.

* * *

It was the second morning that undid her.

They called the watchman first, a stooped man named Pargeter who gave his evidence in the manner of someone reading aloud a letter written by a cleverer person. Yes, he'd seen the glow. No, he hadn't heard any explosion. Yes, the gates of the yard had been shut when he passed at half past eight—he'd noticed particularly, because the Idris dog was usually loose and he'd been glad of it. Shut at half past eight. He'd have sworn to it.

Then a neighbor, Mrs. Enid Cottle, who kept a lodging house on Staves Row and had lost her own coal shed to the spread. She was nervous, wringing her hands inside her shawl, but she answered plainly enough until Mr. Quill asked whether she had observed anything unusual in the days preceding the fire.

"Well," said Mrs. Cottle, "there were men."

"Men, Mrs. Cottle?"

"Come round asking after the hours of the place. When the men went home. Whether there was a night watchman." She twisted the shawl. "One of them bought a pie off my window and asked could he see the back yards, said he was thinking of lodgings for his brother. But he never asked what the rooms cost. Not once. And he looked at the workshop wall the whole time, not at me."

"And when was this?"

"Fortnight before the fire, sir. Maybe less."

A pause, delicate as a dropped stitch. Mr. Quill made a note. "You did not think to mention this to anyone at the time?"

"I didn't think anything of it, sir. Harbor's full of men asking questions. That's most of what a harbor is."

There was a small ripple in the gallery—not laughter exactly, but the sound people make when truth arrives dressed as a joke. Rosalind did not join it. Something cold had begun at the base of her skull and was making its patient way down her spine.

Because she knew about men asking questions. She had received a letter about men asking questions—or rather, she had received a letter that had spoken of fire, of premises unwatched at night, of a warning so strange and so specific that she had filed it in the drawer of her writing desk beneath the household accounts and told herself it was a crank's letter, a suffrage enemy's attempt to frighten her, a hoax. *Check your premises at night,* it had said—no, not her premises; she had read it quickly, standing in the hall with wet gloves on, and the phrasing had lodged somewhere below memory: something about the harbor workshops, something about watching, something unsigned.

She had meant to show it to Elodie. She had even, one evening, taken it out again—and then put it away, because showing it would mean explaining why she'd kept it secret, and explaining would mean admitting she'd received an anonymous letter and given it more than a moment's thought, and giving it thought would make it real, and if it was real then she ought to act, and acting would mean telling her committee, and Mildred would say—

The chain of *and thens* had gone on so long and so comfortably that the letter had moldered in its drawer for weeks, and the workshop had burned, and Thomas Idris lay in the Infirmary with his hands bandaged into mittens, and she had spent the intervening fortnight rehearsing a speech about the franchise.

Guilt, Rosalind thought, watching Mrs. Cottle return to her seat, does not arrive like a thunderclap. It arrives like a bill. On schedule. Itemized.

* * *

The afternoon session brought the insurance assessor, a smooth man from the Northern Union named Pell, who testified that the premises had been insured for a sum he described as "modest and appropriate," and that the policy had been in force eleven years without claim. Asked whether the electrical installation had ever been inspected, he consulted his papers and said that the supply company's records showed a routine inspection fourteen months prior, finding nothing adverse.

"Nothing adverse," repeated Mr. Quill.

"Nothing adverse, sir."

Rosalind wrote the date in her notebook. Fourteen months prior. She had a reason to note it that she could not have explained to the man beside her, and the reason was Elodie—Elodie, who read everything, who had once remarked over the steering wheel, in that mild voice he used for observations he wished to seem smaller than they were, that the harbor pilots' ledgers recorded everything, *everything*, Miss Vane, dates and drafts and inspections, because a pilot who guesses is a pilot who drowns. She had laughed at the time. Now the memory sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Why was she thinking of Elodie? Why did her mind keep circling back to him, to the set of his shoulders under his driving coat, to the way his eyes moved over printed matter the way other men's eyes moved over a street—cataloguing, assessing, remembering?

Because of the letter. Because whoever had sent that letter had known things. Had known about the workshops, known about the danger, known enough to warn. And knowledge of that particular grain did not grow wild. It grew in houses. In households. In the space between a lady's desk and her driver's ear.

She made herself stop. It was unworthy. It was the sort of suspicion that fed on proximity, on intimacy, on the guilty habit of assuming that because one was oneself concealing things, everyone else must be too.

Was she concealing things?

She thought of Tuesday's strategy meeting, the gaslight, Mildred's voice: *If the Harrows are implicated, we implicate ourselves by association. Half our subscription list reads the shipping pages before it reads anything else. We cannot afford the arithmetic, Rosalind. Not now.* She had agreed. She had agreed in six words—"Of course. You're quite right, Mildred"—and had walked home along Park Street feeling the agreement settle into her bones like wet weather, and she had not written to Thomas Idris at the Infirmary, and she had not attended the relief meeting at the mission hall, and she had told herself that a woman preparing a private member's bill could not be everywhere, could not mourn everything, that duty was a matter of triage—

Duty was a matter of triage. She had learned that phrase from her father, who had used it about the family's tenants in Wiltshire, and she had hated him a little for it at nineteen, and here she was at thirty-four using it about burned men.

* * *

The court rose at four. Rosalind stayed in her seat while the gallery emptied, the chestnut boy first, the fishwife gathering her baskets, the reporters hurrying out with their hats already on—they would file their paragraphs within the hour, and she knew what the paragraphs would say, because she had heard the same paragraph twice already in the corridor: *tragic accident, aging wiring, questions raised about the regulation of electrical installations in commercial premises.* Questions about wiring. As though the wiring had opinions. As though the wiring had bought a pie at Mrs. Cottle's window and never once asked the price of a room.

She descended to the corridor, where the light came greenish through frosted glass, and stood a while pretending to consult a notice board about vaccination schedules.

"Miss Vane."

She turned. A young reporter—she recognized his face from the *Mercury*'s offices, where she had once given an interview; Dakers, or Dakin, something with a D—stood twisting his cap.

"I wondered—" He glanced up and down the corridor. "I wondered if you'd speak to the paper. About the workshops. There's those as says the union printing was the real target, and there's those as says that's agitation talk, but I've been round Staves Row myself, miss, and Mrs. Cottle's not the only one saw strangers. There was a car. A motorcar, twice, the week before. Nobody local keeps a motorcar."

"Who says these things?" Rosalind asked. "About the union printing being the target?"

He shifted. "People. People who won't say it to a coroner." His eyes came up to hers, and there was something in them that was not ambition but fear, and she recognized it, because she had seen it in the eyes of women outside polling stations, in the eyes of strikers' wives, in every person who knows a thing and knows also what saying it will cost. "You're the only one as comes to sit through it all, miss. Day after day. Plain clothes, back row. So I thought maybe you was wanting it said somewhere it'd be heard."

For one absurd moment she wanted to take the boy's hand. Instead she said, carefully, "If you find someone willing to be quoted, Mr. Dakin, bring it to me. Not to print—to me first. Can you do that?"

He nodded, and went, and she stood in the green light understanding that she had just done a dangerous thing, and understanding also that she had done it without hesitation, and that the hesitation would come later, itemized, on schedule, like everything else.

* * *

The fog was coming up off the river when she reached the Vane house, the gas lamps lit early, each one wearing its halo of yellow wool. Elodie had the motorcar at the kerb, waiting; he always seemed to know her hours, though she had never once told him them. He opened the door and she climbed in and for a while neither spoke, the wheels hissing through wet streets, the fog pressing at the windows like something trying to read over their shoulders.

"The inquest," he said at last. Not a question. Just the word, laid between them like a card played face-up.

"You read the papers."

"I read everything." A pause. "What did they say?"

"Electrical fault." She watched his profile, the line of his jaw, the way his hands rested on the wheel—steady, always steady, those hands. "Fault. As though it were a fault in the way a lie is a fault. A failure of something to be true."

His hands did not move. But something in the carriage changed—some alteration of pressure, of attention, the way a room changes when a sleeper wakes. "Do you think it wasn't?" he asked quietly.

And there it was: the question she had been carrying since Mrs. Cottle's shawl-wringing hands, offered back to her by the last person she should discuss it with, and she heard herself answer, "I don't know what I think. I received a letter, some weeks ago. Anonymous. It warned of fire—of danger to the harbor workshops. I put it away. I told myself it was a crank." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Thomas Idris is in the Infirmary because I filed a warning under my household accounts."

The silence that followed lasted three streets. When Elodie finally spoke, his voice was careful in a way she had never heard it, each word set down like glassware. "You couldn't have known it was true."

"One can't know an anonymous letter is true. That is precisely what makes ignoring it so comfortable." She turned to the window. The fog had thickened; the world beyond the glass was a smear of lamplight and stone. "I have spent fifteen years—well, ten—being told that caution is principle. That a woman who wants the vote must be beyond reproach, must not associate, must not speculate, must not *feel* loudly. And I have been so well-trained in it that when a warning came, I checked it against the calendar of my respectability and found it inconvenient, and so it became, retroactively, incredible." She laughed, briefly, without humor. "Guilt is punctual, Elodie. I'll grant it that."

He said nothing. She felt his silence the way one feels a held breath—not empty, but full, pressurized, a silence with weight behind it, as though behind it stood a great many words queuing at a door that would not open.

"Elodie."

"Yes, Miss Vane."

"Do you think the fire was set?"

Another long pause. The wheels turned. Somewhere a ship's horn sounded on the river, low and mournful, like the city clearing its throat before lying.

"I think," he said at last, "that Mrs. Cottle's stranger never asked the price of a room."

Her breath caught. "You were at the inquest?"

"I drive you places, Miss Vane. Today I drove you to the coroner's court. A driver waits." He did not look at her. "A driver hears."

*And a driver reads,* she thought, and the old suspicion stirred again, and she pressed it down, and failed, and pressed it down again. Because there was something else now, something that had been assembling itself at the edge of her mind all day and chose this fog-bound moment to step forward: the handwriting. The anonymous warning in her desk drawer—she had examined it, once, by lamplight, telling herself she was assessing its credibility. The hand had been educated but disguised, the loops suppressed, the slant disciplined into uniformity, as though the writer had spent years training his hand to leave no trace of himself. And she had seen that discipline somewhere before, that same strangled neatness, in the delivery notes left on the hall tray, in the neat memoranda that appeared on her desk each morning in a hand she had never once thought about because servants' handwriting was furniture, invisible, part of the house—

No. It was unworthy. It was the fog talking, the guilt looking for a place to live, and any port in a storm, and God forgive her, the nearest port was the man sitting three feet away who had never given her a single reason—

Except the letter. Except the warning that had come from inside somebody's knowledge, somebody's access, somebody's *household.*

The car drew up before the Vane house. Lamplight stood in the windows; she could see the maid moving behind the drawing-room curtains, laying the fire. Home, rendered in warm miniature, and she sat in the cold motorcar outside it and understood that she was about to go in and do nothing, again, and that doing nothing had a schedule too, and hers was running late.

"Elodie," she said, her hand on the door handle. "Tomorrow morning, before my correspondence—I want you to drive me past Staves Row. The ruins. Early, before anyone's about."

Something crossed his face then, quick as a fish under ice—there and gone, unreadable, and she would spend the whole night afterward trying to decide whether it had been fear or grief or recognition.

"As you wish, Miss Vane," he said.

She went up the steps. Behind her she heard the motorcar not start immediately, heard instead a long stillness, as though the man inside it were sitting with his forehead nearly touching the wheel, and then the engine caught, and the sound of it faded into the fog, and she stood in the hall with her wet gloves steaming and thought: *the letter. Tomorrow I will take out the letter and compare the hand.*

She did not yet know that the comparison would keep her awake until dawn, nor what she would conclude, nor that by week's end a different kind of letter entirely—one bearing her own name, forged in a stranger's careful hand—would be waiting to destroy everything the first letter had tried to save.
