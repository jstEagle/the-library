## 27. The Copy That Survived

The knock came at half past six in the morning, and it was not a servant's knock.

Elodie heard it from the kitchen, where he was blacking the boots Rosalind would wear to the inquest's second day, and he knew the difference the way a man knows weather through a wall. Servants knocked with their knuckles, twice, apologetic. This was a fist, deliberate, and it did not wait for an answer before trying the door.

He set down the boot brush. He had been waiting for this for eleven days—not with fear, exactly, though his hands were not steady as he wiped them on his apron, but with the strange flat calm of a man who has already done his arithmetic and is only waiting for the sum to arrive.

Mrs. Pellow answered the front door in her nightcap, and two men came in without being asked. They wore good coats and bad manners, and the one in front carried a folded paper that he presented like a visiting card.

"Warrant," he said. "Concerning the fire at the harbor workshops. We're searching premises connected to persons of interest."

Persons of interest. Elodie stood at the kitchen doorway with his sleeves rolled and said nothing, because saying nothing was the oldest skill he owned, older than reading, older than his name.

There were four of them altogether. Two went upstairs without ceremony, and Mrs. Pellow's voice rose behind them—*I'll have you know this is a respectable house, I'll have the mistress hear of this*—and was absorbed into the general noise of drawers being opened with more force than care. The other two began on the ground floor: the study first, of course, where Rosalind's correspondence lived in locked boxes that they opened with a bent knife rather than ask her for the key. She was not home. She had gone early to the hospital ward where Thomas Idris lay bandaged like a mummy, and Elodie had driven her there and returned alone, and he understood now, watching the men work through her papers with their grimy thumbs, that she had been sent away on purpose. Someone had chosen the hour. Someone knew her habits.

Someone knew *his* habits too, or thought they did.

* * *

They found his room last, which told him something. A man who knows what he's looking for goes to it first. These men were working a list, top to bottom, and his name sat somewhere near the bottom of it—or perhaps they'd saved him deliberately, the way you save the smallest portion of meat, knowing it will be eaten anyway.

His room was under the eaves, narrow as a coffin stood on end, and he had kept it bare on principle for fifteen years. A cot. A washstand. A trunk with a false bottom that held, when they hauled it out and broke it open, nothing but spare collars and a tin of boot polish and a Bible he had never read past the first page. He watched them go through it all with the detachment of a man watching strangers search a house he had once visited. The room was a costume. He had worn it so long he sometimes forgot it was not him.

But they did not treat it like a costume. They treated it like a den.

"Driver, are you?" the tall one asked, not looking up from the mattress he had slit open along its seams.

"I am."

"Name?"

"You'd have it on your list, I should think."

That earned him a look—the first direct look any of them had given him—and he catalogued it as he had learned to catalogue everything: the tall one was the reader, the one who'd presented the warrant; the short one with the scarred knuckles was the doer; the two upstairs were filler. The tall one's eyes went over Elodie's face slowly, the way a man reads a bill of lading, checking figures against figures.

"Elias Marsh," the tall one said at last, reading from a paper. "Employed as driver to Mrs. Vane since 1909. Previously employed—" and here he paused, and Elodie felt the pause like a hand laid flat on his chest, "—previously employed at various positions around the harbor docks. No fixed history before that. No references. No family listed."

"No family listed," Elodie agreed pleasantly.

"We'll want to know where you were on the night of the ninth of October."

"The night of the festival? Driving Mrs. Vane. She spoke at the festival hall; you may check the programmes. There were four hundred witnesses."

"There were four hundred witnesses watching fireworks," the tall one said. "We've established that much."

So. That was how they meant to play it—not the fire itself, which was already being called an electrical fault by men paid to call it that, but the edges of it. The comings and goings. A driver seen near the workshops. A driver with no history. A driver who could be made into anything a magistrate wished him to be, because a man with no past could be given one wholesale.

They burned his room while he watched.

Not all of it—that would have been too honest—but enough. The mattress and its slashed ticking went out the window onto a brazier they had brought in a handcart, along with the papers from his trunk, the few letters he had allowed himself to keep (a laundry receipt, a note about a horse's shoeing), and a bundle of old newspapers he had used to line a drawer. The tall one fed the fire himself, methodically, and the smoke went up past the window in a grey rope that smelled of coal tar and cheap newsprint and, faintly, of the life Elias Marsh had been permitted to own.

"You've no cause," Mrs. Pellow said, wringing her apron. "You've no cause to burn a man's bedding."

"We've every cause," the tall one said mildly, "and a warrant besides. You're welcome to write to your magistrate." And both of them knew, and Elodie knew, and probably the magistrate knew, that no letter would change anything.

* * *

It took them two hours. When they were done the Vane house looked the way a house looks after a modest burglary conducted by gentlemen: everything in its place and nothing where it belonged. Rosalind's correspondence boxes had been relocked badly. Her desk drawers sat crooked in their frames. In the study, someone had taken a knife to the lining of her writing chair, hunting for documents sewn into the upholstery, and had found only horsehair.

And in his room, when Elodie climbed up afterward to stand in the wreckage, he found the ash still warm in the grate where they had burned what wouldn't fit in the brazier. Among the char he identified, with the particular grief of a man reading his own obituary, the corner of a page he recognized. His own handwriting. Not the copy—he had been careful, always careful—but practice sheets, from years ago, when he had taught himself a fair hand by copying passages from newspapers into the small hours. *The Times, March 1898.* He had been nineteen. He had thought himself so clever, hiding even his exercises, and now the exercise had survived and the hiding had not.

They had found nothing. That was the fact he made himself hold on to, turning it over like a coin in the dark. Nothing. The trunk's false bottom had held only collars. The mattress had held only straw. The loose brick behind the washstand—he checked, heart going hard—had been pulled out and replaced carelessly, and the cavity behind it was empty, because it had been empty for three weeks.

Because three weeks ago, on a Tuesday, he had walked down to the harbor pilot office with a delivery of chart corrections for Mr. Prosser the elder pilot, and among those corrections he had slipped four pages of thin copying-paper covered in his neat, compressed hand: the letter from Harrow & Sons' solicitor regarding "clearance of the harbor workshops"; the second letter with the word *fire* where commerce should have been; the third letter naming Rosalind Vane for public disgrace; and his own account, dated and signed with a name he had not spoken aloud in fifteen years, of how these papers had come into his keeping.

The pilot ledgers were copied and archived and never destroyed. Every movement of every vessel, every tide, every official transaction of the harbor's long memory, duplicated in triplicate and lodged where no private hand could burn them. He had known it for years the way he knew everything about the harbor—sideways, incidentally, from letters that passed through his hands. It had taken him a decade to understand what the knowledge was for.

They had found nothing. But.

* * *

But they had come at all. That was the arithmetic that would not resolve.

He sat on the scorched floorboards of his room that afternoon, in the smell of wet ash, and forced himself to think it through as he had taught himself to think through routes: not as a frightened man but as a driver plotting a course through fog.

Cedric Harrow had known since the night of the watchers—since the man named Quill had put a face to the Vane motorcar's driver loitering near the workshops. That was nearly a month ago. If Cedric had wanted him seized quietly, there had been a month of quiet nights in which to do it. Instead: a warrant, a search, a fire in a brazier, and a tall man reading out the name *Elias Marsh* like a charge sheet. This was not an arrest. This was a message delivered through channels, the way all the Harrows' messages were delivered—through solicitors, through magistrates, through smoke.

The message was: *we know what you are, we know where you sleep, and we can reach you.*

But there was a second message underneath it, and it was the one that made his hands cold. Men who burn a suspect's room are men who did not find what they were looking for—and men who did not find what they were looking for go on looking. Somewhere between the warrant and the brazier, someone had decided that Elias Marsh had copies. Perhaps it was only precaution, the thoroughness of hired professionals covering every possibility. Perhaps. But perhaps Quill had seen more than a face near the workshops. Perhaps the old pilot Prosser had mentioned, over his pipe, that the young Vane driver came by regular with chart corrections, regular as tides. Perhaps a clerk in the pilot office had a brother who drank with a brother of one of the Harrows' men, and knowledge moved through Bristol's veins the way coal dust moved through its air—everywhere, settling on everything.

He could not know. That was the thing. Fifteen years of survival had been built on knowing—on reading a room, a face, a letter, a lie—and now he sat in the ash of his own room unable to read the one document that mattered: how much they knew, and how fast they were coming.

What he could calculate was time. The inquiry sat until Friday, then adjourned for the weekend, resuming Monday. Today was Wednesday. The forged letters against Rosalind—the ones he had recognized, the ones whose forger's hand he knew better than the forger's mother knew it—would surface when the Harrows chose to produce them, and every day they waited was a day the scandal ripened. When those letters entered evidence, the inquiry would become a trial of Rosalind Vane's honesty, and the only thing in Bristol that could defeat them was four pages of copying-paper lodged in the harbor pilots' archive, which could not be produced unless a witness stood up and claimed them, named himself as their source, and submitted to whatever the Harrows' counsel could do to him on cross-examination.

Days. Not weeks. He had known it since the pilot's oath, and the burning of his room had only written the number larger.

* * *

In the evening Rosalind came back from the hospital with Thomas Idris's gratitude and the smell of carbolic in her coat, and she stood in the hall of her violated house and looked at the crooked drawers and the knife-slit chair with an expression Elodie could not fully read—which frightened him more than anything else that day, because he had always been able to read her.

"They searched my things," she said. Not a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

"My correspondence."

"Yes."

She was quiet a moment. Downstairs, Mrs. Pellow was loudly restoring order, slamming drawers home like a woman loading a gun. "Did they find anything?" Rosalind asked, and she asked it looking directly at him, and there was something in the question that was not about the Harrows at all—something that had been in her questions since the false errand, since the night he had almost spoken and hadn't, some fracture running under the surface of them both that neither of them had named.

"Nothing of consequence," Elodie said.

It was true. It was also the most dangerous sentence he had ever spoken in that house, because if she believed it, she believed he had nothing hidden—and if she did not believe it, then she knew he was lying to her face in her own hall, and either way the clock ran.

She held his eyes a moment longer. Then she said, "Mr. Marsh. Tomorrow I'll want the car at nine, and I'll want you to take me round by the quay. The long way, past the pilot office. I have a mind to see the harbor."

"The pilot office, ma'am?" His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it.

"The harbor," she repeated, mild as milk, taking off her gloves finger by finger. "Is there a reason I shouldn't drive past the pilot office?"

"No reason at all, ma'am."

"Good." She turned toward the stairs, then paused, her back to him, her hand on the newel post. "Whatever they were looking for today," she said quietly, "I hope it wasn't anything that matters."

And she went up, and left him standing in the hall with the smell of ash still in the house and her words sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat by his window under the eaves until the lamps of Bristol thinned toward morning, doing the only thing left to do, which was to count.

Wednesday night: the ledgers were safe. Prosser was reliable, and Prosser's oath was iron—produce them only to the man who placed them, only in open session, only if that man stood and named himself. Thursday: Rosalind wanted the car at nine, by way of the pilot office, and he did not believe for one moment that it was sentiment. She had seen the search. She had seen what men did when they hunted papers. She was testing the water, or testing him, or—God help him—perhaps she had finally understood, in that hall, with ash in the air, and was offering him a road instead of a question. Friday: the inquiry resumed. The forged letters could fall any day after that. And when they fell, there would be exactly one way to stop them, and it would cost him everything he had spent fifteen years building, which was nothing, which was invisibility, which was the whole of his estate.

He thought of Thomas Idris in his hospital ward, half-blind, bandaged, saying *bring it to the inquiry and I'll swear to it*, trusting a man he barely knew with the absolute confidence of a man who had already lost everything burnable. He thought of the membership rolls, saved. He thought of the children at the harbor wall. He thought of his own name, written once by lamplight and burned, waiting in him like a seed under snow.

Below, in the street, a man stood under the gas lamp across from the Vane house, smoking, and did not move on when his cigarette was done. Elodie watched him for an hour. The man was relieved by another man at two o'clock.

They were watching the house now. Of course they were. The burning of the room had not been the end of the message; it had been the opening paragraph.

Elodie took off his boots so his footsteps would make no sound, and began, very quietly, to pack.
