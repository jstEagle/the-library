## 28. Thomas's Testimony

The ward smelled of carbolic and boiled linen, and beneath both—thin but unmistakable, the way a harbor smell survives any weather—the ghost of burnt pitch.

Thomas Idris had been counting things to keep himself sane. The ceiling boards: eleven of them, one warped near the far window where rain had gotten in some winter before he was born. The bells of St. Mary Redcliffe, which rang the quarters whether a man was dying or not. The nurses' footsteps: Sister Pryce walked heel-first and quick; the young one, Miss Alys, shuffled like she was apologizing to the floor for disturbing it. And the burns themselves, though that was not counting so much as negotiating. The left arm was a country he no longer governed. The right hand still answered its orders most days. His eyes were the worst negotiation of all. The surgeon had spoken of scarring to the corneas with the gentle vagueness men use when they mean *forever*, and now Thomas saw the world through a fine grey gauze, everything softened at its edges like a watercolor left out in the damp.

He had been a foreman for twenty-two years. He knew what a workshop looked like when it was healthy, and he knew what it looked like when it was dying, and on the night of the festival he had stood inside his own dying workshop with the membership rolls under one arm while the roof came down like God clearing His throat, and he had thought, with a clarity that felt almost comic: *so this is what they meant.*

They had meant it since the first letter. He had known it since the first letter. He had stayed anyway, because a workshop that runs is a workshop that's guilty, and because four hundred names written in his own careful hand do not carry themselves out of a fire.

Now he lay in the General Infirmary with his eyes bandaged every second hour, and the whole city was telling itself a story about an electrical fault, and Thomas Idris lay there listening to the story through the walls—the visitors' murmur, the porters' gossip, the chaplain's careful sermons—and understanding that the story would win unless somebody did something about it. Stories always won. That was the first lesson of the docks. The Harrows owned the ships and the warehouses and half the newspapers, and ownership was just another word for *who gets to tell it*.

The trouble was that doing something required a thing Thomas did not have: a way to put the truth in front of people whose livelihoods did not depend on not seeing it.

He had made a list, in his head, of everyone who might help. It was a short list. It got shorter each time he went through it. The union men who'd escaped the fire were scattered and frightened, and two of them had already sent word that they wanted nothing said, nothing done, please God let it lie. The police had come to his bedside once, taken down his statement about the electrical fault with the eager blankness of men writing fiction, and thanked him for his cooperation. The newspapers had printed three inches about the tragedy of the old workshops, illustrated with a drawing of flames that looked, to Thomas's ruined eyes, like a bouquet.

And then, on the ninth day, there were new footsteps in the ward. Careful ones. Soft-soled, deliberate, placed like a man walking across ice he has tested before.

"Visiting hours are over," Sister Pryce called from the far end, without conviction.

"I'll only be a moment, sister." A voice Thomas knew. Low, level, the accent of nowhere in particular—which on the docks meant everywhere and therefore nowhere, which meant a man who had learned young that accents get you sorted and filed. "Mr. Idris? It's Elodie. From the Vane house."

Thomas turned his head toward the sound. Through the grey gauze he could make out a shape: a young man, hat in hand, standing at the foot of the bed with the particular posture of someone who has been thrown out of better rooms than this and expects to be thrown out of this one too.

"The driver," Thomas said.

"That's what I'm told when I'm useful."

"And the rest of the time?"

A pause. Then, quietly, with something that was almost humor and almost not: "I read letters, Mr. Idris. That's mostly what I do. Read letters and forget them."

Thomas studied the shape of him. There had been something about this one, back before the fire—he remembered it now, the memory arriving whole, the way important memories do, late and complete. The only servant from up the hill who ever came down to the workshops and looked men in the eye. Who'd shaken hands like a handshake was a real transaction between real people. Who'd listened to Thomas talk about caulking and steam-bending as if boats were worth hearing about. Thomas had thought at the time: *that one is watching everything.* He had assumed the watching was harmless curiosity. He had been wrong about many things that autumn.

"They say the electrical wiring," Thomas said. "In the papers. Faulty insulation, forty years old, a tragedy waiting to happen."

"Yes," said Elodie. "That's what they say."

"It wasn't the wiring."

"No," Elodie said. "It wasn't."

The word sat in the air between them like a struck match.

Thomas reached for the water jug on the bedside table, more for something to do with his good hand than for thirst, and found it, and poured, and the small clatter of glass against glass seemed very loud in the ward. Somewhere down the row a man groaned in his sleep. Outside, Bristol went about its evening: cart wheels, a barrel organ playing something jaunty and terrible, the low constant conversation of the tide.

"How much do you know?" Thomas asked.

There was a silence long enough that Thomas began to think the question had frightened him off—that the driver would make some excuse, touch his hat, and be gone, and the list of people who might help would grow shorter by one. But when Elodie finally spoke, he spoke the way men speak when they have decided to stop swimming and find out how deep the water goes.

"I know the Harrow family hired watchers on your workshop in September. I know their solicitor wrote about the clearance of the harbor premises in terms that read like commerce until the second letter, which used the word *fire*. I know Cedric Harrow moved the date to the festival night so the rockets would cover the noise and every honest witness in the city would be looking at the sky instead of the water. I know there are forged letters being prepared to prove that Miss Vane took union money into her own pocket, because I have read enough of the forger's correspondence to recognize his hand when it comes." A breath. "And I know that I sent you a note in October telling you to check your premises at night, and that a note is not enough, and that I knew it was not enough even as I wrote it."

Thomas lay very still.

He had spent nine days composing arguments. He had rehearsed them against the ceiling boards: *convince them, shame them, bargain with them.* And here was a man who needed none of it—who arrived carrying the whole weight of it already, unasked, in a voice kept deliberately quiet in a ward full of sleeping strangers.

"You're the one who warned us," Thomas said. Not a question.

"I tried to warn you."

"You did warn me. Twice. The second time through the boy with the fish barrow, and I'll tell you what I told him to tell you: I don't run." Thomas's throat tightened around the words, and the burns along his jaw pulled when he spoke, and he spoke anyway. "A workshop that runs is a workshop that's guilty. You can't build anything on ground where the builders scatter at the first smell of smoke. They'd have burned us out eventually either way—but if we ran, they'd have burned us out cheap, and quietly, and every other shop on the harbor would have learned the lesson: keep your head down, pay your dues to whoever holds the lease, and never, never organize. Four hundred men and women signed those rolls. Some of them signed knowing what signing might cost, because their fathers had been blacklisted and their mothers had watched it happen. I wasn't going to teach their children the other lesson."

"I know," Elodie said. "I've read the rolls."

Thomas went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the ward's draughty windows. "You've *what*?"

"In the ledgers. The pilot ledgers." The young man's voice dropped lower still. "Your membership rolls weren't the only copies of anything that night, Mr. Idris. Since October I've been copying documents—letters, schedules, instructions—and filing them in the harbor pilots' official ledgers, between the pages of tide tables and channel soundings, where nobody looks because official records are sacred and boring. Your press proofs. A delivery schedule marked for the festival week. The solicitor's letters. Everything I could put my hands on, copied in my own hand, dated, and hidden where fire can't reach and searchers wouldn't think to look."

The grey gauze over Thomas's eyes seemed suddenly less like a wound and more like a curtain, and behind it his mind did what it had always done best, which was to see the shape of a thing entire. Press proofs. Schedules. Correspondence. Dated. Hidden in the pilot ledgers—the one archive in the city that was copied, countersigned, and never destroyed, because the Corporation trusted its pilots the way it trusted the tide.

"You've had this for weeks," Thomas said slowly. "Since before the fire. You knew it was coming, and you built the evidence against it, and you—" He stopped himself. He was not a cruel man, but he was an honest one, and honesty in a hospital bed comes easily because there is nothing left to lose by it. "You had it and you didn't take it to the inquiry."

"There was no inquiry then. There was a dead silence and a newspaper story about electrical faults, and if I had walked into a police station with those pages, I'd have been escorted out, and the pages would have been gone by morning, and so would I. The evidence isn't worth anything until there's a room where someone is obliged to hear it." A pause, and when Elodie continued his voice had changed—flatter, more careful, the voice of a man reading out a bill he knows is accurate. "And there will be a room. Because the Harrows aren't finished. The fire was the first act. The forged letters against Miss Vane surface next, at the inquiry they can't prevent now that the Corporation has been shamed into holding one. They mean to destroy her publicly, Mr. Idris. She's on their list. She has been since September."

"Then give them the ledgers," Thomas said. "Give the inquiry everything. Tonight."

"It isn't mine to give." For the first time the driver's composure cracked—not much, just a hairline, visible even through gauze. "The ledgers belong to the pilots. Old Captain Merriam keeps them. And Merriam will produce them—one condition. Whoever puts the evidence into that room must stand up in it afterward and say, in front of the commissioners and the newspapers and the Harrow family, *I am the source. I read these letters. Here is my name.* No name, no testimony, no testimony, no proof. Anonymity is the price, and anonymity is the one coin I've spent fifteen years learning to hoard." A breath. "So that's why I'm here, Mr. Idris. Not for your blessing. For your testimony. The inquiry will need witnesses the police won't interview—your men, the families, and above all you. You were inside. You saw what was set. If the ledgers say *fire* and you say *fire*, then the room has to choose between a shipping family and the truth, and I want the choice made so public they choke on it. But I can't testify and gather witnesses and watch the house and dodge their men all at once. I need—" and here the voice did something Thomas had never heard a gentleman's man do, which was to run out of polish entirely, "—I need someone to tell me it's worth it. That burning my life down for this isn't just arithmetic. Because I've been doing arithmetic for weeks, and the sums all work, and I don't believe a single one of them."

For a long moment Thomas said nothing.

Through the gauze, the shape at the foot of his bed resolved itself—not into a face, his eyes couldn't manage faces anymore, but into a presence. He thought about what he actually knew of this man, and found it was almost nothing, and understood suddenly that almost nothing was exactly the point. Fifteen years ago Thomas had been a journeyman on the *Providence*, and a boy had come aboard at Cork with no name anyone used and no history anyone believed, and the boy had worked double tides without complaint and slept with his boots by the door, and the older hands had said *watch that one, he's running from something,* and Thomas had said, *no—he's running toward whatever he hasn't found yet,* and the boy had jumped ship at Bristol and vanished into the docks, and Thomas had thought of him maybe twice in fifteen years. He had no reason to connect that boy to the man at his bedside. He connected him anyway. Some things a body knows before its eyes do.

"You barely know me," Thomas said. "Why bring this to me?"

"Because you stayed," Elodie said simply. "Everyone else I could ask would weigh it. The committee weighs it. The lawyers weigh it. Even Miss Vane—" a hesitation, quickly smoothed, "—even the best of them weigh it, and weighing is right and proper for people with something to lose. But you went back into a burning building for four hundred names written in ink. You're the only man in Bristol who understands what these papers are. So I'm asking you to tell me what they're worth."

Thomas lay back against the pillows and looked up at his eleven ceiling boards, and the warped one near the window, and let the answer assemble itself, because this deserved assembly.

"Worth?" he said at last. "I'll tell you what I told the committee in March, when they wanted to move the presses up the hill where it was safer. Worth isn't a number. Worth is a door. Right now the Harrows own every door in this city—they open them and close them as suits them, and the rest of us live our lives in whatever rooms they leave us. Those ledgers are a door they didn't build and can't lock. You walk through it, and after you, every man who was ever told to keep his head down gets to see that doors exist." He turned his bandaged face toward the shape of the young man. "As for the price—you're afraid of standing up in that room and saying your name. I understand. I've been poor my whole life and I know what exposure costs the poor; it costs different than it costs the rich, and dearer. But listen to me, boy. I am lying in this ward with my eyes cooked in my skull and my arm off its hinges, and I will stand up at that inquiry if they carry me, because the truth needs bodies in front of it or it stays a rumor, and rumors die by Thursday. If I can do that half-blind, you can do it with your eyes wide open and your name in your mouth." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "Yes. It's worth it. Now sit down, because I can't write anymore, and you're going to take down what I saw that night, word for word, and you're going to carry it to Captain Merriam yourself, and you're going to tell him Thomas Idris vouches for you—and if that old pilot says my vouching isn't good enough, you tell him I'll come down there wrapped in sheets and haunt his chart room until he agrees."

There was the scrape of a chair. Then, quieter, in a voice that had lost its carefulness altogether:

"Why? You don't know me. I could be anyone. I could be working for them—I was in their offices, I drove past their yard daily, I'm nobody from nowhere with no name worth trusting. Why would you hand me your testimony on that alone?"

Thomas smiled, and felt the burn scars pull at his cheeks, and did not care.

"Because I watched you walk into this ward," he said, "past a sister who told you visiting hours were over, past six beds of men too sick to notice you, straight to the one bed in Bristol that matters to you, with your hat in your hands and your feet placed like a man crossing ice—and you never once looked at the door. Men working for the Harrows always know where the door is. It's the first thing they learn." He held out his right hand, the good one, palm up, across the blanket. "Fifteen years ago a boy jumped ship at Cork and worked double tides and slept with his boots by the door. I've wondered what became of him. Sit down and take my dictation, and we'll both find out together."

The hand took his. It was a strong grip, careful of the bandages, and it did not let go quickly, and neither did Thomas.

"Begin anywhere," Elodie said, and Thomas heard paper unfold.

"Begin at the beginning, then," said Thomas Idris, foreman, and closed his ruined eyes, and saw the night of the fire as clearly as he had ever seen anything. "The first rocket went up at nine minutes past eight, and I was greasing the presses..."

Outside the infirmary windows, the lamps of Bristol came on one by one, and somewhere across the darkening city a sealed envelope was already traveling toward the inquiry commissioners with a lie inside it, and somewhere else an old pilot sat among his sacred, boring ledgers, waiting to learn what a young man with no name would ask of him. And in the ward, in the carbolic-smelling dusk, a burned man dictated and a hidden man wrote, and between them passed the oldest cargo on the harbor: trust, given without collateral, across every line the city had ever drawn.
