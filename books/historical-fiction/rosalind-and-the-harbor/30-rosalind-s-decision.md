## 30. Rosalind's Decision

The letter came on a Tuesday, in the second week of October, when the fog off the Cut had turned the windows of the Vane house into grey mirrors and the coal smoke hung in the streets like something drowned.

Rosalind read it twice at her desk before she understood that her hands were shaking.

It was from Margaret Ellery-Swift — not a friend, precisely, but a woman whose friendship was useful and who had never yet sent anything that did not require answering within the day. This one required more than an answer. It required a decision, and it made the shape of that decision plain enough that Rosalind could see the whole architecture of it at once, the way one sees a burning building from across the water: every window lit, every floor already gone.

*A friend of my husband's sits on the inquiry panel,* Margaret had written, in the careful hand of a woman aware that letters could be read by eyes other than those they were addressed to. *He tells me certain correspondence will be laid before the inquiry on Thursday next. Letters. In your name. I will say nothing further in writing. Come and see me if you wish.*

In your name.

Rosalind set the letter down on the blotter and looked at it for a long time without touching it again, as though it might behave differently under observation.

She knew what letters in her name would say. She had known since the night Elodie stood in her study with lamplight behind him and confessed everything — every sealed envelope he had opened in the shadows of her motorcar, every secret he had carried and copied and hidden, the warning he had passed too gently to Thomas Idris, the fire he had seen coming weeks before the first rocket fell. He had told her all of it in a voice that never rose above the level of the rain against the glass, and he had told her last of all about the forgeries: that the Harrows' campaign against her had not ended with the workshop, that the second phase had begun, that somewhere in Bristol a man with a practised hand was writing *her* signature onto pages that would prove she had taken union money for herself.

She had listened to all of it standing up. She remembered that. She had remained standing through the whole confession because sitting down would have been a kind of surrender, and because — though she had not admitted this even to herself — standing kept her farther from him.

That had been nine days ago. Nine days of not speaking to him except through Mrs. Petch, of hearing his footsteps in the hall and the motorcar turning over in the mews at six each morning as punctual as a heartbeat, of eating breakfast in a house that contained a man who loved her and whom she could not look at without seeing everything she had failed to do.

And now this letter, telling her what she already knew: that the forged letters were coming regardless. That Thursday would arrive whether she willed it or no. That the trap Cedric Harrow had built would spring on schedule, with or without her permission, with or without her evidence.

*Only your driver can stop it,* Margaret had not written, because Margaret did not know there was a driver to speak of. But everyone else seemed to know. The committee knew — God, the committee knew, they had told her so in the flat administrative voices of women discussing a leak in the roof. If the ledgers surfaced, if Elodie Galloway stood up in that room and named himself as their source, then the inquiry would vindicate the workshops and destroy the Harrows and, in the same motion, bind her name to her servant's in a scandal that would follow the franchise bill into every newspaper in England. *Miss Vane's chauffeur*, they would write. *Miss Vane's spy.* The bill was eleven days from its second reading. Eleven days.

Her committee had put it to her with great tenderness, as one breaks bad news to the bereaved: distance yourself. Let the inquiry run its course without him. The forgeries might be survived; a scandal of proximity could not be. They had said *we must think of the movement* the way other people said *God will provide*, meaning the same thing: that the individual was small, and the cause was large, and large things required small sacrifices, and hers was simply the one nearest to hand.

She had sat in that committee room on Friday and heard them count, and she had thought of Elodie saying, in her study, with the rain coming down: *You taught me that reading a letter is nothing. What you do with it is everything.* And she had understood, sitting among her allies, that she had spent fifteen years teaching herself the same arithmetic — that a cause was worth any number of quiet sacrifices — and that she had never once asked who got to decide which lives were the sacrifice, because until now it had never been anyone she—

But it had always been someone. That was the truth she had been circling for nine days and could no longer avoid. It had been Thomas Idris, burned and half-blind in the infirmary ward, whose warning she had let die softly in her own ear because the timing was inconvenient. It had been three families homeless on the harbor wall while the committee discussed column inches. The movement counted lives in figures and she had let it, because the figures were always other people's, and because believing in the arithmetic had been easier than believing she was the sort of woman who did.

Elodie had never once asked her to choose him. That was the thing. Nine days of silence and he had not come to her door, had not left a note, had not stood in the mews at dusk wearing the expression of a man waiting to be spared. He had given her the truth the way he gave her everything — completely, and without invoice — and then he had removed himself from the ledger entirely, as though his own claim on her attention were a debt he refused to enter.

She could let him stay removed. She could let Thursday come and the forged letters lie on the inquiry table in her handwriting-that-was-not-her-handwriting, and she could endure it — she was good at enduring, it was practically her trade — and perhaps the bill would pass, and perhaps in ten years some historian would note that Miss Vane had weathered a slander with dignity, and no one would ever know that a man had walked out of the harbor fog carrying the proof of her innocence and been sent back into it because his presence was inconvenient to a timetable.

Or.

Rosalind took up the pen, wrote four lines to Margaret Ellery-Swift declining to call, blotted it, and rang for Mrs. Petch.

"Is Mr. Galloway in the mews?"

"He went out an hour ago, miss. Toward the harbor, I'd say, by the direction he took."

"Then I shall want the small car brought round when he returns. No—" She stopped. She looked out the window at the fog lying along the street like wool. "No. Have my coat brought down instead. I shall walk."

Mrs. Petch, who had served the Vane household for twenty years and had opinions about mistresses who walked unaccompanied into the dock district, held her silence with the skill of long practice, and fetched the coat.

* * *

The harbor in October smelled of tar and cold iron and the green rot of weed below the waterline, and Rosalind walked into it with her collar up and her hat pinned low, feeling the disguise of plainness settle over her the way it had on Sunday walks years ago, before she'd had a motorcar and a driver and reasons to be recognized. The fog thinned as she came down toward the Cut, pulling apart into rags that showed glimpses of mast-tops and crane-arms and the black ribs of the burned workshop still standing at the water's edge like the skeleton of a whale the sea had declined to finish with.

She had not been down here since the fire. She had meant to come — had composed speeches about it, drafted letters of condolence to the three homeless families, done everything except walk the ground itself — and now that she was walking it she understood why she had stayed away. The smell of char was still in the timbers. A child's boot lay in the gutter outside the ruined door, small and upright and unbearably articulate, and Rosalind stepped around it as though it were sleeping.

The pilots' office stood where it had always stood, at the head of the stairs above the Cut, a narrow brick building with a lamp burning in its window against the early dusk. She knew why she had come here. She had known since she put on her coat, and she had permitted herself not to think it in words the whole length of the walk, and now the words arrived anyway, arriving the way truth arrives — all at once, and dressed plainly:

He would not come to her. So she would go to him. And whatever he chose, she would stand beside him while he chose it, and afterward, whatever it cost.

She climbed the stairs and knocked.

Footsteps, unhurried. The door opened, and there he was — not in his driving coat but in a plain jersey, his sleeves rolled, ink on the side of his right hand, a page of close copperplate in his fingers. Behind him an old man with a face like driftwood looked up from a fair ledger and then, deliberately, looked away again, and reached for his cap, and said something about checking the tide-gauge, and went out past her down the stairs with a courtesy so elaborate it could only have been kindness.

For a moment neither of them spoke. The fog moved in the doorway between them like a curtain deciding whether to fall.

"You shouldn't be here," Elodie said at last. Not unkindly. The way a man reports the weather.

"No," Rosalind agreed. "I imagine there is a very long list of places I shouldn't be, and this is somewhere in the middle of it." She stepped inside before he could decide for her. The office was small and warm-smelling — oilcloth, pipe tobacco, old paper. A shelf of ledgers ran along one wall, their spines dated back forty years, and she looked at them and understood, finally and fully, what he had done with the weeks she had spent not speaking to him. "How many are yours?"

He followed her glance. "Eleven," he said. "Scattered. Ferrow enters them as tide observations. Nobody audits tide observations."

"And the inquiry—"

"The pilot has agreed to produce them. On Thursday." He paused, and she watched him decide to finish it, watched the decision move through his face like light moving across water. "On condition that I stand up in that room and say where they came from. My name, out loud, attached to every word. That's his price. Anonymity is the only thing the evidence costs me, so anonymity is what he charges."

The lamp hissed faintly. Outside, somewhere below the stairs, the bell buoy was ringing its slow idiot note into the fog.

"I received a letter today," Rosalind said. "Margaret Ellery-Swift. Her husband's friend on the panel tells her the forged letters will be entered on Thursday. In my name. Whether or not anything else happens." She turned to face him fully. "So you should know that the choice you've been carrying isn't yours anymore. It stopped being yours the moment the Harrows decided their timeline. If you stand up, the ledgers defeat the forgeries and ruin us both. If you don't, the forgeries stand alone and ruin only me — and the bill may survive, and the committee will send you flowers."

"That's roughly how I'd priced it," he said.

"I know it is. You price things very accurately. It's one of the many things about you I have spent two years refusing to notice." Her voice was steady. She was almost surprised by it. "And I have come here to tell you what I have decided, and then I am going to leave, and you are not obliged to answer, because I am not asking."

"Rosalind—"

"Please." And the word cracked slightly, and she let it. "Let me say it while I still can."

He went still. She had learned his stillnesses over two years of watching his hands on the wheel and his eyes in the mirror — the stillness of a man listening with his whole body, the way sailors listen to weather. She drew a breath that tasted of lamp oil and cold air.

"I have spent my life believing that a cause justifies its costs, provided the person paying agrees to pay. That was always the elegant part. Everyone I have ever sacrificed consented to be sacrificed — Thomas, the printers, the families — they believed as I believe, they would have signed the same ledger. But you never signed it. You were never asked. You were simply the man who drove the car, and I let myself not see you because not-seeing was convenient, and I have discovered in the last nine days that convenience and cowardice are the same sin wearing different coats." She heard her own voice drop. "The committee has asked me to disown you. To let Thursday happen without you. They are not villains, Elodie. That is the worst of it. They are good women doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic says the bill is worth more than your name, and eleven days ago I would have signed my own name to that sum without blinking."

"And now?"

"Now I have learned what the sum buys." She looked at the shelf of ledgers, forty years of tides faithfully recorded by men nobody remembered, and felt her throat tighten. "It buys a law. Perhaps. If the newspapers are kind and the weather holds. And it costs a man who read every letter I ever received and kept every secret I never knew I had, and loved me — you do love me, I have known it since the festival eve and I have no right to say it aloud and I am saying it aloud because on Thursday either of us may be finished in this city and I decline to be finished with it unsaid — it costs him everything, so that women like me can stand on platforms and say the word *citizen* in a voice that carries."

The lamp flame bent in some draught from the ill-fitting window and straightened again.

"So here is what I have decided." She made herself meet his eyes. They were dark and level and full of a patience that undid her more than any argument could have. "I will not ask you to testify. I will not ask you to stay silent. Either way I lose something I cannot replace, and I have finally understood that the losing is mine to bear and not yours to prevent. But whichever you choose — whichever, do you hear me — I will stand beside you when you choose it. If you take that stand on Thursday and name yourself, I will be in that room, and when they sneer at Miss Vane and her spy driver, I will say: yes. Him. Deliberately. And if you burn those ledgers tonight and walk away and let the forgeries fall where they fall, I will stand beside you in that too, and let the bill die if it must die, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I traded a law for a man and being — I think — at peace with the exchange." Her voice caught and steadied. "Because I have been choosing the cause over people for fifteen years, and the cause has never once chosen me back. And you have. Every day. Without asking."

The silence after was long. Somewhere across the harbor a ship's siren moaned, far off, like something dreaming.

"You understand what it means," he said quietly. "If I stand up. Your committee. Your work. Fifteen years of it."

"I understand exactly what it means. I have done the arithmetic myself, at last, properly, with all the figures showing." She smiled, and found that the smile hurt and was true. "And I find that I no longer accept the terms. Someone has to teach them that we are not columns in their ledger. It may as well be me. It appears I have been in training for it all my life."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he crossed to the desk, and picked up the sheet of copperplate he had been holding when she arrived, and held it out to her — the topmost page of eleven, tide-tables in a fair hand, and beneath them, in smaller script, the dates and contents of every Harrow letter he had ever read, ending with the word *fire*, underlined twice.

"It's finished," he said. "It's been finished for three days. All I needed was a reason." His hand did not shake. "Come Thursday, the world learns my name. I'd like you to hear it first."

And he spoke it — the dock-orphan name, the one no ledger in Bristol had recorded in fifteen years — aloud, in the lamplight, while the bell buoy rang and rang below the stairs, and Rosalind took the page from his hand as carefully as if it were the only document left in the world.

Outside, far down the harbor road, unseen in the fog, a man leaned against a warehouse wall and struck a match, and began, with a practised hand, to write.
