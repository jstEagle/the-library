## 31. The Night Before

The workshop still smelled of itself. That was the first thing Elodie understood, standing at the edge of the burned ground with the tide going out and the gulls arguing over something dead among the pilings—that a fire did not erase a place, it only changed what the place was made of. Char and lime and the sweet-rot ghost of creosote. Underneath all of it, faint as a voice through a wall, the harbor: tar, salt, rope, fish. The smell of the only home he had ever had before he learned that homes could be taken away by people who never touched them.

He had come after midnight, when even the Harrows' watchers grew honest in their boredom and went to drink. He had told himself he would not come. He had spent the whole day not coming—driving Rosalind to her committee, standing at attention while she spoke to him in the careful, level voice she used now, the voice of a woman managing a wound she refused to look at. *Thank you, Elodie. That will be all, Elodie.* She said his name more than she ever had before, and every time it landed like a coin dropped down a well: he heard it, but he could not tell how deep it went or what it struck on the way.

So he had walked out of Clifton Wood at dusk instead of taking the tram, and walked along the Floating Harbour with his collar up, and told himself at each corner that he was only stretching his legs, and arrived here anyway, because a man can lie to himself all evening and his feet will tell the truth.

The workshop had been Thomas Idris's whole world for twenty years, and now it was a black ribcage open to the sky. The roof beams had fallen inward like the fingers of a hand closing. The great doors hung off one hinge, scorched to the color of old tea. Where the presses had stood there was a mound of twisted iron fused with slagged brick, and someone—Thomas's people, probably, or just neighbors with nothing left to guard—had laid a wreath of dried flowers against the doorstep, already gray with ash that kept falling from the air itself, from the broken walls, from the sky's slow memory of what had happened here.

Elodie stood outside the doorway a long time. He was not afraid of the ruin. He had grown up among ruins; the docks were full of them, and children played in them, and sometimes children died in them, and everyone agreed it was sad and nothing was done. What stopped him was something else. It was the understanding that he had stood in this doorway a hundred times—in sunshine, in rain, holding parcels, waiting with the motorcar—and had always been a visitor. A shadow at the edge of another man's fire. And now the fire had made the place level with him. Now they were both things that had been burned down to their timbers and were trying to decide whether to stand again.

He went in.

The floor crunched. Ash held the shape of everything it had buried—a boot print, a fallen chair, a coil of rope gone to gray lace. Near the back wall, where the heat had been worst, the bricks had spalled and split, and the mortar between them showed white as bone. He found the place where Thomas had kept the membership rolls, or where it had been; the iron box was a puddle now, but the wall behind it was scarred in a fan shape, and Elodie put his palm flat against it without thinking, the way you touch a fevered brow, and snatched it back, though the metal was cold, had been cold for days.

*You knew,* he said to the room. Not aloud. He had not spoken aloud to anything but horses and engines for fifteen years, and it seemed suddenly important not to break the habit now, tonight of all nights, when tomorrow he would have to speak in front of strangers and magistrates and newspapermen and make words come out of his mouth like a man who had always owned them. *You knew in September. You wrote it down in October. You watched them map it in November. And you told yourself a note in a pocket was enough.*

The note had been enough for his survival. That was the arithmetic he had lived by since he was seven years old and had learned that the boy who saw too much was the boy who got thrown in the Frome. A note was deniable. A note cost nothing. A note let you sleep, if your conscience was the size of a farthing.

And yet Thomas lay in the infirmary with bandages over his eyes, and three families slept in the mission hall on straw ticks, and the membership rolls—the names of two hundred men and women who had trusted a printing press in a boatshed with their livelihoods—were saved only because Thomas had gone back into the fire for them, because Elodie's warning had been too soft and Thomas's courage had been exactly the right weight to fill the space where caution should have been.

*I am not a coward,* Elodie thought, and then, honestly, the way he read other men's letters: *I am a man who has practiced cowardice until it looks like wisdom from certain angles.*

He laughed. It came out wrong, a dry bark, and startled a rat somewhere in the rubble. The sound of his own laughter in the ruined workshop was the loneliest sound he had ever made, and he had once spent a whole winter alone under a coal wharf at eleven years old.

He turned to go, and that was when he saw the slate.

It was half-buried near the door—a writing slate, one of the ones the workshop children had used, because Thomas Idris had believed that boatwrights' kids should learn their letters same as the merchants' kids, had taught them evenings for nothing, chalk and patience. The frame was scorched but whole. Elodie crouched and worked it free of the ash with both hands, and underneath it, impossibly, a stub of chalk had survived, rolled into the corner where the frame met the slate, protected like a seed.

He held them and did not know why his hands had begun to shake. He had copied letters by lamplight with steadier nerves than this—letters that named Rosalind for destruction, letters that scheduled a fire between the second and third rockets of a festival. His hands had not shaken then. But those letters had been about other people.

He looked at the blank slate for a long time.

There was a thing he had not done since the winter of 1897. There was a name he had not written, not spoken, not permitted himself even to think in its full form, because names were hooks and hooks caught and the caught were thrown back or worse. He had been seven years old and his mother had died of the fever in a cellar off Narrow Quay, and before the parish men came he had done the one clever thing of his life: he had shed his name the way you shed a burning coat. The workhouse ledger had recorded him as *boy, age approx., no name given*, and later a dockmaster with a taste for poetry had called him Elodie because of some song, and Elodie he had remained, a name like an borrowed coat that fit so long it stopped feeling borrowed.

But he had had a name before that. His mother had sung it. His mother had written it once, in pencil, on the flyleaf of her one book—a Bible with the covers off—and he had read it by candlelight a thousand times before the book was sold for bread, and he had carried the spelling of it in his head through every year since, the way you carry a stone in your shoe that you refuse to take out because it is the last thing she gave you.

He set the slate on a fallen beam, kneeled in the ash—it soaked through his trousers, cold as riverbed—and took up the chalk.

His hand hovered. Fifteen years of discipline sat on his wrist like a gull's weight.

Then he wrote it. Four letters and then five, the small name and the family name his mother had given her father's trade to, in a child's round uncertain hand because his hand had forgotten how to be seven years old and had to learn it again, letter by letter, chalk grating, breath held:

**ELIAS MERRICK**

He stared at it.

Elias Merrick. Dock orphan, cellar off Narrow Quay, mother dead of fever, no kin, no claim, no paper. Elias Merrick, who had hidden under Coal Wharf and eaten from the fishwives' discard baskets and learned his letters from shipping manifests blown against the wharf fence, sounding out *consignment* and *demurrage* syllable by syllable in the wind. Elias Merrick, who had seen two men drowned in a cargo dispute in '98 and understood, with the terrible clarity available only to children and animals, that the boy who witnessed it must not exist, and had therefore stopped existing—became the boy who swept the pilot office for pennies, then the boy who ran errands, then the young man who could drive a motorcar and keep his mouth shut, then Elodie, then nothing, then whatever a man is when he is only useful.

It was not a grand name. It was not a name from anywhere. It was a dock name, a laborer's name, a name that would appear in the inquiry transcript tomorrow and cause a small stir precisely because no one had ever heard it—because the witness was inventing himself on the record, or rather, un-inventing fourteen years of fiction in front of God and the stipendiary magistrate and the Bristol press.

They will ask why you hid it, he thought. They will say a man who hides his name hides something else. Cedric Harrow's counsel will build a gallows out of it, plank by plank: *How do we know this witness is credible? How do we know he is not a suffragist plant, a union agent, a fantasist?* And the answer—the only answer—was the one the old pilot Gower had given him on the quayside three days ago, with the ledgers sitting between them like a sleeping dog:

*"Anonymity's the price, lad. Evidence that comes from nobody is worth nothing. You want them believed, you stand up and say who fed them in. Your name or nothing."*

Your name or nothing. All his life he had chosen nothing, and nothing had kept him alive, and nothing had let the workshop burn.

He picked up the chalk again and beneath the name, slowly, because his hand wanted to shake and he would not permit it, he wrote the date. Then, because it seemed right that the slate should hold something true and not merely biographical, he added one line more, small, in the corner, the way a boy writes a thing he does not want the bigger boys to see:

*I read the letters. I knew the day. I was slow.*

Not a confession anyone would find—not here, not on a child's slate in a burnt workshop that would be cleared within the week. But the writing of it did something to his chest, loosened a valve he had not known was shut, and he sat back on his heels in the ash and breathed, really breathed, for the first time since the night of the festival, when he had run through crowds lit green and gold by fireworks toward a glow on the horizon that was not fireworks at all.

A church bell somewhere across the water counted two.

Tomorrow the inquiry resumed. Tomorrow Gower would bring the pilot ledgers wrapped in oilcloth and swear they were official records maintained in the ordinary course of the pilots' duty, which was even true. Tomorrow the stipendiary would ask who had placed the loose pages among them, and Gower would turn his white beard toward the gallery and say, *Him. The driver. Says his name's Elias Merrick.* Tomorrow Rosalind Vane would sit in that hall and hear, for the first time, the name of the boy under the coat she had never known existed—would hear that the man who served her tea and drove her motorcar and loved her with the dumb constancy of a dog at a gate had been lying to the entire world since before she met him, and lying to her most carefully of all.

Would she forgive it? He had stopped asking the question, because he had realized, kneeling here in the ashes of other people's livelihoods, that forgiveness was not the thing he needed to settle tonight. The thing to settle was smaller and harder: not *will she have me* but *who stands up tomorrow*. If Elodie stood, he could soften it—*a servant misled, a poor devil caught between masters*, pitiable, harmless, dismissible, and the evidence with him. If Elias Merrick stood, he was a man who had read stolen letters for fifteen years and hidden in a great lady's household and watched a workshop burn rather than spend his own name to stop it, and no power on earth would make that comfortable, and the testimony would land like a hammer because uncomfortable men are believed.

He thought of Thomas in the ward, face bandaged, voice gone to gravel, saying: *You'll do it right, lad? All of it? Names and dates and the lot. Half a truth hanged my mate Danny in '09.* Trust given across class lines, absolute, undeserved. He thought of the membership rolls saved, two hundred names in a waterproof tin that smelled now of smoke and iodine. Two hundred people who had never hidden their names from anybody. They had signed them, in ink, to a union, knowing what signing meant. Knowing the list might someday be read aloud by the wrong person, in the wrong room. They had signed anyway.

Against that, what was his little death of anonymity? What was one borrowed coat returned?

Elodie took the slate to the water's edge, where the mud shone flat and silvered under a thinning fog, and knelt, and struck a match from the box he had carried in his pocket since the festival—he did not know why he still carried it, except that a man keeps the tool of his worst night—and held it to the lower corner of the frame.

Slate does not burn. He knew that; everyone knows that. But the chalk—he pressed the flame to the stub of chalk, and the old tallow in it caught, guttering, blue-hearted, and the flame climbed and he fed it the dry wreath-flowers from the doorstep, and the fire stood up on the mud in the windless air, small and stubborn, throwing light on the black ribs of the workshop behind it.

He watched the name blur. Chalk does not burn either, not truly—it crumbles, whitens, goes to powder that the next tide will take. But the letters softened, sagged, lost their edges, and Elias Merrick became a smudge became a pale ghost became nothing legible, and Elodie understood that he was not destroying the name at all. You cannot burn a thing out of you that your mother sang. He was only moving it—from hiding into his chest, from a secret into a decision. Tomorrow he would give it away to strangers and it would never be solely his again; it would belong to the inquiry, to the newspapers, to the sneers of Harrow counsel and the pity of committee ladies. Tonight it was still his, and he held it in the firelight the way you hold your last coin before paying a debt you are proud to owe.

The fire sank. He raked the mud over it with his heel, thorough, a lifetime's habit of leaving no trace, and then—for the first time in fifteen years—deliberately broke the habit. He left the scorched wreath-wire where it lay. He left his footprints in the ash of the doorway, plain as a signature, for anyone to read.

At the top of the quay he stopped once and looked back. The workshop stood black against the paling east, patient as a hull awaiting repair, and above it the first gray light was doing what gray light does to ruins—making them almost beautiful, making them almost bearable.

*Elias Merrick,* he said aloud, to the water, to the gulls, to the woman asleep across the city who would hate him or love him or both by nightfall. His voice cracked on the second word, rusted with disuse, and he said it again, and again, until it came out level, until it sounded like it belonged to a man who had always owned it.

Then he turned toward the town, toward the inquiry hall, toward the longest day of his life, and walked—footprints behind him, name ahead of him, and neither one, for the first time he could remember, a secret.
