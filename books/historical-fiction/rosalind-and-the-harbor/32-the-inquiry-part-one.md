## 32. The Inquiry, Part One

### Rosalind

The Council Chamber of the Bristol Corporation had been built by men who believed that marble could make a lie sound like truth. Rosalind Vane sat beneath the arched window with its coat-of-arms and its leaded light, and she thought: *they have polished every surface in this room except the one they mean to judge me on.*

She wore grey. She had chosen it deliberately the night before, standing before her mirror with the gas turned low—grey wool, high collar, no colour at all. Colour invited comment; black invited mourning; grey invited nothing. She was to be read today as a document, and a document must not distract from its contents. Her hat was plain. Her gloves were clean. Her hands, inside them, did not shake, because she had instructed them not to.

The gallery was full. That was the first thing she noticed when the commissioners filed in behind their chairman—a Mr. Pellworthy, dry as old rope—and took their seats at the long table beneath the city arms. Full, and fuller than any inquiry into a warehouse fire had any right to be. Word had travelled. She caught fragments of it in the murmurs below her: *the suffrage woman* — *the Vane motorcar* — *they say there are letters.* The word *letters* moved through the room like smoke finding a draught.

She did not look up at the gallery. She had decided that too. Looking up would acknowledge that someone up there mattered to her, and today no one must matter, no one must be legible, no one must be able to say afterward that Lady Vane's eyes went searching for a particular face when the particular question was put.

But she knew he was there. She knew it the way one knows weather through a wall.

The fire was three weeks gone. The workshops were a black ribcage against the harbour now, gulls picking at the char, and Thomas Idris lay in the infirmary ward with his hands bandaged into mittens and half his sight burned away, and the official finding—drafted before anyone had testified, if you asked her, though nobody was asking—was *defective electrical wiring.* She had read the preliminary report twice. It was a document written by men who wished to go home.

Then, yesterday evening, the shape of the day had changed. A clerk had come to the house with notice of additional evidence to be entered: correspondence bearing upon the financial affairs of Mrs. Rosalind Vane. Not *Miss*. Not *the deceased's estate*. Her name, spelled out in a clerk's careful copperplate, entered into the public record between an itemised list of burnt timber and a schedule of harbour rates.

She had sat with that notice for a long time. Then she had gone down to the kitchen, where Elodie was cleaning the lamps, and she had watched his back while he worked and said nothing, and he had said nothing, and the silence between them had grown so heavy that the whole house seemed to lean under it.

He knew something. He had known for weeks. And whatever it was, he was carrying it toward this room even now, and she had chosen—God help her, she had *chosen*—not to ask him first.

"Mrs. Rosalind Vane." The clerk's voice cracked across the chamber. "Will you state your name and occupation for the record?"

She stood. Three hundred faces tilted toward her like flowers after sun, and among them, she knew without looking, were the Harrows' solicitors, four of them, arranged along the front bench like cutlery.

"Rosalind Mary Vane," she said, and let the name sit in the air a moment longer than courtesy required. "I am honorary secretary of the Bristol and West Women's Franchise Society. I am also—" and here she permitted herself the smallest pause, "—the owner of a motorcar, which is, I am told, relevant."

A ripple of laughter, quickly suppressed. Pellworthy did not laugh. "You will confine yourself to answers, madam."

"To questions, sir," she said pleasantly, "I will confine myself to answers."

* * *

They began, as she had known they would, with the fire. Wiring, insulation, the age of the building, the placement of the oil stores—all of it conducted in the flat liturgy of officialdom, all of it leading nowhere, which was its purpose. She answered what was asked of her and volunteered nothing. Across the chamber, the Harrow benches were quiet, attentive, innocent. Old Mr. Harrow himself did not attend; his absence was itself a statement, made in the grammar of class: *we are above this room.* But Cedric was there, third row, grey morning coat, watching her with the mild blue gaze of a man observing weather he has already scheduled.

For two hours she forgot about him entirely. That was her error, and she would pay for it.

Because at half past eleven the chief counsel for the Corporation rose—a smooth man named Aylward, retained, she would later learn, through a firm that counted the Harrow shipping accounts among its oldest clients—and said, "My lords, I wish now to enter certain documents into evidence. Correspondence concerning payments received."

The word *received* landed in her chest like a stone dropped into still water.

"The correspondence purports to show," Aylward continued, unhurried, folding his spectacles as though the matter bored him, "that over a period of eighteen months, sums totalling some two hundred and forty pounds were remitted to Mrs. Vane personally, by officers of the harbour unions, in consideration of her—ah—*advocacy* on their behalf. In short, sirs: the lady was paid."

The chamber went very still, the way water goes still before it boils.

"That is a lie," Rosalind said.

It came out of her before strategy could catch it—not shouted, but level, and somehow worse than shouting, because the whole room heard the crack in it, the place where the composure split to show the living thing underneath. Three hundred people leaned forward.

"Madam will have opportunity to respond," Pellworthy said, without sympathy. "Mr. Aylward, produce the letters."

And they brought them out. An usher carried them forward on a wooden tray like relics, and Rosalind watched them come and understood, with the cold clarity that arrives only after disaster, that she had been rehearsing the wrong speech for three weeks. She had prepared for obstruction, condescension, the slow suffocation of procedure. She had not prepared to see, held up before a public gallery, five letters in handwriting that mimicked her own correspondents'—she recognised the union secretary Hobbs's cramped left-slant at once, and it was wrong, the ascenders too neat, Hobbs never crossed his t's so cleanly—and beneath the forged hands, her own name, and figures, and dates.

Two hundred and forty pounds. It was not enough to ruin a woman of means. It was exactly enough to ruin a woman of *principle*, which was the point. The sum had been calibrated. Somewhere in a good chair in Clifton, someone had done arithmetic on her reputation and arrived at a number.

"Mrs. Vane." Aylward's voice was gentle now, which was how she knew the blade had gone in. "Can you account for these sums?"

"I can account for every shilling the Society has handled since its founding," she said. "Our books are open. Our treasurer will testify. These letters are forgeries."

"Forgeries." He smiled faintly. "Convenient things, forgeries. They appear whenever a lady is embarrassed." He turned a page. "Note the date on the second letter—the fourteenth of March. Where were you on the fourteenth of March, madam?"

"In London, meeting with the parliamentary committee."

"How convenient."

The laughter this time was not suppressed. It came from the back benches, from the standing room, from men who had paid their penny to see a suffragist flayed, and it washed over her, and she stood in it with her chin up because that was all that remained—chin up, spine straight, hands still—and she thought, with a strange detached lucidity: *so this is what Elodie has felt his whole life. To be seen and not believed. To have one's interior read by strangers who have already decided what it says.*

The thought of him nearly undid her. She fixed her eyes on the chairman's inkwell and did not look up.

### Cedric

Cedric Harrow had expected satisfaction. What he felt instead, as the letters went round the press table and the reporters bent over them like priests over scripture, was something closer to indigestion—a heaviness under the breastbone, a sense that the mechanism was working but the sound it made was wrong.

He had commissioned the work himself, of course. Not personally—never personally; his father's first law was that a Harrow's hands should touch nothing but ledgers and linen—but he had approved the sum, and the man who produced the forgeries was very good. Good enough that Cedric himself, reading them over brandy a fortnight ago, had felt a flicker of unease at how well Hobbs's hand had been copied, how naturally the dates fell, how plausible the amounts were. Good work always frightened him slightly. It was too much like honesty.

But it was working. Look at the room. The reporters writing fast; the gallery craning; Mrs. Vane standing at the witness table with her grey dress and her grey face and her terrible stillness, taking blow after blow without the wit to sit down, which was the mistake all these women made—they thought endurance read as dignity, when in fact it only gave the audience time to grow comfortable in its contempt. By lunchtime the evening papers would have it: *SUFFRAGE LEADER ACCUSED OF TAKING UNION MONEY.* By tomorrow the bill's friends in the Commons would begin the familiar retreat, distancing themselves like men stepping back from a body on a pavement. His father would pour one glass of port tonight instead of two, which was his way of applauding.

And yet.

Cedric's attention kept sliding, against his will, to the gallery—to the public seats at the rear, where the ordinary people sat on hard benches among the reporters. There was a man there, third bench, hat brim low, sitting with the unnatural stillness of someone who has learned to be furniture. Cedric had noticed him an hour ago and dismissed him, and then un-dismissed him, because the man did not fidget, and everyone fidgeted.

He had seen that stillness before. Outside the workshop gates, in the week before the fire, a driver leaning against the Vane motorcar with his cap tipped down, watching the yard with his hands folded—hands folded *that way*, thumbs hooked, a workingman's rest that looked studied, like a phrase learned from a book. Cedric remembered thinking at the time: *that man watches the way a surveyor watches, not the way a servant watches.*

Now the man sat in his gallery and did not move, and Cedric found that the taste of his victory had begun, faintly, to turn.

"Mr. Aylward," he murmured during a recess, catching the counsel by the elbow near the windows. "The gallery. Third bench. Do we know him?"

Aylward followed his glance, squinted. "Some reporter's man, I shouldn't wonder."

"Find out."

"My dear Cedric, the inquiry concerns a warehouse—"

"The inquiry concerns whatever I say it concerns, or my father's firm withdraws its cooperation and takes its evidence to the newspapers instead. Find out who is sitting on the third bench."

Aylward's smile thinned. Men did not like being spoken to plainly; it stripped the game of its pleasure. But he inclined his head, and Cedric turned back to the chamber, and the heaviness under his breastbone did not lift.

Because here was the thing he could not say aloud, not to Aylward, not to his father, not to himself in the dark: the plan had accounted for everything except being *watched while it happened.* He had built the trap and sprung it and imagined the mouse dead by morning, and he had never once considered that the mouse might have eyes, or that the eyes might belong to someone who had spent fifteen years learning what other people's paper said. Servants were furniture. That was the axiom his father had taught him, the axiom the whole order rested on. Furniture did not read. Furniture did not remember.

And yet the driver of Rosalind Vane's motorcar had stood outside the workshop gates with his thumbs hooked, memorising the yard.

Cedric smoothed his coat and returned to his seat and told himself it was nothing. The letter-work was good. The room was turning. By Friday the bill would be wounded past saving and Mrs. Vane would be a cautionary tale told at dinner parties, and none of it—not one atom of it—would ever be traced to a shipping office in Clifton.

He repeated this to himself the way one repeats a prayer whose god one has stopped believing in.

### Elodie

Elodie Galloway—for he was saying the name to himself now, silently, over and over, like a man rehearsing a line in a play he had agreed to perform—sat on the third bench of the gallery with his hat brim low and his hands folded, and he watched Rosalind take the blows, and every blow landed in him too.

He had thought he understood what this would cost. He had spent three weeks preparing himself: the copying finished, the pages sewn into the pilot ledger between the entries for the spring tides, old Mr. Pellow the pilot sworn and stubborn and ready, the words he would need to say arranged in his head in order like parcels on a delivery route. He had prepared for fear. Fear was an old acquaintance; he and fear had shared lodgings since he was six years old.

What he had not prepared for was the *shame* of sitting still.

Because he knew. He knew the letters were false the moment the usher lifted the first one—he had read the real Hobbs forty times, he knew the man's hand the way you know a friend's tread on stairs—and he knew who had bought the falsehood, and he knew the dates had been chosen to fit gaps in Rosalind's diary, which meant someone had studied her movements, which meant the plot went deeper than fire, deeper than disgrace, into the plain machinery of destruction. All of this knowledge sat in him like cargo in a hold, ballast he could not shift, while downstairs a woman who had once trusted him with her life's papers stood alone and lied about magnificently.

*I can account for every shilling.*

Her voice carried. Even flattened by the chamber's bad acoustics it carried, and he heard in it the thing she would hate him for hearing: not fear. Fury. She was furious the way the harbour was furious in a storm—not loudly at first, but with a rising underneath that would drown whatever stood in it.

Beside him a reporter whispered to another: "Vane woman's finished. Shame—she had the best platform in the West."

"She'll deny it all and no one prints denials," the second said. "Dead copy by six."

Elodie closed his eyes briefly. *Not yet,* he thought. *Not yet she isn't.* And then, because lying to himself had become impossible somewhere between the lamplight of chapter six and the ashes of the workshop, he corrected it: *Not yet—if I stand when I'm called. If I don't fail again.*

That was the wound he carried into this room and would carry out of it: the note he had passed Thomas Idris, that soft, cowardly, anonymous *check your premises at night*, when what the night required was a man standing in the yard with a lantern and a voice. Thomas had stayed with his presses because a stranger's unsigned scrawl was not a reason to abandon thirty years of work. Elodie had known it wouldn't be. He had sent it anyway, because sending it let him tell himself he had acted. He had purchased his conscience at the cheapest price available, and Thomas had paid the difference in skin.

The recess came. The chamber emptied slowly, lawyers clustering, reporters streaming out to file their paragraphs. Elodie did not move. Through the thinning crowd he watched Aylward peel away from the others, speak briefly to a clerk, and glance upward—at the gallery, at the benches, at him.

Their eyes met for half a second. Elodie did not look away. Fifteen years of practice said: *look away, become the wall, become the bench, become nothing.* But the wall had burned down with the workshop, or he had burned it himself, page by page, sewing his evidence into the pilot's ledger, and there was no becoming-nothing left in him. He met the lawyer's glance the way a debtor meets a creditor, and Aylward frowned slightly, uncertain, and turned away.

They know the face, then. Or they soon would. Either way the clock in his chest had begun its last winding.

When the session resumed, Rosalind was called again, and the questions turned crueler—her finances, her society's books, whether she had ever *personally* benefited from funds subscribed by working men for working men's purposes—and she answered each one with a precision that must have cost her physically, he could see the effort of it in her shoulders, the way a swimmer's shoulders show the stroke. Once, only once, at the worst of it, when Aylward read aloud a passage of the forged letter in a voice pitched to suggest intimacy—as if the words themselves were indecent—her gaze lifted, just barely, toward the gallery.

Not searching. Refusing to search. She looked at the gallery the way you look at a horizon you have decided not to hope from.

But Elodie stood up anyway.

Just stood, quietly, at the end of the recess bell, so that for one instant—one heartbeat—her eyes passed across a man on his feet among the departing crowd, hat in his hands, looking directly down at her. No signal. No smile. Nothing that any reporter could print or any lawyer construe. Only this: *I am here. I am watching. You are not alone in this room, whatever it believes.*

Something moved in her face. It was gone immediately—she was too well-made for anything less immediate—but it was there, a single seam of feeling showing through the grey cloth of her composure, and then she turned back to the commissioners with her chin up, higher than before, and Elodie sat down with his heart going like a piston and waited for the afternoon.

It came soon enough. Pellworthy rapped the table. "This inquiry will adjourn until ten o'clock tomorrow morning, at which time the commission will hear further evidence"—he consulted his sheet, and here his dry voice acquired, for the first time all day, a grain of genuine curiosity, the sound of a man reading a line he did not expect—"including testimony offered by the harbour pilots' association regarding the official pilot ledgers of the Port of Bristol."

The chamber rustled. Aylward's head came up sharply. On the Harrow bench, Cedric Harrow went very still—the stillness of a man who has heard, in a routine sentence, the exact pitch of a trap closing.

Elodie folded his hat onto his knee, and beneath the brim, unseen by anyone in the whole marble room, he allowed himself to close his eyes and say the name once more, silently, the dock orphan's name, the true one, getting his mouth around it the way you test a knot before trusting it with your weight.

Tomorrow he would say it out loud.
