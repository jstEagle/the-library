## 38. Letters, Unsealed

The stall had a plank counter, an inkwell with a chipped rim, and a sign painted by Thomas Idris himself in letters too large and too honest for any shopfront: *Letters Read. Letters Written. Fair Rates. Nothing Hidden.*

Elodie had argued for something plainer — *Correspondence Undertaken* — but Thomas had laughed at him, a laugh that still came carefully around the burn scars on his throat, and said, "You spent fifteen years hiding what you could do, man. Let the whole harbor see it written on a board."

So the board stood there, at the edge of the fish market where the cobbles gave way to the quay, and every morning Elodie set out his ink, his blotting sand, his two good pens and one bad one he kept for the men who chewed theirs, and he waited for the harbor to bring him its words.

It came slowly at first. A widow wanting to write to her sister in Cardiff about the price of herring. A stoker who'd been refused his back wages and needed a letter that would frighten a shipping agent without getting its writer blacklisted. A girl of nine who climbed onto the stool across from him, planted her elbows on the counter like a barrister, and dictated a letter to her father at sea that was, in its entirety: *Dear Da. Come home. The dog misses you. I miss you more. Your loving daughter, Bess.* She paid him a farthing from a fist that opened reluctantly, and he took it as solemnly as if it were a guinea.

That was the difference, he thought some mornings, watching the light come up over the masts. That was the whole difference between then and now.

Before, every word he read belonged to someone else's secret. He had been a key that turned in locks without being seen turning — memorizing, burning, forgetting, the liturgy of his survival repeated so many times it had worn grooves into him like water wears stone. Now people sat across from him in full daylight and *gave* him their words, watched him take them down, corrected his spelling of their mothers' names, asked him to read the sentence back twice because they liked how it sounded. There was no shame in it. There was no shadow in it. A woman could hand him a letter from her son in Canada and weep openly while he read it aloud, and half the market might pause to listen, and no one thought anything of it except that the letter-writer read well and did not rush the sad parts.

He had not known, until he sat behind that plank counter, how heavy the secrets had been. Not individually — each one had been light as paper, that was the trick of them — but cumulatively, the way ballast sits in a hull. For fifteen years he had carried other people's correspondence the way a ship carries cargo: sealed, unexamined by daylight, felt only as weight. Now the hold stood open and empty, and he discovered the strange buoyancy of having nothing hidden anywhere in him.

Not entirely nothing. There remained the pilot ledgers, sitting in the archive room above the harbor office, catalogued and official and containing, loose among the tide tables and channel soundings, the true account of the Harrow fire and the plot that preceded it. The inquiry had taken what it needed; the newspapers had printed what suited them; Cedric Harrow had gone to ground somewhere beyond the reach of Bristol gossip, disowned by a father whose name now appeared in the papers only in connection with charitable subscriptions. But the ledgers stayed, and Elodie visited them sometimes the way a man visits a grave — not because anything there needed him anymore, but because he had been the only witness, and witnesses owe their dead a visitation.

And there remained Rosalind, which was not a thing he kept so much as a thing that kept him.

* * *

Winter came down the Avon with the fog, and business changed with the season. More letters home to Ireland, to Cornwall, to the pit villages of Somerset whence the dock families had drifted like sediment. More requests for letters of appeal — to the guardians, to the parish, to the railway company that had taken a boy's leg and offered four pounds compensation. Elodie wrote them all with the same care he had once given to memorizing sealed dispatches, and he found that outrage, when composed openly and on behalf of someone standing beside you, was a cleaner fuel than outrage hoarded.

Thomas Idris came by most weeks, ostensibly to check on the stall's roof, actually to sit and talk. The workshops had risen again on the old foundations — new timber, new glass, a plaque by the door naming the fire and the inquiry in words that managed to be both dignified and unforgiving. The membership rolls hung framed inside the hall now, scorched at one corner, and apprentices were taught their first lesson standing before them: *these are names; learn why names matter.*

"They're asking me to stand for the council," Thomas said one gray afternoon, his gloved hand flat on the counter. "The union. Some of the older men think a face from the fire would do good work."

"Will you?"

Thomas looked out at the water, at the cranes standing against the sky like patient herons. "I don't know. I spent one night refusing to run from a building, and it near killed me, and everyone's decided that makes me brave." He turned back, and his eyes had the shrewdness that had survived the burns better than his skin had. "You'd know something about that. People deciding what you are."

"I let them decide," Elodie said. "That was the arrangement."

"And now?"

Now. The word hung between them. Elodie dipped his pen, though there was nothing to write.

"Now I put my name at the bottom of things," he said. "It seems a small payment for everything else."

Thomas nodded slowly, and before he left he laid a coin on the counter — payment, he insisted, for a letter Elodie had written his wife months ago, a debt he claimed never to have settled. It was not true; he had paid at the time. But Elodie had learned that some men needed to pay twice before a kindness sat easy, and he took the coin.

* * *

The letter from Rosalind arrived on a Tuesday in February, carried by the ordinary post, addressed in her hand.

He knew her hand before he knew her name on it — the slight forward lean of the letters, the way she crossed her t's like small declarations. Fifteen years of reading had left him fluent in handwriting the way sailors are fluent in weather, and hers he could have identified blind, by pressure alone: a hand that bore down on the page as though the page might resist.

The envelope was not sealed.

He held it a long moment in the cold air of the stall, understanding. Anyone could have read it. The postman, the sorting room, the boy who carried the mail sacks along the quay — all of them had passed it along open, or rather closed but unsealed, the flap merely tucked in, an invitation disguised as an oversight. No. Not an oversight. He could hear her voice in it already, dry and precise: *An oversight is a thing one fails to intend, Mr. Galloway.*

Mr. Galloway. That was another matter — the name itself. He had gone by it since he was eleven years old, invented it out of a gravestone and a Sunday school register, worn it so long it fit like his own skin. His real name he had spoken once, at the inquiry, in front of the commissioners and the gallery and the reporters, and it had sounded in his own ears like a stranger clearing his throat. He used it now on official documents and nowhere else. But she had addressed the letter to that name — his dock name, his orphan name, the one he had burned on a paper beside the ruined workshop the night before he testified. She must have known it from the inquiry records. She had chosen it deliberately, the way she chose everything.

He set down the letter without opening it further and served the next customer — Mrs. Penhale, who wanted to tell her brother in Newport that their mother had taken a turn but was rallying, and who cried while he wrote *rallying*, and thanked him twice, and left. Then the quay emptied for the lunch hour, the fog pressing in soft as wool, and he was alone with the letter.

He unfolded it.

*Mr. Galloway —*

*You will notice the envelope is unsealed. This is deliberate. I have spent a year learning what you always knew: that a sealed letter is a small cowardice, a private room in which one says what one dare not say in company. You lived your whole life in such rooms. I built mine out of public speeches, which is only a different architecture for the same fear. We were both, in our ways, hiding.*

*The bill goes to its third reading in the spring. The committee believes it will pass. I believe they are right, and I find I care less than I expected to. Victory, I have discovered, is a room as well — one walks into it and finds the same people saying the same admiring things, and one understands that admiration is a wall with no doors.*

*You will want to know why I am writing. I am writing because you left, and because leaving was correct, and because correctness has proved to be the coldest companion I have ever kept. I told you once that duty was the shape of my life. I have not renounced it. I will not. But I have learned its dimensions, and they do not fill the whole house. There is a room in me that has been dark for years, and you lit it without meaning to, and I have been trying ever since to decide whether to thank you or resent you, and I have concluded that the honest answer is both, and that honesty is owed to you if it is owed to anyone.*

*I said things to you that last night at the Vane house that I have regretted in the manner one regrets wounds inflicted in self-defense. I cannot unsay them. I can say this: the silence you kept nearly broke me, and the truth you finally told saved the movement more than my speeches ever did, and I have never decided which of those facts weighs more, and I suspect I never shall. You were a better servant of the truth than I was, and I was supposed to be its professional.*

*The workshops stand. Thomas sends his regards through channels more direct than this one, knowing him. The harbor remembers you, Mr. Galloway — under both your names, which is fitting, since it was the harbor that gave you the second one.*

*I do not ask you to return. Asking would be a kind of sealing. I leave this letter open, as one leaves a door open — not expecting anyone to walk through it, but declining to lock it. If you come to the rally in March, you will find me speaking by the rebuilt workshop, and I will look for you in the crowd, and neither of us need pretend we are looking for anyone else.*

*If you do not come, then know that somewhere in this city a woman reads the shipping reports every morning, though she has no ships, because it is the closest the paper comes to mentioning the harbor, and the harbor is the closest the paper comes to mentioning you.*

*Yours in no capacity whatsoever,*
*Rosalind Vane*

He read it three times. The third time his eyes blurred, and he set it flat on the counter and smoothed it with both hands as though the paper might buckle under what it carried.

*Yours in no capacity whatsoever.* Only Rosalind could construct a sentence that was simultaneously a joke, a shield, and a surrender. He could almost see her writing it — the pen bearing down, the pause after *whatsoever*, the faintest tightening at the corner of her mouth that he had learned to read from the driver's seat the way he read everything else: sideways, silently, without being caught looking.

She had signed it with her full name. No *R. Vane*, no initials. *Rosalind Vane*, written out entire, in a letter meant to be read by strangers. It was the smallest thing in the letter and the largest.

* * *

He worked the rest of the afternoon. A letter to a widow's insurance society. A note for the fishmonger's boy to take to his sweetheart, which Elodie rendered faithfully despite its complete absence of grammar and complete sufficiency of feeling. As the light failed he lit the lamp, and the fog outside turned gold at the edges, and he sat with Rosalind's letter beside the inkwell like a small sun.

Once — he would swear afterward it was only once — he allowed himself the old habit. He read it again and began, involuntarily, to memorize it, the rhythm of the sentences lodging themselves in the grooves fifteen years had worn. And then he stopped, and smiled at himself alone in the lamplight, and reached for a fresh sheet.

There was no need to memorize. She had left it with him. One did not memorize what one was permitted to keep.

He wrote his reply in the last hour of daylight, and he wrote it slowly, and when it was done he read it over and found it contained none of the eloquence the situation demanded and all of the plainness it actually required:

*Miss Vane —*

*Your letter reached me unsealed and I read it in front of God and the fish market, which I believe was your intention and is certainly your style.*

*The bill will pass. Go and win it. I will be at the rally in March, standing where the crowd is thickest, and I will not pretend to be looking at the speakers.*

*I keep a stall now. People bring me their words in daylight. You would not believe how much lighter they are than the ones I used to steal.*

*Come by sometime. The door is not locked either.*

*E. Galloway*

He looked at the signature a long moment. Then he crossed out *E. Galloway*, and beneath it, in careful letters he had not written since he was eleven years old and a gravestone had given him a different name, he wrote the name the harbor had given him first.

He sealed nothing. He tucked the flap in, the way she had taught him without teaching him, and left it open on the counter overnight where anyone might see it — a letter lying open to the lamplight, saying everything, ashamed of nothing.

Outside, the fog rolled off the water and the harbor lights came up one by one, and somewhere across the city a woman was reading shipping reports for news of a man the papers had forgotten, and did not know yet that in the morning the post would carry her a letter with his true name at the bottom of it — the name she had never heard spoken, the one he had burned and resurrected and finally, at last, given away.

The rally was five weeks away. The harbor, which remembered everything, was already counting the days.
