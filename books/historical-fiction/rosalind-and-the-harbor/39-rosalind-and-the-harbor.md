## 39. Rosalind and the Harbor

**Elodie**

The morning of the rally, Elodie Galloway woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, and lay in his narrow bed above the chandler's shop on Prince Street listening to the harbor wake up around him.

He had a room now—a real one, with a window that faced the water and a door that locked from the inside, though he had stopped locking it months ago. There was nothing in the room anyone wanted. A change of clothes, a razor, a tin of pens, the small stack of letters he kept not for their contents but for their handwriting: the careful loops of a woman signing herself *Mrs. Prewitt* asking after her son's wages, the blunt capitals of a stoker too proud to admit he couldn't spell his daughter's name. He had written that one for him. *Dear Nellie. I am well and thinking of you always. Your father, Jonah.*

The man had wept over it. Elodie had pretended not to see, which was its own kind of skill, and one he was trying, slowly, to give up.

He dressed in his good coat—the gray one, not the driver's black—and looked at himself in the spotted mirror above the washstand. A year ago there had been no mirror worth looking into. A driver checked his hands, his collar, the shine of his boots; a driver did not check his face, because a driver's face was meant to be furniture. Now he caught himself studying it sometimes like a stranger's: the scar through the left eyebrow he'd gotten at nine years old falling off a bollard rope, the deep lines around the mouth that came from fifteen years of holding things behind his teeth. He was thirty-one years old and his face was only now beginning to look like it belonged to someone.

Downstairs, Mrs. Cottle from the chandler's had left bread and dripping on the board as she always did on rally days, wrapped in cloth, with a note in her round schoolroom hand: *For Mr. Galloway, who does the letters proper.* She paid him in food half the time and he had learned to accept it without shame, which had taken longer than learning anything else.

The fog was lifting as he walked down toward the harbor, burning off in rags off the water, and the city smelled the way it always smelled in October—coal smoke and brine and tar, the great animal breath of Bristol waking. But when he came out onto the quay opposite the workshops, he stopped, as he still stopped every time, though by now he must have seen them a hundred times.

They had rebuilt. Of course they had; Thomas Idris would have rebuilt them out of spite if nothing else. But they had rebuilt *larger*, and *lit*, and the new hall where the presses now ran openly, legally, printing union broadsheets under their own imprint with the inquiry's verdict framed beside the door—it stood three stories where the old workshop had stood two, its windows wide and unshuttered, and this morning every one of those windows held a lamp against the early dark so that the whole building glowed like a ship coming into harbor. Someone had strung bunting between the hall's eaves and the old crane on the quay. Red and white. Someone had climbed up there in the dark to do it, and Elodie found himself smiling at the thought of Thomas Idris, bandaged hands and all, directing some boy up a ladder because he could not abide crooked bunting even on the morning of the most important day the harbor had seen in a generation.

The crowd was gathering already. Dockers in their Sunday coats, women with children on their hips, the pilots in their uniforms standing together in a knot near the water like cormorants. And here and there, faces he knew by name now—not by letter, by name, spoken aloud, hand shaken, *Mr. Galloway, good morning*—faces that nodded to him as he passed and made room for him at the front, because he was known here, and being known still sat on him like a coat cut slightly too large, wearing better every week.

Old Prynne the pilot found him by the harbor wall, as Old Prynne always did, and pressed something into his hand: a boiled sweet, wrapped in paper. "Nerves," Prynne said. "You'll want it when she starts talking."

"I'm not speaking," Elodie said. "I'm just—"

"You're just standing where everyone can see you," Prynne said, "in your good coat, waiting for her. Aye. Just that." The old man's eyes were shrewd and kind and merciless. "Eat your sweet, lad."

Elodie ate his sweet.

Across the water, beyond the crowd, beyond the cranes and the moored lighters, the tide was running green-gray and steady, carrying leaves and wood-shavings out toward the mouth of the Avon, out toward the sea, out toward everything. He watched it run and thought about how a man could spend fifteen years learning every current in a river and never once let it move him anywhere.

Not anymore. He was moving. It was slow—everything in him moved slow, like a heavy boat answering her helm—but he was moving, and this morning he could feel it in his chest the way you feel a tide turn before you can see it.

A stir went through the crowd. Somewhere a cheer started, ragged, then gathered itself.

She had come.

* * *

**Rosalind**

Rosalind Vane stood in the doorway of the new workshop hall and let herself be seen.

It had taken her two years and a scandal to learn the trick of it, and even now it did not come naturally. She knew how to be looked at—that was the easiest thing in the world, she had been looked at since she was six years old, at parties, at meetings, across committee tables, always weighed and mostly found wanting or useful. But being *seen* was different. Being seen meant standing still while people took in the whole of you, including the parts you would have preferred to keep, and it had nearly destroyed her once already, so she supposed she ought to be good at it by now.

The hall was full. Not just full—*changed*. A year ago these same families had come to hear her speak with their caps in their hands and their eyes down, grateful for her voice and doubtful of her permanence. Today they filled the benches like shareholders. The men from the night shift sat beside the day shift without the old suspicion; the women of the laundry committee had their own banner, sewn themselves, hanging crooked over the platform because Thomas had insisted it hang exactly where the membership rolls used to hang and no one had argued. The rolls themselves were framed now, glass-fronted, on the wall by the stairs—scorched at one corner, every name legible. She had looked at them for a long moment on her way in. *Every word,* she thought, in a voice that was not quite hers. *Someone read every word.*

"Miss Vane." Thomas Idris loomed up beside her, huge and gentle, his burned hand curled at his side the way it always was now, the fingers gone stiff as cable. His eyes were weaker than they'd been; he squinted at her even at arm's length, and his smile was the same smile it had been the first day she'd ever come to the old workshop, back when she was a name on a pamphlet and he was a name on a list and neither of them knew it. "We're ready when you are. No hurry. Though my wife will tell you I've said that about every fire I ever failed to put out."

She laughed, because he meant her to, and because grief and humor lived in that man side by side like lodgers who had long ago stopped minding each other.

"Thomas." She touched his sleeve, the uninjured arm. "Thank you. For asking me."

"Asking you." He snorted. "Miss, there's four hundred people in this hall and every one of them came to hear you. I didn't ask you to anything. I just sent word of the hour." He leaned close, and lowered his voice to the register he used for the important things, the ones that mattered more than speeches. "There's a man down front. Gray coat. You'll know him."

Her heart did what it always did, the quick treacherous lurch, and she mastered it the way she had mastered it for thirteen months now, which is to say imperfectly and at cost.

"I know him," she said quietly.

"Aye." Thomas straightened. "Well. Go and say your piece, miss. Then go and say the other piece. The private one. Life's short, and I've got the scars to prove it."

She walked to the platform. The noise rose to meet her—not the polite ripple a drawing-room audience gave her, but a sound with bodies in it, boots stamping, a child somewhere calling out and being hushed, the particular roar of people who had built something with their own hands and were defending it with their lungs.

She looked out at them and saw the harbor: the whole living harbor, the Bristol she had given her life to and that had, in ways she was still counting, given her back to herself.

"Ladies and gentlemen," she began, and then stopped, because the speech she had rehearsed on the drive over—the safe speech, the bill speech, the franchise speech with its careful architecture of clauses—suddenly seemed like a sealed letter, and she was done, forever, with sealed letters.

She folded her notes closed and set them aside.

"I had a speech," she said, and the hall laughed with her, warm. "I wrote it last Tuesday. It was very good. My secretary tells me it scanned beautifully." Another laugh. "But a year ago I stood in an inquiry room and heard a man read out words I never wrote, forged in my name, and I learned what it is to have someone else hold your voice. And I learned something else besides. I learned that the truth doesn't keep. It doesn't sit in ledgers waiting politely. Truth has a tide, like this harbor, and if you don't carry it when it runs, it goes out and takes things with it."

She told them about the bill—of course she did; the bill was the spine of everything, and next month's reading would decide whether the women of this city would ever cast a vote, and she gave it the full weight of her conviction, and she felt the hall rise with her. But she wove into it the other thread, the one she had refused for a year to weave in public: the story of the workshops. The fire. The ledgers. The men who had decided that working people organizing for their wages were a cancer to be burned out of the body of the city, and the inquiry that had named them for it, in public, on the record, forever.

And she spoke the name.

Not Cedric Harrow—his ruin was old news and deserved none of her breath. She spoke the other name. The one that mattered.

"Because the evidence that broke them open came from the pilot ledgers, and the pilot ledgers came to us because one man—one man whom nobody in power had ever bothered to learn the name of—read every word that crossed this harbor, and remembered, and wrote it down, and stood up in that inquiry room and said *I was there.*" Her voice carried; she let it. "They called him a spy. My own committee asked me to distance myself from him. I want you to understand what I told them." She paused, and the pause itself was a kind of thunder in that hall. "I told them that a movement which spends the poor as messengers and forgets them afterward deserves to lose. And that I would rather stand in a smaller room with the truth than in a larger one without it."

The cheering went on a long time. She let it, and looked out over the heads, past the windows, across the glittering reach of the harbor—

—and found him.

* * *

**Elodie**

He had not meant to be found. That was the whole art of him, the oldest art, the one carved deepest: stand at the edge, watch, be stone. Even now, even in his gray coat with his name spoken all over the district—even here, some old reflex put him at the back wall, in the shadow of the doorpost, where the lamplight didn't quite reach.

But she found him anyway. Across four hundred heads, across the noise, her gaze went through the crowd like a pilot's eye going through fog, and settled on him, and stayed.

He did not look away. That was the whole victory, right there, and it was small and enormous: he did not look away.

Fifteen years of practice said *down, now, vanish.* Thirteen months of something newer said otherwise. He stood in the light that leaked through the doorway and let her see him—gray coat, scarred eyebrow, the face that was finally his own—and he watched her see him, and neither of them moved, and the crowd roared around them like weather, and it was the longest and shortest moment of his life.

Then she turned back to the hall and finished, and the applause came down like a tide breaking, and people turned toward the doors and the quay, toward the daylight and the banners, and the crowd began to move him whether he wished it or not, out into the open air.

Outside, the fog had gone entirely. The sun was fully up now, October-gold, lying along the water in a long bright road from the far shore almost to his feet. The crowd spilled down onto the quay, singing something—he half-knew it, a union song, Thomas's people—and the bunting snapped overhead in the breeze off the river, red and white, red and white.

She came out of the hall.

He saw her before the crowd did—or rather, the crowd saw her and parted for her, which amounted to the same thing, a lane opening through the bodies like water before a bow. She walked it slowly. Not performing; he knew the difference now, had learned it watching her for years and understanding it only lately. She walked it slowly because she was deciding something, and Rosalind Vane deciding something had a gait all its own, unhurried, level-chinned, terrible.

Thomas Idris fell into step beside Elodie without appearing to have crossed the intervening space, the way big quiet men do.

"She'll ask you to walk the wall with her," Thomas said, eyes forward.

"She may not."

"She will." A pause. "Question is what *you'll* do."

"That," Elodie said, "is the first honest question anyone's asked me all morning."

Thomas laughed, low. "Life's short, lad. I told her the same." He clapped Elodie's shoulder with his good hand, gently, as if handling evidence, and moved away into the crowd.

She reached him. Up close she looked tired in the way she permitted herself to look only in certain company—the fine lines at her eyes deeper than the newspapers printed them, a strand of hair escaped at her temple that she did not fix. She wore the blue dress. He noticed that he noticed, and made no apology to himself for it.

"Mr. Galloway," she said.

"Miss Vane."

"The harbor looks well today."

"It does." He looked past her at the water, the gold road of it, the lighters and the crane and the hall glowing even in daylight. "Better than I've ever seen it."

"Yes." Something moved through her face—grief, gratitude, the two of them braided so tightly now she probably could no longer separate them, and he knew the feeling intimately. "I meant what I said in there. About you."

"I know. Everyone in Bristol knows. My landlady has cut it out of the paper."

"And what did your landlady conclude?"

Elodie considered. "That I do the letters proper," he said, "and that I should eat more."

Rosalind laughed—the real one, the unguarded one he had counted perhaps ten of in thirteen years, each one hoarded like contraband—and then the laughter settled and she looked at him straight, and the harbor noise seemed to arrange itself around them like a held breath.

"Walk with me," she said. "Along the wall. If you're willing."

"I'm willing," Elodie said. And then, because he was done with the other thing, done forever with saying less than the whole truth: "Rosalind. I've been willing for a very long time."

She did not answer at once. She looked out at the tide running, green-gray and steady, out toward the sea, and when she spoke her voice was quiet enough that only he could hear it, which was its own declaration, from a woman whose voice existed to be heard by thousands.

"So have I," she said. "Come. The tide's turning, and I find I don't want to waste it standing still."

They walked. Behind them the singing swelled; ahead the sun lay on the water like an opened ledger, every page lit, nothing hidden; and the harbor—rebuilt, renamed in no official record but renamed all the same in the mouths of everyone who had been there—carried them both forward, out of the shadow of the wall, into the light, into whatever came next.

Nothing was promised. Everything was possible. And for the first time in either of their lives, that was enough to begin with.
