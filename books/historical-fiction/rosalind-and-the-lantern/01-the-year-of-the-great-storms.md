## 1. The Year of the Great Storms

The wicks wanted trimming at dusk, and Orsen Amberdale trimmed them, because that was the one thing in the world he still did perfectly.

He worked by feel more than sight now. Four years of tending the Saltmere light had put the lamp room into his hands the way a psalm sits in an old priest's mouth—without thought, without error. He lifted the glass chimney free, wiped the soot from its inner curve with a rag dampened in spirits, and turned each wick up a quarter-turn past the char before snipping it clean with the little brass scissors that had belonged to his father. The flame steadied. Through the great curved panes, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored all the way to the horizon, and beyond it, somewhere out there, a war was turning.

Orsen did not think about the war. That was his discipline, his craft, the quiet art he had perfected since the funeral: not thinking.

He climbed down the ninety-one steps of the tower—the count was habit, not necessity—and crossed the yard where the grass grew long around the boat that had not gone out since Maren died. Inside the keeper's cottage, he lit the stove, set the kettle on, and sat with the day's only honest work left to him: mending nets. His fingers moved through the mesh on their own, pushing the wooden needle through, drawing the twine tight, cutting it with the little hook. Net-mending was like grief-work. You could not hurry it. You could only keep your hands busy until the hole closed.

Outside, the wind came off the water smelling of kelp and cold iron. Autumn in Saltmere. The year of the great storms, people were already calling it, though the storms had barely begun—a few hard blows off the north, a fishing fleet kept twice to harbor, nothing yet like what the old men swore was coming. *Wait till the equinox*, they said, sucking their teeth on the quay. *This coast has a temper, and this year it's got a grievance.*

Orsen wound the needle, tied off the knot, and let himself, for exactly as long as it took the kettle to boil, remember his wife's hands doing this same work across the table from him. Maren had mended nets faster than any man in the harbor and complained about it louder, too. She'd sit where Rosalind sat now—

No. He stopped the thought at the door, the way you shut a gate against weather. Not thinking. That was the discipline.

The knock came just after dark, three soft raps, spaced like a code. He knew who it was before he opened it. Only one person knocked at his door anymore, and she knocked as if she didn't want the sound to carry past the garden wall.

"Rosalind."

"Orsen." She stood on the step with her shawl pulled close and a cloth-wrapped loaf held against her chest like something rescued from a fire. Wind had put color in her cheeks and loose strands of grey-streaked hair across her face. She was forty-one and looked older in the lamplight, or perhaps just more tired; the war had been spending her slowly for years, the way the sea spends a cliff. "I brought bread. The miller owed me a favor."

"The miller owes everyone favors these days."

"Then we're all rich." She stepped inside, and he noticed—as he always noticed, and always said nothing—that she checked the lane behind her before she let him close the door. A small turn of the head, quick as a bird's. Then she was only Maren's sister again, hanging her shawl on the peg, warming her hands at his stove, filling his cottage with the smell of fresh bread and the particular silence of a woman deciding which questions to ask.

"You've eaten?" she said.

"I've eaten."

"You're lying."

"I've eaten enough."

She cut two slices anyway, thick ones, and buttered them without asking, because asking would have made it a transaction and Rosalind did not deal in transactions between family. They ate standing up, the way grieving people eat, as if sitting down to a proper meal might require conversation, and conversation might require honesty.

"How is the light?" she asked.

"Burning."

"And you?"

"Trimming wicks. Mending nets." He shrugged. "The usual empire."

That earned the ghost of a smile. It was their oldest joke, from the summers when Maren was alive and the three of them had been young enough to find poverty funny: the Amberdale empire, four walls and a tower, sovereign territory of one widower and his lamp. Maren used to say he ruled over more water than the king.

Rosalind settled into the chair by the window, the one that faced the sea, and Orsen went back to his net. This was the shape their evenings took, and had taken for months now: she came with bread, she asked careful questions, he answered carefully back, and neither of them named what hung unspoken in the room between them like smoke.

"How are the boats faring?" she asked. Casual. Almost idle.

"Kept to harbor twice this month. The *Petrel* lost gear on the last blow. Old Tam says his son saw a warship standing off the point, grey hull, no colors flying."

"Did he?"

"He says so. Tam also says he once wrestled a seal, so." Orsen drew the twine tight. "Harbor talk."

"What else does the harbor say?"

And there it was—the question under the question, arriving right on schedule, the way it arrived every visit. He kept his eyes on the net. "It says the war's turning. It says the fighting's moved south along the coast and the armies will want the ports when it's done. It says whoever wins will come counting."

"Counting what?"

"Boats. Nets. Men." He tied off a knot. "Names."

The word sat in the room. Rosalind turned her face toward the window, toward the dark water, and for a moment the lamplight caught something in her expression that Orsen had learned to read the way he read cloud banks: recognition of a change in the weather, still distant, still deniable, but coming.

"Well," she said lightly, too lightly. "Let them count. We haven't anything here worth taking."

"No," Orsen agreed. "Nothing at all."

* * *

He walked her home partway, as far as the bend where the cliff path split—one branch down to the town, one along the headland to the widow's cottage she rented above the cove. She never let him walk the whole way. She never explained why. He had stopped asking sometime in the second winter after Maren drowned, when he understood that his sister-in-law lived a life with rooms in it he was not invited to see, and that loving her meant pretending those rooms weren't there.

The town was quiet under a thin moon. Smoke stood straight up from the chimneys, which the fishermen said meant rain within two days, and the harbor lay black and still, masts crossed like winter branches. At the Shipwright's Arms, light spilled yellow from the windows and voices carried out over the water—louder voices than usual, with an edge on them. Orsen slowed despite himself. He couldn't help hearing. Nobody in Saltmere could help hearing; the whole town had become an ear pressed to a door.

"—telling you, the garrison at Marlow fell. My cousin's wife's brother drove cart for the commissary, he saw them running—"

"—and when the new lot come, what then? Reckon they'll ask who sold fish to the old garrison? Who ran cargo past the blockade? There's men in this town ought to be worried—"

"—worried! Half the harbor's been running something for years, and nobody ever asked whose side the salt herring fight on—"

Laughter, then, ugly and nervous, and someone said something Orsen didn't catch, and someone else said, quieter, "Mind your mouth. Walls have ears and the wrong ears have names."

Orsen walked on. He had gotten very good at walking on. It was, he sometimes thought, the defining skill of his widowhood—not the trimming of wicks, not the mending of nets, but the walking on. Past conversations that named him. Past the clerk's office where Maren's death was recorded in a ledger he had never been able to bring himself to look at. Past the churchyard where he did not go, because going would mean standing at a stone with her name carved in it, and a name carved in stone was a thing you could not un-know, whereas a grave you simply avoided was almost, almost, a wife who might still walk up the cliff path whistling.

Invisibility. He had built it plank by plank, the way you build a breakwater. Keep your head down. Answer questions with weather. Sign nothing, join nothing, owe nothing, know nothing. The army had come through twice in the early years asking about cargo and crews, and both times Orsen Amberdale had proven so dull, so grief-struck, so entirely occupied with the maintenance of his lighthouse, that the officers had left him alone with something like pity. *The widower*, they called him. *Half a man keeping a whole light.* It stung, the first dozen times. Then it became armor. Then it became simply true, or true enough, and the truth of it kept him safe.

Safe. He tested the word as he climbed the tower stairs that night, as he checked the mechanism and topped up the oil reservoir and watched the beam swing its slow white arm across the water. Safe was what Maren had not been. Safe was what the eleven boats' worth of drowned men had not been, the storm two winters back that had taken the *Merry Alice* and the *Sweet Betsey* and seven others in a single night while the whole town stood on the seawall holding lanterns that helped nothing. Safe was a country he had emigrated from, and he had no intention of going back.

But Rosalind's questions were getting more careful, and careful questions, he knew, were the sound of a woman measuring a doorframe to see if something large could be moved through it.

* * *

Three nights later the first real storm of the season came down the coast like a debt collector.

It arrived at dusk with a wind out of the northeast that made the tower hum, a low note in the stonework like a struck bell that wouldn't finish dying. By full dark the sea had gone from pewter to black to a churning white-hipped animal, and Orsen worked the lamp room with the door braced and spray rattling against the glass like flung gravel. This was the work he was for. Every keeper on this coast knew the bargain: the storm is God's business, the light is yours, and yours is the only part of the night you can answer for.

He trimmed, he fed the lamp, he wiped the salt from the panes with his sleeves already soaked. Below, the town showed its scattered lights, and beyond the harbor mole he could see the fishing fleet riding at double anchors, riding it out, every hull that mattered to anyone in Saltmere held safe inside the arms of the breakwater.

Except one.

He saw the trouble before he understood it—a light out past the point where no light should be, bobbing, vanishing, rising again. A boat outside the mole in this sea. For a long minute he stared, telling himself it was a trick of the spray, a star torn loose and drowning. Then the light rose high on a swell and he saw the silhouette beneath it: a small craft, lateen-rigged, running for the cove on the far side of the headland. Running dark except for that single swinging lamp, which was madness, which was suicide, which was—

Which was none of his business.

That was the discipline. That was the armor. A boat in the night, a lamp where no lamp should be, a course for the hidden cove below Rosalind's cottage—none of it was his business, and a man who minded his own business in the year of the great storms would live to mind it a long time.

Orsen turned back to his wick. He adjusted it a quarter-turn. He watched the beam sweep out and sweep back, patient as conscience.

But he found, as the storm wore on toward morning, that his eyes kept going to the window. And he found that some cold, clear part of him—the part that counted the ninety-one steps, the part that never missed a trimming—had already done the arithmetic he refused to do aloud: that the cove below his sister-in-law's cottage had deep water close in, and a shingle beach hidden from the town, and that in four years of widowhood he had never once seen a boat use it, until tonight, in the worst weather of the autumn, when no honest boat would be abroad.

Toward dawn the wind fell. The sea eased from fury to grumble. Orsen banked the lamp, went down his ninety-one steps, and stood in the grey yard watching the light come up over a littered, wind-scoured world.

There was bread on his doorstep. Fresh, wrapped in cloth, still warm.

He stood looking at it for a long time. Then he picked it up, and carried it inside, and did not eat any of it, and told himself—standing in his own kitchen, in his own sovereign territory, with the storm washing itself out over the whole grey coast—that he had seen nothing, knew nothing, and was, as he had been for four years, only the widower, half a man keeping a whole light.

The lie fit worse than it used to. He noticed that. He noticed it the way you notice a net with a tear in it: small now, but every tide works at a tear, and the sea is patient, and the year was not half over.

Two days later, the town clerk came knocking at the lighthouse door with a ledger under his arm and an apology on his face, and asked Orsen Amberdale for help copying the parish record.
