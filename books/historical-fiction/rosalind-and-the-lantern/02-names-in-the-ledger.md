## 2. Names in the Ledger

The town clerk's name was Pell Harrow, and he had held his post for thirty-one years, which in Saltmere meant he had outlasted two wars, three floods, and one epidemic that had taken the east row of houses and left the west standing as if by favoritism. He was a small man made mostly of wool and ink stains, and he stood at Orsen's door holding the parish ledger against his chest the way a man holds a child too old to be carried but too precious to set down.

"Copying," he said again, when Orsen only looked at him. "The record. It wants copying, Orsen. Paper this age, salt air, damp — it doesn't last forever. And with the war turning the way it is—" He stopped himself there, the way everyone in Saltmere stopped themselves, as though the war were a stair they might trip on if they spoke its name.

Orsen stepped back to let him in. "Why me?"

"Because your hand is good." Pell set the ledger on the kitchen table with both hands, gently, like lowering an anchor. "Because you're careful. And because you don't talk."

That last was true, or had been. Orsen lit the lamp, though it was barely afternoon; the light through the salt-fogged windows had gone the color of dishwater. He fetched the good paper from the drawer where he kept the lighthouse logs — the smooth stock, not the rough — and a fresh bottle of iron-gall ink, and he said nothing about any of it, because saying nothing was what he did, and what he was paid for, in a way: the Crown paid a keeper to keep, and keeping meant quiet.

Pell watched him arrange the pen, the blotting sand, the spare nibs, with the satisfaction of a man who has chosen well. "It's every entry since the year of the great frost. Births, deaths, marriages, the wreck lists. The drowned." He said the last word plainly. In Saltmere you said it plainly or not at all. "The copy goes into the strongbox at the church. Two of everything. That's how a town keeps itself. One fire, one flood, one careless officer—" He coughed. "One accident, and a whole people is a rumor."

"One accident," Orsen repeated.

"The times being what they are." Pell pulled his chair close and opened the ledger to the first page, and the smell of it came up out of the binding — tallow, brine, and years.

They began.

* * *

There is a particular silence that falls over two men copying names, and Orsen had never heard it before that day, and would hear it in his sleep for seasons afterward. It is not the silence of work. It is the silence of reading aloud without sound — each name passing through the eyes, across the tongue's ghost, down into the hand. *Maren Oduya, born in the herring year. Tomas Oduya, her son, lost off the Gullet, age eleven.* The pen moved. The lamp burned. Outside, the sea kept its own ledger, and did not show it to anyone.

By the second hour Orsen had found the rhythm of it, the way you find the rhythm of hauling line, and it was in the rhythm that the danger arrived — because rhythm is what lets the mind wander, and his mind wandered down the columns of names and began to see what was written between the lines.

The wreck lists came first. Every winter of the last decade had its entry, sometimes a dozen names in a single storm, written in Pell's older, steadier hand: *lost off the point*, *lost with the *Kestrel*, *lost, body never given back*. Orsen knew these dead. Not all of them — no one in Saltmere knew all of anyone — but he knew their faces, their dogs, their way of walking up from the harbor with the day's luck or lack of it slung over one shoulder. Reading them in ink was different from mourning them in church. In church they were grief. Here they were fact. Here they were permanent, and permanence, he was beginning to understand, could be a kind of wound.

Then came the other entries. The ones that were not wrecks.

*Died suddenly.* Three words, when the truth had been fever, and fever was no shame, so why the softening? *Departed for kin in the north.* Departed — not moved, not gone to work the northern boats, but departed, the way a debt departs. *No further record.* A phrase Pell used four times in the pages they copied before noon, always in the same small script, as if writing smaller could make the absence smaller. No further record. Orsen read those four words and felt something cold walk up the back of his neck, because he knew what the phrase covered. Everyone in Saltmere knew, in the wordless way coastal towns know things. Boats went out at night that weren't fishing boats. People left whose leaving had been arranged, whose passage had been paid in coin nobody asked about. The sea gave some of them back and not others, and the parish ledger recorded the ones it gave back as drowned, and recorded the rest as nothing at all.

He glanced up. Pell was watching him — not accusingly, but with the weary attention of a man who has waited thirty-one years for someone else to notice.

"You see it," Pell said. Not a question.

"I see gaps."

"Gaps." Pell took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "A ledger is supposed to be the truth, Orsen. That's the whole of it. A ledger is a town telling the future what happened. And ours tells the truth the way a drunk tells it — most of it right, the important parts slurred." He turned pages forward, past the frost year, past the first war's brief bitter column of *killed in service*, to entries Orsen recognized now, seeing them together: the sudden deaths clustered like birds before a storm, the departures, the silences.

"Some of these people aren't dead," Orsen said quietly. "And some of the dead aren't here at all."

"No."

"And you want me to copy it as it stands."

"I want the record preserved," Pell said. "All of it. Even the lies are evidence, Orsen. A lie written down can be caught. A lie spoken and forgotten is just weather." He put his spectacles back on. "But I'm an old man with a bad chest, and my hand shakes after the third hour, and if the copying stops halfway—"

"It won't stop halfway," Orsen said.

It was more than he'd meant to promise. It was, he realized with a strange sinking feeling, the longest sentence he'd spoken to another living soul since spring.

* * *

Rosalind came at dusk, as she always did, with bread wrapped in cloth and her hood up against the drizzle. She let herself in the way family does — no knock, just the latch lifting — and stopped when she saw the table: the ledger open, the fair copy beside it half-filled with Orsen's even hand, the sand scattered like pale sand indeed across the wood.

"You're copying the parish book," she said.

"Pell asked."

She set the bread down without taking off her cloak. "Did he say why?"

"Paper rots. War turns." Orsen dipped the pen, wiped the nib. "Times being what they are."

For a moment she didn't move. Then she pulled the second chair to the table — his late wife Maren's chair, though neither of them ever called it that anymore — and sat, and looked at the ledger the way sailors look at a change in the wind.

"Read me where you are," she said.

He read. It was only names. That was the trouble with names; they sounded like nothing, a list, a chore, until one of them was yours. *Bett Ancel, died suddenly. Josper Lane and wife, departed for kin in the north. Child, no further record.*

Rosalind's hand came out and rested flat on the page — not touching the ink, hovering above it, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping child to feel whether it still breathed.

"This one," she said. "Josper Lane. Tell me about Josper Lane."

"I didn't know him."

"He fished the Gullet. He had a laugh like a gull getting strangled." She said it lightly, but her finger had not moved from above the entry. "He left on a night in March two years back. There was fog you could cut. And the ledger says departed for kin in the north, and there's no kin in the north, Orsen. There never was. His kin were all under this roof of water we live beside." She withdrew her hand at last and folded it in her lap. "Who writes the truth down? That's what I want to know. Who holds the real book?"

Orsen looked at her. Rosalind had been coming to this lighthouse twice a week for four years — bread, mending, careful questions about his health, his stores, the state of the stairs, the mood of the harbor. Four years of visits and he had never once seen her look the way she looked now, bent over a dead man's softened lie with something fierce banked behind her face like a fire in a closed stove.

"Why does it matter to you?" he asked.

"Because names are all any of us get," she said. "You live, you die, and if you're lucky somebody writes it straight. These people—" she touched the page again, briefly, "—these people got a clerk who was afraid. It isn't his fault. Fear writes smaller than courage does. But somebody should write it large, someday. Somebody should write *departed* and mean it, and write *saved*, God help us, because nobody in this whole rotten beautiful book has ever once been written down as *saved*."

The word hung in the lamplight. Saved. Orsen heard it land in the room and stay there, and heard — or thought he heard — the faintest catch in her breath after it, as though she had said more than she'd carried in intending to.

"Rosalind," he said carefully, "why would anyone need to write saved?"

She rose. She took the bread to the shelf and unwrapped it and cut two slices with her back to him, and by the time she turned around her face was ordinary again, sisterly, tired. "Eat," she said. "You copy worse when you're hungry. My sister used to say that."

"She said it about steering," Orsen said.

"She said it about everything." Rosalind smiled, and the smile reached her eyes, and yet underneath it, for just a moment, Orsen saw the other thing again — the banked fire, the ledger-look. Then it was gone, packed away wherever she kept such things, in whatever dark hold below her waterline.

She stayed until the foghorn hour, talking of small things — the price of flour, the widow Ancel's goats, whether the autumn storms would come early — and then she wrapped herself and went out into the drizzle, and Orsen stood in the doorway and watched her go down the cliff path with the lantern swinging, steady as anything, and thought: *I have known that woman since she was nine years old, and tonight is the first time I have ever wondered what she carries.*

* * *

He worked alone the next day, and the next. The rhythm returned, and the wandering mind with it, and on the third afternoon he reached the entries from eighteen months past — the winter just ended, the winter of the courier boat — and his pen slowed.

Here the softening was thicker than anywhere. Whole families, *departed*. A cluster of five *died suddenly* entries within one fortnight, when no sickness had run through the town that Orsen had seen, and he saw everything; he was the highest point in Saltmere, and he watched the town the way the light watches the water. Five households, gone in fourteen days, and not one burial entered, not one grave dug that he had witnessed from the gallery. And interleaved among them, in Pell's smallest script: *no further record. No further record. No further record.*

Orsen set down the pen.

He sat looking at the page for a long time. The lamp hissed. Below him the tide worked at the rocks, patient, keeping its own accounts.

Because here was the thing he had understood in pieces and understood whole now, sitting in his kitchen with the parish's whole memory spread under his hand: paper was not safe. He had spent four years believing the opposite — believing that the world's dangers were out there, in the weather, in the war, in the grey coats that everyone whispered were coming eventually — while the truth sat in his house in a binding of cracked leather. Paper remembered. That was its purpose and its peril, the same property viewed from different sides of the table. Whoever held this book held every secret Saltmere had ever tried to keep, every escape dressed as a death, every rescue disguised as a gap. And wars, when they turned, did not turn gently. Wars sent men with clean uniforms and requisition orders, and requisition orders had a line for *records*, and records were exactly the sort of thing thorough people wanted.

He thought: *if the wrong hands take this book, the drowned will have drowned for nothing, and the saved will simply cease — no record, no further record, as if the sea had swallowed them entire.*

He thought: *and if the right hands lose it — fire, flood, one accident —*

He took up the pen and copied the five impossible entries faithfully, softly, letter by letter, including every *no further record*, because Pell was right: even the lies were evidence. But as he wrote the last of them he became aware of a sensation in his chest he had not felt since Maren died — not grief exactly, but its cousin, the feeling of being responsible for something fragile that the world intended to break.

When Pell came at closing time, stiff from the chair, gathering his coat, Orsen heard himself speak before he had decided to.

"How many copies exist of this book? Truly. Beyond yours and mine."

Pell paused in the doorway with the ledger hugged to his chest, and the fog behind him was thick enough to hide the harbor entirely, and his face, for a moment, was very old and very frightened and very honest.

"One," he said. "This is the one. There has only ever been the one." He managed something like a smile. "Which is why I chose you, Orsen. Careful hands, and no talk." He stepped out into the white. "Lock your door."

Orsen stood listening to the clerk's footsteps fade down the rock path, and then he did something he had not done in four years of keeping: he climbed to the lantern room, though the sun was still up and the wicks needed nothing, and he stood in the glass drum looking down at Saltmere — the roofs, the smoke, the little harbor with its boats riding at moorings, each one of them a household, each household a fistful of names — and he thought about the weight of paper, and the patience of the sea, and the year, which was not half over.

Far below, small as a stitch, a figure in a dark cloak came up the cliff path toward the lighthouse. Coming early. Coming without bread, by the quickness of her walk.

Rosalind, at a pace he had never once seen her use.

Orsen put his hand on the lamp housing and watched her climb, and felt the tear in the net widen, quietly, tide by tide, exactly as he'd known it would.
