## 3. Grey Coats at the Harbor

Rosalind saw the ships before anyone else did, because she had spent three years learning to see things first.

She was on the cliff path above the north point, ostensibly gathering samphire from the rocks—a basket on her arm, a knife at her belt, the picture of a widow's sister earning her bread. In truth she was watching the horizon the way she watched everything: without appearing to watch it at all. The morning fog had burned off early, leaving the sea flat and silvered, and against that silver there were shapes. Three of them. Grey hulls, grey sails furled tight, a wake like a scar drawn straight across the water.

Not fishing boats. Fishing boats came home tired, wallowing, their decks cluttered and their crews lean over the rails. These came in like punctuation marks at the end of a sentence—deliberate, final, arriving with the certainty of something already decided.

She counted again. Three ships. She thought of the number of men a transport that size could carry and made herself stop thinking it, because the arithmetic only went one direction and she did not want to arrive there standing still.

The bell in the harbor tower began to ring—not the storm bell, not the tide bell, but the slow, heavy tolling the town used for funerals and for news too large to be spoken. Rosalind stood very still on the path and let the sound move through her chest, and beneath the fear, beneath even the cold calculation already running—how many routes were open, how many people would need to move tonight, whether the Merrow's Daughter could put to sea in this weather—a single thought surfaced, clean and terrible:

They have come for Saltmere.

She turned and walked down toward the town, and she did not run, because running was the one thing she could never afford to do.

* * *

By the time she reached Harbor Street, the ships had rounded the breakwater and the whole town had come out to stand in doorways and on quays, the way people do when the thing they fear is still far enough away to look at. The grey coats lined the railings in silent ranks. Rosalind moved through the crowd with her basket on her arm and her eyes down, and she listened.

"They're saying it's just administration," muttered Tam Brellow, the cooper, to his wife. "Requisition, they call it. Grain, billets, that's all."

"Billets." His wife said the word the way one says a disease.

"Where are we meant to put them?"

"Where do you think? Where they tell you."

Rosalind kept walking. Requisition. The word was doing exactly what such words were designed to do: making an occupation sound like paperwork. She had heard the victors' vocabulary described once, in a smuggler's tavern two hundred miles up the coast, by a man who had fled another town that had fallen the same way. They don't burn anything at first, he'd said. They file it. A thing filed can be found later. A thing found can be taken. A thing taken can be destroyed, and no one is left who can say it existed.

She reached the fish market square, where the crowd was thickest, and stopped at its edge as the soldiers came ashore.

They disembarked in good order, boots ringing on the wet cobbles, and Rosalind studied them with the dispassion of long practice—their uniforms clean, their kit squared away, their faces carefully blank in the way of men ordered not to frighten the population and not quite managing it. At their head rode a woman on a dark horse.

That surprised her. Rosalind had expected a colonel, some red-faced veteran of the interior campaigns. Instead: a woman of perhaps forty, dressed in the same grey as her men but better cut, with a leather satchel slung across her body and a small book already open in one gloved hand. Her hair was pinned back with severe efficiency. She did not look at the crowd the way conquerors look at the conquered—with contempt or triumph or hunger. She looked at them the way a surveyor looks at ground she intends to measure.

She reined in at the foot of the market steps, closed the book, and spoke in a clear voice that carried across the square without effort.

"People of Saltmere. By authority of the Provisional Administration, this port and its surrounding parish are placed under requisition effective this hour. This is not a punishment. It is an administrative necessity." She said it as though reading from the book, and perhaps she was; Rosalind suspected the woman knew the words so well she no longer needed paper. "You will continue your trades. Your fishermen will fish. Your compliance will be noted."

A silence. Then old Marta Quill, who had buried two husbands and feared nothing living, called out: "Noted where?"

The officer turned her head and found Marta in the crowd without any apparent effort. "In the record," she said. And something in the mildness of it—the sheer administrative reasonableness—made the square go quieter than any shout could have.

"I am Captain Veyra," the woman continued. "I am responsible for the civil administration of this district. Billeting assignments will be posted by nightfall. Resistance to requisition will be treated as obstruction and dealt with accordingly." A pause, precise as a ruled line on a page. "There will be an accounting of the parish. That begins tomorrow."

Rosalind felt the words go into her like a fishhook going into flesh—clean entry, barbed hold. An accounting of the parish.

She turned and walked home fast now, basket forgotten, because she knew exactly what the parish kept its accounts in. Every town on this coast kept one ledger: births, deaths, marriages, burials—and, unwritten but present in every margin and every clerk's careful hand, the other things. The boats that left with twelve souls aboard and arrived with none recorded lost. The graves marked with wrong names and right dates. The children entered as nieces and nephews who were nothing of the kind. For three years, while the war rolled along the coast like weather, Saltmere's ledger had been quietly, deliberately inaccurate—and Rosalind had been the reason why.

Her network's names were all in it. Not under their own names, mostly—but under patterns, and patterns could be read. Veyra looked like a woman who read patterns.

* * *

The billeting notices went up at dusk, nailed to the market cross and the chapel door, and by full dark the grey coats were moving through the streets with their kit bags, knocking on doors, being let in by families too frightened to refuse. Rosalind watched the lit windows of Saltmere acquire strangers the way a body acquires cold—one room at a time.

She sat in Orsen Amberdale's kitchen, because the lighthouse cottage was the one house in town she could sit in freely, and told him what she'd seen.

"Three transports," Orsen said. He was mending a net by the fire, though his hands had stopped working; the mesh hung slack between his fists. "I counted two at first light."

"There were three. Two infantry companies and a headquarters party, I'd judge. And a captain." She hesitated. "A woman. Veyra. She talked about the record, Orsen. She said the accounting begins tomorrow."

Orsen set the net down. He had a face built for calm—long, weathered, slow to change expression—and Rosalind had lived in his household long enough to know that the calmer it looked, the more was moving underneath. "The parish ledger," he said.

"The parish ledger. Which lists every soul in Saltmere. Which lists—" She stopped. Looked at the fire.

"Say it," Orsen said quietly. "You'll feel no better, but you'll think better."

"Which lists the discrepancies." She made herself meet his eyes. "Every boat that came back short-handed with no losses recorded. Every burial entered under a name that doesn't match the death. Every child the clerk wrote down kindly and wrongly. I told Aldous Wren what to write, Orsen. For three years. He kept the true figures in his head and the false ones in the book, and he did it because I asked him to. If she sits down with that ledger and an informant or two, she won't find my network. She'll find its shape. And then she'll only need the names."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. The lamp in the tower above them turned, and its beam swept the kitchen window in slow white strokes—darkness, light, darkness—like a slow heartbeat.

"What do you need?" he said at last.

That was the thing about Orsen, she thought. Not courage, exactly—or rather, courage of a particular make, the kind that never announced itself. He did not ask whether she was certain, or whether it was worth the risk, or any of the questions whose real function was to shift the weight onto someone else. He asked what she needed.

"Aldous warned tonight, if he can get word out," she said. "And I need to know when that ledger leaves the clerk's office and where it goes. Because it will leave. 'Administrative review' means they'll take it somewhere with a table and a lamp and guards at the door. And once it's behind their lines—" She shook her head. "Paper burns, Orsen. But worse than burning—paper gets corrected."

* * *

The knock came at the lighthouse door two evenings later, and Rosalind was upstairs with the copy of a tide-table she was not really studying when she heard Orsen answer it.

She did not breathe. She listened to boots on the flagstones—two sets, maybe three—and a voice, clipped and reasonable, asking questions. Then Orsen's voice, low and steady, and then footsteps on the stairs, and Rosalind slipped into the little sleeping room and stood behind the door with her heart going like a gull in a storm and her hands perfectly, uselessly still.

They did not enter. Boots paused outside the door—she could hear the fabric of a coat shifting, someone considering the handle—and moved on. She heard Orsen's voice below, answering something, and the tone of the reply, and then the door closing, and then nothing but the sea.

When she came down, Orsen was standing in the middle of his own kitchen looking like a man who has found water in his boat's bilge: not sinking yet, but aware of it now, permanently.

"Two of them," he said. "Billeting. One's to sleep in the oil store loft." His jaw worked. "In my oil store, Rosalind. Under the lantern I keep."

"And upstairs?"

"One looked at the doors up there. Didn't open them." He met her eyes. "He wanted me to know he hadn't."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "That's how they work. The not-opening is the message." She sat down across from him. "What did they ask?"

"Who lives here. Who visits. Whether I'm alone." He almost smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen on him. "I said yes."

The lie hung in the kitchen between them. Rosalind understood what it cost him—he was a man whose whole survival had been built on being nobody, saying nothing, giving the world no purchase on him—and she understood, too, that the lie changed everything. A man who lied for you was a man in your network, whatever he believed about himself.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Don't be sorry," Orsen said. "Be quick. Tell me about the ledger."

* * *

It happened faster than even she had predicted. On the fourth day after the landing, the requisition office opened in the old customs house, and at midmorning four soldiers marched to the clerk's office on Chapel Lane and came back carrying the parish ledger between them on a stretcher board, wrapped in oilcloth like contraband, which in every way that mattered it was.

Rosalind watched from the doorway of Brellow's cooperage, a barrel stave in her hands for appearance's sake, as the procession crossed the square. Aldous Wren walked beside his ledger with his hat in his hands, looking like a father following a coffin. At the customs house door waited Captain Veyra.

Rosalind had seen officers receive surrendered documents before, in other towns, in reports that traveled the coast ahead of the armies. There was always a moment—the taking of the thing—and the moment always revealed the taker. Some grabbed. Some sneered. Some performed reluctance, as though the taking pained them.

Veyra received the parish ledger of Saltmere the way a librarian receives a rare volume. She took off one glove. She opened the cover with both hands. She turned perhaps six pages, slowly, and Rosalind, fifty yards away across a crowded square, saw the exact moment when the captain found what she was looking for.

It was nothing dramatic. Veyra simply stopped turning pages. Her gloved finger came to rest on the paper and stayed there, and her head tilted slightly, the way a person's tilts when they reread a line. Then she raised her eyes—not to Wren, not to the soldiers, but to nothing, to some middle distance where only her own thoughts lived—and Rosalind watched a mind begin to work.

Then the captain closed the book, thanked Aldous Wren courteously enough that the old clerk actually bowed, and carried it inside herself, under her own arm, like a mother carrying a sleeping child she intends to question later.

Rosalind put down the barrel stave and walked home along the harbor wall, and the fear that had been riding her since the ships appeared finally caught up and settled on her shoulders like a wet cloak. Because she had seen that tilt of the head before, in her own mirror, on nights when a figure didn't reconcile and she had to decide whether to chase it or let it drown. It was the look of a person who reads numbers and sees the people hiding behind them.

They had come for Saltmere's grain, its boats, its beds. Fine. Those could be endured, waited out, survived the way the town had survived storms for four hundred years.

But the ledger was not grain. The ledger was every lie Saltmere had ever told kindly, written in a clerk's patient hand—and in its margins, invisible to anyone who didn't know how to look, was Rosalind. The routes. The money. The boats that went out heavy and came back light. The ninety-one souls she had moved to safety whose official records said drowned, or departed, or had never been.

Somewhere in the customs house, behind a guarded door, a thorough woman was about to begin finding them.

Rosalind climbed the cliff path to the lighthouse that evening and stood a long time in the wind, looking down at the lit windows of the requisition office, where a lamp burned late.

She thought of one thing, and one thing only: whatever Veyra was reading tonight, Rosalind needed to read it first—and she needed a copy of the truth that lived nowhere in that building, before the correcting began.
