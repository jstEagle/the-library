## 4. The Widow's Bread

The grey coats came to Saltmere on a Tuesday, in the hour when the gulls went quiet and the harbor water lay flat as poured tin.

Orsen heard them before he saw them—boots on the cobbles, too many boots, moving in step the way no fisherman or clerk ever moved. He was at the kitchen window with his second cup of tea going cold in his hand, watching six soldiers march up Harbor Lane past the smokehouse and the chandlery, their coats the color of weather that meant to stay. Behind them rode two officers, and behind the officers came a cart with an army stencil on its side, empty now, which somehow frightened him more than a full one would have.

He had spent forty years learning what the sea would do next by reading small signs: the color of the light on the far headland, the way the swell changed its mind, the behavior of birds too far out to matter. He read these men the same way and did not like what they told him.

They were not passing through. Nothing passed through Saltmere.

He set down the cup. Upstairs, in the room that had been Maren's before it was anyone's, Rosalind was folding linen—or pretending to fold linen, which was her particular genius, making herself look like furniture, like weather, like something that had always been in a house and therefore belonged there. She had been living under his roof for three weeks now, sleeping in Maren's bed, wearing Maren's shawl, and Orsen still caught himself each morning with a jolt of grief so sharp it took a moment to understand it wasn't grief at all but resemblance—the set of her shoulders as she reached for the bread knife, the way she hummed when she thought no one listened, the same tune Maren used to hum.

"You should go down to the harbor," she said that first morning of the soldiers, standing in his doorway with flour on her hands. "A keeper who hides looks like a keeper with something to hide."

"And you?"

"I'll be mending nets. I've been mending nets since I was nine years old. No one looks twice at a woman mending nets." She smiled, and it was Maren's smile exactly, and it hurt. "That's the whole art of it, Orsen. Be the thing nobody looks twice at."

* * *

By noon the whole town knew: the war was over, or this part of it was, and Saltmere was requisitioned. A notice went up on the customs-house door in three languages, only one of which most people could read, and Captain Veyra stood beneath it while it was nailed there, hands folded behind her back, reading the faces of the crowd instead of the paper.

Orsen hung back at the edge, as he always hung back, half in the shadow of the rope shed. He watched her the way he'd watch a squall line. She was younger than he'd expected—not young, but younger than the rank—and she wore her uniform the way some men wore borrowed clothes, neatly but without love. Her eyes were grey and patient and missed nothing. When her gaze crossed his, it did not linger; he was a lighthouse keeper, a widower, a nobody, and she filed him away in whatever drawer officers kept nobodies in, and moved on.

He told himself the cold feeling in his stomach was relief. Being overlooked was a skill, and he had practiced it all his life.

The billeting began that evening. Soldiers in twos and threes at doorways up and down the town, the Harrow family giving up their front room, old Tam Wicker made to sleep in his own boatshed. At the Anchor's Rest, the soldiers drank little and talked less and paid in scrip, and the silence they carried with them was worse than drunkenness would have been.

Rosalind washed dishes in Orsen's kitchen and said nothing, and scrubbed a pan long after it was clean.

"They'll come here eventually," she said. "Every house gets a list drawn up of who lives in it. It's how it worked in Correnholt." She said the name of the burned town lightly, the way people say the names of graveyards.

"Then I'll tell them my sister-in-law lives here. Which is true."

"It is true," she agreed. "But my name isn't Rosalind Amberdale to them. It's Rosalind Vane. And there are men in the capital who know that name." She dried her hands slowly. "Maren never told you any of it, did she. Not properly."

"Maren kept things," Orsen said. It was not an accusation; it was simply the shape of the truth, and they both knew it.

"She kept me," Rosalind said quietly, and went back to her pans.

* * *

They came to the lighthouse on the fourth day, near dusk, when the light was due to be lit and the sky over the water had gone the color of pewter.

Orsen saw them coming up the cliff path—four soldiers and Captain Veyra herself, which was wrong, because officers did not walk house searches, and the wrongness of it put a bell ringing somewhere below his ribs. He turned from the window.

"Cellar," he said. One word. He didn't know where the word came from; it seemed to come from some older part of him than thought, the part that trimmed wicks and bailed boats and did the necessary thing without pausing to be afraid.

Rosalind was already moving. That was the terrible efficiency of her—no question, no argument, no wide eyes. She took the lamp off the table, lifted the trapdoor behind the pantry, and went down the ladder into the dark under the tower where the oil drums stood and the salt air crept through the stone. The trapdoor shut. Orsen dragged the woven mat over it, set the bread basket on top—heavier than usual tonight, he noticed distantly, she must have baked extra, why would she bake extra—and then stood in his own kitchen with his heart going like a pump in a flooding hull.

Three knocks at the door. Not a request.

He opened it.

Captain Veyra stood on his step with a ledger under her arm and a corporal at her shoulder. Two more soldiers waited at the gate, looking out at the sea with the boredom of men who had searched forty houses already today.

"Keeper Amberdale." She consulted her papers. "Widowed four years. Lives alone." Her eyes came up. "Alone?"

"Yes," said Orsen Amberdale, and lied for the first time in fifty-one years.

It came out wrong immediately. He heard it himself—the fraction of a delay, the voice pitched a hair too flat, the word delivered like a coin set down on a counter rather than spoken. Fifty years of telling fishermen the truth about weather had built no muscle at all for this. Captain Veyra's eyes changed. They didn't narrow. They simply became attentive, the way a compass needle becomes attentive to north.

"May we come in?"

"There's bread rising," he said, which was insane, which was the worst possible thing, and he knew it even as he stepped aside to let them pass.

She walked his kitchen slowly. She looked at the two cups drying on the rack—one large, one smaller, a woman's cup—and said nothing about them. She looked at the basket of bread, six loaves where one man might bake two, and said nothing about it. She looked at the mat behind the pantry, and Orsen felt his soul leave through the top of his skull and stand in the corner watching, and still she said nothing.

"You keep the light every night?" she asked.

"Every night. War or peace. That's the arrangement."

"The arrangement," she repeated, tasting the words. "With whom?"

"With the sea, ma'am."

Something flickered across her face—not amusement, quite, but its cousin. "There are orders coming regarding the light. You'll receive them formally. Until then, carry on." She paused at the pantry doorway, and her hand came to rest on the frame, fingers drumming once, softly, and Orsen aged ten years in that drumming. "You had a wife. Maren Amberdale."

"Aye."

"And her sister? There was a sister, I believe. In the parish record."

Orsen looked at the floor, at the hearthstone, anywhere but at those patient grey eyes. "Gone south," he said. "Years back. Before the war even."

"Gone south." Veyra wrote nothing down. Somehow that was worse—if she'd written it down, it would have been finished, filed, dead. Instead she held it in her mouth like a stone. "People who go south often write. Has she written?"

"No, ma'am."

"People who don't write," Veyra said mildly, to the corporal rather than to him, "are usually either dead or hiding. And the dead," she added, turning back, "don't leave two cups on the rack."

The silence stretched. Out beyond the windows the light came on above them—the clockwork tripped, the lens began to turn, the beam swung out over the darkening water—and Orsen clung to the sound of it, the great slow breathing of the mechanism overhead, because it was the one honest thing in the room.

Veyra straightened. "Thank you for your hospitality, Keeper. We'll see ourselves out." At the door she stopped, half-turned. "Six loaves is a great deal of bread for a man who lives alone."

"I give it to the billeted soldiers," Orsen said. And this lie, at least, came easily, because it was nearly true and because desperation had finally taught him the trick of it. "The boys at the Anchor's Rest. Growing lads."

"Generous," said Captain Veyra, and her eyes stayed on his face a moment longer than comfort allowed, and then she was gone, down the path, the boots fading, the gate clicking shut.

* * *

He did not move for a long time. He stood in the middle of his kitchen listening to the boots fade down the cliff road, listening to the beam turn overhead, listening to his own blood.

Then he knelt, dragged the mat aside, and lifted the trapdoor.

"Is she gone?" Rosalind's voice came up out of the black, steady as ever. Steady as ever, and yet when his eyes adjusted he saw her sitting against the oil drums with the lamp unlit beside her and her hands clasped so tightly in her lap that the knuckles had gone white. Steady everywhere except the hands.

"She's gone."

"What did she ask?"

"Whether I lived alone. Whether my sister-in-law wrote to me." He sat down heavily on the top step, the cold of the cellar stones seeping through his trousers. "I told her you'd gone south. Years ago."

Rosalind closed her eyes. For a moment she looked, for the first time since he'd known her, tired—all the way through tired, down to the bedrock. "South," she said. "Where Maren's letters said I'd gone. Good. That's—" She stopped. Started again. "Orsen. Do you understand what you did just now? What you've done since the day you took me in?"

"I baked too much bread and left two cups on the rack. I understand that much."

"You lied to an officer of the occupying army." She laughed, but there was no humor in it, only a strange raw wonder. "Do you know how many people in this town can say that? How many have even been asked? You've spent your whole life being nobody, Orsen, and you've just stopped. The moment you lied for someone, you stopped. There's no going back to invisible. She'll remember you now. She'll think about you. That's the price."

He looked down into the dark of the cellar, at the oil drums ranked like a congregation, at his sister-in-law sitting among them with flour still faintly ghosting her sleeve.

"Maren lied for you," he said. "Didn't she. All those years. All those trips to Correnholt she called market errands."

Rosalind was quiet a long moment. Above them the beam swung, swung, swung, patient as tide.

"Maren carried things for me," she said at last. "Money. Papers. Names." Her voice dropped, and something in it made the hairs rise on his arms. "Names, Orsen. There are names in this town that exist nowhere else in the world but in her handwriting and mine. And if the army takes the parish ledger—"

She stopped again, because boots were sounding on the cliff path again, quick ones, running, and a voice was shouting from the direction of the harbor—a soldier's voice, cracked with urgency:

*"Captain! Captain Veyra—the courier's boat, the storm last week—it's washed something up on the shingle—"*

Orsen and Rosalind looked at each other across the open trapdoor, the lamplight between them, and neither of them moved.
