## 5. What the Sea Gave Back

The storm came in on a Tuesday, though by then the days had lost their names and become only weather. Orsen Amberdale stood in the lantern room at dawn and watched the sea work itself into a frenzy of grey and white, the waves coming in long, deliberate ranks like an army that had all the time in the world. The wind screamed against the glass in a voice he had learned over twenty years to hear as speech: *hold, hold, hold.* So he held. He trimmed the wicks, wound the clockwork that turned the lens, and kept the light burning through a night when no sane captain would have put out from any harbor on this coast.

By morning the wind had spent its fury and gone sullen, blowing hard but without conviction, and Orsen climbed down the hundred and eleven steps to make tea and find that Rosalind had already been there. She left bread under the oilcloth the way she always did now—since the soldiers came, since the grey coats at the harbor, since Captain Veyra's clean handwriting began appearing on notices nailed to the fish market posts. She did not knock anymore. She let herself into his kitchen like a woman returning to her own house, which in some sense she was, though neither of them said so.

He was eating the bread, standing up, when he saw the figure on the shingle below the lighthouse point.

At first he took it for driftwood—one of the great black trunks the storms sometimes threw up from wrecks older than anyone could name. Then it moved, or rather the sea moved it, rolling it gently in the retreating wash with the terrible tenderness the sea reserved for its dead. Orsen set down the bread. He took his oilskin off its peg and went out into the wind.

* * *

The body lay face-down in a wrack of kelp and broken spars, half-buried in shingle, one arm flung forward as if reaching for the tideline. A man, young, dressed not in fisherman's wool but in a dark coat of good cloth, soaked through and heavy as anchor chain. His boots were army-issue but not army-polished; they had been dyed over, badly, brown paint over government black. Orsen had seen boots like that before, years ago, on men who came to Saltmere after dark and left before the fishing boats went out.

He turned the body over with a gentleness that surprised him. The young man's face was bloodless, the lips grey-blue, the eyes half-open and fixed on nothing with the particular astonishment of the newly drowned—as if, even now, he could not quite believe what the water had done to him. There was a gash above his ear where the rocks had finished whatever the sea had started.

Orsen crouched beside him for a long moment. It was not the first drowned man he had attended. In the year of the great storms, Saltmere had grown practiced at grief; there was a whole liturgy to it, unspoken, that every keeper of a coastal town knew by heart. You closed the eyes if they would close. You crossed the hands. You looked for identification, because someone, somewhere, would come asking—or worse, because no one ever would, and the nameless dead needed names even more than the named ones.

In the dead man's inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth and sewn shut with waxed thread, Orsen found a packet the size of a prayer book.

He knew what it was before he touched it. He had helped Maren Hale bury two such packets in her garden three winters past, back when the war was far away and the smuggling was only rumor. He knew the weight of them, the way they were made to survive salt water, the way the men who carried them wore their boots dyed and their collars plain and their faces forgettable. Couriers. The word arrived in his mind uninvited, and with it came Rosalind, standing in his kitchen doorway last autumn asking careful questions about which coves were watched and which were empty, and him answering because she was his wife's sister and because he had decided, without deciding, not to wonder too hard about why she asked.

The packet bore no address on the outside. But the oilcloth had been opened and resealed—hastily, at sea perhaps—and beneath the wrapping Orsen could see the corner of the paper inside, and on that corner, in ink that had run only slightly, a single letter.

*R.*

He sat back on his heels in the wet shingle and the wind pushed at his shoulders as if impatient with him.

There were perhaps forty people in Saltmere whose names ended in R. Half of them were children. Of the adults, most were men—Rennick the cooper, old Ruan who mended sails, Rilla Marsh who sold cockles from a barrow and had never in her life received correspondence of any kind. Orsen ran through them the way a man runs through a locked house checking doors, and at the end of the corridor, waiting for him as he had known she would be, stood his wife's sister.

Rosalind.

He thought: *burn it.* The thought arrived whole and reasonable, wearing the face of prudence. Burn it on the beach, scatter the ashes in the tidewash, tell no one, tell even himself it never happened. The army was in Saltmere now. Grey coats walked the harbor road in pairs, and Captain Veyra sat in the requisitioned customhouse with the parish ledger spread before her like a surgeon's chart, striking out names. If a courier had been carrying a message to R—if the resistance, the network, whatever you called the thing Rosalind had been doing for years under the nose of everyone who loved her—if that machinery existed and ran on letters like this one, then the letter was not a letter. It was a fuse. And the man holding it, the widower who kept his head down, the man whose entire strategy for staying alive consisted of being forgettable—that man was standing on an open beach in broad daylight holding a fuse.

Burn it. Go home. Trim the wicks. Be invisible.

Orsen Amberdale had been invisible for four years, ever since the fever took Edele and grief taught him that the safest place in the world was the inside of a routine. He had perfected it. He was the lighthouse keeper, and lighthouse keepers were furniture, part of the coast, noticed the way you notice a rock. He had told himself it was humility. Some nights he had almost believed it.

But he was kneeling next to a boy—younger than his nephew would have been, if he'd had a nephew—who had carried this thing through storm-water and enemy patrols and God knew what else, and had died within sight of the light Orsen kept. Within sight of it. Had died looking at it, maybe, in his last moments, that white wheel turning in the dark, and had thought: *there, I'm nearly there.*

Orsen could not burn that.

He got up, looked once up and down the beach—the tide was going out, the sand flat and naked, nowhere to hide anything—and did the arithmetic of concealment. The packet went inside his shirt, against his skin, cold as a fish. The body he could not move alone and should not be seen moving at all. So he did the ordinary thing, the forgettable thing: he walked up to the harbor road and told Tobbin the harbormaster that there was a drowned man on the shingle below the point, and Tobbin blew the signal horn, and by mid-morning there were six men and a handcart on the beach and a blanket over the young courier's face, and Orsen stood among them saying nothing, being furniture, while inside his shirt a small square of oilcloth pressed against his heartbeat like a second heart.

* * *

They buried the courier two days later, in the corner of the parish yard where the nameless went. No priest came—the vicar had been requisitioned along with everything else, sent inland to minister to garrisons—but Tobbin read the burial service from memory, getting three-quarters of the words right, and the six men who had brought him up from the beach stood bareheaded in the drizzle. The army sent no one. A drowned stranger in a dark coat was of no administrative interest.

Except that Orsen, watching the first spades of earth go down, caught sight of Corporal Dunmore at the churchyard gate, and Dunmore was not passing by. Dunmore was watching. He wrote something in a little book, and walked on.

Orsen kept his face still and his pace unhurried and walked home the long way, past the fish racks, past the net-loft, doing what he had done all his life: nothing worth remembering.

* * *

Rosalind came at dusk.

She came up the cliff path in the failing light with her hood up and the bread under her arm, and Orsen met her in the kitchen, and for a moment they simply looked at each other across the room the way two people look at each other when both of them know something has changed and neither wants to be the first to say it.

"You buried him," she said. Not a question.

"You knew him."

"I knew of him." She set the bread down on the table very carefully, as if the table might break. "I didn't know his name. That was the rule. We weren't given names." Her hands, he noticed, were not steady. "Tobbin says you found him."

"Tobbin says a lot of things."

"Orsen." She said his name the way you say a word you've been rehearsing. "Was there anything on him?"

And there it was. The moment. He had carried the packet for two days—buried it in the flour bin, then dug it out and buried it in the coal scuttle, then taken it out again because coal dust gets everywhere and a search of the lighthouse would find coal dust on an oilcloth wrapper and draw conclusions. Two nights he had slept badly with it under the loose hearthstone, and twice he had gotten up intending to burn it in the stove, and twice he had knelt there with the paper in the tongs over the flames and found that his hands would not open.

Because burning it meant choosing. It meant telling Rosalind, *whatever you are, whoever you answer to, whatever my wife knew and hid from me—it ends here, the sea has closed that account.* It meant safety. It also meant that somewhere inland, people waiting for news coded into that little book would wait forever, and some of them might be waiting in cells, or on beaches, timing their escapes to instructions that would never arrive.

And it meant lying to Rosalind. Not the polite silence he had maintained for years—not the careful not-asking that had passed between them since Edele died—but an actual lie, spoken aloud, to the last person in the world who shared his grief.

"I'm going to ask you something," Orsen said, "and I want the truth, because I think I've earned it and I know I need it."

She waited.

"The night Edele was dying—three weeks, we had, at the end—she talked. Fever talk, mostly. Names I didn't know. Coves. She said—" His voice caught, and he let it catch; there was no use pretending otherwise in front of this woman. "She said, *tell Rosa the north route is burned, she has to know.* And I held her hand and I said yes, love, yes, and I never told you, because telling you meant knowing what it meant. Four years I've not known what it meant. Is that right? Did I understand her?"

Rosalind had gone very pale. She sat down slowly, without being asked, on the bench by the stove, and for a long moment she looked at her hands.

"She figured it out," she said finally. "I don't know how much, or when. Near the end, I think. She never confronted me. She just—" A breath that was almost a laugh. "She started leaving the cellar door unlocked. And extra candles in the pantry. And once, a purse of coin where I'd find it, with no note. She knew, Orsen. She knew and she never said a word, because saying it would have made me stop, or made her complicit, and she loved me too much to do either." She looked up at him. "That's the truth. All of it. Now ask me what you're actually going to ask."

Orsen reached into his shirt and laid the packet on the table between them.

For a moment Rosalind did not touch it. She stared at it as if it were a snake, or a relic, or both. Then she unwrapped it with fingers that trembled only slightly, and Orsen saw the page inside, the cipher columns, the small running *R* in the corner, and heard her breath change.

"Where," she said. "Where exactly."

"Inner pocket. Sewn in. He'd opened it at sea and resealed it—badly."

"He read it." Something moved behind her eyes, quick and dark. "Or tried to. Oh, God. If the wrong people find out he read it—"

"No one knows but us. And Tobbin, and five other men who'll say he had nothing on him, because I asked Tobbin before I asked anyone else, and Tobbin understands the sea." Orsen sat down across from her. "Now you understand me, Rosalind. I'm giving you this. I want you to know exactly what I'm giving you, because I've thought of nothing else for two days. This is my neck. If Veyra finds this in your possession, or mine, or hears that a drowned courier reached Saltmere alive with dispatches, she will take this town apart board by board until she finds the rest of your network, and she will start with the ledger, and she will end with the people in it. I know that. I've done the sums."

"I know."

"And I'm giving it to you anyway." He pushed it an inch across the table toward her. "Because Edele left candles in the pantry for you, and I can do less than that. Because that boy swam and drowned within sight of my light rather than lose this. And because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because my wife spent her last weeks protecting your secret without ever letting herself know it, and I have spent four years protecting my ignorance, and one of us in this family ought to be braver than that."

Rosalind looked at him across the packet, across the cipher, across the ghost of Edele Marchetti-Amberdale standing in the warm kitchen with them both, and her composure—which Orsen had admired for years, envied, resented, depended upon—cracked clean down the middle.

"You sound like her," she whispered.

"I'll take that as thanks."

"It is." She gathered the packet up, rewrapped it, and tucked it inside her coat against her ribs. Then she stood, and steadied, and became again the woman who had run escape routes through occupied coastline for years without a single name attached to her own. "There's a boat due Thursday, north cove. This changes the timetable. And Orsen—" She paused at the door, hood in hand. "Veyra's clerk has been asking Tobbin questions about the drowned man. About whether anything was recovered. Tobbin's covered you. But she doesn't stop, that woman. She adds things up. Whatever you do next—do it knowing she's counting."

She slipped out into the dark, and Orsen stood alone in his kitchen with the bread on the table and the fire low, listening to the sea work at the shingle below, patient as a creditor.

Upstairs, the lamp turned. Out in the parish yard, a nameless boy slept under new-turned earth. And down at the customhouse, a lamp still burned—Orsen could see it from his window, small and yellow and relentless—where a thorough woman sat late over her papers, adding things up.

Thursday was three days away.
