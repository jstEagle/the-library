## 6. The Captain's History

Captain Elara Veyra had once believed that history was a discipline of humility.

She had said as much in her doctoral defense, eleven years ago, to a panel of three aging professors who had nodded over their spectacles and awarded her distinction. *The historian's first duty*, she had written then, *is to admit how little she knows.* She could still see the sentence, ink-brown on cream paper, the marginal note her advisor had added in his small precise hand: *Yes — but someone must decide what is known.*

That was the trouble with archives. They did not assemble themselves. Someone chose the box; someone labeled the folder; someone decided which letter was worth keeping and which was fuel. History looked like a river from the far bank, but stand inside it and you felt every stone that had been placed by a hand.

Now her own hands were doing the placing.

She sat in the requisition office above the Saltmere customs house, formerly the harbormaster's counting room, and looked at the parish ledger lying open before her under the good lamp she had brought from headquarters because the light here was salt-dulled and yellow. Outside the rain came down in long grey sheets, the third day of it, drumming on the slate roof like a clerk tapping a pen he has no intention of using.

The ledger was beautiful. That was the first thing she had noticed, and the first thing she distrusted about her own affection for it. The clerk's hand — one hand throughout, decades of entries, a man named Pellwin according to the flyleaf, who had died two winters ago and been replaced by no one, which explained the recent entries' growing disorder — wrote a fair round script with flourishes only on capital letters, as if each name deserved a small ceremony at its beginning. Baptisms. Marriages. Burials. And, kept separately at the back in a section Pellwin had titled *Departures and Returns*, a record of who had left Saltmere by sea and whether they had come back.

It was this section that had brought her north.

Headquarters wanted an account of the coastal towns: population before, losses during, current disposition of residents, identification of persons who had aided enemy movements across the water. Standard requisition procedure. In most towns this meant a stack of half-rotted parish books and a priest or clerk who wept or lied or both. Saltmere gave her something else. Saltmere gave her a ledger so complete it read like a confession, and a town so quiet it might have been painted rather than built.

*Thoroughness*, she reminded herself, dipping the pen, *is a form of respect.* If she was going to write these people into the official record, she would do it properly. No shortcuts. No convenient rounding of numbers. Every name checked twice, every discrepancy noted.

Her notebook lay open beside the ledger, already dense with columns. She had worked out her method on the second evening: names cross-referenced against the muster rolls taken when the army arrived; burial entries against the storm-year dates; departure entries against the naval intercept reports, which listed boats sighted crossing the strait in the dark months. Where all three agreed, she entered the fact in black ink. Where they disagreed, red.

There was a great deal of red.

* * *

By the fourth week she had filled two notebooks and begun a third, and she had learned Saltmere the way one learns a text: by repetition, by structure, by noticing where the grammar breaks.

The grammar broke in interesting places.

Take the departures. In the storm years, forty-one names appeared in the *Departures and Returns* section marked with a small symbol Pellwin had used elsewhere for vessels lost — a short horizontal stroke beneath the date. Forty-one people who left Saltmere by sea during the worst weather in living memory and were never recorded as returning. The obvious reading was shipwreck. The storms had killed plenty; the burials column confirmed it, week after grim week, coffins and coffins, some entries noting only *remains, unidentifiable, given number*.

But there were too few bodies for the missing. That was the arithmetic that would not sit still. She had done it four times, on four evenings, hoping the total would change. It did not. Forty-one gone by sea, and grave-space and testimony accounting for perhaps twenty-six drowned in all, including fishermen lost from working boats. Somewhere between ten and fifteen people had left Saltmere in open boats in winter gales and simply not died — or not died here.

People did not cross a war-strait in winter for trade. Trade waited for spring. People crossed in winter to escape, or to carry things, and both categories interested her office equally.

And there was the matter of the bread.

She had noticed it the way she noticed everything now — obliquely, first, a pattern surfacing before its meaning. The widow Amberdale. Orsen Amberdale, keeper of the lighthouse, widower of Maren Amberdale, deceased four years past. He appeared in the ledger rarely, as such men do: his marriage, his wife's burial, nothing else. But in the household survey her lieutenant had conducted upon billeting, one entry read: *Amberdale, Orsen, keeper. Household of one. His sister-in-law visits weekly with provisions.*

Sister-in-law. Rosalind Marchetti, per the marriage register of one Bren Aldous to Maren Amberdale, née Marchetti. Rosalind Marchetti, who did not appear anywhere else. Not in the household survey — she was registered nowhere in Saltmere at all, though three separate informants mentioned her, always casually, always the same way: *she brings bread up to the lighthouse, poor man, keeps him fed since his wife went.* Not in the ration rolls, because she drew no rations. Not in the tax lists. A woman of thirty-odd years, living in a town of nine hundred souls, recorded nowhere but in the margins of other people's accounts.

Veyra had spent enough years in archives to know what an unrecorded person meant. Records were gravity; ordinary lives fell toward them inevitably. Births pulled you in, taxes pulled you in, marriage and death and debt and the militia census. To remain unrecorded required effort. It required someone standing between you and the paper, deliberately, year after year.

Or it required you to be the one holding the pen's loyalty.

She turned back to the departures section and read the forty-one names again, slowly. Then she took a fresh page and began a new column: *Persons connected to departed individuals.* Family members still resident. Neighbors. Employers. It was slow work and she did not enjoy it, precisely — enjoyment was not the right word, and she was careful with herself about words — but she recognized the feeling underneath it, which was the old archival hunger, the pleasure of a pattern closing like a well-made argument.

Halfway down the page she stopped writing.

Because the names clustered. Not randomly — she had expected random, scattered kinship, the usual web. Instead, twelve of the forty-one had connections running through the same handful of households, and those households shared something else. She checked it against the burial records. In nearly every case, the family of a departed person had also suffered a death in the storm years — and in nearly every case, the burial entry carried a small annotation in Pellwin's hand, one she had initially catalogued as clerical habit: *rites provided privately* or *interred without public service* or, in three instances, simply a dash where the officiant's name should be.

Private rites. Quiet graves. Grief handled off the record.

You buried people quietly, she knew, when the funeral itself was dangerous — when gathering mourners meant gathering witnesses, or when the dead person's name could not safely be spoken aloud. These were the burial customs of occupied countries, of plague towns, of persecuted sects. She had catalogued them in her dissertation. Chapter Three: *Subterranean Literacies — How Communities Write When Writing Is Forbidden.*

Saltmere had been reading her dissertation, apparently, and taking notes.

* * *

The informant came on a Tuesday, escorted in past the sentry by Lieutenant Corr, who announced him as "the fish merchant" with the faint curl of the lip Corr reserved for civilians whose usefulness outstripped his contempt for them.

His name was Doss Merren. He sat where she indicated, hat turning in his hands, eyes moving over the ledgers stacked on her table with an expression she had learned to read early in the occupation: the hunger of a small man imagining himself large. Informants always looked at the records that way. They understood, better than the honest did, that paper was power.

"You've questions," he said. "About who's been helping the crossings."

"I have questions," Veyra said. "Whether you have answers remains to be seen."

He told her about boats, first — the general material, the stuff she already had. Old hulls gone missing from the moorings. Nets mended with unfamiliar twine. A chandler who ordered more pitch than his invoices justified. She let him run, making small marks, letting silence do the work silence does. Then, when he wound down, she asked the question she had prepared.

"When a boat leaves Saltmere in winter and doesn't come back, and no body washes up, and no family sits shiva — where does that family go to church?"

Merren blinked. "Church?"

"The burial entries." She turned the ledger toward him, open to the annotated pages. "*Rites provided privately.* Whose rites? Which house?"

For a moment something flickered behind his eyes — not guilt exactly, but the animal quickness of a man realizing the game was larger than the coin he'd been paid. "Folk keep their grieving close, Captain. Bad times. You don't want the wrong ears at a funeral."

"No," she agreed. "You don't."

She dismissed him with a chit for his payment and sat alone afterward for a long while, listening to the rain. He had confirmed nothing and revealed something anyway: the town protected its silences collectively. Even the informants spoke in the passive voice. Boats *went missing*. Rites *were provided*. Nobody did anything, in all of Saltmere; things merely happened, like weather.

In her experience there were two kinds of towns under occupation. There were towns that broke — that informed on each other eagerly, that produced collaborators the way wet wood produces rot — and there were towns that closed, that became a single organism with a single will, impenetrable not because they resisted loudly but because they withheld. Breaking towns were easy. Closing towns were expensive.

Saltmere was closing. And the closure had a center of gravity. She could feel it the way she had once felt a flawed argument in a student's essay — not yet able to name the error, but certain it was there, certain because everything else was too smooth. The ledger was smooth. The informants were smooth. The town's grief was smooth. Smoothness, in her long experience of documents, meant editing.

Someone had edited Saltmere.

* * *

That night she began the draft.

Official histories were written to a formula — she had received the circular herself, *Guidelines for Administrative Histories of Requisitioned Districts*, forty pages of prescribed sections and approved phrasings — and she followed it faithfully, because she was a faithful servant of procedure even where procedure was foolish. *District of Saltmere: Pre-War Population and Economy.* *Storm-Year Losses: A Statistical Summary.* *Current Civil Disposition and Loyalty Assessment.*

She wrote the numbers cleanly. Nine hundred and six souls before the wars. Two hundred and seventeen dead across the storm years, by sea and sickness and one bad fire. Forty-one departed by sea, unreconciled. She wrote the sentences that headquarters wanted: *the district has proven largely compliant; isolated elements engaged in unauthorized maritime activity; the population, weary of deprivation, greets the restoration of order with relief.*

Then she sat back and read what she had written, and heard her advisor's voice in her ear, dry as dust: *Yes — but someone must decide what is known.*

Every sentence was defensible. Every figure was sourced. And the whole of it, assembled, was false — not false the way lies are false, but false the way a portrait is false when the painter leaves out the eyes. A reader in the capital, consulting her history fifty years hence, would learn that Saltmere had lost two hundred and seventeen people and gained an army garrison, and would learn nothing at all about the town that actually existed: the town that buried its dead in secret and ferried its fugitives through winter gales and passed bread up a cliff path to a lighthouse keeper, week after week, through the worst years anyone remembered.

She thought of the widow's sister again. Rosalind Marchetti. Registered nowhere; present everywhere; the negative space in the shape of the town. Veyra had interviewed forty-three residents formally. Sixteen of them, unprompted, had mentioned bringing bread to the lighthouse, or receiving bread from the widow Marchetti, or seeing her at the harbor at odd hours. No one had said anything consequential about her. Everyone had said something. It was like watching a flock of birds all swerving around an invisible wire.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would call at the lighthouse. Formally. A census verification visit, nothing more. She wanted to look at the keeper's face when the sister-in-law's name was spoken aloud. Faces were documents too, and she had learned to read them.

She dipped the pen and added a final line to the draft, in the space reserved for *Outstanding Administrative Matters*: *Discrepancy in Departures and Returns section unresolved. Recommend continued inquiry. Persons of interest: unidentified coordinator of maritime departures; R. Marchetti, sister-in-law to district lighthouse keeper.*

She looked at the name for a moment. Then, because thoroughness was a form of respect and she respected even the people she might ruin, she turned to a clean page and wrote at the top, in her own hand, smaller than the official script:

*Maren Amberdale, d. four years past. Sister: Rosalind. Question: who taught the town to bury quietly — and when did the teaching begin?*

Because that was the question under all the others, the one the guidelines had no section for. Networks did not spring up fully formed. Trust was built in peacetime, in small transactions, in years of bread and favors and shared silence. Whoever ran the crossings had been running something in Saltmere for a long time before the war made it matter — and Maren Amberdale, dead four years, sat at the center of the oldest cluster of private burials like a spider at the middle of a web she had woven in life and left behind in death.

The rain thickened against the window. Down in the harbor, the lighthouse beam swept its slow arc through the murk — steady, punctual, the one thing in Saltmere that kept its appointments honestly — and Veyra watched it turn, and thought about the man who kept it, and the woman who climbed to him with bread, and the forty-one names in the back of a dead clerk's book.

Then she blew out the lamp, and in the darkness the beam swung through the window and crossed her face, once, like a finger pointing.
