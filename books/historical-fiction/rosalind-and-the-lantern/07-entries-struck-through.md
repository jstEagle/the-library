## 7. Entries Struck Through

The ledger came back on a Tuesday, in the fourth week of the occupation, carried up the cliff path by a private so young his collar chafed him. He set it on the clerk's counter in the customs house the way a man sets down something hot, touched his cap to no one in particular, and left.

I was there because Tam Wicker had asked me to be there. "They said it's to be checked against the original," he'd told me the night before, in the low voice men used now for anything that mattered. "You copied half of it yourself, Orsen. They'll want steady hands if there's arguing to do."

There would be no arguing. That was the first thing I understood when I saw it lying there.

The ledger of Saltmere parish—births, deaths, marriages, and, since the storms began, the other column that Tam had kept in his own careful shorthand: who went into the water, who washed ashore, who left by night and never came back, who arrived by night from places with worse news than ours. Eleven years of my wife's town in one book. I had held it in both hands through the copying, page after page, and I knew its weight the way I knew the weight of an anchor.

It did not weigh the same coming back.

* * *

Tam opened it with hands that were not steady at all, and I stood at his shoulder as the light from the harbor window fell across the first altered page.

There is a particular kind of ink the army uses—a grey-black, iron-gall cut with something else, something that dries harder than the paper around it. I learned its name later, from Rosalind, who learned it from a printer in Corrow who'd seen the victors' scribes at work in the occupied towns to the north. *Registration ink*, they called it. Ink meant to outlast everything written under it. When you struck a line through a name in registration ink, you were not crossing it out. You were burying it under something stronger.

The first struck name was Maren Ostrey.

Maren Ostrey had drowned—or the ledger had once said she had—in the second storm of the great year, along with her two daughters. Except she hadn't. I knew she hadn't because I had stood in my own kitchen eleven months ago and watched Rosalind count coins onto my table while Maren wept into her apron, and three nights later a boat I was not supposed to see went out past the point with its lamp hooded, and Maren and her girls were gone north to her sister's family in Fennwick. Rosalind had arranged it in my house. She had used my bread tin to carry the money. I had pretended, all that winter, not to know what my own kitchen was for.

Someone in a clean uniform had drawn a single line through Maren Ostrey and her daughters and written beside them, in a small precise hand: *Entry unreliable. No corroborating record of loss.*

No corroborating record of loss. The words sat there like a joke told by a man who does not know he is joking. There was no record of their loss because there had been no loss. There had been a rescue. And the record could not hold a rescue, because a rescue implied someone fleeing, and fleeing implied a reason, and a reason implied that the victors' rule was a thing a mother would take her children out into a winter sea rather than stay under.

So the entry became unreliable. So Maren Ostrey, alive and safe in Fennwick, ceased officially to have ever been in danger—and her daughters ceased, in some colder way, to have ever been anything at all.

"Orsen." Tam's voice was very quiet. "Turn forward."

* * *

I turned forward.

They had not struck everything. That was what made it terrible—the selectiveness of it, the care. A drowning from the third storm, old Hollis the netmender, still stood untouched, the date and the tide and the finding of the body all intact, because nobody disputed that old Hollis died. The sea's honest work was allowed to stand. What had been struck was every entry where the story bent. Every departure by night. Every arrival unexplained. Every death without a body. Every name that appeared in the ledger's margins in Tam's shorthand—a small cross for smuggled out, a circle for harbored, a dash for passed through—and I counted, as the pages turned under my thumb, and nearly every cross and more than half the circles had been buried under registration ink.

Bram Tullow. Struck. I had mended his trawl lines the spring before he went; he'd paid me in smoked herring and talk of his boy learning letters. Gone out one dark tide with nine others, all of them struck now, all of them marked *no corroborating record*.

The Fenner household—six circles in the margin over two pages, six people passed through Saltmere on their way somewhere safer. Struck, all six, and beside the last one a note in that small precise hand: *Pattern suggests systematic falsification. Refer to informant summary 12.*

Informant summary 12. Someone in Saltmere had given them summaries. Someone had sat in the requisition office across from Captain Veyra with tea or without it and walked her through the ledger page by page, saying this one, not that one, this one, and she had taken the notes down in her small precise hand and then come back with iron-gall ink and a ruler—I was sure a ruler had been used; the strikes were too straight for anything freehand—and edited my town.

And at the bottom of the final amended page, a certification: *Reviewed and corrected for administrative accuracy, Capt. E. Veyra, Requisition Office, Saltmere.* Dated eight days ago. Eight days, and the book had been sitting in her office being made true.

"Eleven years," Tam said. His hand was flat on the open page as though holding the paper down against wind. "Eleven years I kept this book. My father kept it before me, and his before him, and in all that time the worst thing anyone ever asked me to write in it was a lie about a stillborn to spare a mother's feelings, and I refused even that."

"I know."

"They didn't burn anything." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "That's the clever part, isn't it? Burning you'd notice. Ashes you'd mourn. But this—" he tapped the grey-black line through Bram Tullow's name, "—this is just tidying. This is a book agreeing with itself. In ten years no one will look twice. In ten years this will simply be what happened."

I looked at the struck names and felt something I did not have a word for yet. Not anger—anger comes hot and passes. This was colder and it settled lower, somewhere under the ribs, the way cold seawater settles into a boot. It was the feeling of watching weather change over open water and knowing, before the first drop falls, exactly how bad it will be.

Because here was the thought that would not leave me alone as I stood in that customs house: Veyra had not done this out of malice. I had met her twice, once across my own kitchen table while soldiers went through my cellar above her head, and she was not cruel. Cruelty is sloppy. This was the opposite of sloppy. She believed—she genuinely believed—that a ledger full of escapes and rescues was a ledger with errors in it, errors of sentiment, errors of loyalty, errors committed by frightened clerks in a losing cause, and that correcting those errors was not destruction but *service*. She was making the record accurate. She was making the record agree with the victory. And she would sleep soundly after, and she would be right to, by every rule she had ever been taught.

That was the horror. Not that a villain had gotten hold of our names. That a careful person had.

* * *

I carried the news home the long way, along the cliff, because the harbor road had eyes on it now—two sentries at the fish gate and a corporal who liked to stop men and ask them questions he already knew the answers to, just to watch them answer. The wind had come around into the northeast, which meant weather somewhere behind it, days off yet but building. The gulls were flying high and stiff-winged. I read the sky the way I read everything now, automatically, keeping one part of my mind always on survival, and the rest of it stayed down in the customs house with the dead who weren't dead.

Rosalind was in my kitchen when I got in. She was always in my kitchen these days; she came with bread from Mrs. Petchy's oven and stayed as long as the light did, and neither of us named what that was becoming. She had her sleeves pushed up and was rendering fat for the lamps—my stock of oil was requisitioned, like everything, and we burned what we could make—and she looked up when I came in and saw my face before I'd said a word.

"The ledger," she said. Not a question. News traveled through Saltmere faster than any army could requisition it.

"It's back."

"And?"

I put my coat on its peg. I took my time about it. There are things a man delays saying because once said, they cannot be unsaid, and the kitchen was warm and smelled of fat and bread and had been, for an hour, a place where none of it was true yet.

"Maren Ostrey," I said. "Her girls. Bram Tullow and the nine. All six Fenners. The whole Kestrel night, everyone who went out on the spring tides last year. Struck through. Grey ink. Marked unreliable."

Rosalind did not move. She had a wooden spoon in her hand and she did not move, and I watched her go very still in the way of a woman who has spent years practicing stillness, and I understood that she was doing arithmetic. Not grief first. Arithmetic. How many routes compromised, how many families exposed, how many safe houses named in margins now legible to whoever read the corrected book next.

"The margins?" she said.

"Circles and crosses. Most of them struck too."

"Struck, or—" and her voice did something complicated around the word, "—or *read*?"

"That's the same question," I said, "isn't it."

She set the spoon down. She crossed to the window and looked out at the harbor, at the grey coats moving along the quay in their unhurried way, cataloguing boats, and when she spoke again her voice had gone flat and level, the voice she used for the things underneath all her other voices.

"There were thirty-one crossings out of this harbor in three years. Thirty-one that I ran or helped run. Two hundred and six people, Orsen, counting the ones passed through toward the north coast. Every one of them is either in that ledger or traceable through it. I built the network on top of the parish book because the parish book was the one thing in Saltmere no one would ever dare touch." She laughed, very softly, without any humor in it at all. "And they didn't dare touch it. They improved it."

"She'll do more than strike names," I said. "Tam thinks so too. A correction is a beginning. Next she'll want the corrections explained—she'll want to know *why* the entries were false, and there's only one reason entries like that get falsified, and the why has a face, and the face lives in this house most evenings and eats bread at this table."

"I know how long the thread is, Orsen." She turned from the window. The light caught her and for a moment I saw my wife in the set of her jaw—the family stubbornness, the Marchetti chin—and then it passed and it was only Rosalind, tired, exact, afraid in the specific way of people who are afraid for others. "I've been following it since before you knew there was a thread. But listen to me." She came close. She took my wrist, the one with the rope scar, the grip firm and cold. "The ledger they corrected is Tam's ledger. It's the official one. Whatever it says now, that's what the historians will print, that's what the inquiry will read, that's what Maren Ostrey's grandchildren will believe happened to her—which is nothing, which is that she never existed in danger and therefore never existed in courage. Unless there's another one."

"Another what?"

"Record." Her eyes held mine and did not let go. "Another ledger, Orsen. A true one."

The fire ticked. Out on the water the gulls were dropping lower now, and the northeast wind had found the chimney and was worrying it, a low note like a held breath. I stood in my own kitchen with my sister-in-law's hand on my wrist and felt the ground shift the way it shifts before a storm—not the storm itself, only the certainty of it arriving.

"What true ledger," I said. "Rosalind. What true ledger?"

She let go of my wrist. She looked at the lamp she'd been filling, and the stair beyond it, and the tower above us where the light would turn again tonight whether the world deserved it or not.

"Before I answer that," she said quietly, "you should sit down. And you should understand that everything I say next, you'll wish I'd said three winters ago—and so will I."
