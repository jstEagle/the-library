## 8. Rosalind's Confession

Rosalind had rehearsed the confession for eleven years.

She had rehearsed it in the cellar of the chandlery while the first boat slipped out with three families aboard. She had rehearsed it walking the cliff path at night, the words timed to her footsteps, so that if Maren ever asked—*what did you do while I was dying, sister?*—she would have an answer that was not a lie. She had rehearsed it again after the funeral, standing at the churchyard gate with wet wool clinging to her arms, watching Orsen carry his grief the way he carried everything: close to the body, silent, unbending.

She had never once delivered it.

Now she climbed the lighthouse stair with a loaf under her arm and the truth like a stone sewn into her coat hem, and every rehearsal deserted her at once. The stair turned above her, ninety-three steps she could have counted in her sleep, because for years she had counted them—coming up with bread, coming up with warnings, coming up with nothing at all except the need to see her sister's husband's face and confirm that he was still alive, still invisible, still safe from everything she touched.

The lantern room door stood open. Evening light came through the glass in long amber panes, laying stripes across the boards, and Orsen sat on his stool beneath the great lens with a whetstone and a filleting knife, working the edge in slow circles. He did not look up when she came in. He never looked up quickly; that was one of the first things she had learned about him, back when Maren was alive—Orsen Amberdale moved through the world as though any sudden attention might cost him something.

"Storm coming from the southwest," he said. "You'll want to be off the cliff road before dark."

"Orsen." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. She set the loaf on the shelf beside the oil cans, where bread always went, and then did not know what to do with her hands. "Sit down. Please. I have something to tell you, and I'd rather you were sitting."

The whetstone stopped. He looked at her then—really looked, the way he almost never did—and she saw him take inventory of her the way he took inventory of weather. The set of her shoulders. The fact that she had not touched the bread. The fact that she had said *sit down* instead of *good evening.*

He laid the knife on the cloth and rose, stiff in the knees, and sat on the sea chest by the wall instead of the stool, which put his eyes below hers. An old habit too, she thought. Making himself small. She had spent years mistaking it for humility. Now she understood it was armor—the same armor she wore, hammered from different ore.

"I've been thinking," she began, and stopped, and hated herself for stopping. She had faced requisition officers in this town. She had stood in Veyra's borrowed office with her hands folded and lied about tide tables with a steady pulse. But this man had fed her for eleven years without asking why a childless widow ate alone, and the debt of that silence was heavier than any officer's suspicion.

"The ledger," she tried again. "You saw it. When Clerk Hobbett had you copying."

"You know I did."

"Some of what you copied was true. The births, the marriages, most of the deaths." She drew a breath. "Some of it was not true. And some of what was true is going to be struck out, because I made sure it went into the book in ways that looked like clerical error. Misspellings. Wrong dates. Names entered twice, once wrong, once right. A record that lies badly enough looks like incompetence, and incompetence is not worth burning."

Orsen's brow furrowed slowly, the way a sea fog comes in—not all at once but in layers. "You're telling me you altered the parish ledger."

"I'm telling you I *wrote* half of it." The words came out of her now like water through a sprung seam, and she let them. "Hobbett's eyes have been failing for six years. Since the second winter of the war. He couldn't manage the fair copy anymore, and he was frightened—frightened of the wrong people asking questions, frightened of the right ones too. So I did the entries. All of them. Every name that came through Saltmere between the second winter and last spring went through my hand before it went into the book."

"Every name." He said it flatly, testing the weight.

"Ninety-one people came through this town that the official history will say never existed here. Fishermen who refused the conscription oath. Two deserters from the coastal garrison. A family of five from Harrow Point whose house was burned over their heads. Merchants, midwives, a schoolteacher, three children born on the crossing." Her voice held. She had learned to make it hold; that was another thing the war had taught her, along with ciphering and forgery and how to smile at soldiers. "They slept in your cellar, some of them. In the net loft. Behind the salt store. They ate bread I baked in your oven while you slept."

The lamp above them ticked as the mechanism turned, patient as a heart. Outside, the light was going coppery over the water, and far out, where the sky met the sea, there was already a bruise-colored line that might have been cloud or might have been the coming storm.

Orsen was very quiet. Then: "Maren."

"Maren knew." Rosalind sat down at last, on the upturned crate by the door, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her. "Maren knew from the beginning. Before the beginning. It was Maren who brought me the first name, Orsen. Do you remember the winter she took in that boy from the north coast—her cousin's son, she called him? There was no cousin. There was no boy from the north coast. There was a man named Aldous Renwick who had printed pamphlets in Kingsbridge, and Maren hid him in our bedroom for nine days and cooked his meals and told me afterward, calmly, over the dishes, that if I wanted to help I should learn which harbormaster took bribes and which one didn't."

"She never—" Orsen's hand opened and closed on his knee. "Eleven years. Eleven years I lived with her, and she never said a word."

"She said a hundred words a day that weren't words. That was the whole art of it. You were the safest man in Saltmere, do you understand that? You kept your head down, you trimmed your wicks, you gossiped about nothing with everyone, and because nobody suspected you, nobody watched this house. This lighthouse was the only building in Saltmere the army never searched until this autumn. Do you know why?" She leaned forward. "Because Orsen Amberdale is boring, Orsen. Boring as bread. Boring as tide tables. I built half my network inside the shadow of your respectability, and Maren built the other half, and neither of us ever thanked you properly, because thanking you would have meant telling you, and telling you would have ended the shadow."

The color had gone out of his face. Not anger—she had braced for anger and found instead something worse, the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. "The money," he said. "The winter the boats didn't come in and we ate well anyway. The winter Maren bought the good flour and paid the rent early and said the herring had been kind."

"There was never any herring," Rosalind said gently. "There was a fund. Exiles pay their passage when they can, and those who can't are carried anyway, and the difference is made up from the fund. Maren kept it in the flour tin. Third shelf, behind the oat jar. She moved it after you broke the crock in '43 because you were too honest to steal from even if you'd wanted to, which is the highest praise she ever gave anyone besides me."

A sound came out of him then that was half laugh and entirely grief, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes, and for a long moment the only motion in the lantern room was the turning of the lens and the shiver of the glass as the wind found it and tested it.

"She died," he said finally, "and I thought I had known her. I thought the worst secret in this marriage was that she prayed to the old sea-mother under her breath and never told the priest." His jaw worked. "Ninety-one people. In my house. Under my roof. While I mended nets."

"While you mended nets," Rosalind agreed, "we moved them north in your fishing smack, usually on nights you'd logged as engine trouble. I'm sorry. For all of it. For the lying and the using and the eleven years of bread I brought you partly out of love and partly because a woman carrying a loaf is invisible, and I needed to be invisible, and you never once made me feel used." She swallowed. "That's not true either. Some weeks I felt used down to the bone. But you never made me feel it. That was Maren's doing too. She told me once: *Orsen gives without counting, and that's rarer than courage.*"

Outside, full dark had come down, and the beam swung its slow white arm across the water, once, twice, steady as a promise. Somewhere below, the sea was gathering itself for the storm that would arrive tomorrow or the day after, and the air in the lantern room smelled of oil and cold brass and the loaf of bread going stale on the shelf between them.

Orsen lowered his hand. His eyes were red-rimmed and dry, and they fixed on her with a new steadiness that frightened her more than anger would have.

"The ledger Veyra has," he said. "The real one. The names as they actually happened."

"Hobbett surrendered the official copy. What she's striking through now are the entries I garbled—the false spellings, the wrong dates, the drowned who aren't drowned. She thinks she's cleaning up sloppy record-keeping." Rosalind's mouth twisted. "She isn't wrong to strike them. Those entries were traps, not truths. But buried inside the errors is the true sequence, and only two people alive can read it. One is me."

"And the other?"

"Is dead, and taught me the cipher when I was nine years old, and I have never been more grateful to her than I am tonight." Rosalind stood. Her legs held. "Veyra's historians will have a clean version drafted within the month. A Saltmere of loyal fishermen and honest drownings, where nobody fled, nobody hid anybody, and the resistance that ran sixty-two people to safety in eleven years is a rumor the war invented. Once their version is bound and sealed, ours is a madwoman's scribbling. After that, there is no ours. There's only theirs, forever."

"So copy it." He said it simply, as if she had asked him to pass the salt. "Copy the true ledger. Here, at night, in the light."

Rosalind stared at him. Eleven rehearsals, and not one of them had included him agreeing before she finished asking.

"It's not that simple. If they find us—"

"They won't find you copying in my lighthouse any faster than they found ninety-one refugees sleeping under my roof." Something moved in his face that was not quite a smile but was adjacent to one, worn thin and strange, like a coin rubbed smooth. "I have been invisible for eleven years because my wife and my wife's sister decided invisibility was the safest thing for everyone. And it kept people alive—I'm not ungrateful, Rosalind, don't mistake me, I'd have done the same in your place and worse in hers. But they burned four pages yesterday. Four. Old Tam Wetherall's daughters were on one of them, and Tam stood at the harbor wall this morning like a man reading his own obituary, because officially his girls drowned, and the paper says so, and the paper is the only thing left of them."

He rose. He crossed to the shelf, took down the loaf, and tore it in half with his hands, and handed her one piece as though they were breaking bread at a table instead of standing in a tower above a black sea with an army in the town below.

"I'm done being the shadow you both hid in," Orsen said. "Tell me what you need me to write."

Rosalind took the bread. It was warm still, from the oven she had filled for eleven years with secrets, and it tasted like nothing so much as forgiveness, and it nearly undid her.

"There's a problem," she said, when she could speak. "The original entries. The cipher key, the route maps, the fund accounts—they were never in the parish ledger. They were never anywhere Veyra could burn. Maren kept a second book. The true ledger, the whole of it, routes and names and dates and the money, in her own hand." She paused, and the wind pushed against the glass, and somewhere far below a shutter banged in the keeper's cottage. "And after the funeral, when I went to collect it from wherever she'd hidden it, it wasn't where I looked. It wasn't in any of the places we'd agreed. She moved it, Orsen. Near the end, she must have known she was dying, and she moved it, and she never told either of us where."

Orsen had gone very still. The kind of stillness she had seen in him only once before, the day they pulled Maren's boat out of the shallows.

"She wouldn't hide it from us," he said slowly. "Not from you. Unless—" His eyes lifted, past Rosalind's shoulder, up into the great turning lens above them, where the flame stood in its cage of glass and brass, throwing its light out over the water in a wheel of white. "Unless she hid it where she knew you'd never think to look, because you'd think it was too obvious. Too dangerous. Too much like her husband."

Rosalind turned to follow his gaze, and the realization came over her like cold water rising—slow, then all at once, filling her boots, her chest, her throat.

The lantern room. The one place in Saltmere no searcher had ever opened, guarded by a boring man and a burning light.

"Orsen," she whispered. "When did you last go through her things in the oil store?"

* * *

They went down together, the ninety-three steps two at a time in the dark, Rosalind's candle throwing their shadows huge and wavering up the spiral wall. Below, the oil store smelled of tallow and tar and eleven years of ordinary life. Orsen lit the lamp on the workbench with hands that were not quite steady, and they pulled out the crates together—her sister's winter coat, folded and kept; the box of letters tied in ribbon; the spare wicks, the spare glass, the tin of tools.

It was Rosalind who found it, because Rosalind knew her sister's hands. Inside the hollowed-out base of the spare lamp housing—inside the *lamp*, of course, inside the light itself, where else, where *else*, you sentimental impossible woman—wrapped in oilcloth and waxed string, lay a book.

It was smaller than the parish ledger, thicker, bound in grey sailcloth gone soft with handling. Maren's initials were burned into the cover, small, near the corner, the way a fisherman marks gear he expects to lose and hopes to get back.

Rosalind held it and could not open it. Eleven years of rehearsal, and her hands shook like a girl's.

Orsen stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him, and did not touch the book, and did not hurry her. Outside, the first gusts of the coming storm walked down the cliff and rattled the shutters, and far overhead, through the floorboards, the great lens turned its patient wheel of light.

"Tomorrow night," Rosalind said at last, her voice rough. "We start copying tomorrow night. Both books. Every name. Before Veyra's historians write us out of the world."

Above them, faint through the ceiling, the lamp turned—and turned—and turned.
