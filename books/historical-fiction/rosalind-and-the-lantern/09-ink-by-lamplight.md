## 9. Ink by Lamplight

The first night, we nearly ruined everything with caution.

Rosalind came up the lighthouse stair after midnight, moving in that way she had learned during the war years—heel to toe, weight rolled forward, silent as smoke. She carried the true ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a child, and when she set it on the chart table beneath the great lamp, her hands were shaking.

"Only an hour," she whispered. "If anyone sees light—"

"The beam turns every seven and a half seconds." I struck a match and touched it to the small spirit lamp I kept for soldering wicks. Its flame was no bigger than a thumbnail. "No one watching from the town can distinguish one more light in this room. The whole lantern is a blaze. That's the trick of it, Rosalind. We don't need darkness. We need only to look like what we are."

She looked at me then—really looked, the way she had not since Maren died—and something in her face eased, just slightly, the way a rope eases when you take half its weight onto your own shoulder.

"All right," she said. "Show me how a keeper works."

I unwrapped her ledger and laid mine beside it. Two books on one table: the parish record Veyra's clerks had returned to me with their red strikes through the names of the saved, and the older book, the one Rosalind had kept since before the first storm season, written in her own hand in a cipher of her own devising—numbers standing for boats, flowers for families, tides for dates. It had taken us three evenings of ordinary daylight, sitting in the cellar among the oil drums while I pretended to inventory stores, for her to teach me the code. Three evenings of her voice low and steady in the dark, saying *the jasmine means the Halloway family, two adults, three children; the jasmine crossed out but not erased means they reached Corren's Head alive.*

Now, by lamplight, we began the translation into plain ink.

*Halloway, Tomas. Wife, Edith. Children: Bram, Susanna, little Nell.* My pen moved across the page and each name landed like a stone dropped into still water. On the parish ledger beside me, those same names bore their red lines—the clerks' neat, bureaucratic verdicts: *unreliable entry. No corroborating record.*

"They drowned," Rosalind said quietly, watching me write. "That's what the struck line says. Not *unreliable*. Drowned. The victors' historians will read it as drowned."

"Then we make them un-drown them." I did not look up from the page. "Ink is patient. So am I."

* * *

We fell into a rhythm over the following nights, and the rhythm became the shape of our days.

Rosalind arrived between the second and third hours after midnight, always by different paths—once along the cliff track through the gorse, once up from the boat cove where the rocks hid her from the harbor watch, once, daringly, straight down the high street past the billeted soldiers' sentry post, because she said fear was a habit and she refused to feed it. She would knock twice, pause, once more—a signal older than the war, one Maren had used bringing bread to smugglers before either of us understood what the bread was buying—and I would let her into the stair.

Up we climbed, ninety-three steps, past the oil store where the copied pages now lived inside a false-bottomed drum marked *whale oil, spoiled*, past the watch room with its logbooks and barometers, up into the lantern itself, where the great Fresnel lens turned its slow wheel of light above us and the wind spoke in the glass all around.

The work itself was simple. Rosalind read her cipher aloud, entry by entry, and I wrote. But nothing about it felt simple. Every name required a decision. Was Edith Halloway *alive at last report* or *confirmed safe in Corren's Head*? Had the Penhale brothers gone out on the *Kestrel* or been put ashore at the smugglers' steps before she sailed? The difference between one phrase and another might be the difference, someday, between a widow drawing her husband's pension and a widow being told her husband never existed.

"You write like a clerk now," Rosalind said on the fourth night, watching my hand. "Maren used to say you had the neatest figures on the coast. She'd be proud."

"She'd be furious," I said. "She told me once, the year we married, that the safest life was the one nobody wrote down. No debts recorded, no quarrels entered, no name in any book worth burning."

"And you believed her?"

"I built my whole life on it." I dipped the pen. "Twenty years of keeping my head below the parapet. And here I sit, committing treason by lamplight, because some captain in a grey coat decided my neighbors' deaths were a clerical error."

Rosalind was quiet a moment. The lens turned overhead, throwing its long blade of light across the sea, across the town, across us, again and again, like a slow heartbeat.

"Maren knew," she said. "About the routes. From the beginning."

I set down the pen.

"I know," I said. "You told me in the cellar."

"I told you the outline. Not the whole of it." Her hands were flat on the table, steadying herself or the ledger or both. "Orsen, she didn't just know. She funded it. The money that bought the boats at the start—that was hers. The insurance settlement after the *Amberdale Girl* went down. Every coin. And the house in Saltmere with the loose slate over the cellar door, the one they searched in October—that was rented in your name because she asked me to use it, and I let her, and I have hated myself for it every hour since the soldiers kicked that door in."

The wind pressed against the glass. Below us the town lay dark except for the requisition office, where Captain Veyra kept late hours; her window glowed on the hill like a second, lesser lantern.

"Why are you telling me this now?" I asked.

"Because you're writing these people back into existence, and you deserve to know what it cost your wife. Because if they take you—if it ever comes to that—you should know exactly what you're protecting." Her voice did not break. It went quieter, which was worse. "And because lying to you has started to feel like drowning, and I've spent enough nights underwater to know the feeling."

I looked at her—this woman who had slept in my cellar and eaten my bread and answered my careful questions with careful answers for two years—and I found, searching myself, that there was no anger waiting. Only a strange, cold clarity, like the moment a wave passes over you and you see the surface from underneath.

"She chose it," I said at last. "Maren chose. You know better than anyone she was never a woman who could be talked out of a thing once her mind was made." I picked up the pen. "So we finish it. For her sake too. She paid the first installment; we'll pay the rest."

Rosalind exhaled, long and slow, like someone surfacing.

"Together, then," she said. It was not quite a question. It was not quite anything. But when she bent again over her cipher, her shoulder rested near mine, and neither of us moved away.

* * *

The nights taught me things about her I had never known in all the years she had been my sister-in-law and occasional guest.

I learned that she counted under her breath when she was anxious—not numbers but names, running through her network like a housewife checking her pantry: *Tamsin, Hedley, the Marchant girls, old Cobb, the ferryman's boy.* I learned that she could not abide the sound of boots on stone, and that when the sentries passed below the tower on their rounds, she would go absolutely still, pen suspended, until the footsteps faded, and only then would her breath come out of her in a thin thread.

And she, in turn, learned me. She learned that I could not write a name without speaking it aloud first, a habit from the parish copying, so that the dead got their syllables even if they got nothing else. She learned that my hand cramped after an hour and that she could work the cramp out of my fingers with her thumbs, firm, unsentimental, the way one works a kink out of rigging—and that this practical ministration left me, embarrassingly, light-headed.

On the ninth night, we argued.

It was over the Marchant girls. Rosalind wanted them listed as *smuggled to safety, route three*, full detail, dates and landings, the complete truth. I wanted them listed as *departed Saltmere, destination unknown*, because route three ran through the Fenwick farm, and the Fenwicks were still there, still billeted upon, still one informer's whisper away from a cell.

"If the ledger is seized—" I began.

"It won't be."

"If it is, Rosalind, every name attached to a route becomes evidence. We're not just recording the rescued. We'd be recording the rescuers. The Fenwicks. Tamsin. The ferryman's boy. All of them, itemized for whatever tribunal comes next."

"And if we blur it, we lose the proof!" She stood, and the turning light swept across her face, lit her, shadowed her, lit her again. "Don't you see, that's the whole battle? Veyra doesn't fear rumors. She fears *corroboration*. Dates, landings, witnesses. If we write vague, she wins by default, because vague is what her struck-through ledger already says. We have to be exact, Orsen. Exactness is the only weapon we have that she doesn't."

"And it's a weapon that cuts everyone holding it!"

The words rang in the glass. Above us the lamp hissed, indifferent. Rosalind stared at me, and I watched the anger drain out of her face and leave behind something rawer.

"Yes," she said softly. "It does. It cut Maren. It may cut us. That's what I've been trying to tell you since the night I brought the ledger up these stairs." She sat back down, slowly, and put her hand flat on the open page. "Every name in this book is a person who took a risk for a stranger. I won't erase their courage to keep them comfortable. They weren't comfortable. They were brave. Write it brave."

I looked at her hand on the page—at the ink stains on her fingers now, matching mine, earned over nine nights of theft from the dark.

Then I wrote: *Smuggled to safety via route three, the twelfth of Stormmonth. Conducted by T. Fenwick. Landed Corren's Head, all living.*

"Brave," I said.

She laughed—actually laughed, short and startled, the first laugh I'd heard from her since the war began—and the sound went through the lantern room and out through the glass into the dark like something freed.

* * *

There were nights when the tenderness crept in despite both of us.

Not announced. Not decided. It came the way the tide comes, in increments too small to protest. It came in the way she had begun to bring things other than bread—on the eleventh night, a jar of honey from a farm upcoast, and she watched me eat it with an expression I remembered from twenty years ago, from a kitchen where three people had been young together. It came in the fact that she had stopped sitting across the table and now sat beside it, close enough that our sleeves touched, and neither of us remarked on it, because remarking would have required deciding something, and neither of us was ready to decide.

Once, near dawn of the thirteenth night, she fell asleep mid-sentence. Her head came down onto my shoulder as naturally as fruit falling, and I sat without moving for the better part of an hour, my pen idle, the copied ledger open, the light wheeling above us, and I thought: *Maren would have laughed herself sick.* And then I thought: *Maren would have locked the door from the outside and thrown away the key, if throwing it away meant the two of us finally stopped being careful.*

When Rosalind woke, she did not apologize and did not move away at once. She blinked at the page, found her place, and said, "Where was I?"

"The Penhale brothers," I said. "Out on the *Kestrel*, or ashore at the steps?"

"Ashore." She rubbed her eyes. "Always ashore. The *Kestrel* was lost with all hands a week later; if they'd sailed, they'd be in the drowned column." She paused. "Write that too, when you get there. That the sea almost got them anyway. People should know luck isn't a moral quality."

I wrote it. *Ashore at the smugglers' steps, by the grace of God and R. Marchetti's network, and so spared the wreck of the Kestrel.*

"By the grace of R. Marchetti," she read aloud, when she saw it. "You've named me."

"Somebody has to be named," I said. "That's rather the point of the exercise."

* * *

But the world outside the glass was not standing still while we wrote.

On the fourteenth night, coming up the stair, Rosalind stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into her. She held up one hand, listening. Below us, faint through ninety feet of stone, I heard it too: voices in the yard. More than usual. And the particular crisp cadence I had learned to dread—the voice giving orders in complete sentences, unhurried, thorough.

Veyra.

Rosalind's eyes found mine in the dark. "At midnight?" she breathed. "What business does she have at the lighthouse at midnight?"

"Perhaps none. Perhaps a walk." But we both knew the requisition office window had been burning later and later, night after night, and we both knew what an officer does with late nights and a stubborn problem: she walks the ground of it. And the ground of our whole enterprise stood directly beneath her feet.

We stood frozen on the stair, the true ledger heavy in its oilcloth against Rosalind's chest, ninety-three steps of stone between us and the yard, and listened to Captain Veyra's boots come to a stop directly below us—directly below the oil store, directly below the false-bottomed drum with fourteen nights of treason sleeping inside it—and heard her say, clearly, to the sentry at her shoulder:

"Interesting, isn't it. A keeper who trims his wicks to the minute, logs his weather like an astronomer—and yet." A pause. I could hear the scratch of her pencil, even through the stone. She was making a note. She was always making a note. "Yet the oil consumption doesn't match the log. He's burning more than he records. Find out what he's burning."
