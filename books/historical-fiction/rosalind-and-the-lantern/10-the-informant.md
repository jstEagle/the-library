## 10. The Informant

The requisition office in Saltmere had once been a counting house for herring merchants, and it still smelled faintly of brine and old money. Captain Elara Veyra had come to appreciate the smell. It was honest. Fish and ledgers did not lie about what they were.

She sat at the merchant's desk—too large for her, built for a man who had grown fat on the herring trade—and worked by lamplight because the requisition office kept army hours and army hours began before dawn. Outside the salt-fogged window, the harbor was a grey suggestion of itself: masts like burnt matches, the breakwater a long dark scar, and beyond it all the lighthouse standing on its knuckle of rock with its lantern dark, as regulations required between dawn and the evening watch.

Dark. She had checked it that morning, as she checked it every morning now, without quite admitting to herself why. A habit, she told herself. Thoroughness. The same thoroughness that had her rising at four to cross-reference the third draft of *A Civil History of Saltmere and Its Environs During the Storm Season* against the parish ledger and eleven informant statements.

Thoroughness was the only virtue the army had left her after the university took back her post. She intended to be virtuous.

* * *

The knock came at half past six, which meant it was either the quartermaster or trouble, and the quartermaster knocked twice.

"Come."

Sergeant Hallowes entered with the particular stiffness of a man carrying news he'd rather have been spared. He was a good soldier in the way of men who followed orders and felt each one land somewhere in his chest; Veyra valued him precisely because he was not comfortable here. Comfortable officers wrote sloppy histories.

"Captain. There's a man at the door says he has information." Hallowes paused, choosing his words with visible care. "He won't give his name. He's asking for payment in advance."

"Payment for what?"

"He wouldn't say. Only that it concerns—" Hallowes lowered his voice as though the walls of the counting house might still be loyal to the town, "—the lighthouse."

Veyra set down her pen. The nib left a small black star on the margin of her draft, next to a paragraph she'd been struggling with since midnight—a paragraph about the fishing fleet's losses, which refused to reconcile with the ledger's numbers no matter how many times she ran the arithmetic. Eleven boats lost, by every account. But the ledger recorded nine drowned among their crews, and eleven boats could not sink with only nine men between them unless some of those men had never been aboard, or had gone ashore elsewhere, or—

Or unless the ledger was wrong. Or unless something else was true, and the truth had been walking past her office every morning in oilskins.

"Show him in," she said. "And stay. If he asks you to leave, tell him I don't conduct business alone with men who won't give their names."

* * *

He was a crab fisherman, or had the hands for it—knuckles swollen, nails split, a smell of bait and tar that preceded him into the room like a dog. Middle-aged, weathered to the color of the breakwater stones. He would not sit when offered a chair. He stood with his cap in his hands and his eyes moving over the room: the ledgers stacked on the shelf, the map of the coast pinned above the hearth, the lamp, the door behind Hallowes.

"You're paying," he said. Not a question.

"The Crown pays for verified information," Veyra said. "I pay for accurate information. Verified comes first. Tell me what you have and how you know it, and we'll see what it's worth."

His jaw worked. He looked at Hallowes, then away, then made his decision the way men of the coast seemed to make all decisions—quickly, with the whole body, like jumping.

"There was light in the lighthouse last night," he said. "Past two bells of the morning watch. And the night before that too. I saw it from my boat. I moor past the point sometimes, when the wind's wrong for my nets." His eyes flicked up, defiant, daring her to press the point of why a fisherman would be anchored in a cove that was, by regulation, closed to civilian traffic. She let it pass. One secret traded for another; that was how informants were made.

"The lighthouse is ordered dark after the evening watch except in declared storm conditions," Veyra said carefully. "There was no storm declaration Tuesday. Or Wednesday."

"No, ma'am."

"And you're certain it was the lantern room. Not a reflection. Not the moon off the glass."

"I've watched that light my whole life, ma'am." Something almost offended entered his voice. "I can tell you what oil they burn by the color. It wasn't the beacon—it was low, steady, yellow. Lamp-oil light. Like a room lit below the great lens." He swallowed. "Like someone working late by a desk lamp."

Veyra looked at him for a long moment.

Someone working late. The phrase settled into her mind and lay there, cold and precise as a scalpel.

Smugglers did not work late by lamplight. Smugglers moved cargo in the dark and doused everything that burned; light was death to a smuggler. Whatever happened in the lantern room at two in the morning, it was being done by people who needed to see what they were doing. People handling paper, perhaps. People reading. People writing.

"Who keeps the lighthouse?" she asked, though she knew. She wanted to hear him say it.

"Amberdale. Orsen Amberdale. Widow-man. Keeps to himself." The fisherman hesitated, and then, because informants always gave one thing more than they intended, one thing that cost them: "There's a woman goes up there some evenings. Dark hair. Comes with bread, most days. Folks say she's his wife's sister." He shrugged. "Folks say a lot of things."

"What do folks say about her?"

Another hesitation. Longer this time. She watched him weigh loyalty against coin and watched the coin win, and felt nothing in particular about it—that was the part of this work she had stopped apologizing to herself for, the nothing. In her first month she had lain awake feeling the weight of every name she bought. Now she filed them.

"Folks say she came from nowhere," he said. "Say she turned up after the war started and nobody'd ever heard of her before. Say Amberdale vouched for her and that was that, because nobody crosses the widow-man, he buried half the town's dead with his own hands." He turned his cap another rotation. "Folks say she asks questions. About who got out. Who went where."

Veyra's pen was already moving. She hadn't decided to write; her hand had simply begun, the way it did when the historian in her overtook the officer. *Female, unknown provenance, sister-in-law to subject A (Amberdale, O., keeper). Arrived post-war-start. Queries re: displaced persons.*

"How much?" the fisherman asked.

She named a figure that would keep him coming back and not so much that he'd start inventing. He took it, counted it twice, and left with the particular gait of a man who has crossed a line and is trying to walk as though the ground hasn't changed under him.

When the door closed, Hallowes cleared his throat. "You believe him?"

"Every word. That's what worries me." Veyra stood and walked to the map. Her finger found the lighthouse, the cove beyond the point, the channel. "A smuggler reports lights. A smuggler would know better than to report them—he'd be reporting himself, if the cove's his landing spot." She traced the coastline south. "No. This is a fisherman selling a fisherman. Which means whatever happens in that lighthouse at two in the morning isn't smuggling of goods."

"What else is there to smuggle, Captain?"

Veyra looked at the shelf. At the parish ledger sitting on top of the stack, its spine cracked, its pages softened by three months of her handling. Nine names struck through in red ink. *Unreliable.* Nine entries that refused to reconcile with eleven lost boats, with the testimony of the harbormaster, with the arithmetic that had kept her awake until the lamp burned down.

Paper, she thought. You could smuggle paper. You could smuggle truth, if you were fool enough to think truth weighed anything, or brave enough not to care.

"We're going to find out," she said.

* * *

She did not go to the lighthouse that day. That was the discipline of it—the part of the work she had learned from her old professor, God rot him, who had taught her that an investigation announced was an investigation ruined. Instead she spent the morning doing what she did best: reading.

She pulled the billeting reports. The lighthouse was exempt from billeting—operational structure, single occupant, army use of the light during storms—and so no soldiers had ever stood inside it. No searches. The cellar had never been inventoried. The oil store had never been counted.

Then she pulled the informant files again, all eleven of them, and laid them across the merchant's great desk like a hand of cards, and played the game she was beginning to suspect the town was playing against her.

Statement four: the cooper's wife reported that Amberdale had asked after a family name—the Drells, lost off the Gannet rock in the second storm—for no reason he would give. Statement seven: a boy hired to carry oil to the lighthouse said the cellar smelled wrong, "like ink and tallow mixed," which he'd thought nothing of, boys thinking nothing of anything, but which Veyra thought a great deal of. Statement nine: the harbormaster's clerk, her best source, noted that Rosalind—she had the name now; it surfaced in a billeting dispute, *Rosalind Amberdale, household of O. Amberdale*, a name that appeared in no record earlier than eighteen months ago, no baptismal entry, no marriage line, no arrival manifest, a woman who had stepped onto the page fully formed like a character in a bad novel—had been seen at the harbor on the night of the third storm, talking to boatmen, handing something small and flat from sleeve to sleeve.

Flat. Paper-shaped.

And statement eleven, the newest, from a laundress who washed for the requisition office itself and was paid more than any of the others precisely because she saw everything: that Captain Veyra's own returned ledger had caused talk in the town. That families with struck-through names had come to the clerk's widow and asked to see the red lines. That they had stood in the widow's kitchen, six or seven at a time, looking at the word *unreliable* written beside their fathers and sons, and had said nothing—but had looked at one another afterward in a way the laundress found hard to describe.

"They look like people memorizing something," was how she'd finally put it.

Veyra sat back in the merchant's chair, which groaned, and regarded the ceiling beams, and admitted the shape of the thing to herself for the first time.

They weren't hiding from her. They were copying from her.

Every document she demanded, every review she conducted, every name she struck—they treated her bureaucracy not as a wall but as a template. They were building a counter-record. The lighthouse burns at two in the morning because someone is writing. The woman with no history dictates. The keeper writes—he had clerk's hands; she'd noticed at their first meeting, noticed the ink stain on his right middle finger and filed it under *lighthouse maintenance*, a filing error she now corrected with a small, bitter pleasure. And the town, instead of breaking under the red lines, was gathering around them like a congregation around a banned book.

It was elegant. It was, she thought with a discomfort she declined to examine, admirable.

And it meant that somewhere in Saltmere there existed—or was being created—a second ledger. An alternative history. The losing side's version, written in secret, by lamplight, in the one building in the town her authority had never entered.

* * *

In the afternoon she walked the harbor alone, which she did sometimes, unescorted, because a captain who moved predictably learned nothing. Gulls screamed over the gutting tables. Nets hung drying like the rigging of ghost ships. And everywhere, the looks: quick, level, measuring, gone the instant she returned them. Three months ago the looks had been empty—shock-numbed, war-tired, the gazes of people waiting for the next bad thing. Now they were not empty. Now there was something being held behind them, communally, the way a town holds a secret the size of a coffin.

They know, she thought. Most of them don't know what, but they know something is being kept for them. That was the difference. Fear made people scattered; hope made them organized. Somewhere in the last weeks, Saltmere had quietly become organized.

At the end of the quay she stopped and looked up at the lighthouse. White tower, black gallery, the great lens sleeping behind its panes. Smoke rose thin from the kitchen vent—bread baking, or a kettle. Ordinary. Everything about it was ordinary, and she had learned in three years of requisition work that ordinary was the costume secrecy wore best.

The keeper came out while she stood there. Of course he did; he'd seen her from the gallery, or heard her boots on the stone. Orsen Amberdale descended the outer stair with the unhurried tread of a man whose legs knew every step in the dark, and stood at the bottom with his hands at his sides, and waited.

"Captain."

"Keeper." She gestured at the tower. "Fuel consumption reports are due monthly. I haven't received yours since September."

Something moved behind his eyes—small, controlled, gone fast. A man hearing a trap described in administrative language.

"Storm season," he said. "Burn rates vary. I keep my own figures. I can bring them round Friday."

"Do." She smiled, and made it the polite, bloodless smile she used for inspections. "I'm compiling material for the civil history. The lighthouse should feature. Its keeper especially." She paused, watching his face the way she watched documents, for the alteration beneath the surface. "History favors the well-documented, Mr. Amberdale. I've often thought the survivors of a place owe their dead a good accounting. Don't you?"

For a moment—one breath, no more—something came up through the man's careful blankness like a fish rising under still water. Grief, or anger, or both braided together. Then it sank.

"Aye," he said quietly. "That's just what I've been thinking, Captain."

He held her gaze a beat longer than a frightened man would dare, and less than a guilty one would need, and turned back up the stair.

Veyra stood on the quay a while after he'd gone, the wind pulling at her coat, and felt the net close around her own thinking, tighter and tighter, toward a center she could almost see. Light burning at two in the morning. Ink-smelling cellars. A woman with no past dictating a future. Names passed hand to flat hand. A town memorizing itself.

Not smuggling, then. Worse than smuggling. Scholarship.

She turned back toward the counting house, and her stride lengthened, and behind her the lighthouse stood white against the grey sky, patient as a witness.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would request the lighthouse fuel stores for audit. Tomorrow she would inventory the cellar and count the oil.

Tonight, she would finish the chapter on the fishing fleet. Eleven boats. Nine names. Two men unaccounted for, alive somewhere, or dead somewhere unwritten—and somewhere in Saltmere, she would have wagered her commission, a second ledger already knew which.

The first storm warnings of the season's end were posted at the harbor office by the time she reached the door. She read them twice.

Barometer falling fast. Gale expected within two days.

She almost smiled. Storms brought ships in, drove ships onto rocks, filled ledgers with new names. Storms gave everyone an excuse.

Whatever the lighthouse was doing in the dark, she would see it done in daylight soon enough—or she would catch it burning, and burn brighter, in the worst weather the sea could send.

She went inside to sharpen her pen.
