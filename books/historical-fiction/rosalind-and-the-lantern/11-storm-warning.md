## 11. Storm Warning

The barometer began falling on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the glass had dropped so far that Orsen Amberdale tapped it twice with one knuckle, the way a man taps a mute child, as if to say: are you certain? It was certain. The needle lay flat against the stop pin like a thing dead, and outside the lantern room the sky had gone the color of pewter scraped raw.

He had kept this light for nineteen years. He knew storms the way other men knew their wives' tempers — by the small signs that came before the large ones. The gulls had gone inland two days early. The sea had turned an oily green-black in the deep water past the point, and the swell was coming in long and silent from the southeast, wave after wave with no wind behind them yet, which meant the wind was still out there somewhere, gathering itself like a debt being called in.

By Thursday noon the wind arrived.

It came off the sea in the first hard gusts of the season's worst blow, flattening the coarse grass on the headland, tearing the last leaves from the stunted elms along the harbor road, and driving sheets of spume so far inland they salted the windows of houses three streets up from the quay. Orsen stood at the gallery rail and felt the tower shudder under him, a slow groan through the stone, familiar as his own pulse. Below, Saltmere was pulling in its boats. He could see them from up here — small dark shapes hauling at capstans, dragging hulls above the tideline, women carrying children and hens indoors with that bent-shouldered hurry that meant everyone had counted the hours and knew what was coming.

The fishing fleet would not go out. No sensible skipper would put to sea tonight. But the light did not exist only for sensible men in home waters. Somewhere out there in that gathering black were coasters running ahead of the storm from the northern ports, trawlers caught too far out to make harbor before it broke, maybe a naval packet, maybe worse — boats that would come feeling their way down this coast in the dark with the sea breaking white over the Teeth, those black rocks a half mile offshore that had drowned more Saltmere men than all its wars combined.

The light had to burn. That was the whole of his life, distilled: the light had to burn.

He went down the hundred and six steps to make his preparations, and it was at the bottom, in the oil store where the copied ledger pages slept inside a false-bottomed crate under sacking, that he heard the boots on the causeway road.

* * *

There were four of them, grey coats plastered wet, and at their head Captain Veyra, who did not get wet because she wore an oilskin that actually fit her, which told Orsen something about how the requisition office spent its funds. She stood in the doorway of the lighthouse base shaking rain from her shoulders with the brisk economy of a woman who resented every minute weather cost her.

"Keeper." She did not use his name. In seven weeks she never once had. "I'll be brief, because I have a town to secure before nightfall. By order of the Provisional Administration, the Saltmere lighthouse is to remain unlit until further notice."

Orsen heard the words and understood them individually and could not make them into sense. "Unlit," he said.

"The coast is closed to hostile traffic. A lit beacon assists enemy vessels in navigating waters we control. Therefore the beacon goes dark. You will confirm this in writing."

"Captain." He kept his voice level, which took work; there was a tremor in his hands he pressed against his thighs. "There's a storm coming in from the southeast. The worst of the year, by the glass. There will be boats out in it — not navy boats. Fishing boats that got caught, coasters, God knows what. If they can't see the Teeth—"

"They should have thought of that before they put to sea."

"The sea doesn't ask their politics, Captain. The rocks don't either."

Something moved in Veyra's face — not softness, he'd seen enough of her to know she didn't keep any stock of that where he could see it — but the flicker of a scholar meeting an argument she considered beneath refutation. "This is not a moral question, Keeper. It is an administrative one. The order stands. Confirm it."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you obstruct a lawful requisition order in wartime, and you will be arrested, and the lamp will be dark anyway, and your obstruction will have achieved nothing except your own ruin." She said it without heat, the way she might recite a footnote. That was always the terrible thing about Veyra: she never threatened. She merely described consequences, accurately, as though reading them aloud from a text she trusted completely.

"I need to trim the wicks," Orsen said. "Maintain the mechanism. Even dark, the lens turns. If I leave it—" 

"Then maintain it. Dark." She paused at the door, and for a moment the rain roared loud in the gap, and she looked back at him with something almost like professional courtesy. "For what it's worth, Keeper, I read the tide tables this morning. I know what's coming. I also know that orders do not bend, and neither do I. Good evening."

They left. Orsen stood in the cold stairwell listening to the boots fade on the causeway, and then he climbed the hundred and six steps, slowly, one hand on the wall, thinking.

* * *

Rosalind was already there when he reached the lantern room. She came most evenings now with bread or oil or simply herself, and lately the copying had become their shared labor — her voice low and steady dictating names while his pen moved and the beam swept above them like a slow heartbeat. Tonight she stood at the south glass with her arms folded tight, watching the horizon disappear.

"He came," she said. Not a question. Word traveled in occupied towns faster than light.

"Veyra herself. The light goes dark by order."

"And you signed?"

"No."

She turned. In the grey afternoon gloom her face was pale and set, and older than her years, and he thought — as he had been thinking for weeks now, in the space between dictation and sleep — that grief had carved her into something both harder and finer than the girl Maren used to describe. "Orsen. Listen to me. They will arrest you. They told you they would arrest you, and you stood there and said no, and now they will come back with irons, and when they search this place they will find—"

"They won't find the pages. I'll move them tonight."

"That isn't the point." She crossed to him, and her voice dropped to the register she used for the network, the one that carried no further than the ear it was aimed at. "The point is that a dark lighthouse in this storm kills people. And a lit lighthouse saves them. And you cannot be here to light it if you're in the requisition cell under the harbor office. So think, husband-of-my-sister, think like a keeper and not like a martyr, because martyrs are cheap and keepers are scarce."

The endearment — Maren's old teasing name for him — landed in his chest like a coal. He looked at her a moment too long, then made himself turn to the lamp.

"There's another way," he said.

"Tell me."

"A dark lamp is an order about the lamp. Nobody ordered the town dark." He touched the great lens, cool even through the glove. "Every house on the headland has lamps. Every boat has a lantern. The fishermen know these waters blindfolded, but strangers don't, and strangers are the ones who'll break on the Teeth tonight. If the tower goes dark, fine. Let it go dark. But the light doesn't have to come from the tower."

Rosalind stared at him, and then he saw it land — saw the whole shape of it unfold behind her eyes, the way she saw routes unfolding, safe houses, tides, timing. "Bonfires," she said. "Beacon fires on the headland."

"Older than any lighthouse. Older than any army. You can't requisition a fire that fifty households lit from their own hearths. Whose order do they disobey, exactly? Whose door does Veyra kick in first?"

"She'll kick in all of them."

"Some. Not fifty. Not in a storm." He was pacing now, working it, the old arithmetic of the light reasserting itself after nineteen years of quiet. "But someone has to start it. Someone has to stand on the headland in that wind and light the first one, and be seen lighting it, so the others dare. And Rosalind — that someone can't be me. If I'm seen defying the order, I'm finished, and the copying stops, and everything we've done since October dies in a crate of lamp oil."

"So it has to be someone with nothing left to lose," she said quietly. "Someone the grey coats have never bothered to learn the name of."

"You," he said. Then, hearing it: "No. Not you. Rosalind, if they take you—"

"If they take me, the network holds. That's why I built it the way I built it — no name connects to more than two others. But if they take *you*, the record dies." She said it flatly, without drama, and it was somehow worse than drama. She pulled her hood up. "Get me dry kindling and a storm lantern from the store. The good one. And Orsen—" she stopped at the stairhead, and the wind screamed against the glass above them like something alive, "—light the lamp anyway. After midnight, when the storm's at its worst. Let it turn. One night of disobedience in a tower full of smoke and chaos — they'll suspect the storm knocked something loose, or that you lost your nerve, anything but the truth. Because the truth is that bonfires alone won't bring a stranger past the Teeth in that sea. The truth needs the real light. And so do I, tonight, to believe we're still the kind of people who keep it."

She was gone down the stairs before he could answer, which was, he suspected, the point.

* * *

The storm made landfall at dusk.

It came in the way the old pilots had warned each other about for generations — not gradually but all at once, as though a door had opened somewhere in the sky. The wind hit the tower with a force that made the stone hum, and the rain came sideways, and the sea stood up off the point in walls of green-black water that burst white against the cliff foot hard enough to shake grit from the ceiling of the oil store. Orsen worked through it by touch and habit: checking the fuel lines, the clockwork that turned the lens, the spare wicks laid ready, the shutters tested. Twice he went down and moved the ledger pages deeper — first into the empty pickle barrel, then, thinking better of it, into the double bottom of the oil crate again, wrapped in waxed canvas, because a barrel was the second place any searcher looked and the crate had held for seven weeks.

From the gallery, just after nine, he saw it: a spark on the headland. Small, guttering, nearly smothered by the rain — then fed, catching, rising. Rosalind's silhouette bent over it, hood whipping, feeding wood with the patience of someone who had waited out worse things than weather. Then a second fire, west of the first — the Tullivers, he guessed, or the Brandt brothers. A third. A fourth, down by the fish-curing sheds, dangerously close to the timber. By half past nine there were a dozen fires burning along the headland like a string of defiant orange beads, and figures moving between them, and somewhere below he could hear the town — actual voices, carried up in the gaps of the wind, shouting encouragement or defiance or simply names, he couldn't tell.

At ten o'clock the grey coats came for the fires. He watched the lantern-light of a patrol moving along the ridge, watched them douse two of the flames, watched the crowd close around the third like water closing over a stone, and when the patrol moved on the third was burning again before they'd gone thirty yards. You cannot arrest a whole town in a storm. Veyra would understand that better than anyone; she had probably already written it down somewhere.

And at quarter past eleven, with the storm screaming at its full fury and the sea coming over the top of the cliff itself in white explosions, Orsen Amberdale stood alone in the lantern room and struck the match.

* * *

The wick took on the first strike, the way it always did, faithful as a dog. He trimmed it, seated the chimney, opened the fuel valve, and wound the clockwork, and the great lens gathered the flame and threw it out across the drowning world — one revolution every eight seconds, the beam sweeping the black water like the hand of a clock counting down toward morning. He stood back and watched it turn, and felt the old peace come over him, the peace that had nothing to do with safety and everything to do with doing the one thing he knew how to do.

Through the south glass, past the turning light, he could see the headland fires still burning. Through the west glass, far out, a smudge of movement — a sail, or the ghost of one, running for shelter with the seas breaking over her. Toward the light. Toward home.

He did not see the lantern at the foot of the tower until it was nearly at the door — two lanterns, rather, bobbing up the causeway through the deluge, and behind them the grey bulk of soldiers, six of them this time, and at their center a figure in a fitted oilskin who stopped at the base of his tower and looked up, all the way up, at the turning beam.

Even at this distance, even through the rain, Orsen felt the look like a physical weight.

Veyra had come herself.

She stood there a long moment — he could see her clearly in the lightning flashes, one after another, freezing her into a series of still portraits: face upturned, unreadable, rain streaming off the brim of her hood. Then she raised one arm and pointed, not at the lamp, not at the fires, but directly up at the gallery where he stood, and shouted something the wind tore apart before it reached him. The soldiers started up the causeway stairs.

Orsen looked at the lamp turning faithfully above him, at the distant sail crawling toward the light, at the fires on the headland, and then at the stairwell door, where the wind-driven rain was already blowing through the gap like smoke.

A hundred and six steps between them, and a night's worth of storm, and whatever Veyra had decided he was worth.

He barred the door.
