## 12. The Light That Would Not Go Out

The storm came in from the southwest like something that had been promised and had finally arrived to collect.

Rosalind heard it before she saw it—a long, low groan out at sea, the sound the water made when it gathered itself to break everything. She was in the lighthouse cellar with the copied pages, wrapping them in oilcloth for the hundredth time, when the first gust struck the tower and the whole structure shuddered like a struck bell. Dust sifted down from the joists. Somewhere above, the shutters she and Orsen had battened that morning groaned against their frames.

She climbed the cellar stair two steps at a time.

Orsen stood at the lantern room window, one hand braced against the glass, watching the harbor. Even in the dark she could see his face had gone the color of the foam below.

"How bad?" she asked.

"Bad." He said it the way a man says a diagnosis he has known is coming. "The *Merrow* went out at dawn with the Halloway boat and the *Sweet Betsey*. Old Tam swore the window would hold. I told him. I told him the barometer's been falling since Tuesday."

"Can they run for it now?"

"Too late to make the outer anchorage. If they try to come home in this, they'll need the light." He turned to her, and in the lamplight his eyes were the eyes of a man standing at a crossroads he had been walking toward all year. "And the army ordered it dark."

The order had come at noon, delivered by a wet, apologetic corporal: by command of Captain Veyra, the Saltmere light would be extinguished at nightfall. No signals to be shown. No communications with any vessel at sea. The lighthouse would keep its lamp cold until further notice. The corporal had not met Rosalind's eyes when he said it. Soldiers rarely did, when the order was one they were ashamed of.

"They think we're signaling the resistance," Rosalind said. "They'd rather let three boats drown than risk a lamp being seen."

"I know what they'd rather."

"Orsen." She took his arm. His sleeve was damp and cold. "If that light stays dark and those boats come in blind, they'll go onto the Teeth. All three of them. Every man on them. And the town will watch it happen from the seawall with the army standing in their way, and they will never—*never*—forgive the victors for it. Or forgive us for obeying."

"I know," he said again. And then, quietly, in the voice of a man setting down a weight he had carried for eleven years: "I've kept my head down my whole life, Ros. Since Maren died, since before. I kept it down because it was easier, and because I told myself it was safer, and because the dead don't need anything from a coward." He looked up the spiral stair, toward the lamp room where he had spent thirty years trimming wicks and polishing glass. "But they're going to need the light."

Rosalind felt something in her chest turn over—not fear, or not only fear. It was the feeling she had learned to know in the war years, the feeling that came just before she committed to something that could not be undone. The moment the route opened and you stepped onto it.

"Then we light it," she said.

* * *

They worked fast, because there was no time for anything else.

Orsen went up the stair with the oil can on his shoulder like a man shouldering a coffin, and Rosalind followed with the matches and the spare mantles. The lantern room was a cage of glass at the top of the storm; the wind hit it in fists, and the whole tower leaned and groaned and leaned again, and through the glass the sea was a churning black waste stitched with white.

Orsen filled the reservoir with hands that did not shake, though Rosalind could see the effort it cost him. He trimmed the wick. He checked the clockwork that turned the lens, that great slow eye of brass and crystal, and gave the winding crank six hard turns.

"Veyra will come," Rosalind said. She was watching the harbor road through the rain-streaked glass. "The moment they see the light, she'll come."

"I expect so."

"She'll bring soldiers. Orsen, she'll bring twenty."

"Then you'd best mind the lamp," he said, "and let me mind the door."

He struck the match.

The wick caught, flared, steadied. Orsen turned the feed valve and the flame rose, and the lens took it up and turned it, and the great beam swung out over the water—once, twice, three times a minute, the way it had swung every storm-season night for a hundred years before the army came and would swing a hundred years after they left, if there was anyone left to trim the wick.

Rosalind watched the beam sweep the black water and thought: *There. Now it is done, and there is no taking it back.* She had crossed that threshold many times in the dark years, and she knew the strange calm that waited on the other side of it. Fear did not leave. It simply stopped being in charge.

"Rosalind." Orsen was at the stair head, his hand on the rail. He looked older and younger at once, the way men do when they have finally stopped pretending to be something they are not. "If they take me—"

"They won't."

"If they do, the pages in the cellar—"

"Are safe. Go."

He went down the stair, and she turned back to the lamp.

* * *

Minding the lamp was work she knew better than the soldiers would ever guess. She had kept a light burning herself, in the war years—not a lamp of glass and oil, but the same principle: you kept the flame alive, you kept it fed, you kept it turning, and you did not flinch when the wind came for it. She trimmed the wick when it guttered. She wiped the salt spray from the inside of the glass with a rag kept for the purpose, working fast between the beam's rotations. She wound the clockwork. She fed the lamp oil and watched the level in the reservoir the way a nurse watches a pulse.

Below her, she heard the storm change its mind and come at the tower from a new quarter, and she heard—faint, beneath the wind—the sound she had been waiting for.

Boots on the harbor road. Many boots.

Then voices, and the crash of the outer door being tried, and then the hammering.

It came up through the tower's bones, a fistbeat at the bottom of the stair: *bang, bang, bang*, iron-shod, patient, furious. She heard a voice bellow through the wind—she could not make out the words, but she knew the cadence. *Open in the name of the requisition authority. Open, or it will be forced.*

She did not open. She fed the lamp.

The hammering stopped. There was a sound of argument below, muffled by stone and storm, and then a new sound: the splintering of wood. They were coming in. She heard Orsen's voice, low and even, and a soldier's voice, high and angry, and then Orsen again, and something in his tone made her stop with the rag in her hand and listen.

He was not shouting. He was not begging. He was saying, over and over, in the flat reasonable voice of a man who has finally run out of reasons to be quiet: "There are three boats on that water. There are nine men on those boats. The law says I keep the light. Not your law. *The* law. It was written before your captain was born and it will be written after she's gone. You can put me in irons, but you'll do it here, on this stair, and every minute you spend doing it is a minute that light keeps turning."

She could not hear the answer. She heard the boots start up the stair.

Rosalind did the arithmetic she had done a hundred times in the war years, the cold arithmetic of the resistance: how long to the top of the stair, how many men, how fast could they move a man who did not want to be moved. Orsen was fifty-one years old and thin as a gull's wing. He could hold the stair for perhaps two minutes against one man. Against four, not at all.

She looked at the lamp. The flame stood steady in its glass house, and the beam went out and swept the sea and came back, and far out in the black—so far she might have imagined it—there was a flicker. A lantern answering. A boat, coming home.

She went to the stair head and stood there, and when the first soldier's helmet rose into the lantern room's light, she was standing beside Orsen with her back to the lamp and her feet planted on the iron floor.

"Madam," the soldier began—"move aside—"

"No," said Rosalind Marchetti, and discovered that the word had not worn out with use.

* * *

It was not a battle. It was something stranger than that.

They were four soldiers, young, wet to the knees, their lanterns guttering in the wind that poured down the stairwell. Their sergeant was a heavy man with a broken nose and the expression of someone who had joined an army and found himself assigned to police fishermen. He pushed past the others and planted himself in front of Orsen.

"Keeper. You were ordered dark."

"I was," Orsen said. "And there are three boats out there that will die on the Teeth without the light. Come and look, Sergeant. Look at the water, and then tell me to put it out."

And the extraordinary thing—the thing Rosalind would remember for the rest of her life, the thing she would write down years later in the restored parish record in her own careful hand—was that the sergeant looked.

He stepped to the glass, past her, past Orsen, and he looked out at the sea. The beam swept over him, lighting his face white and then leaving it dark, white and dark, white and dark. And out in the black water the storm heaved and the lightning stood up along the horizon like the spines of drowned churches, and somewhere out there, nine men were rowing toward a light that the army had forbidden.

"Captain's orders," the sergeant said, but he said it to the glass.

"Captain's orders drowned no one yet," Orsen said. "Yours could."

The silence that followed was the longest of Rosalind's life. The wind screamed. The lamp turned. The sergeant's jaw worked.

And then, from below, up the stairwell, came a sound none of them expected: voices. Many voices. The outer door had opened again—not forced, opened—and boots were coming up the stair in numbers, and the voices were not soldiers' voices. They were Saltmere voices. Tam Halloway's wife, with her carrying contralto. The Widow Penhale. Young Cobb from the chandlery. The mason Brand and his two grown sons, who between them had shoulders like a sea wall.

They came up into the lantern room one after another, dripping, wind-blasted, saying nothing, and they arranged themselves around the lamp—around Orsen, around Rosalind—until the soldiers stood in a shrinking island in the middle of them, and the sergeant looked from face to face and found nowhere to put his authority.

"Stand aside," he said, without conviction. "All of you. This is requisition property."

"It's our light," said the Widow Penhale. She was seventy years old and four feet ten inches and she said it the way she said everything, as though daring the world to argue. "Our fathers built it. Our husbands kept it. You can write your name in your book, Sergeant, but you can't write the light out of us."

"We're not asking you to fight," Rosalind said to the soldiers, and her voice carried the way it had carried in cellars and haylofts for six years, the voice of a woman who had talked frightened people onto boats in the dark. "We're asking you to stand where you're standing and let nine men come home to their children. When your captain asks, you can say you were overborne. No one will contradict you. I promise you that."

The sergeant looked at his men. His men looked at the sea.

And out on the black water, close enough now to be unmistakable, a lantern flickered. Then a second. Then a third.

"Stand down," the sergeant said.

* * *

They brought the boats in by hand, in a manner of speaking—Rosalind and Orsen and a dozen townsfolk crammed into the lantern room and the gallery, watching the beam walk the water, calling the boats in by the old signals, the ones the fishermen's fathers had used: two flashes for the north channel, three for the gap in the Teeth, one long hold for *easy, easy, you're over the bar*.

Orsen worked the shutter with hands that had done it ten thousand times, and Rosalind held the lamp glass steady against the wind's fists, and the town stood around them in a silent ring, and the storm did its worst and could not help them.

The *Merrow* came in first, riding a wave the size of a chapel, and made the harbor mouth with six feet to spare, and the whole gallery made a sound that was not quite a cheer and not quite a sob. Then the Halloway boat. Then the *Sweet Betsey*, last, wallowing, her mast stepped wrong and her crew bailing, and she came through the gap so close that Rosalind could see old Tam's face turned up toward the light, and his mouth moving—she was sure, she would always be sure—around two words.

*Thank you.* Or perhaps *the light*. Or perhaps just a name. It did not matter. The boats came home. Nine men came home.

Below, on the seawall, the town had gathered in the rain, and when the third boat made the inner harbor the sound that rose from the crowd was the sound Rosalind had waited six years to hear in Saltmere: not grief, not fear, not the careful hush of the occupied. It was a roar. It was the town finding its voice all at once, like a sea coming back over a bar.

The soldiers had gone. All but the sergeant, who stood at the foot of the stair a long time, looking at the crowd, at the boats, at the tower. Then he turned and walked away into the rain, and Rosalind understood that he was going to make his report, and that everything would come of it.

* * *

They came for Orsen at dawn, when the storm had blown itself to rags and the sky over Saltmere was the color of a healing bruise.

Rosalind saw them from the lantern room: a file of soldiers coming up the headland road, and behind them, small and upright in a grey coat, Captain Veyra herself. Rosalind had time to move the pages—she had moved them already, twice, in the night, from the cellar to the oil store and back again, and then to the oil store once more, wrapped in oilcloth inside a cask marked *LAMP OIL, RESERVE*, because the one place no requisition officer would search for contraband paper was a cask that might explode.

She came down the stair as they hammered, and she opened the door herself, before Orsen could.

Captain Veyra stood in the grey morning light with rain still beaded on her shoulders. Up close she was younger than her voice, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the storm. Her eyes went past Rosalind to the stair, to the man standing on it with oil on his hands and no apology anywhere on him.

"Orsen Amberdale," Veyra said. "You are charged with obstruction of requisition, unauthorized use of army-controlled property, and willful disobedience of a direct order. You will come with me."

"I'll come," Orsen said. "But write this down, Captain, since you're a woman who writes things down. The light stayed lit. Three boats came home. Nine men are alive this morning who would be dead under your order, and every soul in Saltmere saw it. Whatever you do to me, that's on the record now. You can't strike it through."

Veyra looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—Rosalind had spent six years reading the faces of people deciding whether to be decent, and she saw it there, brief as a fish turning.

"Take him," Veyra said quietly.

They walked him down the headland road between the soldiers, and the town came out of its doors to watch—not hiding now, not behind shutters, but standing in the open in their doorways and on their steps, every one of them, silent, watching him go. Old Tam took off his hat. The Widow Penhale did not.

Rosalind stood at the lighthouse door until they were gone from sight. Then she went down to the oil store, put her hand flat on the cask, and felt through the wood the small solid weight of every name they had copied by lamplight.

They had taken the keeper. They had not taken the light. She would have it burning for him—every page, every name—before he ever saw the inside of a cell.

She went to find the town.
