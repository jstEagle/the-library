## 13. Arrest at Low Tide

They came for him at low tide, when the harbor lay open and brown and the causeway to the lighthouse stood bare as a spine. Orsen saw them from the gallery before he heard the knock—four grey coats picking their way across the shingle, and behind them, small against the pale water, Captain Veyra with her ledger case under her arm like a schoolmistress come to collect overdue lessons.

He had half a day's warning in his bones. He had been expecting it since the night the light refused to die, since the boats came home through the black, since the town had stood on the seawall in the rain and watched three crews walk out of the surf alive because a lamp burned that had been ordered dark. You could not do a thing like that quietly. Saltmere had tried, but joy is louder than fear, and forty people cheering on a seawall carries further than any signal flame.

He went down the stairs slowly, winding the clock as he went, because the clock still needed winding whether he was a free man or not. That was the whole of his creed these days: the wicks still need trimming, the glass still needs polishing, the light still needs lighting. A man could hang his life on such small certainties and they would hold.

The knock came anyway. It always comes, whatever you do to prepare for it.

* * *

"Orsen Amberdale." The officer at the door was young, with a chapped face and an air of having rehearsed this. "By order of the requisition authority, you are detained for obstruction of requisition duties."

"Obstruction," Orsen said. It was not a question so much as a place to stand while he thought.

"You were ordered to extinguish the lamp on the night of the fourteenth. The lamp was lit. Three vessels returned to port under its guidance." The young man recited it like scripture he didn't entirely believe. "Ships are military traffic now. You knew that."

"I know rocks," Orsen said. "There are rocks below the point, and there were three boats out in it. I know what happens to boats on rocks in the dark. I've pulled enough of what's left of them out of the water to last two lifetimes."

Behind him, at the edge of the kitchen where her shadow met the wall, Rosalind stood very still. They had agreed on nothing, in all the nights of copying and dictation and lamplight—there had never been a plan for this, no whispered contingency, because neither of them would say aloud the thing that made a plan necessary. But she looked at him now across the small room where they had written down every name the victors wanted struck from the earth, and her eyes said *don't*, and then said something else beneath the *don't*, and he understood both.

"Your sister-in-law will accompany us as well," the young officer said. "For questioning."

"She's my late wife's sister," Orsen said. "She keeps house. She knows nothing about the lamp except how much oil it burns, and I can tell you that number myself if it saves you the trip upstairs."

Veyra stepped past the soldiers into the doorway then, and the room seemed to arrange itself around her the way water arranges itself around a stone. She looked tired, Orsen thought. Not soft—never soft—but worn in a way she had not been in the autumn, when she first came asking after ledgers with her clean gloves and her terrible thoroughness. Months of Saltmere had gotten into her somewhere, the way salt gets into everything.

"Mr. Amberdale," she said. "I'd hoped it wouldn't come to this."

"You hoped the light would go out," Orsen said. "It's the same hope wearing different clothes."

Something moved behind her eyes—not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of one keeper meeting another across a difference of office. "We're taking the oil store inventory while we're here," she said, turning to the soldiers. "Standard procedure. Requisitioned fuel stores must be accounted for."

And Orsen felt the floor tilt under him, slow and silent, the way a boat tilts when the water comes in somewhere below the waterline.

* * *

The oil store was a stone vault dug into the bluff below the lighthouse, cool even in summer, smelling always of whale oil and hemp and the iron tang of the great casks. Into the false bottom of the oldest cask—the cracked one, the one any sensible quartermaster would condemn—he and Rosalind had sewn, page by page over six weeks, the true ledger of Saltmere. Two hundred and eleven names. Every soul smuggled out by the resistance routes. Every drowned man the official record called *unreliable*. Every hidden family, every forged pass, every night rowed through the sound with muffled oars. Written in Orsen's careful hand, dictated by Rosalind's flawless memory, copied twice more and hidden separately—one set in the cellar of the chapel, one with old Marta Tuller who kept goats above the cliff and had buried three husbands and feared nothing living.

But the master copy was here. In the cask. Twenty feet from where Veyra was standing.

"The store's through there," Orsen said, and his voice came out level, which surprised him. "Mind the third step. It's loose."

Rosalind moved first. That was the thing he would remember afterward, in the cell, in the long grey hours when helplessness sat on his chest like a stone: she moved first, without hurry, without a glance at him that might betray anything, folding her shawl tighter as though the vault were cold—and it was cold—and going ahead of the soldiers into the dark with the lamp she'd taken up from the hook as naturally as if fetching it were her household duty. Because in a sense it was. She had been moving through occupied houses for years with exactly this face, this unhurried gait, this talent for being furniture. He had spent his whole life learning invisibility. She had been born to it, or grief had taught it to her, and watching her now he understood how much of her he had never seen until the copying began.

"The manifest lists fourteen casks," the quartermaster's clerk said, unrolling his paper. "Full or empty?"

"Two empty, twelve full," Orsen said. "The full ones are toward the back. The cracked one's the second from the door—it leaks. I wouldn't roll it if I were you; you'll have oil on your boots and it never comes out."

Every word was true. That was the trick of lying well, he was discovering far too late in life to be good at it—you told the truth about everything except the one thing that mattered, and you gave it away freely, generously, the way you'd give a neighbor kindling. The cracked cask leaked. It did leak. It had leaked for a decade, and the pages inside were wrapped in waxed canvas for precisely that reason.

The soldiers began hauling casks into the daylight to gauge them. Orsen counted under his breath without meaning to. Each cask took perhaps a minute. There were thirteen between the door and the cracked one.

Then the sky tore open.

* * *

It came off the sea with no grace at all—no long grey approach, no warning swell, just a wall of weather that had been standing offshore all morning deciding, and chose. The wind hit the harbor like a hand hitting a table, flattening the water, tearing canvas covers off the boats, and in the first moment of it every soldier in the yard turned to look at the sea, because men who had grown up inland still looked at the sea like that when it showed them what it was.

"Get those casks secured or you'll lose your inventory!" Veyra shouted over the wind. "Officer Brenner—down to the harbor, see if the fishing fleet needs hands on the warps!"

It was chaos. It was beautiful, terrible chaos—casks rolling, soldiers cursing, rain coming sideways now in sheets, and through the middle of it Rosalind walked with the lamp still in her hand, calm as Sunday, and put herself between two struggling soldiers and the cracked cask.

"That one's mine to move," she said clearly. "It leaks, I told you. If you tip it you'll lose thirty gallons and the captain will have your pay for it. I'll carry it up to the shed myself—it's lighter than it looks, mostly empty."

Mostly empty. The words hung in the rain. Orsen's heart stopped and started again.

A soldier hesitated. Rain streamed down his face. Behind them Veyra was shouting about the harbor warps, and the world was wind, and the soldier—cold, wet, wanting only to be elsewhere—stepped aside.

Rosalind bent and lifted the cask. Orsen saw the effort cross her face for half a heartbeat, the true weight of it, wood and iron hoops and two hundred and eleven names, and then it was gone and she was walking, steady, into the rain, toward the coal shed that stood between the oil store and the cliff path. Nobody followed her. Everybody had somewhere worse to be.

Only Veyra looked. Orsen saw it—saw the captain turn from shouting at Brenner, saw her eyes find Rosalind crossing the yard with a leaking oil cask in her arms, and hold there one second too long. Not suspicion, yet. Arithmetic. The beginning of it. Veyra was a historian; she noticed what did not fit the pattern, and a housekeeper carrying heavy casks did not fit.

Then a warp parted down at the harbor with a crack like a musket shot and a boat began to swing broadside to the pier, and every eye including Veyra's went to the water, because the sea does not wait for arithmetic.

Rosalind reached the coal shed. Set the cask down inside among the sacks. And was gone into the rain without it—empty-handed, shawl streaming, walking back toward the vault with the expression of a woman who has done a chore and means now to do another.

* * *

They arrested him in the yard, in the rain, because there was no better time and the tide was turning and the requisition authority did not schedule its business around weather.

"Orsen Amberdale," the young officer said again, and this time there were irons, cold ones, and the crowd was already gathering along the seawall because Saltmere had learned to gather fast. Old Marta Tuller stood at the front with her stick planted like a halberd. The Harrow brothers. The widow Cane whose husband's name was struck through in the official ledger and alive in the true one. Forty people, sixty, standing in the rain saying nothing, which in Saltmere under occupation was a shout.

"You'll want to note the time," Orsen said to the clerk, nodding at the lighthouse behind them, where the great lens turned and turned above the storm, faithful as a heartbeat. "The lamp's due trimming at four. My sister-in-law knows the routine. She'll see to it."

"He'll be processed at the garrison and held pending inquiry," the officer said, to nobody, to the form he was filling out with a board pressed against his knee, rain blotting the ink so he had to write twice.

Veyra stood apart, hair plastered dark with rain, watching Rosalind. Watching the lighthouse. Watching the town line the seawall like a jury. Orsen, passing her on the way to the cart, stopped—irons clinking, rain running off his chin—and spoke low, so only she could hear.

"You counted the casks," he said. "You'll find thirteen accounted for and one in the coal shed, and you'll wonder why a housekeeper carried it there, and you'll wonder about a lot of things now, Captain, because that's what you are—wondering is your trade. So let me save you some trouble. Whatever you think we hid, wherever you think we hid it—ask yourself what it's worth. Paper's cheap. You burn a page, it's gone. But there were forty people on that wall just now who watched you take me for keeping a light burning over rocks, and every one of them has a name somebody died saving, and names don't burn the same way paper does. You can strike them out of a book. You can't strike them out of a town."

Veyra looked at him for a long moment, rain sliding down her face, and did not answer. But she did not look away either, and that was its own kind of answer.

The cart lurched forward. The crowd along the seawall did not cheer—they were past cheering, past fear, into the flat country beyond both where people simply stand together and cannot be moved—and as the cart turned onto the coast road Orsen twisted around for one last look.

The lighthouse stood against the boiling sky, beam sweeping, sweeping, sweeping. And below it, small in the rain, Rosalind climbed the cliff path toward the goat track above the point, alone, unhurried, carrying nothing anyone could see.

He did not know where she was going. He did not know if the coal shed would be searched by morning, or whether Veyra's arithmetic would finish its sum. He knew only that he had bought her the hour with his name and his freedom, that the pages were out of the vault and into the weather, and that the next move belonged to the one left outside.

The cart took him down the coast road through the storm, toward the garrison, toward the cell, toward whatever came. Behind him the light swept on over the water, and ahead of him the rain closed like a door.
