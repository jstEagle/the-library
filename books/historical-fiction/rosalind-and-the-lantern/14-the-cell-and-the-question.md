## 14. The Cell and the Question

The cell beneath the requisition office had been a herring store before the war, and it still smelled of salt and old fish oil, of nets left to rot in corners. Captain Elara Veyra noticed these things because she had trained herself, years ago in the archive halls of the capital, to notice what rooms remembered. A room was a document. It told you what people valued before they had time to arrange their faces for you.

Orsen Amberdale sat on the plank bench with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a ferry. He did not fidget. He did not look at the two soldiers by the door. He looked at her, when he looked at anything at all, with the mild patience of someone who had spent thirty years watching weather.

Veyra dismissed the soldiers. She heard their boots climb the stair and the door close above, and then it was just the lamp between them—the one concession she had made, a proper oil lamp rather than the wall bracket, because she wanted him comfortable enough to be honest. She set her notebook on her knee but did not open it. Not yet. The notebook could wait. The notebook was for after.

"You've been here three days," she said. "You haven't asked me once what you're charged with."

"I know what I'm charged with." His voice was low and even, a voice worn smooth by wind. "Obstruction of requisition."

"And you don't dispute it?"

"The light was lit. Boats came home." He shrugged, a small motion, as though the matter were arithmetic. "I'd do it again tonight."

She believed him. That was the trouble with Amberdale; everything about him invited belief, the way a still pool invites you to believe it is shallow. She had read his file twice through the night before. Widow. Lighthouse keeper, nineteen years. No political affiliations. No recorded speech against the Crown of Aldemar or its army. Fined once, years ago, for under-reporting his oil consumption—a triviality, the kind of small dishonesty that keeps small men fed. Otherwise: nothing. A man who had made himself so quiet that the paper trail of an entire war had passed over him like weather over stone.

And yet.

"You know why you're really here," she said.

He said nothing. But something moved behind his eyes—not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who has been expecting a letter and finally hears the knock.

"Three nights running," Veyra said, "a light burned in the lantern room after hours. Not the navigational light. A second flame. Someone was working up there—reading, writing, copying. And the night we took you, my men found the oil store disturbed. Barrels moved. Floor swept." She let that sit. "Goods are smuggled out of occupied towns every week, Keeper. Grain, brandy, ammunition. But nobody smuggles barrels of lamp oil into a lantern room at midnight to sweep the floor afterward. So tell me what you were moving."

Amberdale looked down at his hands. They were broad hands, rope-scarred, the nails cracked from salt work. When he spoke again, it was not an answer. It was a question of his own.

"Do you have family, Captain?"

"That isn't relevant."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But you asked me a question I didn't answer, so I'll take the liberty."

Something in the flatness of it made her want to smile, and she refused the impulse. *Careful*, she thought. *This is how they do it. This is how the losing side fights now—not with rifles, with patience.* She had seen it in two other ports. The war was over everywhere except in the record rooms, and there it raged nightly.

"My mother," Veyra said, surprising herself. "In Correnn. Why?"

"Is she alive?"

"She is."

"Then you understand," Orsen said, "that there are people whose whole remaining existence is a name on a page somewhere. You strike the page, and they're gone twice."

*There it is,* she thought. She opened the notebook at last, unhurried, and uncapped her pen. "Let's begin properly. Your name."

"Orsen Amberdale. Keeper of the Saltmere light."

"Full name. As registered."

"Orsen Tomas Amberdale."

She wrote it. Then, without looking up: "Your wife."

A pause. Long enough that she glanced up. His jaw had tightened—not much. A rope taking strain.

"Maren Amberdale. Born Maren Calloway. Drowned four years ago, the ninth of Stormmonth, off the Teeth."

"Drowned," Veyra repeated. "Not lost. You're certain?"

"I pulled her coat from the water myself." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "I'm certain."

Veyra turned back a few pages in her memory rather than her notebook. The parish ledger of Saltmere—requisitioned in the first week of occupation, along with the town's boats, its granary, and its dignity—had been her project since autumn. Two hundred and eleven households. Four hundred and six names. And, since her clerks had begun the cross-verification, a growing column of entries marked *unverified*, *unreliable*, *struck*. Struck names were names the official history would not carry forward. Names of the drowned whose deaths could not be confirmed by any witness willing to stand up in an occupied town and say so. Names of people who had, according to no record at all, simply never existed—which meant, in the clean grammar of the victors' history, that whatever had been done to them had never happened either.

Forty-three names struck so far. Veyra had signed off on every strike herself, because that was her job, and because she had convinced herself long ago that thoroughness was a virtue. An inaccurate record was a wound that never closed. A corrected record—even if the corrections favored the winners—was at least a record.

She had believed that right up until the lighthouse light would not go out.

"Your wife's name is still in the ledger," she said. "Confirmed drowning. Witnessed. It stands."

"Good."

"But your sister-in-law does not."

Now the silence had weight. Veyra watched him the way she had learned to watch documents: for the place where the ink trembles.

"Rosalind Calloway arrived in Saltmere eight months before the war ended," she went on, reading now from her notes. "No entry of residence. No ration registration. No tax record. No marriage, no employment, no death. By every administrative measure, Keeper, the woman does not exist. Yet your neighbors queue at your door with bread for her. My informants say she knows the tide tables better than the harbormaster. And the night my officers searched your house, you told them—" she consulted the page— "you told them the cellar held pickled herring, and when they opened the third barrel they found it packed with straw and a woman's cloak."

"They didn't find a woman."

"They found where a woman had been standing not ten minutes earlier. There was warmth in the straw, Keeper. Straw holds heat for perhaps twenty minutes." She closed the notebook. "You lied to a requisition officer. Badly. And in four days of custody you have lied only once more—this morning, when you told the duty sergeant you hadn't slept, when the shadows under your eyes say otherwise. Do you know what interests me about that?"

He waited.

"A man who lies badly is a man who hasn't practiced. Which means the lying isn't the point of him. Something else is. And I want to know what it is before my superiors arrive next month and start asking cruder questions than mine."

* * *

The lamp burned lower. Veyra rose, trimmed the wick herself—an unconscious habit from her archive days, keeping the light steady over the page—and sat again, closer now, on the same bench's far end. Up close, Amberdale smelled of lamp oil and brine, and she saw that he was older than his file photograph, the way everyone in the occupied territories was older than their photographs.

"I was a historian," she said. "Before. Did you know that?"

"Captain Veyra, formerly Lecturer Veyra, Correnn Academy," he recited. "It's on the requisition notices."

"Then you know I care about records. So let me put my question to you honestly, man to officer, and I'll ask you to answer it honestly, because I think you're capable of it and I think very few people in this town are." She set the notebook aside entirely. "Why should the record favor the losing side?"

He looked at her then—really looked, for the first time—and she had the strange sense of being read herself, catalogued, entered into some ledger of his own.

"Say it plainer," he said.

"Saltmere was a smuggling port. Smuggling is treason. The ledger contains names of people who aided treason—some of whom drowned doing it, some of whom fled, some of whom are sitting in this room's vicinity as we speak. The Crown won. History is written by the Crown. If we record the smugglers as heroes, we teach the next generation that treason works. If we strike the unverifiable names, we get a clean, accurate account of a law-abiding town that suffered a hard winter. Both versions can't survive. Mine is defensible. Every fact in it can be sourced." She spread her hands. "So I ask you plainly, Keeper: why should the record favor your side?"

For a while there was only the lamp's small hiss and, faintly, through the stone, the sound of the sea working at the harbor wall.

Then Orsen Amberdale began to speak, and he did not argue.

"Tobias Wren," he said. "Fisherman, forty-one. Went out in the second storm of the season carrying three passengers to the Corvess run—one of them a child of seven with fever. Boat lost off the Teeth. Body never recovered. Is Tobias Wren in your record?"

Veyra did not answer.

"He is not," Orsen said. "Because the passengers weren't documented, so officially he was fishing alone in a closed sea lane, and that makes him a criminal, and criminals who drown get struck. His wife Merren is living on cockles and charity. She watched your clerk draw a line through his name and she hasn't spoken a full sentence since." He shifted slightly on the bench. "Edith Marlowe. Kept the toll house. Hid eleven people in her cellar over two winters. Your soldiers took her in the spring. She died in transit—dysentery, the escort says, though the escort also says she was alive when the wagon left and no one will show the manifest. Her name's struck too. Unverifiable. Eleven people are breathing today because of her, Captain, and your history says she never existed."

"Those are claims," Veyra said quietly. "Claims need sources."

"Sources," Orsen repeated. He said the word like he was tasting it. "You want sources. I'll give you sources. Sela Pennhallow, drowned, the fourth of Stormmonth, two years past—her sister saw the boat go down from the headland and can tell you the hour. Joss Calloway, drowned the same night—no kin of mine, before you ask, half the coast is named Calloway—his daughter buried a lock of his hair because there was nothing else to bury, and she can tell you what he carried in his pockets. Bram Tuller. Old Nan Ferrow. The Fenwick boy, Aldous, sixteen years old, who steered a leaking boat nine miles with a broken oar and got six people to the cliffs at Corvess alive, and died of the effort two days later in a stranger's barn." His voice had not risen. It had gotten lower instead, and steadier, the way the sea gets when it means business. "Sixty-one people went out from this harbor in rescue runs during the storms and did not come back. Sixty-one. I know because I kept the light for every single boat that left, and I counted them out and I counted them home, and the ones that didn't come home, I wrote down. In my own hand. With dates. With tides. With the names of who they were carrying, as best anyone knew."

Veyra felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does on a ship when the swell changes. "Where is that record?"

"Safe."

"If it exists—if it says what you claim—it contradicts the parish ledger in forty places."

"It doesn't contradict it," Orsen said. "It completes it. That's what you can't see, Captain. You think there are two histories fighting. There aren't. There's one history, and your version of it has holes cut in it where the dead used to be. I'm not asking you to favor the losing side. I'm asking you to stop striking names out of the dark and calling the darkness accuracy." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time he looked tired, deeply, humanly tired. "You asked why the record should favor us. It shouldn't favor anybody. That's the whole point. Merren Wren doesn't want Tobias made a hero. She wants him *entered*. Dates, cause, tide, done. She wants to walk to the churchyard wall and know there's a line of ink somewhere with his name in it that no clerk can rub out. You of all people should understand that. You trim lamps in cells. You keep lights burning over pages. What do you think you've been doing all these years, if not exactly that?"

Veyra did not move. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the office floor, and the sound fell into the silence like a stone into a well.

"Names," she said at last. "You answer every question with names."

"Because names are the answer." He straightened. "You can threaten me, Captain, though I notice you haven't. You can hold me until your superiors come, and they'll be less patient than you. But you asked me a real question, and I gave you a real answer, and I'll give you one more thing for free." He stood, slowly, joints cracking like ship's rigging, and faced her. "The sixty-one drowned aren't the losing side. Neither am I. Neither is the woman in the straw. We're not a side at all. We're the part of the story that happened whether you write it down or not. The only question—the only one you'll ever face in that uniform—is whether the ink tells the truth or lies about us. Everything else is weather."

He nodded to her, almost courteously, and returned to the bench, and sat, and folded his hands, and waited, as though the interview were hers to end.

Veyra gathered her notebook and lamp. At the foot of the stair she stopped, one hand on the cold iron rail, and did not turn around.

"The oil store," she said. "The night of your arrest. My sergeant reported barrels moved and fresh mortar dust on the east wall. There's a cavity behind it, isn't there. There was something hidden there, and it was moved—in the chaos, within minutes of the arrest. Somebody got to it fast, and quietly, and knew exactly where it was going." She heard him shift behind her but she kept her eyes on the stairs. "I've ordered the harbor sealed for the inquiry. Every house, every net loft, every hull searched. And I've posted a clerk in the lantern room starting tomorrow, Keeper, around the clock. If your record exists, it will surface. And then you and I will both find out which of us the ink serves."

She climbed the stairs. Above her, the town lay dark under curfew, and beyond the breakwater the true lighthouse turned its slow white arm across the water—steady, indifferent, counting.

Behind her, in the cell that smelled of herrings and old oil, Orsen Amberdale sat alone in the lamplight she had left burning, and smiled for the first time in four days.

Because she had just told him exactly where to look.
