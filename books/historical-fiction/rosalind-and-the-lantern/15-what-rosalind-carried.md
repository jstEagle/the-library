## 15. What Rosalind Carried

The oil store smelled of lamp-black and salt, of the years Orsen had spent keeping the light burning through weather that would have drowned lesser men. I had not meant to bring him here. I had meant to keep this room closed until the war ended, or until I died, whichever came first—and for three years those two events had seemed likely to arrive in the same week.

But the copied pages were safe now, moved in the chaos of his arrest, folded into a false bottom beneath the spare wicks where no requisition officer would think to look, because requisition officers did not look at the things that kept lighthouses running. They looked at ledgers. They looked at names.

And so, on the third night after they released him—bruised at the wrists, quieter than I had ever known him—I climbed down into the cellar with a lantern and told him to follow me.

"You could have told me," he said from the top of the stair. His voice was careful. Not angry. Orsen was never angry; anger required a belief that the world owed you something better, and my brother-in-law had made a religion of expecting nothing. "Three years, Rosalind. You could have told me what you were carrying."

"I could have," I said. "And then you'd have been carrying it too."

"Isn't that what I've been doing since the night you brought the courier's note up from the tide line?"

I set the lantern on an empty oil drum and turned to face him on the narrow stair. He filled it almost completely—a big man gone leaner these past years, hair going grey at the temples, hands that knew rope and wick and the weight of a drowned man pulled from the shallows. Maren's husband. My sister's widower. The one man on this coast who had never once asked me why I came to his door with bread twice a week for three years, or why I always left before dark, or why my questions about the harbor had the particular shape of questions asked by someone who needed answers rather than wanted them.

"Maren knew," I said.

It landed the way I had known it would land. He did not move. He did not speak. But something behind his eyes went very still, the way the sea goes still before it decides what it is going to do to you.

"She knew," he repeated.

"She helped me build the first route." I gestured downward, into the dark below the stair, toward the stone shelf against the far wall. "Before the army came. Before Veyra. Back when the war was only rumors and the smugglers were only smugglers. She carried messages between the fishing families and the Quaymaster's people when it wasn't safe for me to be seen doing it. A widow's sister can go anywhere in a town like this and be nobody. A widow's *husband*—" I shook my head. "She protected you by keeping you ignorant. She made me promise."

"How long?"

"From the beginning. From the first winter."

He came down the last steps slowly, the way a man descends into water he already knows will be cold. He stood over the false-bottomed chest where his own hands had nearly found the pages a hundred times while restocking wicks, and I watched him do the arithmetic of it—the number of nights I had sat at his table eating his bread with a ledger's worth of secrets under my tongue, the number of mornings Maren had kissed him goodbye knowing exactly which boats would come home and which ones she had arranged never to sail.

"She died not knowing if I'd have approved," he said finally. It was not a question, but I answered it anyway.

"She died knowing exactly what you'd have said. That's why she didn't tell you."

Something that was almost a laugh went out of him, brief as a struck match. Then he knelt beside the chest, and I knelt beside him, and together—we, and I want to write that word because it matters, because for three years everything had been I—we lifted out the false board and the wrapped oilcloth beneath it, and I unwrapped the second ledger into the lamplight.

* * *

It was older than the parish book. Older than the war. It had begun its life as a harbor master's tally, its first third filled with cargo entries in a dead clerk's cramped hand—barrels of herring, coils of tarred rope, the mundane arithmetic of a working coast. I had bought it from the Quaymaster's estate for six coppers because the paper was good and the binding was strong and because I had learned, in the first frightened months, that the safest place to hide a record of the disappeared was inside a record of fish.

The middle third was mine.

I watched Orsen turn the pages with the reverence other men reserve for scripture, and I suppose that is what it was. Names, dates, tides. *Edda Calloway and her two boys, out on the *Petrel*, 14th of the storm month, delivered to the caves north of Grey Head, taken inland by the miller's cart.* *Tomas Brand, wanted for the burning of the grain requisition, hidden in the lighthouse cellar nine days.* Nine days in the very room where we knelt. *The Ferrow family, five souls, passage bought with the silver from Maren's wedding spoons.*

His finger stopped on that entry. Stayed there.

"The spoons," he said.

"They were hers to give."

"They were—" He stopped. Started again. "I wondered, afterward, where they'd gone. I thought perhaps she'd sold them. We were short, that winter. I thought she'd sold them for flour and hadn't the heart to tell me."

"She gave them for the Ferrow family's passage," I said. "Five souls, Orsen. Mother, father, grandmother, two children. All five alive today because your wife liked her spoons less than she liked strangers living."

He closed his eyes. I let him. There is a grief that needs privacy even when it happens in company, and this was that grief—the grief of a man discovering that the woman he had mourned for years had been more than he knew, and that every ordinary evening he remembered had been lit by a fire he had never seen.

When he opened his eyes again, he turned to the final third of the ledger—the part still blank except for what I had added in recent months, since the requisition began, since Veyra's grey coats started striking names out of the parish record with the tidy strokes of a woman editing a manuscript.

"What are these?" he asked. The newest entries. Not rescues. Something else.

"Testimony," I said. "Or the beginnings of it. Go back to the inquiry list Veyra posted—testimony to be taken *from the ledger, not the living*. She thinks that's her advantage. She thinks that because the dead can't speak and the struck-through names can't be verified against the official record, her version is the only version left standing." I took the book gently from his hands and laid it open on my knee. "But she has made one mistake, and it is the same mistake every thorough person makes: she believes the paper is the truth, when the paper is only ever the memory of the truth. The truth is standing in kitchens all over Saltmere tonight. Edda Calloway is alive. Tomas Brand is mending nets on the east quay. The Ferrow children are in school learning their letters from a schoolmistress who owes her post to the same network. Every name she struck through has a body attached to it, and every one of those bodies remembers."

"You mean to testify."

"I mean to do worse than testify. Testimony is one voice asking to be believed. What I mean—" and here I felt the old fear rise in my throat, the fear that had lived in me since the first night I wrote a stranger's name in a dead clerk's book, and I spoke through it, "—is that we stop being a secret. Three years I have run this thing the only way it could be run: alone, in the dark, one name at a time, each helper knowing only their piece. That is how you survive a war. But the war is ending, Orsen. The inquiries are coming. And a secret that stays a secret through the war dies in the peace, because peace is when the victors write their books." I put my hand flat on the page. "So we come out of the dark. All of us. At once. On one day. In one room."

* * *

We spent the next hour making the list, and it was the strangest hour of my life, because for three years I had held every thread myself—never writing them together, never speaking two names in the same breath, so that no arrest could unravel more than a single strand. Now I said them aloud to Orsen one after another, and each name felt like pulling a stitch out of my own skin: Edda Calloway, who had fed the cave parties through two winters and never once asked how the food got to her door. Tomas Brand, whose arson I had arranged and whose silence I had bought with work and trust. Old Nettie Ferrow, the grandmother, who had been a girl during the last war and remembered how these things end. The schoolmistress. The Quaymaster's widow, who had inherited her husband's routes along with his debts. Eleven households. Fourteen witnesses willing to stand in front of a victors' inquiry and say *this person lives, and here is how, and here is who saved them*—which was, of course, the dangerous part, because behind every rescued name stood mine.

"You understand what you're asking them to do," Orsen said. He was copying the list in his slow, sure hand, the same hand that had copied the parish ledger night after night in the lantern room while the beam turned above us. "Veyra released me because I was a minor case. She won't release fourteen people. Some of them will be detained. Maybe worse, depending on how the wind sits in the capital."

"Yes."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm not asking." I met his eyes across the oil drum, across the lamplight, across three years of bread and careful questions. "I'm telling them what I know, and what I know is this: if we stay silent, Veyra's history wins. Not because it's true—because it's the only one written down. Our children will grow up reading that the people we saved never existed, that the boats that came home came home by luck, that Saltmere sat out the war with its hands clean. Silence doesn't protect anyone anymore, Orsen. Silence is just dying slowly, on their schedule instead of ours."

He set down the pen. For a long moment he looked at the second ledger—not at the names, but at the object itself, the good paper and the strong binding, the dead clerk's fish tallies wrapped around the living proof of what this town had done.

"When?" he asked.

"The inquiry convenes within the fortnight. So: before. We go household to household, starting tomorrow, starting with Edda Calloway because she has the loudest voice and the least to lose and the most reason to hate them. Each family with a struck-through name stands at the hearing and says it aloud—that was your idea, and it was a better one than mine, and I am not too proud to use it. And when they call for testimony from the record—" I tapped the ledger's cover, "—the true ledger enters the room."

"They'll seize it."

"Let them. Seized evidence gets sealed, and sealed evidence gets preserved—it becomes part of the official record precisely because they'll be too procedural to destroy it in public. Veyra's own thoroughness will cage her." I paused. "Though I confess I don't know her well enough to swear to that. You've spoken with her more than I have."

"I lied to her badly in my own kitchen," Orsen said, "and she knew it, and she let it pass, and I have been trying for weeks to decide whether that was mercy or bait."

"Then we plan for bait."

* * *

We went to Edda Calloway's house the next morning in broad daylight, which felt like walking naked through the market square after three years of moving in shadow, and it was Orsen who knocked, Orsen who said, "Edda. Rosalind has something to ask of you," and Orsen who stood at her hearth while I laid the ledger open on her table to the page with her sons' names in my handwriting.

She read it standing, both hands flat on the table like a woman bracing against weather. When she finished she did not cry. She straightened up and looked at me with the flat bright fury of a mother who has been waiting three years for someone to give her permission.

"Say the names at the hearing," I said. "All of them. Yours and everyone else's. Say them loud enough that the historians have to choose between writing them down and admitting in front of the whole coast that they refuse to."

Edda Calloway rolled up her sleeves.

"I've been saying them every night since they struck them out," she said. "To my kitchen wall. It'll be a pleasure to say them to a room with soldiers in it."

By evening we had been to four houses. By the following dusk, nine. Word traveled the way it does in fishing towns—along the quay, over garden walls, in the pauses of conversations that were ostensibly about nets—until people I had never recruited began finding *us*: a boatwright pressing Orsen's hand at the water stairs and murmuring, "Count me among the witnesses"; the schoolmistress leaving a chalked sign in her window, three marks and a circle, the old network signal that meant *ready*. The town that had kept its head down through the whole year of the great storms was lifting it, household by household, the way a sea lifts before it decides to break.

On the fourth night, Orsen and I climbed to the lantern room with the second ledger wrapped in oilcloth under my coat, because there was one entry left to make and only one place it could properly be made. The beam swept above us; the great lens threw its slow wheel of light across the water, and below, in the town, I could see candles in windows where for a year there had been darkness—small, defiant, multiplying.

"Read it to me," Orsen said. "Whatever you're adding. It should be heard once before it's written."

So I stood in the lantern room of the Saltmere light, with the storm season closing around us like a fist, and I read aloud the entry I had saved for last—the full account of the network, the routes, the helpers, the money, and at the end, plainly, without alias or initials, my own name: *Rosalind Marchetti, keeper of this record.*

When I finished, neither of us moved for a moment. Then Orsen took the pen from my hand.

"There's ink enough for one more line," he said, "and I've spent three years being nobody in my own town. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly."

And it was while he was writing—while the beam wheeled overhead and the candles multiplied below and somewhere out in the dark a requisition patrol walked its rounds—that we saw the light answering from the headland road. Three lanterns, swinging in a pattern I knew too well.

Not a signal from our people.

A signal to the garrison.
