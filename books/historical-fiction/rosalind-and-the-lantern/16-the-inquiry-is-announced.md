## 16. The Inquiry Is Announced

The order came on a Tuesday, nailed to the customs house door in a frame of fresh pine, as though the victors thought a handsome border would make the words kinder. Orsen read it standing in a knot of fishermen who had not stood so close together in public since the requisition began. Nobody spoke. The paper did the talking.

*By authority of the Provisional Administration: a Commission of Inquiry into Civilian Conduct during the late hostilities shall convene at Saltmere on the first day of the herring moon. All records pertaining to the movement, loyalty, and disposition of the civilian population shall be submitted for review. Testimony shall be received in written form only.*

Written form only.

Orsen read that phrase four times, and each time it landed heavier, like ballast going into a boat. He heard the men around him murmur the words back and forth—*written form, written form*—as if repetition might reveal some mercy hidden in them. It would not. He had spent enough nights with Veyra's red-inked ledger to know exactly what *written form* meant. It meant the dead would be questioned instead of the living. It meant the struck-through names would answer for themselves, and struck-through names could say nothing in their own defense. It meant the historians had already won, unless—

Unless there was another book.

He walked home along the cliff path with his collar up and his mind running ahead of him like a current. The commission would come to Saltmere. Veyra's clerks would unpack their crates of paper in the customs house, unroll the parish ledger on a long table under good light, and read the town its own edited history aloud. Every name Rosalind's network had carried to safety across the water would appear in the record as drowned, or fled without cause, or simply vanish between one line and the next, the way a stone vanishes when you drop it down a well. And no one—not Maren Cotter whose brother was saved off the Blackrock skerry, not old Tam Pellin whose two sons were rowed out on a fog night by a woman the town pretended not to see—no one would be permitted to stand up and say otherwise.

*Testimony shall be received in written form only.* They had learned something from the lighthouse, then. From the light that stayed lit while soldiers hammered at the door. A town full of mouths was dangerous. A town reduced to paper was manageable.

Orsen climbed the stair to the lantern room, where Rosalind was waiting with the second ledger open on the chart table, her copying hand still inked to the knuckle. She looked up when he came in, and he saw she already knew. Of course she knew; word traveled faster than any army. But he told her anyway, reading the notice aloud from memory because he could not get the phrases out of his head.

She listened the way she listened to everything now—with a stillness that had been trained into her by years of danger, a stillness he had once mistaken for coldness and understood now was armor.

"First day of the herring moon," she said. "That's nineteen days."

"Nineteen days," Orsen agreed.

"And they'll take testimony from the ledger." She turned her hand over and studied the ink stains on her fingers as though they might tell her something new. "From *their* ledger."

"Their ledger has our names in it too," Orsen said quietly. "Yours among them, if they ever trace the routes. Mine, now, after the lamp."

"Yes." She smiled without much humor in it. "We've made ourselves legible, Orsen. That was the whole point, wasn't it? We wanted to be seen." She closed the copied pages and tied the string around them with quick, practiced movements. "Now we have nineteen days to make sure we're seen correctly."

* * *

His release came three days later, and it came the way weather comes in from the sea—sudden, gray, and impossible to argue with.

A corporal arrived at the lighthouse before dawn with a folded paper and a lantern he didn't need, since Orsen's own light was still burning overhead, restored to its lawful hours now that the army had decided the lamp was more useful obedient than dark. The corporal was young, chapped with cold, and clearly uncomfortable with whatever he'd been sent to do. He handed over the paper, waited while Orsen read it, and shifted his weight from foot to foot like a boy outside a schoolroom.

*Prisoner of inquiry no. 114, Amberdale, O., widower, keeper of lights, having been determined a case of minor obstruction and no further consequence to the administration, is remanded to civilian status effective immediately. Detention records sealed pending the Commission.*

"No further consequence," Orsen read aloud. The words tasted strange, like fish past its season.

"That's what it says, sir." The corporal wouldn't meet his eyes. "Captain's orders. She said—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "She said to tell you the Commission will want the town quiet. That it's better for everyone if Saltmere gives them nothing to trouble about." He said it mechanically, a message recited rather than meant, and then, in a lower voice, with a glance up at the turning beam above them: "My father's boat came home the night you kept the light burning. The *Kestrel*. I just—I thought you'd want to know someone knows."

Orsen looked at the boy for a long moment. "Thank you," he said, and meant it more than the boy could know. Then, because invisibility was a habit he could not fully shed even now: "You didn't hear that from me."

"No, sir," the corporal said, and almost smiled. "I never do."

He went back down the cliff road at a march, and Orsen stood holding the release paper in the wind, feeling less free than he had in the cell. Because he understood now what the release was. It was not mercy. It was arithmetic. Nineteen days before the Commission arrived, and Captain Veyra—a woman who cross-checked everything twice—had done the sums. A town with an imprisoned keeper was a town with a grievance, and grievances made people talk, and talk at an inquiry was precisely what she could not afford. So she had cut him loose the way you cut a net loose when it snags the propeller: not because the net was harmless, but because the boat mattered more.

*Better for everyone if Saltmere gives them nothing to trouble about.*

Which meant, Orsen realized with a slow cold certainty settling in his chest, that she knew. Not everything—perhaps not even much—but enough. Enough to know there was a copy somewhere. Enough to know the town was holding its breath around the lighthouse the way a household holds its breath around a sickbed. And enough to understand that the surest way to find the true ledger was not to search for it, but to announce a place and a date where it would have to appear—or be proven absent.

The Commission wasn't just an inquiry. It was a trap with a schedule.

* * *

That evening the town gathered in the church—not for worship; the victors had repurposed the building as a storehouse for requisitioned grain—but in the yard behind it, where the old elm still stood and the wind couldn't carry voices toward the harbor patrols. Rosalind had called no meeting. There had been no note, no signal, nothing that could be seized or read. There had only been a word passed door to door in the oldest way, mouth to ear, the way her network had always worked: *elm tree, after supper.*

They came in twos and threes, muffled against the autumn cold, until forty souls stood in the shadow of the tree and the last light died off the water. Orsen counted them from the edge of the gathering and felt something turn over in his chest. Forty. When the light had defied the army, three boats' worth of families had found their courage. Now, with a date fixed and the stakes named, forty people had chosen to stand in a cold churchyard and be counted—at least among themselves.

Rosalind stood beneath the elm with the wind pulling at her shawl, and Orsen watched her become, in front of his eyes, the woman his wife must have known all along—the sister who ran routes and moved money and kept a second ledger in a lighthouse while the world burned. She did not raise her voice. She never needed to.

"You've heard the notice," she began. "The Commission comes at the herring moon. They'll sit in the customs house with their clerks and their ink, and they'll take testimony in written form only—which means they intend to question a book." A low murmur went through the crowd. "Their book. The one with the struck-through lines. The one where your brothers and daughters and sons are recorded as lost, or disloyal, or never existed at all."

Maren Cotter spoke up from the front, her voice cracking on the cold air. "And we're to stand by and let them?"

"We are to do better than that." Rosalind's eyes moved across the faces. "Because here is what I've learned about ink, these past months. Ink is patient. Ink waits to be corrected. They think a ledger is finished once it's bound and stamped and struck through in red." She held up her own stained fingers. "But every record ever kept was kept by hands. And hands can keep another record. Hands can testify."

Old Tam Pellin pushed forward, hat in his gnarled fists. "They said written form only. If we try to speak, they'll clear the hall."

"They will," Rosalind agreed. "So we won't speak first. We'll write. Every family with a name struck through that line—that name, in your own hand, on your own paper, signed, dated, witnessed by two neighbors. What happened. Who was saved. Which boat, which night, which fog. Nineteen days. Bring them to the lighthouse." A breath of wind stirred the elm overhead, and she waited for it to pass. "And on the day of the Commission, when they call for submissions, Saltmere will submit. Dozens of statements. Hundreds of pages. Let their clerks drown in the truth of us. Let them discover that this town kept its own record all along, in forty different hands, and that not one of those hands can be arrested for remembering."

Someone laughed—a short, startled sound, half fear and half wonder—and the laugh spread through the little crowd like warmth through a cold room. Orsen felt it move through him too, and underneath the laughter, something harder and quieter: the finish line had been drawn, yes, but it was drawn in *their* direction. Nineteen days was not a wall. Nineteen days was a tide table. And tides, in Saltmere, were something the town knew how to read.

As the crowd thinned into the dusk, slipping away in ones and twos toward their doors, Rosalind remained beneath the elm, and Orsen came to stand beside her. For a while neither of them spoke. Down in the harbor, the requisition officers' lanterns made small yellow islands on the black water, and above them both, faint and steady, the lighthouse beam swept its slow arc across the sky.

"She let you go," Rosalind said at last. "Veyra."

"For the peace. To keep the town quiet."

"Mm." Rosalind's gaze stayed on the harbor. "Orsen, I've run from clever people for six years, and I'll tell you what I know about them. Clever people don't release pieces off the board unless the board itself is the game." She turned to look at him then, and the last grey light caught the lines of exhaustion and resolve on her face. "Nineteen days. She'll spend every one of them looking for the second ledger. And we'll spend every one of them filling the town with testimony she can't burn—because you can't strike through forty signatures at once without the whole world asking why."

Orsen nodded slowly. But as they walked back up the cliff path together, the beam sweeping overhead, one thought kept circling him like a gull over a wake:

Veyra had released him. Veyra had announced a date. Veyra, thorough as winter, had left the lighthouse standing and its keeper walking free—and Captain Veyra left nothing standing or walking free by accident.

Whatever she intended, it would begin the moment they stepped inside the customs house. And the true ledger was still sleeping in the lantern room above them, one flight of stairs and a locked oil-store door away from discovery, waiting for a race that now had a finish line—nineteen days out, dead ahead, straight into the storm season's last and worst blow.
