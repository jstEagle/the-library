## 17. Every Name Read Aloud

Rosalind had spent seven years learning how to move through Saltmere without being seen. She knew which doorways swallowed a figure whole, which lanes the soldiers avoided after dark, which windows had eyes behind them and which were only glass. She could cross the town from the fish sheds to the churchyard in eleven minutes without once setting her full weight on a creaking board.

Now she was asking herself to do the opposite of everything those years had taught her. She was asking an entire town to stand up in front of the victors' inquiry and be seen.

It began, as most dangerous things in Saltmere began, with bread.

She baked on the morning after the announcement was nailed to the requisition office door—four lines of printed proclamation declaring that a Commission of Inquiry into Civilian Conduct would convene at the harbor customs house, that testimony would be taken from official records, and that citizens wishing to submit statements might do so *in writing, subject to administrative review*. The word *review* did half Veyra's work for her. Everyone in town understood what review meant now. Review meant ink struck through names. Review meant paper deciding who had existed.

Rosalind carried loaves down the hill to the Trenoweth house first, because Maren Trenoweth had lost two sons—one to the sea, one to a boat that had left at midnight with no name on its manifest—and because Maren's kitchen was where the town's courage had always gone to warm itself.

"They want written statements," Maren said, wiping flour from her hands as if it were blood. "Written by us, read by them, judged by them. They'll have our words before they're done having our names."

"Then we don't give them words," Rosalind said. She set the loaf on the scarred table between them. "We give them voices."

Maren looked at her the way people had learned to look at Rosalind over the past weeks—with a wariness that was half suspicion and half hope. "Say what you mean, girl."

"At the inquiry. When they call the struck-through names from their ledger—" Rosalind kept her voice level, though her hands had gone cold around the bread knife, "—the family of each one stands up. And says the name aloud. Says it back to them. So there's no mistaking whose history is being edited."

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, gulls argued over something in the tide line, and far below, a soldier's boots rang on the harbor stones.

"You'd have us stand," Maren said slowly, "in front of Captain Veyra and her grey coats, and contradict the record."

"I'd have us stand in front of the record," Rosalind said. "Veyra is only holding it."

* * *

Word traveled the way everything traveled in Saltmere: through bread, through nets mended side by side on the shingle, through the churchyard where the women still laid flowers on graves the army's maps marked as *unverified interments*. By evening, Rosalind had spoken to nine households. By the second evening, nineteen.

Not all of them said yes.

Old Tam Pellowe, whose brother's name sat struck through in the requisition ledger—a man who had drowned off the Gullet rocks in the second storm, not smuggled anywhere, simply drowned, and somehow still unreliable—shook his head until his neck creaked. "I'm seventy-one years old, Rosalind Marchetti. I've buried a wife and a brother and most of my friends. I kept my head under this occupation same as everyone. You'll forgive me if I don't lift it now for the pleasure of a captain's frown."

"The record will outlive you either way," Rosalind said gently. "The only question is what it says when it does."

"And the record says nothing good comes of arguing with men with guns."

"The record is being written by a woman with a pen," Rosalind said. "And pens can be answered with voices. That's the whole point of what we're doing, Tam. Not to fight them. To correct them."

He grumbled. He poured her a cup of cider she didn't drink. And three days later, when she passed his cottage on her rounds, she found him practicing under his breath in the yard, saying his brother's name over and over like a man learning a foreign word—*Aldous Pellowe, Aldous Pellowe*—getting the weight of it right.

That was how she knew it would work. Not everyone would come. But some would practice.

* * *

Orsen met her at the lighthouse on the third night, climbing the spiral stair with a covered lantern held low, and she saw before he said anything that he had been rehearsing too—not words but posture, the set of his shoulders, the way he would carry himself into that room.

"They posted the schedule," he said. "Testimony opens in six days. The ledger reading is the second day. They'll go through the entries alphabetically." His jaw worked. "Amberdale isn't in it. But half my parish is."

"Then we go alphabetically too," Rosalind said. "Right along beside them."

She spread her own papers on the lantern room floor—the true list, copied in Orsen's careful hand across forty nights, every name the network had moved, every family sheltered, every boat that had left in the dark carrying someone's daughter or son toward a coast that wasn't watching. Beside it she laid the struck-through copy she'd bribed a clerk's boy to let her see for ten minutes: thirty-one names crossed out in red, each annotated *entry unsubstantiated* or *subject unreliable* or, in four cases, simply *no record corroborates*.

Thirty-one names. She had memorized all of them. She had been dreaming in them.

"Here's how it goes," she said. "When Veyra's clerk reads a struck name, the family stands. Just stands—that's all, at first. Then the eldest says the name aloud, and says: *This person lived. This person was here.* Nothing else. No argument, no speech. If they ask questions, silence. We don't give them material for a new file."

"And if they arrest people for it?"

"Then they arrest grandmothers in front of the whole commission," Rosalind said, "and the inquiry stops being about civilian conduct and starts being about what the victors do to widows who say their dead existed. Veyra is thorough, Orsen. Thorough people count costs. We're going to make sure the cost of every silenced voice is visible."

Orsen turned the pages slowly, lamplight catching the ink. "You've thought about me," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"You present the true ledger. Both ledgers—their copy and ours, side by side, so the comparison does the talking. When they ask your name for the record—" she looked up at him, and the beam wheeled overhead, sweeping its light across the sea and the dark and back again, "—you say it plainly. Orsen Amberdale. Keeper of the Saltmere light. No hedging, no half-name, none of the quietness you've hidden inside for seven years."

He was silent a long moment. The lamp above them hissed and turned, hissed and turned.

"I haven't said my own full name aloud since Mera died," he said finally. "Isn't that a strange thing. Seven years of signing myself O. Amberdale, speaking when spoken to, standing at the back of every gathering—and now you're asking me to walk into a room full of the people who burned names out of a book and introduce myself."

"I'm asking you to testify," Rosalind said. "Introducing yourself is just the first sentence of it."

* * *

On the fifth night, she called the meeting.

There was no safe way to gather twenty households under occupation, so she didn't try. She announced it as a net-mending bee on the shingle below the churchyard wall, and the town came the way towns come to funerals and festivals—slowly, in ones and twos, glancing over their shoulders, until the beach was crowded with forty souls and the smell of tar and the rasp of hemp, and the grey coats on the harbor road saw nothing but a fishing town doing what fishing towns do.

Rosalind stood on an overturned dinghy, and the sea wind pulled at her hair, and she felt—for the first time since the army had marched in—the strange lightness of not hiding.

"You know why we're here," she said. She didn't raise her voice; she didn't need to. The shingle carried sound the way a drum carries it. "In six days, strangers are going to sit in the customs house and read a book that tells them who we are. Some of us are in that book. Some of our dead are in it, with lines drawn through their names, as if the storms hadn't taken enough already."

Nobody spoke. A child near the waterline stopped throwing stones.

"I'm not asking anyone to fight," Rosalind went on. "I'm not asking anyone to lie, or smuggle, or run messages in the night—I've done enough of that for all of us. I'm asking something harder and simpler. When they read a struck-through name, the family of that name stands up and says it back. That's all. One name, one voice, one moment. They have pens. We have throats. Let's see which lasts longer."

An old woman near the fire—Widow Casker, whose grandson's name had been struck with the note *no record corroborates*, though Rosalind herself had put that boy on a boat with her own hands—rose to her feet with the slow dignity of a ship coming about.

"Aldous Pellowe," she said, testing the wrong name first, then laughing at herself, wet-eyed. "No. Tam, forgive me. *Edmund Casker.* Edmund Casker, born in the house on Rope Walk, drowned in no sea at all but delivered safe to a shore I'll never see, and God keep him, and let them write *that* in their book."

And then Tam Pellowe stood, steadying himself against the churchyard wall, and said, "Aldous Pellowe. My brother. Drowned honest, buried honest, and no red line has the right to him."

Then Maren Trenoweth, both sons' names, one after the other, her voice cracking only on the second. Then the Brays, the Quillens, young Josper Hale who had ferried three crossings himself and named the people he'd carried like a man reciting a prayer he'd finally been permitted to pray. Name after name, rising off the shingle into the salt wind, and Rosalind stood on her overturned boat and counted them as they came, and by the end her face was soaked and she couldn't have said whether from spray or otherwise.

Forty-one voices, when it was done. Forty-one names practiced against the dark.

Orsen stood at the edge of the firelight, and when their eyes met across the crowd he nodded once—small, steady, keeper's nod—and she understood it for what it was. Not *I love you*, though it was that too. It was *the light holds. Say your names. I'll hold the light.*

* * *

The last night before the inquiry, Rosalind walked the town one final time, checking her work the way she'd once checked escape routes: the loose shutter here, the sympathetic ear there, the boy who carried messages between houses and needed reminding that this week he carried nothing at all. Silence was part of the plan too. The town had to look ordinary until the moment it chose not to be.

At the customs house she paused across the street, hood up, invisible as ever, and watched lanterns moving behind the windows where clerks prepared the chamber. Through the gap in the shutters she saw Captain Veyra at a long table, alone even among her own officers, bent over papers with a straight-backed stillness Rosalind had come to recognize the way sailors recognized weather.

She had spent the occupation hating that woman in the abstract way one hates a flood. Somewhere in the past months the hatred had grown more complicated, and Rosalind distrusted complications. But she understood this much: Veyra believed in the record. It was the one thing they shared, the ground they would meet on tomorrow. Veyra thought the record belonged to whoever held the pen.

Tomorrow, forty-one families were going to teach her otherwise.

Rosalind pulled her hood lower and turned for home, and behind her, in the customs house, a clerk's voice called the captain's attention to something on the table—something brought in late, wrapped in oilcloth, that had not been on any inventory she'd seen.

She was halfway up the hill before the wrongness of it caught her, and she stopped, and stood very still in the wind.

She had not told anyone outside the shingle meeting where the true ledger would sleep tonight.
