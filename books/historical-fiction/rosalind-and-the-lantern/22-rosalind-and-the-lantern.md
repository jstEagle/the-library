## 22. Rosalind and the Lantern

The storms came later now, or perhaps Orsen only felt them later. Age had a way of softening the sea's approach the way a good coat softened the wind—still there, still cold at the edges, but no longer something that went through you to the bone.

He stood in the lantern room with his hand on the brass rail, watching Rosalind work.

She was sixty-one and moved like she was forty, quick and certain, her grey-streaked hair pinned up out of the way as she trimmed the wick with the small silver scissors that had been Maren's before they were hers. The lamp caught her face from below, throwing warm light up along her jaw. She did not look at him. She never did, on these evenings, until the flame was lit. It was a kind of ceremony, though neither of them would have called it that. Ceremony was a word for churches and inquiries. This was just what they did.

"Glass needs a cloth on the north side," she said. "There's salt haze."

"There's always salt haze."

"Then there's always a cloth."

Orsen took the cloth from its hook and wiped down the north pane, slow, feeling the grit of the sea under the wool. Outside, the sky over Saltmere was going the color of pewter, the last blue draining out of it toward the headland. Below, the town was already gathering. He could see them from here—the little procession of lanterns coming up the cliff path, families walking two and two, children running ahead and being called back. Storm-season eve. Thirty years since the year of the great storms, and the town had not missed one.

"You're smiling," Rosalind said without turning around.

"I'm wiping glass."

"You're smiling *at* glass."

He folded the cloth and hung it back on its hook. "Thirty years," he said.

"Mm." She struck the match.

The flame took, and she fed it to the lamp, and the great lens gathered it and turned it into the beam—the same beam, the same old turning heart of the lighthouse, sweeping out over the darkening water. Somewhere past the point where the light reached, the fishing fleet would see it and know their way home. That part never changed either. Wars came and requisitions came and historians came, and the light turned anyway, because someone had to keep it, and keeping things was what Amberdales did.

What had changed was everything else.

* * *

They came early, as they always did, to have the ledger open before the townsfolk arrived. Rosalind carried it herself—she always carried it herself; she said a record should travel in the arms of the person answerable for it—and set it on the reading stand by the window, where the last daylight could reach the pages.

It was not the parish ledger. The parish ledger, the original, the one Captain Veyra had returned with names struck through like birds shot off a fence, sat sealed in the archive at the provincial capital, evidence of an inquiry concluded long ago, preserved by the very bureaucracy that had tried to unmake it. There was an irony there that Rosalind appreciated more than Orsen did. She liked to say that the surest way to make the victors keep a thing was to declare it dangerous.

This was the second ledger. Her ledger. The one that had lived in the lantern room through the whole war, wrapped in oilcloth behind the spare prism panels, holding every name: the drowned, the smuggled, the hidden, the saved. Every route, every date, every boat. Written in her hand first, then in his, then in both their hands together through those nights of ink and lamplight while Veyra's officers walked the streets below.

Now it held thirty years more.

Rosalind untied the cord and opened it flat, smoothing the page with two fingers, and Orsen watched her do what she always did first: find the earliest entries, the ones in faded brown ink, and rest her fingertips on them a moment. Maren's friends. The first boat out. Names of people who had trusted a woman who ran bread and messages and, eventually, human lives across a hostile coast, and who had mostly died not knowing how many they had saved.

"They're all still here," he said.

"They're all still here." She closed her eyes briefly. "Every year I expect one to be missing. Every year I check."

"And every year you find them."

"Superstition," she said, opening her eyes, and smiled at him, and it was the same smile from the lantern room all those storm-seasons ago when they'd copied pages by lamplight with soldiers hammering on doors below—tired, fierce, lit from underneath. "Don't tell anyone the keeper of the record is superstitious."

"The keeper of the record can be anything she likes. She's earned it."

Below, the door banged, and footsteps clattered up the stair, and the children arrived.

* * *

They came up in a flood of noise and cold air—the Merrow children first, always first, three of them red-cheeked and breathless, followed by the Hollis boy who stammered but refused to be left behind, and the little Dane girl whose grandmother had been pulled from the sea by a Saltmere fisherman twenty years before any of this generation was born. Behind them the parents, and behind the parents everyone else, filling the gallery and pressing into the service room below, faces tilted up toward the light. Old Tam Breckley, eighty now, deaf in one ear, who took his place against the wall every single year and stood through the entire reading without sitting. Widow Anselm. The ferryman's family. Half the town, it seemed, and more every year, because every year there were more descendants of the saved.

Rosalind waited for quiet the way she always did—not by asking for it, but by simply standing at the stand with her hands on the ledger until the noise wore itself out against her silence. Orsen had seen her use the same trick on a room full of inquiry officials once, and it worked just as well on children.

"Storm-season eve," she said, and her voice carried the way voices carry in a lantern room, round and clear. "Same as last year. Same as the year before. We read the names."

"We read the names," said the crowd, the response worn smooth as beach glass from thirty years of saying it.

"We read them so that no one can ever say they weren't here. Not a king, not an army, not a historian with a clean pen and a dirty conscience." A ripple of laughter; some of the older ones knew exactly which historian she meant, and knew too that Captain Veyra—retired, half-forgotten, living out her days in a house full of books two provinces away—had been the one who entered both ledgers into the record and saved them. Rosalind had written to her once a year since. Veyra wrote back rarely, briefly, always correcting some minor archival detail. They were, in a way Orsen found strange and Rosalind found perfectly natural, correspondents.

"Tam," Rosalind said. "You're first. As always."

Old Tam stepped forward, cleared his throat with the gravity of a man opening parliament, and read the first entry aloud. His voice was ruined and magnificent. "*The ninth of Storm-month, in the year of the great storms. Lost to the sea: Joss Merrow, fisherman.*"

"*Saved by the routes:*" the crowd answered, in the antiphon the town had made of it, "*Marta Merrow, his wife, and child.*"

Down the line it went. Each family with a name in the book stood and spoke it—a descendant reading a drowned ancestor's name, then the crowd answering with the saved. The stammering Hollis boy got through his grandfather's name on the second try, red-faced and triumphant, and the gallery applauded until Rosalind raised a hand. The little Dane girl read her grandmother's rescuer's name, a fisherman dead fifty years, and pronounced it carefully wrong, and nobody corrected her, because the name was the thing and the sound of it was only the vessel.

Orsen stood by the stair and listened.

He had heard these names hundreds of times. He could recite pages of the ledger himself, had done so on the nights Rosalind was ill, had done it badly and been forgiven. But every year, hearing them spoken aloud in a room full of living people—the drowned named by their grandchildren, the smuggled named by their children who existed only because a woman had rowed them out through a minefield of weather and patrol boats—every year it landed in him fresh, like cold water. Paper holds more danger than the sea, he had thought once, young and afraid, watching ink dry. He had been right about the danger and wrong about everything else. Paper also holds more life than the sea. The sea takes and gives back nothing. Paper gives back everything, forever, if someone keeps it.

Rosalind's voice rose over the last entries, taking the oldest ones herself, the ones with no descendants left to speak them. She read each name slowly, giving it its full weight, and after each one the beam swept the windows and the crowd answered, and outside the dark sea went on doing what the dark sea does, indifferent, enormous, and thoroughly, permanently defeated on this one narrow point.

* * *

Afterward, when the gallery had emptied and the children had been sent home with honey-cakes and salt in their hair, Orsen climbed back up to find her standing at the open ledger, not reading, just looking at it.

"You're at the end of the book," he said.

"I'm at the end of the book." She turned a page, and another, to the last written leaf. "I've been avoiding it all evening."

"The final entry?"

"Mm." She didn't look up. "I've been thinking about what goes there. For about ten years, if I'm honest. Every year I think, this is the year I'll write it, and every year I find a reason to leave the page blank. Blank pages are useful. You never know who'll need to write in a book after you're done with it."

"Anyone who comes after us will need a new book."

"That's not true and you know it. This book isn't finished. It's never finished. That's rather the point of it." She touched the blank leaf. "But there's a difference between unfinished and unsigned. And I've decided—" she stopped, and laughed at herself, quietly. "I've decided I've been a coward about one page for a decade, which is embarrassing in a woman who once lied to an entire requisition office."

"You lied to one captain."

"She was the entire requisition office, effectively." Rosalind finally looked up at him. "Write it with me."

His chest did something complicated. "Write what?"

"What do you think? The truth. In our own hands, in our own book, on the record." She dipped the pen, held it out to him. "Two names. One light. That's what it's all been, hasn't it? Everything we did, everything Maren started, everything the town carried—it all comes down to people keeping a light for other people, and writing down who they were. So write it. You're the one who taught me that testimony has to be in your own name to count for anything."

He took the pen. His hand was not steady, and he let it not be.

He bent over the page and wrote, in the careful clerk's hand the parish records had taught him thirty years ago:

*Final entry for this volume, being none, for the record continues elsewhere. Entered for remembrance: Orsen Amberdale, keeper of the light, who kept his head down until keeping it down became the greater disloyalty. And Rosalind Amberdale-Marchetti, keeper of the record, who was a ghost in this house and a ghost on no paper, until the paper learned to say her name.*

He paused. Then, beneath, smaller:

*Two names. One light. Read aloud every storm-season eve, so long as anyone remains to read.*

Rosalind read it twice. When she looked up her eyes were wet and she was entirely unashamed of it, which was also, Orsen thought, rather the point of her.

"You put yourself second," she said.

"I put myself after the light."

"You always did." She signed beneath him, her hand swift and sure, and blotted the page, and closed the book, and tied the cord. Then she stood there a moment with her palms flat on the cover, as if she could feel the heartbeat of everything inside it—all those names, all those boats, all those nights of ink and lamplight and fear and stubbornness—and Orsen put his hand over hers and felt it too, or believed he did, which amounted to the same thing.

Outside, the beam turned. Far below, the sea breathed against the rocks. Somewhere beyond the light's reach the fleet was coming home, and the first drops of the season's first storm ticked against the glass, patient, familiar, almost gentle.

"Do you hear that?" Rosalind asked.

"Rain."

"The beginning of it. The front's earlier than they said." She lifted her chin, listening, and he saw the change come over her face—the old alertness, the look she used to get when a courier knocked after dark—and then soften into something new, something that belonged to this older, quieter life and yet had never really left her. "It'll be a bad one. Maybe the worst in years."

"The fleet's already in."

"Not all of it. The *Kestrel* ran supply up the coast—she won't make harbor before midnight." Rosalind turned to the lamp, checked the oil gauge with a glance as quick as breathing, and began rolling up her sleeves. "And the Merrow girl is on watch at the point tonight, alone, and she's never sat through a real blow."

Orsen looked at his wife of nineteen years, standing in the lantern room with the storm coming and the ledger signed and the light burning, already reaching for her oilskins, and he felt the old familiar turn inside himself—the one that had ended his invisibility, cost him everything, and given him back more than it took. He picked up the second trimmer.

"Well then," he said. "We'd better keep the light burning."

The beam swept out over the dark water, steady as a promise, and went on turning.
