## 1. The Weight of Glass

The lamp wanted trimming at four, same as it always did, and Sable Almeida was awake before the alarm could argue with her about it.

She had learned years ago that grief and routine were made of the same material — repetition, mostly — and that one could be dressed up to look like the other if you kept your sleeves rolled and your eyes forward. So she rolled her sleeves now, in the cold dark of the oil room, and lifted the burner assembly free of its housing with hands that knew the weight of every part by heart.

The wick had burned unevenly on the night watch. She could tell before she even got the lens cap off; there was a smudge in the beam's edge, a softness where there should have been a blade of light. She trimmed it square with the little brass cutters Edric had bought in Greywater town the spring Rosalind was born, back when buying things for the light still felt like buying things for their future.

"Uneven burn," she said aloud, because the oil room was the one place she allowed herself a voice. "Carbon buildup on the inner chimney."

The oil room said nothing back. It never had, even when Edric was alive. He'd been a man who worked in silence the way other men worked in conversation, and she had loved him for it right up until the silences started meaning something else.

She wiped the chimney glass with the soft rag kept for that purpose alone, turned it under the lamplight to check for cracks, and found none. Good glass. The good kind, the heavy kind, ground thick enough to hold its shape against a storm that meant to shake it loose. Everything at Greywater Point came down eventually to the weight of glass — how much it could carry, how much it could take, what happened when it broke.

Sable carried the trimmed burner up the ninety-one iron steps to the lantern room, counting them out of habit rather than need. Ninety-one. Edric used to say there were ninety-two and she would count twice and prove him wrong and he would grin and say he knew, he only liked watching her count.

Two winters gone, and she still caught herself braced for the grin.

* * *

By six the wind had come around to the northeast, which meant weather, which meant she wrote it down.

The logbook lay open on the desk in the watch room, its pages ruled in faded ink lines, and Sable dipped the pen and set down the morning's truth in her small, level hand:

*Wind NE, freshening. Barometer falling since midnight, 29.4 and steady drop. Sea moderate, building. Glass clear.*

Glass clear. She almost smiled at that. The whole language of lighthouse keeping was built out of words that meant something else entirely to everyone who didn't keep them. *Glass clear* — as if clarity were a thing you could log, as if she hadn't spent two years learning that clarity was a thing you manufactured, hour by hour, wick by wick, whether or not you felt it.

Below her, the house attached to the tower was beginning to make its own noises: floorboards ticking as the stove heat reached them, the kettle starting its low argument in the kitchen. And then footsteps, quick ones, and Rosalind came up through the hatch with her hair unbrushed and her collar crooked and her eyes already fully awake, because fourteen-year-olds are born knowing things their mothers have forgotten.

"The barometer's falling," Rosalind said. Not a question. She'd taken to reading the instrument on the kitchen wall the way other girls read serial fiction — daily, hungrily, looking for the turn in the story.

"It is."

"How far?"

"Since midnight? A tenth and dropping."

Rosalind considered this with a seriousness that Sable found both wonderful and slightly alarming. "Ferry'll run today, then. Maybe tomorrow too. After that—" She shrugged, elaborate and casual, the way she did when she cared most. "Ice season."

"Ice season," Sable agreed, and wrote another line in the log so she wouldn't have to look at her daughter's face while they both pretended this was an ordinary thing to discuss. Every winter the river closed. Every winter Greywater Point became less a place on the map and more a lit candle at the end of the world, and the Almeidas kept the candle. That was all. That was always all.

"There's eggs," Rosalind said. "I mean, there's three. I gave you the big one."

"You gave me the big one."

"You're climbing stairs all day." Rosalind said it like it was arithmetic, not tenderness, and was already turning for the hatch, and Sable watched her go and thought: *Edric in miniature.* Not in the face, particularly — Rosalind favored her own mother in the jaw, in the wide flat brow — but in the way she moved through rooms like someone with somewhere better to be, and the way she rationed her kindness into units small enough not to be refused.

Downstairs, the eggs were exactly as advertised, and the kettle had won its argument, and for eleven minutes the kitchen held the three of them — Sable, Rosalind, and the empty chair — without anyone remarking on the third party present.

That was the arrangement. That was the treaty they'd signed in silence the first winter and ratified every day since. The chair stayed at the table because removing it would be an event, and events required words, and words about Edric were the one cargo Sable had never figured out how to load.

* * *

The storms took him on the ninth of November, two years past, though *took* was doing generous work in that sentence. What actually happened was smaller and stupider than the word allowed.

A late-season blow had come down the river valley with no manners and no warning worth the name, and the Meridian freight boats — the big ones, the ones that ran coal and timber down to the salt despite every prudent voice on the coast saying the season was done — had put out anyway, because Meridian put out anyway, because schedules were money and money did not read barometers.

One of them, the *Corvus*, lost steering in the white water off the point and began drifting broadside toward the shallows. Standard procedure said stand off, signal, let the harbor tugs work the problem. But Edric Almeida had spent twenty years pulling men out of that exact stretch of river, and his boat — the *Kindly*, forty feet of oak and stubbornness — went out with two volunteers and a tow line into weather that the gulls themselves had abandoned.

They got the line aboard. Witnesses on the cliff swore to it afterward: the *Corvus* steadied, swung her bow toward the channel, began to come round. And then a following sea took the *Kindly* on the quarter and rolled her, and the river — grey, freezing, indifferent — folded the whole business shut.

They recovered the boat three days later, stove-in, half-buried in the shingle below the north rocks. They recovered one of the volunteers. Edric and the other man they did not recover at all; the river kept them, and keeps them still, somewhere out in the deep cold past the bar where Sable's beam swept over them every seven seconds for two years without once bringing them home.

Afterward, the town had called him a hero in the newspaper, in the churchyard, over the counter at the chandlery, and Sable had stood in all those places with her hat in her hands and let them say it, because it was true, and because the true version left no room for the other questions — the small, disloyal, 3 a.m. questions that lived in her like splinters working toward the skin.

Why had he gone out against his own standing rule, the one he'd carved into the doorframe of the boathouse himself: *NO LINES IN RUNNING SEAS*?

Why had the *Corvus*'s manifest, when it finally surfaced, listed cargo that didn't match what the harbor records said she carried?

And why, in the weeks before he died, had Edric Almeida — silent, steady, legible-as-a-logbook Edric — begun to seem, to the woman who shared his bed and his table and his ninety-one stairs, like a man rehearsing a speech?

She had asked him once. Only once. A Tuesday evening, October, three weeks before the end. He was mending net in the kitchen and she'd come in from the lamp room and found him stopped mid-stitch, staring at his hands as if they belonged to the inquiry into some other man's drowning.

"You're somewhere else lately," she'd said, lightly, hanging her coat. "Where is it?"

And Edric had looked up at her with an expression she had turned over ten thousand times since — not guilt, exactly, and not fear, but something adjacent to both, the face of a man standing at a door he has just realized opens outward onto a very long drop — and he had said:

"Nowhere I can't walk back from, Sable. Give me till the ice breaks."

Then he'd smiled, and finished his stitch, and the moment had folded itself away into the ordinary fabric of their days like a coin dropped into deep water — visible for one bright turning second, then gone.

He never got till the ice breaks. The ice came early that year, after all. It came up out of the river and took him instead.

* * *

At noon the wind confirmed its intentions, and Sable logged them.

*Wind NE strong. Barometer 29.1. Sea rising; spray over north jetty. Visibility good between squalls. Ferry service expected suspended by evening.*

She wrote it in the level hand, blotted it, squared the book on the shelf between its brothers — thirty years of Greywater Point weather in a row of green spines, her mother-in-law's hand fading into Edric's hand, Edric's hand ending mid-page two Novembers ago and hers beginning, the transition so clean a stranger would never find the seam.

Only a stranger would never find the seam. She found it every time she opened a volume. Her handwriting was neater than his; that was all anyone would see. But she could feel the seam, the way you feel a mend in a garment, and some mornings she resented it — resented being the continuation, the spare pen, the hand that kept the record going after the record-keeper went into the river.

Most mornings, though — most mornings the logbook was simply the day's first honest conversation, and she was grateful for a listener that never interrupted and never needed feeding.

Rosalind appeared at the watch room door at half past twelve with bread and cheese on a plate and the ferry schedule folded in her fist.

"They posted the winter notice," she said. "Early this year. Last crossing Saturday, weather permitting. After that, nothing till the ice breaks. Nothing, Mama — not mail, not supply, nothing."

Sable took the paper. The print was the usual harbor-office boilerplate, but the date was wrong — or not wrong, precisely, but ahead of itself. Last year the last crossing had been the third week of December. This year the notice said the second week of December, with a line beneath about *extraordinary ice conditions forecast*, and a signature from the harbormaster's office that she didn't recognize.

"That's early," she said.

"Ten days early." Rosalind sat on the window ledge, knees drawn up, and looked out at the water as if she could read the forecast in it directly. "Papa used to say the river tells you what it's going to do before it does it. You just have to listen."

"He said that?"

"About everything. Ice, weather, people." Rosalind picked at the hem of her sleeve. "You don't talk about him much, so sometimes I say his sentences instead. Just to keep them in circulation."

The sentence landed in Sable's chest and sat there, heavy as ballast. She set down the schedule. "You can always talk about him with me."

"I know." Rosalind said it fast, the way you close a door on a draft. Then, slower, watching the water: "There's a supply boat coming Thursday. Before the freeze. Mr. Quill at the chandlery sent word up the coast path — extra oil, extra glass."

"Extra glass?"

"For the lantern. Two spare panes instead of one." Rosalind turned, and there it was again, that look — sharp-eyed, restless, a girl assembling a picture out of pieces the adults kept moving. "Why would they send double glass, Mama, unless somebody thinks we're going to need it?"

"Because it's going to be a hard winter, and glass is cheap insurance."

"Nobody sends cheap insurance ten days early and doubles it." Rosalind unfolded herself from the ledge and paused at the door, and for a moment Sable saw her husband's expression cross their daughter's face — that same look of a person at a door, weighing a drop. "You've been different since the notice went up. Since before that, even. Since the equinox. You sleep in the chair. You check the oil room twice a night when once is regulation. I count your footsteps on the stairs, Mama. I always have."

"Rosalind—"

"I'm not asking tonight." She said it gently, which was somehow worse than if she'd demanded. "But the river tells you what it's going to do before it does it. You taught me that. Or he did. One of you did."

Her footsteps went down the iron stairs, quick and light, ninety-one of them, and Sable stood alone in the watch room with the schedule in her hand and the wind shouldering the glass like a creditor at the door.

* * *

Evening came down early, the way it did in the shortening days, and at dusk Sable climbed to light the lamp.

This was the part of the day that had survived the drowning intact — the one ceremony grief had failed to confiscate. She lit the wick, watched the flame take the mantle's shape, wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and stepped back as the beam gathered itself and went out across the darkening water: one flash every seven seconds, white, steady, the oldest promise on the coast.

*Here is the shore,* said the light. *Here is where the land decides to stop pretending it's anything else. Come no closer than that.*

She stayed a while in the lantern room, as she often did, hands on the rail, watching the beam swing its slow arc over the black river. Out past the bar, the water was building. Somewhere under it, in the cold and the current, the river kept what it kept.

"Give me till the ice breaks," she said to the glass, quietly, testing the words against the two-year weight of them. "Well, love. The ice is coming early this year. Whatever you were going to say —"

The lamp turned. The beam crossed the water, crossed the north rocks where the *Kindly* had come ashore in pieces, crossed the shingle at the base of the point —

— and for half a heartbeat, in the sweep of it, Sable saw something pale lying at the tideline below the north jetty. A shape. Box-like. New-come, because yesterday that stretch of beach had been bare as a scraped bone; she logged that beach the way she logged the weather, and she knew its inventory the way she knew her daughter's face.

The tide was going out. Whatever the river had decided to return, it was leaving it there, stranded above the wrack line, patient as a letter waiting to be opened.

Sable stood in the warm circle of lamplight with the cold pressing at the glass all around her, and did not move for a long moment. Then she took the logbook and wrote the evening's entry in her small, level hand — wind, sea, visibility, lamp burning well — and beneath it, after a pause, added one more line, the first entry in two years that wasn't about weather at all:

*Object washed up below N. jetty. To investigate at first light.*

She blotted it. She squared the book on the shelf.

And all night, as the wind rose and the beam turned and turned, the keeper of the Greywater Point light lay in her narrow bed listening to the tide fall, and understood — the way you understand a barometer dropping — that the river had begun telling her what it was going to do.
