## 3. The Ledger in the Oil Room

The oil room smelled the way it always did: kerosene and cold iron and the ghost of every winter Sable had spent inside it. She went down after supper, while Rosalind worked sums at the kitchen table under a lamp that flickered more than Sable liked to admit. The wick needed trimming again. Everything in this house needed something.

She told herself she was going down for oil. That was true, in the way most lies are true — she carried the brass can in one hand and let the other trail along the stone wall as she took the stairs, counting her steps out of habit. Eleven from the top landing to the door. The door stuck in damp weather; tonight was dry enough that it opened on the first pull, groaning anyway, because the hinges hadn't been oiled since Edric died and Sable had never gotten around to it. Some silences you kept because they were comfortable.

Two winters since the Meridian went down off the point with eleven men aboard. Two winters since the inquiries came and went, since the papers called it weather, since the shipping company paid its settlements quietly through lawyers whose names nobody at Greywater could pronounce. Two winters since Edric's boat was found empty, hull stove in on the shoals, and the sea declined to explain itself.

Sable filled the can. The pump gurgled. She capped it, set it down, and stood there a moment in the lamplight doing nothing, which was how she'd been spending most of her evenings lately.

The torn pages were upstairs, wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of her sewing basket, beneath the buttons Rosalind would never miss. She'd read them nine times now. Every time, the same three sentences snagged like fishhooks: *received of M.S. Co., per arrangement. For silence kept. E.A.*

Edric's hand. Unmistakably. The man she'd watched write grocery lists and tide logs for twelve years, the man who pressed so hard his letters left grooves you could feel with a fingertip. *For silence kept.* Nine times she'd read them, and nine times some part of her had said: pages tear loose. Water carries things. Maybe he found them. Maybe he was going to turn them over.

Maybe. The word had been doing all the work lately, hauling loads it wasn't built for.

She should have gone straight back up. Instead she found herself standing at the back of the oil room, looking at the floor.

The oil store proper sat against the north wall — a raised timber platform, built by the lighthouse board's engineers before Sable's time, designed to keep the drums off the stone in case of flooding. There was a gap underneath, six inches of shadow between the platform and the floor, swept clean because everything here was swept clean. Edric had been particular about the oil room. Particular about it in a way he'd never been particular about anything else in the house. She'd teased him once, early on — *you love this room more than me* — and he'd laughed, but not with his whole chest, the way he laughed at other things. She remembered that now. She remembered it the way you remember a splinter only when it moves.

She crouched. Held the lamp low.

Dust lay everywhere except in a long rectangle near the middle of the platform's edge, where the boards met the stone — a patch of floor slightly cleaner than its neighbors, scuffed at two corners. A drag mark. Old, but there. Something heavy had come out from under there and gone back in, or gone in and never come out.

Her heart began a slow, stupid knocking, like someone testing a door they weren't sure they wanted opened.

"Sable," she said aloud, to the empty room, "don't."

She reached in anyway.

* * *

The boards were fixed. She learned this quickly, kneeling on cold stone with her arm buried to the shoulder in the dark under the platform, fingers walking the underside of the timber. But the second board from the end — the one above the clean patch — shifted. Not loose, exactly. Lifted. Someone had cut the pegs and refitted it so carefully that only a person who knew the platform's voice would hear the difference, and then hidden the knowledge under a skin of dust.

There was no pry bar in the room. She used the flat of a chisel from the maintenance shelf, working it into the seam, and the board came up with a sound like a held breath released.

Beneath it: a cavity lined with tarred canvas. And in the cavity, wrapped in more of the same, a book.

Not papers. Not loose leaves. A ledger — bound in grey board, spine cracked, tied shut with waxed cord in a knot she recognized because Edric had used it to tie off the dinghy line every single morning of their married life. Her hands shook so badly she had to set the lamp down on the floor to untie it.

The cover bore no title. Inside, the first page bore only a date — two winters past, the spring before the Meridian sank — and beneath it, in Edric's cramped, hard-pressing hand:

*Meridian Shipping Co. Greywater agency. Accounts current.*

Accounts. She almost laughed. Almost. It came out of her as a sound like a cough, alone in the oil room with the sea muttering beyond the wall.

She turned the page.

*April 9th. Received of M.S. Co., per arrangement, £14. For statements collected and burned. Silence kept re: cargo manifest discrepancy, voyage of the Corinne L.*

She stared at it. Read it again. *Statements collected and burned.* Witness statements — she knew what those were, everyone on this coast knew, because the inquiry into the Corinne L.'s sister loss years ago had made the word a kind of curse among fishing families. Statements were what widows gave. Statements were what companies made disappear.

*May 2nd. Received £20. For silence kept re: testimony of T. Voss, pilot. Paid also to T.V., £6, his portion.*

T. Voss. Tomas Voss, who had piloted the Meridian boats through the narrows for twenty years, who had drowned with the rest of them when the Meridian broke up. Whose widow still sold mussels on the quay and wouldn't look anyone in the eye anymore.

*June 17th. Received £14. Silence kept re: surveyor's note re: hull plates, thin-gauge substitution. Note destroyed.*

Thin-gauge plates. Sable had heard the talk, the way you heard weather talk, half-listening in doorways and on the dock — that the Meridian's hull had been patched with steel too thin for winter runs, that the company knew, that the surveyor who flagged it had suddenly taken work inland. Talk. Nothing anyone could prove. Nothing anyone had ever put in writing, because the people who might have written it down were dead or bought.

Except Edric. Edric had written it down. Dated it. Numbered the payments. Kept accounts, neat as a grocer, on the business of burying men's words.

She turned the pages faster. Entry after entry, month after month, the amounts growing as the seasons turned toward winter — £14, £20, £26 — and the reasons shrinking into shorthand she couldn't always parse but didn't need to: *re: the mate's letter. Re: the boy at the yard. Re: the manifest.* And each entry signed, or initialed, or simply ended with that phrase that had already followed her up from the shore and into her sleep:

*Silence kept.*

The last dated entry was from the week before the Meridian sailed on her final run. *Received of M.S. Co., £30, final settlement re: survey matter. Matter closed.* Matter closed. Three weeks later the ship went down with eleven men, and the company paid its quiet settlements, and the inquiry called it weather.

And then nothing. No entries after that. As if whatever Edric had been doing had ended with the wreck — or as if he'd stopped recording it somewhere else, somewhere she hadn't found yet.

She sat back on her heels on the cold stone floor and looked at the ledger lying open across her knees, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the waterlogged pages on the shore had not washed away from this book. This book had never touched water. The pages in her sewing basket were separate — loose, torn out, sent or lost or hidden elsewhere. Which meant Edric had handled both. Had torn pages free and done something with them. Had hidden this one under the oil like a man caching coins against a bad season.

Which meant he'd known exactly what he was keeping. And meant to keep it.

* * *

She heard Rosalind's footsteps overhead — the particular rhythm of her crossing to the pantry, the clink of the tin lid, the pause. Fourteen years old and sharp as broken glass, that girl. If Sable stayed down here another quarter hour she'd come looking, and there were questions Sable could answer and questions she couldn't, and the ledger was the second kind.

She wrapped it back in the tarred canvas. Stopped.

Burn it.

The thought arrived simple as arithmetic. There was a stove right there — the little iron one Edric had rigged for warming the oil in hard freezes, its flue running up through the wall. Kerosene within arm's reach. The book would go up in ten minutes, and the ash would be ash, and the name Almeida would be just a keeper's name again, poor and unimportant, the kind of family inquiries skimmed past on their way to richer game.

No one would ever know. That was the terrible part — how easy it was. She had the means, the moment, the privacy. The sea outside would keep its counsel. Rosalind would grow up grieving a father instead of despising one, and maybe that was mercy, and maybe mercy was just a lie you told for someone who couldn't object.

Her hand was actually moving toward the stove when the image stopped her: not fire, not ash — paper. The torn pages in her sewing basket, softened and stained by salt water, ink blooming at the edges. Those pages existed somewhere out in the world apart from this book. Whoever tore them out — whoever sent them loose into the tide — might have copies. Might have more than copies. Papers don't burn twice. If the ledger vanished tonight and a page surfaced tomorrow bearing Edric's initials beside the words *silence kept*, then the Almeidas wouldn't be fools caught holding a bad inheritance. They'd be concealers. Accomplices after the fact. People with something to hide, caught hiding it.

And there was the other thing, the smaller colder thing underneath, the one she was ashamed of even as she admitted it: if she burned the book, she'd never know the whole of it. Never know why. Never know whether Edric was a coward or a prisoner or a man saving evidence the only way a lightkeeper could — by sitting on it, year after year, waiting for courage or an opening. *He meant to do something with it.* She had no proof of that. She had only twelve years of marriage and a stubborn refusal to believe that the man who taught Rosalind to gut fish gently had sold eleven families their grief without a reason that would shame him.

You can't judge a man from one page. She'd need all of them.

So: she wouldn't burn it, and she wouldn't keep it either — not in the house, not where she'd have to look at it, not where Rosalind's restless eyes might find it. Back under the board. Let the oil room keep the secret it had apparently been keeping all along. She was only borrowing the truth, she told herself. Holding it in trust until she understood whose it really was.

She wrapped the ledger, laid it in the cavity, fitted the board down until it sat flush and silent, and swept the dust back over the seam with the side of her palm. Then she knelt there a moment longer, listening to the building breathe.

It occurred to her, finally, plainly, that she was doing exactly what Edric had done. Hiding a thing under the floor and sweeping it clean and calling the sweeping tidiness. Grief, she thought, rising stiffly, joints cracking, was just another kind of silence kept.

* * *

Upstairs, the kitchen was warm and yellow and ordinary. Rosalind had finished her sums and moved on to sketching the ferry schedule from memory onto the back of an old chart, annotating it with sailing times she considered wrong, her lips moving as she worked. She looked up when Sable came in, and her eyes did the thing they'd been doing all week — the quick inventory, mother's face, mother's hands, the empty space where the oil can should be.

"Where's the oil?"

"Filled the reserve drum. We're good through the week." Sable hung her coat on its peg. Her hands were steady. She'd made them steady. "Wick needs cutting tomorrow. I'll do it before school."

"You've been down there a long time," Rosalind said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way she noted everything now, logging her mother like weather. "The oil doesn't take that long."

"Floor needed sweeping."

Rosalind looked at her a beat too long — long enough that Sable felt the lie land somewhere visible, though the girl said nothing, only bent back over her chart. Sable got the kettle going. Her reflection floated in the black window, faint over the dark water, a woman holding a kettle in a warm room with everything, apparently, in order.

Under the floor below her feet, eleven dead men's names sat in a stranger's handwriting, wrapped in canvas, patient as seed.

She thought: I'll read every line tomorrow. I'll understand it. I'll decide what Edric was.

She thought, less steadily: God help us if the wrong person comes asking before I do.

And far off across the water, though she could not have known it, a coastal runner was already beating north through the dark with a sealed letter in his coat, addressed to the keeper at Greywater Point.
