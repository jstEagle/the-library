## 4. Rosalind Counts the Days

My mother had started lying in small ways, and that was how I knew something was wrong.

Big lies announce themselves. Big lies have weight, like a trunk carried down stairs—you hear them coming. But small lies slip through a house like water under a door, and by the time you notice the floor is wet, they've reached every room.

The first one: she stopped singing.

Mama sang while she trimmed wicks. Not songs anyone taught her—she'd made up her own years ago, half tune and half counting, numbers stitched into melody like beads on a string. *Four turns of the glass, five scoops of oil, six knots and a prayer.* I'd heard it my whole life, so constantly that most days it vanished into the background of Greywater Point the way the gulls had, the way the foghorn did. You only notice a sound when it stops.

It stopped on a Tuesday. I know because Tuesdays were bread days, and I was kneading dough at the table with flour to my elbows when I realized the house had gone quiet except for the wind. I looked toward the oil room. The door stood open a hand's width. Mama stood inside it with her back to me, perfectly still, holding something against her chest the way you hold a bird you've caught but haven't decided whether to keep.

She didn't sing again that day. Or the next. By Sunday the silence had become the new song, and I hated it the way you hate anything that replaces something you loved without asking permission.

* * *

The second lie was smaller, and meaner, because it involved me directly.

"Take the mended net down to the rocks," she said that same week, "and see if the tide's left anything worth keeping."

That was normal. That was *our* normal. The sea gave gifts and took payment, and we walked the tideline after every storm like beggars at a rich man's gate, collecting what drifted: lumber, rope, once an entire ship's figurehead that Papa swore looked like his mother. Except Papa wasn't there to swear anymore, and Mama knew as well as I did what the tide left now.

Nothing. The tide left nothing. It had been leaving nothing since the night it took his boat two winters past, and we both kept walking the beach anyway because stopping would mean admitting the sea had closed its account with us.

But that morning she wanted me out of the house. I understood this the way I understood weather—instinct first, explanation later. She watched me from the window as I went, and when I turned at the top of the path she didn't look away fast enough. There's a particular guilt in a person's face when they're caught watching someone leave, and my mother wore it badly. She was a terrible liar. This should have comforted me. It didn't. Terrible liars who keep trying anyway are liars who believe the truth will cost them everything.

I walked down to the rocks. I picked up a piece of driftwood, considered it, put it down. And then I cut back along the gorse line, low, the way I'd learned to move when I didn't want the gulls announcing me, and came up behind the oil room wall where the stone still held the morning's warmth.

Through the gap where the mortar had crumbled—a gap I'd known about since I was nine and had never told her about, because a girl at Greywater Point learns early which secrets are structural—I watched my mother kneel beside the oil store and slide aside a board that I had never once seen move.

What she lifted out, I couldn't see clearly. A book. A thick one. She held it open on her knees for a long time, turning pages slowly, and twice she pressed her hand flat against the paper as if the pages were trying to escape and she meant to pin them there.

Then she wrapped it in oilcloth, put it back beneath the floor, replaced the board, and sat in the middle of the oil room floor for another quarter hour doing absolutely nothing, which frightened me more than everything else combined. My mother did not do nothing. My mother was a machine of small necessary motions, wicks and logs and loaves and lamps. A still Sable Almeida was a lighthouse with the lamp dark.

* * *

Here is what people get wrong about being fourteen: they think it's the waiting that's hard. It isn't. Waiting is easy if you trust the thing you're waiting for. What's hard is the arithmetic—the constant, involuntary counting of everything, measuring yourself against everyone, adding up what you have and subtracting what you've lost and carrying the remainder around like stones in both pockets.

I counted everything that week.

I counted that Mama burned the kitchen fire hotter than usual, though coal was dear. I counted that she read the ferry notices board twice when we went into Greywater village for flour, standing in front of it with her basket on her hip, and that she wrote something on the back of her hand with the stub of pencil she kept in her cuff.

And I counted this: the winter schedule was posted early.

Everyone at Greywater Point knows the rhythm of the postings. The evacuation schedule goes up after the first hard freeze, never before—not because the ferry masters are careless but because the ice is honest and doesn't keep to paperwork. You post the schedule when the river tells you to, not before. Old Marta Kessel, who has run the toll house longer than the bridge has existed, says the paper schedule is a courtesy and the ice is the law.

This year the schedule was nailed to the board before the first frost had even silvered the fence rails. Early by three weeks at least. And it wasn't the standard broadsheet either—it was a fresh printing, sharper ink, the kind that comes off the magistrate's press in the county seat and not the ferry office.

One berth per household. Last crossing before the river closes. That part was ordinary. Households had been reduced to single berths ever since the storms started taking boats and then taking the roads, and everyone had learned to do the arithmetic of families split across water.

But underneath the berth notice, in that sharp official ink, was a line that hadn't been on any schedule I'd ever read:

*Persons named to the winter inquiry must present themselves to the magistrate's office upon arrival or remain subject to summons.*

Inquiry. I said the word over to myself on the walk home, rolling it like a pebble. Inquiry meant questions. Questions meant answers somebody already had and wanted checked. And the notice was posted early, which meant somebody important already knew something was coming this winter, something that needed a list of names.

I thought about the book beneath the oil store floor. I thought about my mother's hand pressed flat against its pages.

* * *

Rosalind Almeida keeps lists. I'll admit it plainly, because Mama always said a keeper's daughter should be able to state a fact without flinching. Here is my list of things I knew for certain that autumn:

1. Papa's boat went down two winters ago with three other men aboard, and the sea returned none of them.
2. Papa kept no ledger that I ever saw. He kept accounts the way he did everything—scratched on slate, wiped clean by Friday.
3. Something written in Papa's hand—or in a hand someone wanted me to believe was Papa's—had come ashore in a crate weeks ago. I saw the crate. I helped burn the broken boards. I did not see inside, because Mama sent me to fetch sand for the path and by the time I came back the papers were gone and her hands smelled of lamp oil.
4. There is now a book under the oil room floor.
5. My mother touches that floor the way other people touch graves.
6. Someone in the county is making lists of names, and my mother copied something off the notice board onto her skin.

Seven items if you count the singing, which I do, because absence is data. A girl raised in a lighthouse learns that what a light *doesn't* show can tell you more than what it does. Dark water between two flashes might be safe passage or might be a shoal, and the only way to know is to have memorized the water when it was lit.

So I began memorizing my mother.

I noted that she wound the clock at odd hours now, though it hadn't drifted. I noted that she'd taken to sitting facing the door at supper, which she'd never done before—Papa used to sit facing the door, sailor's habit, watching for his own return. I noted that when Tobias's name came up in the village—because Tobias, Papa's brother, sends a letter every solstice full of charm and no substance, and Mrs. Kessel's granddaughter asked after him—Mama said "he's well, I'm sure" in a voice with no certainty anywhere in it.

And I noted the night she stood at the kitchen window past midnight with the lamp unlit, watching the road, though nobody traveled the road in November and both of us knew it. I watched her watch nothing for an hour from the stair, and when she finally turned away she pressed her palm flat against the window glass, and the glass kept the shape of her hand for a moment after she let go. Heat leaves a ghost. Everything does, if the surface is right.

* * *

I want to say, in fairness to myself, that I tried not to do what I did next.

There is a version of me—call her the good daughter—who takes her mother's silences at face value, who believes that grief explains everything and that love means not looking through floors. That version of me lasted eleven days. On the twelfth day Mama lied to my face about something so small it barely qualified as a lie, and that was the day the good daughter drowned.

It was Thursday. I'd been to the village; I'd seen Mrs. Kessel; Mrs. Kessel, who misses nothing and pretends to miss everything, had squeezed my wrist at the counter and said, "Your mama's not been in for her sugar this month." Sugar. My mother bought sugar every month the way the tide comes in. We had a tin of it above the stove, half full, which I had assumed meant she'd bought extra.

At supper I said, "We're low on sugar. I could take the eggs in tomorrow and trade."

"We're fine for sugar," Mama said.

Three words. We're fine for sugar. And the tin above the stove was half empty, and she hadn't been to Kessel's in a month, and her eyes stayed on my face exactly one beat too long, the way a person's eyes do when they are checking whether the lie has landed. She wasn't lying about the sugar. She was lying about something adjacent to the sugar, some neighboring country of the truth, and the sugar was just the border post.

I said, "All right," and cleared the dishes, and washed them, and dried them, and put them away, and went up to bed, and lay in the dark listening to the foghorn counting its slow numbers out over the water until the house went quiet enough to creak.

Then I got up.

* * *

The oil room lock was never locked. That was the first absurdity—that she hid the thing in a room with a latch worn smooth from use, as if hiding a book beneath a floorboard were the same as hiding it from a daughter who had been raised on this exact floor. I brought no candle. Keeper's daughters don't need candles in their own houses; we navigate by the beam. Every forty seconds the light swept through the oil room's high window and laid a stripe of white across the wall, and in the dark between sweeps I moved.

The board came up easier than I expected. Easier than it should have—it had been lifted often. The hinges of routine. Underneath: oilcloth, folded with a keeper's neatness, and the weight of it in my hands was wrong, denser than a book that size had any right to be. Dense the way ledgers are dense. Dense with entries.

I climbed the stairs to the lamp room with it under my shirt, because the lamp room was mine. That sounds like a child's claim, but it was true in a way that mattered: Mama kept the light; I kept the lamp room. It was where I went to be alone, where I kept my own collections—sea glass sorted by color, a gull's feather, the brass fitting off Papa's boat that the tide finally gave back last spring, the one gift the sea ever made good on. Nobody came up here. The beam turned overhead and the water turned below and I could open any book in the world and no one would know.

I untied the oilcloth on the gallery floor, in the sweep of the rotating light so I could read by flashes, and the light came around and showed me the cover, and the light went away, and I sat with my hand on the cover in the dark counting—one, two, three, up to forty—and the light came back and I read:

**Almeida.**

Not Papa's name alone. *Almeida*, the household name, our name, the name painted on the boat that sank and stenciled on the oil drums and spoken by schoolmasters with that little pause they give names they're deciding how to treat. Our name was on the cover of a ledger, and under it, smaller, in a different ink: *Greywater Point Station.*

The light swung away. I counted. The light came back.

I opened to the first page.

I am going to be honest, which is more than the book was: I did not understand it, not all at once. Ledger entries look like weather to the untrained—numbers and dates and abbreviations, a language you can read word by word and still miss the storm. Payments. Dates running back three winters. Initials I didn't know. A column headed with a phrase I read four times before it stopped sliding off my eyes:

*For silence kept.*

The light came around. Forty seconds of white. Forty seconds of dark. Somewhere below me my mother slept, or lay awake, or stood at her window watching a road that led nowhere, guarding a book she thought was hidden from a daughter who had inherited her eyes and her patience and her terrible, necessary need to count.

*Silence kept.* Whose silence? Kept from whom? Paid by whom? And why did our name sit on the cover of a book full of other people's hush?

I read until my legs went numb beneath me. I read until the sky at the edge of the windows turned the grey of dishwater, which is the exact color of the hour when the night admits it's losing. I read until I had copied three dates and two sets of initials into the margin of my own notebook, because whatever this was, it was arithmetic, and arithmetic is the one language I have never once failed.

Then I heard the stair door.

Footsteps on the iron treads—slow, deliberate, the tread of a woman who had decided hours ago that this moment would come and had dressed for it in her mind. The light swung. In the white flash I saw the ledger in my lap and, beyond it, through the lamproom glass, nothing but the frozen beginning of the river and the far shore where the ferry would come.

The footsteps stopped one turn below me. Her voice came up through the hatch, steady as a logged wind reading.

"You found it, then."

Not a question. Keepers don't ask questions they've already answered.

I put my hand flat on the page, the way she had, that first Tuesday, pressing the paper down as if it might escape. Below us the river waited for its ice. Above us the beam turned, and turned, and turned, keeping its own count of everything we said next.
