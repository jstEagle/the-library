## 5. A Letter Under Seal

The runner came on a Tuesday, which Sable would remember later because Tuesdays were the days she cleaned the lantern glass, and the glass was in her hands when the knock came—a small, official-sounding rap, three strikes, evenly spaced. Not the knock of a neighbor. Neighbors knocked like weather: irregular, apologetic.

She set down the cloth and opened the door to a boy of perhaps seventeen, salt-crusted at the cuffs, a canvas satchel across his chest and frost in his eyebrows. He had run the coast path from Harrowgate, six miles of it, she judged by the state of his boots.

"Letter for the keeper of Greywater Point." He said it like an incantation, something he'd been made to repeat until it lost all meaning. "Signature required."

"Nobody requires signatures out here."

"This one does." He produced a board, a pencil worn to a stub. His fingers were red with cold and he held them out from his body slightly, as if they belonged to someone else. "It's sealed matter. Magistrate's office."

The word *magistrate* landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She took the board, signed, watched him copy the name onto his manifest with lips moving—*Almeida*—and only then did he hand over the letter itself: heavy paper, grey-blue, a wax seal pressed with the county arms, unbroken.

"For the record," the boy said, already turning away, "I didn't read it. We're not allowed."

"I didn't ask."

He hesitated on the step, glancing past her into the house—at Rosalind's coat drying by the stove, at the smell of bread, at the ordinary warm evidence of a life—and then he went, running, back down the path toward the world that sent letters with seals on them.

Sable stood in the doorway a long moment before closing the door.

* * *

She did not open it immediately. That was the strange part, the part she would turn over later in the dark: she carried it through the kitchen, past the stove, up the spiral stair with its ninety-one iron steps she could climb blindfolded, into the lamp room, where the great lens turned and turned above her with its sound like a slow breath drawn through teeth. She sat on the keeper's bench beneath the light and held the letter on her knees and let it sit there while the beam swept out over the whitecapped water, once every seven seconds, counting the boats that weren't there.

There were two reasons she didn't open it.

The first was simple: letters with seals were rarely good. In twenty years of coastal living she had received perhaps four, and three had concerned deaths.

The second reason she would not have admitted aloud, even to herself, not yet. It was this: some part of her had been waiting for it. Ever since the crate washed ashore in October. Ever since she'd knelt in the oil room with the ledger open across her thighs and read, by lamplight, entry after entry marked *for silence kept*, sums paid out against dates she recognized—the Meridian wreck, two winters past, eleven men drowned off Corben Shoals when a hull that should never have sailed went out anyway, overloaded, uninsured, its owner paid to look away. Edric's handwriting marching down the page, patient and neat as tide tables. Money received. Silence kept.

She had hidden the ledger again. Beneath the oil store, behind the false panel Edric must have fitted himself, in the same dark place it had waited for her to find it. And then she had gone upstairs and trimmed wicks and logged the weather and fried fish for her daughter and said nothing, and each day the saying-nothing grew heavier, until carrying it felt less like concealment than like freight.

So when the sealed letter came, some quiet chamber of her mind recognized it as the echo of a shot fired weeks ago. The trap had closed in October. The letter was only the sound arriving.

Still, her hands were steady when she broke the wax.

* * *

*To the Keeper, Greywater Point Light Station.*

*You are hereby notified that the name ALMEIDA appears upon a schedule of persons implicated in the affair of the vessel MERIDIAN STAR, lost with eleven souls off Corben Shoals, being an inquiry convened under the authority of the Admiralty Court and the County Magistracy.*

*Be advised that formal proceedings shall commence upon the breaking of the ice, at which time all named parties will be required to appear and give account. Until such time, you are instructed to retain any papers, correspondence, or records pertaining to the said vessel or the said affair, and to surrender nothing, destroy nothing, and speak to no person concerning the same excepting officers of the court.*

*Failure to comply will be construed as consciousness of guilt.*

*By order of*

*Magistrate H. Verrall*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way she read charts—not for information but for what lay between the lines.

*Retain any papers.* They knew about papers. Not what they were, perhaps, or where—but that there were papers. Someone had talked, or someone had guessed, or someone had found enough of a thread to pull. And the instruction itself was a noose dressed as courtesy: *destroy nothing*. As if they knew she might want to. As if burning had already occurred to them, and to her, in the same hour.

And underneath all of it, plain as paint: *the Almeida name*. Not Edric Almeida, deceased. Not the estate of. The name entire, hanging in the air of their house like smoke, settling on everything. On the mugs. On Rosalind's schoolbooks. On the girl herself, asleep downstairs, fourteen years old and innocent of all of it, who would wake tomorrow and eat breakfast in a house the magistrate's office now regarded as a place where guilt was stored, pending collection.

Sable folded the letter along its creases and sat beneath the turning light and felt the shape of her own life rearrange around her.

She had believed, these two years since the sea took Edric's boat, that grief had made them invisible. That was the bargain she'd struck without knowing it: we have suffered enough; leave us be. The storm that drowned her husband had seemed like a full accounting, a debt paid in the only currency the sea respects. She and Rosalind had been permitted to shrink—to become a lighthouse with a light and no history, a household of two at the edge of the map, noticed only as a flicker on the horizon.

But invisibility, she understood now, holding the magistrate's grey-blue paper, had never been anything but an illusion purchased with distance. They had not been unseen. They had been *unexamined*. And examination, when it finally came, came with a seal on it.

* * *

She hid the letter inside the oil room, in the same cavity as the ledger, and immediately regretted it—hiding evidence beside evidence, the way you don't put a lit lamp near lamp oil—and so she took it back out and spent a foolish quarter hour deciding where a magistrate's letter ought to live in a keeper's house. In the end she put it in the tin box where Edric's certificate of appointment was kept, flat against his neat signature, which felt either like a betrayal or an act of loyalty; she couldn't tell which.

Downstairs, the bread was done and going cool. Rosalind was at the table doing figures, her pen scratching, her hair tied back with whatever had been nearest—a strip of rag, blue, that Sable recognized as coming from her own sewing basket and said nothing about.

"You were up in the lamp room a long time," Rosalind said, not looking up.

"Glass needed cleaning."

"You cleaned it Sunday."

"The salt doesn't keep a calendar."

Rosalind made a small noncommittal noise, the kind Sable had learned, over the past year, to hear as a flag rather than a dismissal. The girl missed nothing. That was the trouble with raising a sharp child: you taught them to observe, and then observed things became dangerous. Rosalind's eyes flicked up—quick, cataloguing: her mother's face, her hands, the tin box on the shelf that hadn't moved in months and somehow now mattered—and went back down to her figures.

"Post today?" Rosalind asked, too casually.

"Runner came. Nothing for us."

The lie was smooth and Sable hated how smooth it was. It was becoming a skill, this. Two months of hiding a ledger had given her a craftsman's touch.

"Nothing," Rosalind repeated, in a voice that meant *I don't believe you, and I am filing this away.*

They ate. Sable talked about the weather coming in from the northwest, the pressure falling, the likelihood they'd need to double-check the moorings on the skiff. Ordinary talk. Anchor talk. Rosalind answered in monosyllables and watched her mother with those eyes, and Sable thought: *she is learning me the way I learned the channel markers. She is charting my changes.*

Afterward Rosalind washed the dishes and Sable went to the window and looked out at the water, grey and restless, and thought about the phrase that would not stop repeating: *when the ice breaks.* The inquiry would convene when the ice broke. Which meant that all winter—every frozen morning, every short day, every night of keeping the light—they would live in the gap between now and then, the way a condemned building stands between condemnation and demolition. Still standing. Already gone.

And there was a worse arithmetic under that one, which she made herself face at the window with her breath fogging the glass: *when the ice breaks* also meant *when the river closes.* Every year the freeze stopped the ferries, cut Greywater Point off entirely, reduced them to what they could hold in their own walls. If the inquiry convened at the thaw, then the winter was not merely a season. It was a deadline with weather in it. Whatever she decided about the ledger—burn it, keep it, surrender it, use it—she would decide it alone, sealed in with her daughter and her dead husband's secrets and whatever else the ice decided to shut in with them.

She thought of the ledger beneath the oil store, patient in the dark, weighing more every day though its pages hadn't changed.

*Speak to no person concerning the same.* The order was almost funny. She had spoken to no one. She was the best-kept silence on this coast.

* * *

That evening she climbed to the lamp room at dusk and lit the mantle and watched the flame take, blue then gold, and set the clockwork turning, and stood a moment in the warm circular room while the beam went out across the darkening sea, seven seconds, seven seconds, faithful as a pulse.

Below her, Rosalind's lamp went out. Above her, the sky went the color of slate. Around her, the whole coast settled into the long exhale of winter.

Sable opened the logbook to the day's page and wrote, in her keeper's hand, the entries required of her: wind northwest, freshening; barometer falling; visibility moderate. Then, because the logbook was the one place in her life where she wrote only truth—truth small enough to be safe—she paused, pen hovering, and added a line she probably shouldn't have:

*Letter received. Signed for.*

Let whoever audited these pages wonder. Let it stand in the record, plain and dateable, proof that on this day the outside world had reached her and she had signed her name to it. She was done pretending the sea around Greywater Point was empty.

She blew out her reading lamp and stood in the rotating light, and that was when she saw it—far down the coast road, a figure walking toward the point through the last grey light, unhurried, coat collar up, a bag over one shoulder. Too far to make out a face. Too deliberate to be chance.

Nobody walked to Greywater Point in December unless they were coming to her.

The figure raised one hand—not a wave, quite. A greeting, or a claim.

Sable's hand went flat against the cold glass of the lantern, over the turning light, as if she could feel the stranger's approach through it, and she heard herself think, clearly, in the voice she used for emergencies: *Whoever you are—you're too late. The letter already came.*

She watched him walk on, closer, closer, and did not go down to meet him, and did not look away.
