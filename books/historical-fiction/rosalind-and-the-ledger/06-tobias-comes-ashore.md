## 6. Tobias Comes Ashore

Sable saw the boat before she saw the man—a dark shape beating up against the ebb tide, too small for a fishing smack, too stubborn for a pleasure craft. She stood at the gallery rail with her spyglass and watched it tack twice, badly, then give up on elegance and row the last half mile through the chop.

Nobody rowed to Greywater Point in November unless they had to.

She went down the spiral stair two steps at a time, wiped her hands on her apron out of habit, and was at the landing when he hauled his bowline over the iron mooring ring. He looked up. Even wind-burned and salt-stiff, even older than grief had made her expect, there was no mistaking him.

Tobias Almeida had Edric's smile.

"Sable." He said her name like a man testing ice. "You're looking well."

"I'm looking cold," she said. "Get your gear inside before the tide takes it back."

He laughed—Edric's laugh, lower, rougher—and came up the rocks with a canvas bag over one shoulder, moving like a man who'd spent years learning how to occupy space without apologizing for it. That was Tobias. At Edric's wedding he'd told stories until the candles burned down; at the funeral he'd held Sable's elbow at just the right moments and said nothing wrong, which was its own kind of performance.

"You didn't write," she said.

"Didn't know I was coming myself till Tuesday." He stamped his boots on the mat. "The *Kestrel* put me ashore at Millbrook. Captain owed me a favor."

"The *Kestrel* doesn't run this late."

"It does now. Money talks even in winter." He looked past her into the house, into the kitchen where the kettle sat and Rosalind's grammar book lay open on the table. "Is that tea I smell, or are you going to make me stand in your doorway like a bill collector?"

She stepped aside. It was what you did with family. It was also, she noticed as he passed close enough that she caught river water and tobacco off his coat, exactly what someone would do if they wanted to see inside a house without asking.

* * *

Rosalind knew who he was before her mother said the name.

There was a photograph on the parlor shelf she wasn't supposed to touch: three young men on a dock, arms slung around each other's necks, all of them squinting into the sun. Papa in the middle. The one on the left had a crooked grin and one eyebrow higher than the other, and the man dripping on their doormat wore both like a coat he'd never taken off.

"Uncle Tobias," Mama said, and her voice did something strange—not warm, not cold, but level, the way it went when Mama was counting something.

"The very same." He spread his arms. "Come to see my girls."

Rosalind gave him her hand because that was manners. His grip was dry and quick, like a bird's. Up close he was thinner than the photograph, with gray coming in at his temples and eyes that moved around the room while his face stayed fixed on you. They touched the window glass. The stairs. The lamp room door, closed as always.

"You've grown," he told her. "Last time I saw you, you were this high and afraid of gulls."

"I was six."

"And now you correct your elders. Even better." He winked. She found herself almost liking him, and distrusted the feeling immediately, the way you distrust a floorboard that gives under your weight.

Mama put tea in front of him and bread and the last of the smoked fish, and he ate like a man making up for a crossing, talking the whole time—Millbrook gossip, the price of coal, some senator's scandal he swore he'd heard firsthand. He was good at it. That was the thing about Uncle Tobias: he filled a room so completely you didn't notice he'd never once said why he'd come.

Rosalind noticed. She'd gotten good at noticing since October, since the ferry schedule went up early, since Mama started locking the oil room and carrying keys in her pocket like a jailer.

"So," Tobias said, setting down his cup. "How have you been keeping?"

"Well enough," Mama said.

"Alone out here. Just the two of you. Must be hard." He turned the cup slowly on its saucer. "And after everything with Edric's affairs. All that paperwork."

Rosalind saw it happen. It was small—her mother's shoulders drawing up a quarter inch, her thumb pressing flat against the table edge—but she saw it, and she saw that Tobias saw it too, and pretended not to.

"Affairs?" Mama said.

"He was always terrible with documents. Our father used to say Edric could lose a deed in a room with one drawer." A light laugh. "I only ask because there may be papers of his floating about. Shipping correspondence, that sort of thing. The company's solicitors have been writing me. Dreadful nuisance. If anything turned up here, I'd want to spare you the bother of it."

"What sort of papers?"

"Oh—" he waved a hand, breezy as weather, "—invoices, manifests. Nothing that matters to a lighthouse keeper." His eyes went to the oil room door again. Held there a half second too long. Came back smiling.

Rosalind looked at her mother and understood, with the cold certainty of a girl who had learned to read tides from foam patterns, that there were papers. And that her mother knew what kind. And that the man at the table had not come four hours against the current for invoices.

* * *

Afterward, Sable stood at the sink with water too hot for her hands and let it scald her, because the pain kept her voice level.

Papers. He'd said *papers* the way a pickpocket says *excuse me*—a word meant to slide past while the fingers do their work. He hadn't said ledger. He hadn't said Meridian. But he'd looked at the oil room door twice, and he'd watched her face when he said *affairs*, and Tobias Almeida had never in his life crossed open water in November for family.

She dried her hands. Through the window she could see Rosalind walking him along the cliff path toward the boat store, pointing out things—the winch, the signal mast—while he nodded and glanced everywhere else. Her daughter walked like an escort. Her daughter was learning surveillance from watching her mother learn it, and that thought sat in Sable's chest like swallowed stone.

She went out to them. The wind had teeth now, coming straight off the water, and Tobias had his collar up and his charm banked low.

"Beautiful station," he was saying. "Father would have loved it. He always said Edric got the sea legs and I got the ledgers—" He stopped. Heard himself. Smiled wider. "Figure of speech."

"Is it," Sable said.

They stood in the wind, the three of them, and nobody spoke, and the silence had a shape to it, and the shape was a locked door.

"Tobias." She made her voice gentle, because gentleness disarms better than steel. "Why are you here?"

His face did several things quickly, like cards shuffled by a professional hand—hurt, amusement, calculation—and settled on candor, which fit him worst of all. "Can't a man visit his brother's widow?"

"Not in November. Not by the *Kestrel*. Not asking after papers within ten minutes of sitting down." She held his eyes. "I loved Edric. You loved Edric. So I'll say this once, kindly. Whatever you think is in this house, whatever anyone told you is in this house—I will decide what happens to it. Not you. Not the company. Not their solicitors. Me."

For a moment something real moved behind his face, something tired and frightened, and she thought: *there he is.* Then it was gone, packed away, and the smile came back up like a lamp being lit.

"Fair enough," he said lightly. "Fair enough. I'll stay the night, if you'll have me, and be gone with the morning tide. I only wanted—" He gestured at the grey water, the grey sky, the tower standing black against it. "To see where he lived. That's all. Truly."

It was the first true thing he'd said, or the best imitation of one, and she couldn't tell the difference anymore, and that frightened her more than the questions had.

* * *

Rosalind lay awake in the loft listening to the men's voices below—no, the voice, singular, because Mama barely spoke. Uncle Tobias talked and talked, and the talk had a rhythm she recognized from the ferry schedule, from the locked oil room, from every sealed thing in her life lately: the rhythm of people arranging words around a hole.

She crept to the top of the stairs where the boards didn't creak.

"—and of course I told them, no, Edric was meticulous, if he kept records they'd be exact ones." A pause. Ice clinked in a glass. "Sable. Sister. Listen to me. There are men coming to this coast when the ice breaks. Not solicitors. Men with warrants. And they won't ask nicely the way I'm asking."

"They haven't written me."

"They wouldn't." Another pause. When he spoke again his voice had dropped its charm entirely, and what was underneath was worse—something raw and quick. "Sable, I'm begging you. Whatever he left you. Burn it. Tonight, before I go. Please."

The fire ticked. The wind worked at the shutters. Rosalind pressed her palm flat against the cold wall and felt her own heart going like a hammer on a hull.

Then her mother's voice, quiet and clear as a bell buoy:

"No."

One word. Rosalind waited for the rest—for *because*, for *what is it*, for any explanation that would finally open the sealed rooms of the last two months. It didn't come. Below, a chair scraped. Glass rang against the decanter.

"Then we understand each other," Tobias said, and the charm was back, smooth as varnish, and Rosalind heard in it what she hadn't heard at the tea table: that the smile was a weapon, and it had just been pointed at her mother, and her mother had stood in front of it anyway.

She went back to bed and lay staring at the rafters, doing arithmetic she hated. Papers her father had kept. Papers her mother protected. Papers an uncle would cross a freezing river to burn. And somewhere beneath it all, the wreck everyone still whispered about—the *Meridian*, two winters back, the night the coast went quiet and stayed quiet.

Her father's name was in that whisper. She'd heard it once, at the Millbrook market, from women who stopped talking when she came near.

Rosalind pulled the blanket to her chin. Tomorrow the tide would take Uncle Tobias away, and the house would seal itself again, and her mother would carry those keys in her pocket like a jailer.

But tomorrow night, when the house slept, Rosalind intended to find out what a jailer guards.
