## 7. Entries in Another Hand

Sable waited until the house had gone quiet.

Tobias slept in the spare room off the kitchen, the one that had been Edric's before it was anyone's, and his breathing came through the wall in a slow, satisfied rhythm she found herself resenting. Rosalind was asleep too — or seemed to be; the girl had developed a talent for seeming. And the lamp below turned its long arm across the water, sweeping the dark on schedule, indifferent to what went on above it.

Sable carried the ledger to the kitchen table and set it down the way she'd set down live shells once, years ago, when Edric had brought one home from the breakwater to show her. Something that had been made to do harm and hadn't yet decided whether to.

She lit two candles, then a third, because the entries were small and the ink had faded unevenly, and because some part of her wanted to see this clearly even if another part would have preferred not to.

She had read the ledger twice in the days since finding it beneath the oil store. Twice through quickly, the way you read anything you're afraid of — eyes skimming for your own name, for Rosalind's, for confirmation of the worst. She had found no names but the dead ones: the witnesses, the sums, the dates. *For silence kept.* Over and over, in Edric's hand, or what she had believed was Edric's hand. Two winters of payments, logged with an accountant's patience, tied always back to Meridian Shipping and the wreck everyone in Greywater still spoke about in lowered voices.

Tonight she meant to read it slowly.

She started at the beginning, forcing herself through each line as though she were checking stores, as though it were just another inventory. That discipline lasted four pages. On the fifth, something snagged — not the content, which had already done its damage, but the lettering itself.

The *f* in *February*. It leaned wrong.

Edric's hand was a keeper's hand: upright, economical, formed by ten thousand logbook entries in bad light with cold fingers. He wrote numbers beautifully and words grudgingly, and every *f* he ever set down stood straight as a mast. Sable had teased him about it once, early on — *you write like the navy pays you by the stroke* — and he had laughed and said someone in a marriage ought to be legible.

The *f* on this page leaned like a man hurrying.

She told herself it was the water. The pages had dried warped; ink bloomed where damp had gotten at them. She turned back to the first entry and compared, entry against entry, month against month, holding the candle close enough that wax dripped onto the table and she didn't notice.

The first three months were Edric's. She knew it the way you know your own name spoken in a crowd — the cramped *M* of *Meridian*, the way he crossed his *t*s low and quick, the little hitch he made in the numeral 4 that she had watched him write into weather logs a thousand times. *Received of Meridian Shipping, £14, for silence kept re: statements of J. Calloway.* Dated eleven days after the wreck. Eleven days after they had pulled what they could out of the water and Edric had come home gray-faced and silent and held Rosalind so hard the girl had complained.

Then, seven months in, the hand changed.

Not all at once. That was what made it terrible — how gradual it was, how reasonable. A word here. An entry there. As though the ledger had been written by two men passing a task back and forth, each covering for the other's fatigue, except that neither man was tired and both were being paid. The leaning *f*s multiplied. The numerals lost their hitch. The 4s opened up at the top, flourished slightly, a small vanity Edric had never permitted himself.

Sable knew that vanity. She had seen it on envelopes, on Christmas cards, on the gift tags Tobias attached to presents he mostly forgot to send. Tobias wrote the way he talked — a little grander than necessary, a little faster than the truth required.

*Received of Meridian Shipping, £20, for silence kept re: statement of T. Voss.*

Her husband's brother's handwriting, in her husband's secret book, recording money taken to bury a living man's testimony.

Sable sat back. The candles guttered in a draft from under the door, righted themselves.

She thought: *he didn't do it alone.* And then, immediately, the worse thought: *which means he might have wanted to stop.* Because there were gaps in the ledger now, weeks where only Tobias's hand appeared while Edric's vanished entirely, and then a stretch near the end where Edric returned — briefly, reluctantly, if a hand could be reluctant — and then stopped altogether, eight months before his boat went down, leaving the last page blank except for a single smudged line she couldn't yet make out.

Two men had written this book. One of them was dead, and she had spent two winters mourning him as though grief were a settled account. Now the account reopened, and she did not know which column to enter her sorrow in.

She heard Tobias turn over in the next room and went very still, one hand flat on the ledger, the other near the candles, as though she might snuff them and deny the whole evidence of the evening. His breathing resettled. The lamp swept past the window, and its light crossed the table for half a second, illuminating the leaning *f*s like a row of men tipping their hats.

She closed the book. She did not put it back under the oil store. Instead she wrapped it in oilcloth and took it up to the lamp room and locked it in the chart cabinet, the one place in the house with a lock, because if Tobias had come ashore asking after papers, he would search the places a wife would hide things — the bedroom, the kitchen, the loose brick by the hearth. No man searched the working heart of a lighthouse unless he already knew what lived there.

And Tobias, she suspected, knew exactly what lived there. He had helped write it.

* * *

Sleep didn't come, and around three in the morning Sable gave up on it and let herself remember instead, because there was nowhere else for the memory to go.

Edric's final visit home. Not his final day — his final *visit*, the distinction mattering now in a way it hadn't then. He'd been away six weeks on the coastal run, longer than usual, and when he came up the path from the landing he looked wrong in a way Sable had registered and then, guiltily, filed away, because Rosalind was sick with a fever and the house needed tending and there is never time to examine a husband's face properly.

He'd stood in the doorway longer than a man stands in his own doorway. Just looking at the room. At Sable stirring the pot, at Rosalind flushed and small on the settle, at the ordinary lamplit life of it. Looking the way a man looks at a thing he is memorizing, or saying goodbye to, or both.

"You're thin," Sable had said, because tenderness came out of her sideways in those days, dressed as complaint.

"Work's heavy." He'd hung his coat on its peg and missed the peg, and laughed at himself, and the laugh had been Tobias's laugh — quick, performing ease — and she had noticed that too and let it go.

At supper he barely ate. He watched Rosalind do her sums by the fire and said, "Where'd you learn to carry the tens?" and Rosalind said, "Papa, I've always known," and he'd smiled and looked at Sable and said, quietly, "She's sharp. Sharper than either of us. Keep her away from ledgers."

He'd said it like a joke. Everyone had laughed. Sable could still hear the exact grain of that laugh, and tonight, replaying it in the dark kitchen of memory, she understood it had not been a joke at all.

Later, when Rosalind was down, he'd sat across from Sable with his hands around a cup of tea he never drank, and he'd started three sentences and abandoned all of them. *Sable, I need to tell you — * No. *There's something about the winter of the wreck — * No. Finally: "If anything happens to me, the boat insurance is in the strongbox at the bank, and—" and then he'd stopped, and looked at her with an expression she had spent two winters reading as love, and said, "You'd keep things honest, wouldn't you. If it cost you."

"I'd keep them honest," she'd said. "It's the only inheritance worth anything."

He'd nodded slowly, like a man confirming coordinates, and gone to bed, and left on the morning tide without waking her, and six weeks later the sea gave back his boat empty.

She had built her grief on that visit the way you build a cairn: stone by stone, each memory placed to hold the others up. *He was tired. He was worried about money. He loved us. The sea is careless.* A structure that had borne her weight for two winters.

Now the stones shifted. Because a man planning to confess does not leave his wife a riddle; a man complicit and comfortable does not look at his daughter like a farewell. And a man whose own hand stops appearing in a bribery ledger eight months before he drowns — a man who has perhaps refused, perhaps threatened, perhaps said the word *testimony* aloud to the wrong brother —

Sable pressed her palms against her eyes.

*He was trying to get out,* she thought. *And the hand that changed was the hand that held him in.*

She hated herself for thinking it, and thought it again anyway, thoroughly, the way you probe a bruise. Tobias had come ashore charming and desperate, asking pointed questions about papers, sleeping in his dead brother's bed. What kind of mourning was that? The kind that needs to know whether the evidence survived the sea.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater. Sable had not slept and did not go to bed. She made bread because her hands needed occupation and the smell of it would give the house a lie of normalcy, and when Rosalind shuffled into the kitchen, sleep-rumpled and watchful — the girl was always watchful lately, missing nothing, a quality that had lately begun to frighten her mother — Sable slid the memory of the night into a drawer and shut it.

"Uncle Tobias says there'll be herring run early this year," Rosalind said, dropping into a chair.

"Does he."

"He knows a lot of things." Rosalind tore the heel off yesterday's loaf, not eating it, turning it. "Mama. Did Papa know him well? Better than us?"

The question landed with the precision of a thrown knife, and Sable understood that her daughter was not asking about affection. She was asking why a man who knew everything was in their house.

"He's your father's brother," Sable said. "Eat your breakfast."

Rosalind ate her breakfast, slowly, keeping her eyes up the whole time, cataloguing.

Tobias appeared mid-morning, washed and cheerful and wearing Edric's old shore coat without asking, and announced he'd walk to the village for news before the weather closed in. He paused at the door with his hand on the frame — pausing, Sable noted, exactly where a man pauses when he wants to seem casual — and said, "You sleep well, Sable? House makes noises at night, this old place. Pipes and such."

"Like a churchyard," Sable agreed pleasantly.

His eyes went, briefly and professionally, to the ceiling. To the floor above. To the lamp room, where a locked chart cabinet held a book full of his own handwriting, and where, if he climbed those stairs while she slept, he would find it, and the river would freeze over one clean crime the sooner.

After the door shut behind him, Sable stood a long moment in the quiet kitchen, listening to his footsteps fade down the shingle.

Then she went up to the lamp room, unlocked the cabinet, and drew the ledger out — because there was one more comparison she had to make, one she'd avoided all night. She laid Edric's last weather log open beside the ledger, page against page, and matched the hands entry by entry, and confirmed what she already knew in her bones:

Eight months before he died, her husband had stopped writing in that book forever.

And the final smudged line — the one she'd skimmed twice and flinched from twice — she now bent over the candle to decipher, letter by letter, her heart climbing into her throat as the words began to surface through the blot like bodies rising in water:

*"I mean to tell the magistrate everyth—"*
