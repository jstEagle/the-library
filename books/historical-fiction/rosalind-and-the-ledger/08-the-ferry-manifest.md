## 8. The Ferry Manifest

The paper went up on the ferry office door the same morning the gulls turned strange.

Rosalind noticed the gulls first. They'd come inland off the water in a ragged, complaining line, settling on the rooftops of Greywater village like a jury taking its seats, and she stood in the lane with her breath smoking and thought: they know something we don't.

Then she saw the crowd gathered outside the ferry office, and the fresh sheet of paper nailed to the door at an angle, as if whoever posted it had been in a hurry to be somewhere else. She shouldered through the knot of fishermen and dockwives until she could read it.

*By order of the Harbour Commission and the Magistracy of the River District: owing to forecasts of the severest storm season on record, evacuation of all low-lying households shall commence upon posting. The last ferry before full ice shall depart Greywater dock on the morning tide of the ninth day hence. ONE BERTH PER HOUSEHOLD. Priority to children, then elders, then able adults. Households failing to register by the sixth day forfeit their berth entire.*

Below the official seal, someone had written in pencil, small and neat: *God help us choose.*

Rosalind read it twice. The words arranged themselves and rearranged themselves and refused to mean anything comfortable.

One berth per household.

Her household was three people now — her mother, herself, and Uncle Tobias, who had appeared out of nowhere two weeks ago with his good coat and his better smile and had not left since. Three people. One seat.

She did the arithmetic standing right there in the cold while the grown-ups around her muttered and clutched their hats and began, immediately, to bargain. She was fourteen; she had been doing sums since before she could write her own name properly, because her mother believed a lighthouse keeper's daughter should be able to count anything — lamp-oil hours, storm days, miles of dark water. So Rosalind counted.

Three people. One seat. Two losers.

The hope that rose in her chest — *they'll take me, I'm the child, the paper says children first* — curdled almost at once into something colder. Because if there was one seat and she took it, then who didn't? Her mother, who had kept the light alone through every bad night since Papa drowned? Or Uncle Tobias, who laughed too easily and asked questions sideways, like a man feeling along a wall for a door?

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome — what if nobody said *you*, what if they looked at her and decided a fourteen-year-old girl could be spared more easily than a grown man or a keeper with a commission? What if being the obvious choice and being the expendable one were the same thing wearing different coats?

"Rosalind." Mrs. Quill, the netmender, touched her elbow. "You'll tell your mother, will you? She's up at the Point."

"I can read," Rosalind said, sharper than she meant to.

"I know it, love. It's just—" Mrs. Quill glanced at the paper, then away, and her face did something complicated. "It's different, reading it for yourself."

Rosalind walked home along the shingle with the wind pushing at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades. Nine days. The ninth day. She counted again without meaning to, the way you worry a loose tooth.

At home, her mother was in the oil room.

That was the first wrong thing. Sable Almeida spent her days in the lamp room or the kitchen or down on the rocks checking the moorings; the oil room was storage and spiders and the smell of old brass. But for two weeks now — ever since Tobias came ashore, Rosalind realized, ever since exactly then — her mother had been going down there at odd hours, staying too long, coming up with her hands smelling of lamp oil and her eyes carrying something Rosalind couldn't name. Not fear exactly. Fear was loud. This was quiet, which was worse.

"Mother." She held out nothing; she just stood in the doorway of the oil room, blocking most of the light. "There's a notice posted. Evacuation orders."

Sable looked up from whatever she'd been doing — her hands were empty now, too quickly empty, and behind her a shelf sat slightly ajar where Rosalind was almost certain it used to sit flush. "I heard the gulls."

"The last ferry is nine days off. One berth per household." Rosalind made herself say it flatly, like reading a shipping list. "We're three."

Something moved behind her mother's face — quick, submerged, gone. Like a fish under ice. "I know."

"You knew already?"

"I can count, Rosalind."

"That's what everyone keeps saying." Her voice cracked upward and she hated it. "Counting. As if counting makes it fine. Three and one, Mother. Do the sum out loud if you're so good at it. Who goes on the boat?"

For a long moment her mother said nothing. Then she crossed the oil room and put both hands on Rosalind's shoulders, and her grip was steady but her thumbs were cold even through Rosalind's sweater. "No one is deciding anything today."

"Today is when it starts deciding us." She'd heard Tobias say that once, over cards, some clever borrowed phrase, and she hated that it lived in her mouth now. She pulled back. "Uncle Tobias knows?"

"He was at the dock this morning."

"Of course he was." Rosalind heard her own voice go thin and hard. "He's always wherever the important things are."

Her mother's eyes sharpened. "Say what you mean."

"I don't know what I mean!" And that was truest thing she'd said in weeks. She turned and climbed the stairs to her room, where the window faced the river, and sat on her bed with her knees drawn up, watching the grey water and doing the same sum over and over until the numbers wore grooves in her.

Three and one. Three and one.

* * *

Tobias came to her that evening, which he never did.

He was a creature of the kitchen and the common room, of her mother's company, of firesides where he could perform his charm for an audience. He did not seek out the cold north bedroom where Rosalind kept her books and her father's old brass compass and her private, growing suspicion of him. So when his knock came — two light raps, always two, like a man asking permission twice — she sat up sharply on her bed.

"Come in, then."

He opened the door halfway, filling the gap with his shoulders and his smile. In the lamplight he looked tired in a way he usually managed to hide. "Your mother says there's a notice."

"There's a notice."

"One berth." He leaned against the doorframe, arms folded, casual as a man discussing weather. "You've done the arithmetic, I expect. Your mother says you're the counter in this family."

"My mother says a lot of things."

He smiled at that, genuinely, and for a second he looked so much like Papa that Rosalind's chest hurt. Same brow. Same way of tilting his head when something amused him. She had spent two weeks trying not to see it and seeing it anyway, everywhere, like a watermark.

"It won't come to choosing," Tobias said. "These notices. They post them to frighten people into registering early, that's all. There's always room on the last boat. Always has been."

"Has there?" Rosalind said. "This year?"

The smile stayed but stopped reaching anywhere. "Fair point."

"Why are you here, Uncle Tobias?" She'd never asked him directly. Directness felt dangerous with him, like pointing a lamp straight into fog — you might see what was there. "Really."

"I wanted to see how you were bearing it." He said it smoothly enough. But his eyes went past her, briefly, to the shelf above her bed — the one with her books, and beneath the books, wrapped in flannel because she liked the weight of it, Papa's old sea chest that she used as a bench. Just a flicker. A man checking a room out of habit, or checking whether a room was checking him.

"They're bearing fine," Rosalind said.

"Good. Good." He pushed off the doorframe. "Whatever happens with the berth, Rosalind — your mother will do right by you. Whatever else, remember that. She'll do what she thinks is right." He paused at the threshold, half-turned. "Even if it doesn't look right at the time. Even if it looks like the opposite."

"What does that mean?"

But he was already going down the stairs, whistling something low and tuneless, leaving the question hanging in the cold air of the corridor like smoke.

*She'll do what she thinks is right.*

Rosalind lay awake a long time turning it over. It sounded like reassurance. It also sounded, if you held it up to the light and turned it slowly, like a warning. Like a man telling himself something. Like a man who had reason to know that her mother's idea of right might not include him.

And underneath that, quieter, the other thing — the thing she'd been not-thinking for two weeks. The oil room. The shelf. The way her mother's hands had been empty too fast. The way Tobias's eyes had found her sea chest, of all things, in a room full of everything else.

Everyone in this house was counting. Everyone in this house was hiding something worth counting.

* * *

On the third day, Rosalind registered the household at the ferry office herself, because her mother was keeping the light through a squall and Tobias claimed business in the village and she was tired of being the child things happened around.

The clerk — a young man with ink-stained fingers and a ledger of his own, a proper government one — asked name, household number, occupants. She gave them. *Almeida. Greywater Point station. Three.* His pen paused at *three.*

"Occupants' names, miss, in order of priority for the berth."

Priority. The word sat on the page like a stone.

"Sable Almeida," Rosalind said. "Keeper of record."

"And below?"

"Rosalind Almeida. Her daughter." Her mouth went dry. She could stop there. Two names, let him assume, let the form be short. But Tobias's name would surface eventually — he'd make sure of it, a man like that — and a lie on a magistrate's form was a stone around the neck of whoever told it. "And Tobias Almeida. Brother to the late Edric Almeida."

The clerk wrote it all down without comment, which somehow made it worse. On his page they were just names now, ranked, weighed, waiting for someone upstream to strike two lines through them.

"Berth assignments post on the fifth day," the clerk said. "Ferry sails the ninth, morning tide, weather permitting." He glanced up, and for a moment he wasn't a clerk anymore, just a young man who'd seen a hundred families stand where she stood. "Weather rarely permits, this season."

Outside, the gulls were still on the roofs. The river had a skin of ice along its edges now, thin as window-glass, thickening by the day. Nine days, and after the ninth the water would close like a book.

Rosalind walked home slow, kicking the frozen wrack at the tideline, and admitted the thing to herself that she'd been circling for three days:

Nobody was going to give her the berth out of love. They were going to give it to her out of arithmetic — child first, rules are rules — and the arithmetic would let everyone off the hook except the two people left on the shore. And she had watched her mother's face in the oil room, watched that fish-under-ice movement, and she understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand a wave is going to break a half-second before it breaks:

Her mother had already chosen.

Maybe not the name yet. Maybe not even consciously. But the shape of it was there — in the oil room, in the sleeplessness, in the way she looked at Tobias when he laughed, measuring him against something Rosalind couldn't see. Something was in that house that mattered more than any of them, and it was going to decide who stood on the dock on the ninth morning.

Well. Rosalind was the counter in this family. If choices were going to be made in the dark, she meant to be holding the lamp.

That evening she waited until her mother climbed to the lamp room for the first watch and Tobias went out "to walk off supper" — he walked toward the village, always toward the village, never along the shore — and then Rosalind went down to the oil room with the stub of a candle and her father's compass in her pocket for courage.

The shelf was ajar. It had been ajar for two weeks, and her mother kept touching it shut and it kept drifting open, the way a secret does.

Rosalind set her candle down, took hold of the shelf, and pulled.

Behind it, the wall was not a wall. There was a gap, a hand's depth of darkness, and inside the darkness something heavy with leather corners and brass clasps, something that smelled of oil and years and — when she drew it out and set it on the workbench and opened it — of her father.

His handwriting. Page after page of her father's handwriting, dates and figures and sums, and beside them words that made no sense and every sense at once, words that seemed to reach up out of the page and take her by the throat:

*Received, for silence kept—*

Down in the lamp room overhead, she heard her mother's footsteps cross the floor, slow and steady, tending the light.

Rosalind did not move.
