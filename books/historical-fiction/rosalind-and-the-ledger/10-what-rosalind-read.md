## 10. What Rosalind Read

Rosalind had learned her mother's hiding places the way other children learned prayers.

The loose brick behind the flour tin, where the coins lived. The hollow under the third stair tread, where the good candle stubs were kept against a dark winter. The false bottom of the sewing box, which had held nothing interesting in all her fourteen years except a thimble and a letter from a cousin nobody spoke about. Rosalind knew the house the way she knew her own hands. So when her mother began locking the oil room—and her mother had never once locked a door at Greywater Point, not even the night the storm took the roof off the boat shed—Rosalind did not ask. She watched. She waited. She counted.

Her mother counted things too. Weather in the logbook, hours on the lamp, coins in the tin. But Rosalind counted differently. She counted the times her mother's hand went to the pocket of her coat, checking for something small and flat. She counted the nights the oil room lamp burned past midnight, its thin line of light under the door like a held breath. She counted the days since the coastal runner had come, since her mother had stood at the window with a letter in her hand for a full minute before folding it away, and had not mentioned it, not once, not even when Rosalind asked directly, *was there anything in the post*, and her mother had said, *nothing that concerns you*, in the voice she used for weather reports and other small lies.

Fourteen days. That was the count as of Tuesday.

And there was the uncle, too, which was its own arithmetic. Tobias had been at the station six days, eating their bread, telling his long warm stories, asking his questions that were not questions. *Your mother keeping well? Keeping everything in order? She always was an orderly woman, your mother.* Rosalind had smiled at him and answered and watched him the way you watch ice on a river—knowing it looks solid, knowing it is lying.

On Wednesday morning, the house emptied itself. Her mother went down to the landing to take delivery of the last oil casks before the freeze, and Tobias went with her, offering his arm and his charm, and Rosalind said she had lessons, and her mother said, *stay out of the oil room*, without being asked, which was as good as drawing a map.

Rosalind waited until they were out of sight past the black rocks. Then she went to the kitchen, took the ring of keys from the hook by the door—there were only three, and her mother had never once thought to count them—and she went down the stone steps to the oil room with her heart going like a gull in a gale.

* * *

The lock was stiff but the key turned. Inside it was cold and smelled of lamp oil and stone, that smell that had been the whole background of her life, so constant she didn't notice it, the way you don't notice the sea until it takes something. Barrels in their racks. The oil store with its brass tap. And beneath the oil store, a shelf that had always held rags and spare wicks.

The shelf was not sitting right. Rosalind had spent enough years watching her mother's exact hands to know when something had been moved and put back by someone who thought they were careful. She lifted the rags. Beneath them, the shelf board lifted out entirely, and there was a space in the stone, and in the space, a book.

Not a book. A ledger. She knew ledgers; her mother kept the station accounts in one, columns of oil and wicks and weather. But this one was different. It was bound in grey leather gone soft with damp, and it had no title, and when she opened it, the first thing she saw was her father's handwriting.

Rosalind sat down on the cold floor with her back against the barrels, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

She knew her father's hand the way she knew his face—from memory, from the backs of envelopes he'd sent from ports, from the logbook pages from before, which her mother kept and which Rosalind had read a hundred times, hungry for any scrap of him. *Edric Almeida*, he wrote, in letters that leaned forward like a man walking into wind. She would have known it anywhere. She had dreamed of finding more of it. She had imagined, in the two years since the sea took his boat, finding letters, a journal, anything, and had imagined what it would say: that he loved them, that he'd been thinking of her, that the sea was cruel and he was good.

She turned the pages.

It started as accounts. Dates from two winters back. Names she half knew—Meridian Shipping, the *Corinne B.*, the harbor office at Saltmarsh. Sums of money, entered neatly, the way her mother entered oil. And then, in the column where her mother wrote *wind* or *fog* or *clear*, her father had written other things.

*For silence kept.*

*For silence kept. J. Harrow, deposition withdrawn.*

*For silence kept. Saltmarsh office. Two statements not filed.*

*For silence kept.*

Rosalind read the phrase until it stopped being words. It became a sound, like the sea against the rocks at night, the sound you stop hearing because it never stops. Her father had written it over and over. Her father, who had taught her to gut a fish and name the clouds and never lie about the weather because the weather always tells the truth in the end. Her father had taken money to make people quiet, after the *Corinne B.* went down, after eleven men drowned within sight of the light her mother kept, and Rosalind remembered that winter, remembered her mother burning the lamp through the storm knowing some of what it swept over was wreckage and men who would not come home.

She kept reading because stopping was impossible and going back was impossible and the floor was very cold and she was very still. There were entries in another hand, she noticed—a hand smaller and more careful, and she thought she knew that one too, thought she'd seen it on the tags of her birthday parcels from Saltmarsh—and she set that thought aside like a lamp turned low, because her mind could only hold one fire at a time and the fire was her father's name.

The last entry was smudged, water-marked, the ink run as if the page had been wet. She could barely read it. Something about *the magistrate*. Something about *tell it all*. The date was three days before the boat went out. Three days before the sea took him.

Rosalind closed the ledger and held it against her chest and did not cry, which surprised her. What she felt was not grief. Grief was an old coat; she knew its weight. This was something with teeth. Her father had not drowned good. Her father had drowned in the middle of a sentence he had finally decided to say, and the sentence had been a confession, and the sea had closed over it, and her mother had found this book and read it and put it back under the floor and locked the door and said *nothing that concerns you*.

Her mother had known. Her mother had known for weeks—maybe longer—and had let Rosalind go on mourning a good man. Had let her keep the father Rosalind had built out of envelope-backs and logbook pages, and had not said one word, because Rosalind was a child, because it did not concern her, because she was to be protected, kept, silenced—*for silence kept*—the phrase turned over and over, and suddenly it was her mother's phrase too, her mother keeping her the way Edric had kept the witnesses, gently, for her own good, with money or with love, it made no difference in the end, silence was silence.

The ledger was under her arm before she decided to carry it. Her feet took her up the stone steps and through the kitchen and up the spiral stair, up and up to the lamp room, because that was the highest place in the house and the only room where she could see far enough in every direction to feel less cornered. She set the ledger on the chart table, in the middle, square, the way her mother set everything, and she sat down in the keeper's chair, which was too big for her, and waited.

* * *

She saw them coming up the path a long while before they reached the door. Her mother first, quick, the way she walked when something was wrong; Tobias behind, and even from the lamp room Rosalind could read the change in him, the charm dropping off him like a coat. They had found the keys off the hook. They had found the open lock.

Her mother came up the spiral stair fast, and stopped in the doorway, and Rosalind watched her take in the scene: the ledger square on the table, the daughter in the chair. Her mother's face did something Rosalind had never seen it do—it came apart, briefly, like a wave, and then put itself back together, and Rosalind hated her a little for that, for the putting-back-together, for the competence of it, for being able to be calm when the whole world had just turned over.

"Rosalind."

"You locked the door," Rosalind said. Her voice came out steady, which was a surprise. "You never lock anything. You locked it because of me."

Her mother came into the room slowly. "Give it to me."

"No."

"Rosalind—"

"I read it." She stood up. The chair scraped. "All of it. *For silence kept.* Do you know what that is? That's Papa. That's his handwriting, I'd know it anywhere, I've read every page he ever wrote, I've read them a hundred times because I miss him, and he was—" Her voice broke and she hated that too, and pushed through it. "He was paid. He was paid to keep people quiet after the *Corinne B.* Eleven men, Mama. We burned the lamp for that storm. You told me to watch the water and I watched it and they were in it."

"Sit down," her mother said, and it was not an order, it was a plea wearing an order's clothes.

"I will not sit down. And you knew." Rosalind's hands were shaking now, the whole of her shaking, the steadiness gone. "You knew and you put it back under the floor and you locked the door and you said *nothing that concerns you*. I asked you. I asked you straight out and you lied to my face."

"I was going to tell you."

"When? When the ice breaks? When the inquiry comes? Was I going to find out from a magistrate, the way you found out from a letter you didn't show me?" She saw her mother flinch and knew she'd scored, and the scoring felt terrible, like cutting yourself. "You always say the weather tells the truth in the end. You taught me that. You taught me to log what I see, exactly what I see, no rounding, no guessing. And then you hid this. You hid *him*. You let me love a man who doesn't exist."

"He existed," her mother said, and her voice was low and rough. "He was a man, Rosalind. Men are not one thing. I loved him and he did this and both are true, and I hid it because—" She stopped. Her hands, those exact hands, were not exact at all; they were gripping each other at her waist like drowning men.

"Because what?"

"Because I was afraid." It came out flat and hard, like a stone dropped on a table. "Because I didn't know what the truth would cost us. Because you were all I had left and I thought I could carry it alone. I was wrong. I see that I was wrong."

Behind her, in the doorway, Tobias had appeared, and Rosalind saw her mother's shoulders set at the sound of him on the stair. He came in smiling the smile that never reached anywhere, and looked at the ledger on the table with an expression Rosalind would remember all her life—hunger and fear in equal parts, the face of a man looking at a thing that could feed him or kill him.

"Well," Tobias said lightly. "Family reading hour."

"Get out," Rosalind said.

"Rosalind—"

"Get *out*." She was surprised at the size of her own voice. It filled the lamp room, it rang in the glass. "You knew too. That other handwriting. I know your handwriting, Uncle Tobias. It's on my birthday parcels. You wrote entries in my father's book. You helped him do it. You came here with your stories and your questions and you sat at our table eating our bread and you never said one true thing."

Tobias looked at her mother, and something passed between the two of them, some old agreement breaking apart in real time, and Rosalind watched it and understood that she was watching the exact moment the family stopped being a family and became three people in a room with a book.

"Rosalind," her mother said quietly. "Come down from there. We're going to talk. All of it. Everything. I promise you."

"Your promises," Rosalind said, and could not finish, because finishing would have meant crying, and she was done with being protected, done with being kept, done with being the last person in her own house to know the truth of her own father.

She picked up the ledger and held it against her chest, and below them the sea worked at the rocks, patient as arithmetic, and out on the grey water, far off, she could see it: the ferry, small and black, coming up the river toward the dock, ahead of the ice, ahead of everything.

Her mother saw it too. Rosalind watched her face change, watched the arithmetic begin behind her eyes, and Rosalind was fourteen years old and had just learned that everything she mourned was a lie, and she held the ledger tighter and said the thing that broke the room open:

"How many seats are on that ferry, Mama?"
