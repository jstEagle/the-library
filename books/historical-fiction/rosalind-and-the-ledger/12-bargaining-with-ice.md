## 12. Bargaining with Ice

The storm came in from the northeast the way bad news always did at Greywater Point: first a rumor on the wind, then a certainty, then a thing you had to live inside of.

Sable had spent the morning battening what could be battened. She'd lashed the skiff down double, stacked spare oil drums inside the oil room where the floor stayed dry, and walked the gallery rail twice, testing every bolt with her palm the way Edric used to. The sky had gone the color of pewter by noon, and by two o'clock the sea was running white-capped all the way to the horizon, as if someone had crumpled it and thrown it back down unfinished.

Tobias had not left.

He'd been at the station four days now, sleeping in the old guest bunk below the lamp room, eating their bread, telling stories about Edric that were half true and half polished, and asking—always circling back, casual as gulls—one question: *where are the papers?*

She hadn't told him about the ferry manifest. Not yet. But he'd seen the notice posted at the harbor office when he came ashore, same as everyone. One berth per household. Last crossing before the river locked. He did arithmetic the way other men breathed.

She found him in the kitchen that afternoon, standing at the window with his hands behind his back, watching the water. He'd dressed carefully even for this—wool coat brushed, boots cleaned—a man who believed presentation was a kind of armor. Rosalind was out at the woodshed, which meant either she was splitting logs or she was avoiding him, and Sable suspected both.

"You should come away with us," Tobias said without turning. "When the time comes."

"Us," Sable said. She set the kettle on the stove because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. "There's one berth, Tobias. You've read the notice same as I have."

"One berth per household." He turned then, and smiled, and the smile was the one he'd worn at Edric's funeral, warm and wrong. "But households can be defined generously. A brother is household. A brother-in-law—" he spread his hands, "—practically blood."

"The magistrate's clerk won't care how we define it."

"The magistrate's clerk," Tobias said, "will be busy. There's going to be an inquiry, Sable. Dozens of names. Hundreds of pages of testimony. Nobody will miss one keeper's wife and one shipping man if they simply... aren't there." He said it lightly, the way you'd discuss a change in the weather. "The world forgets people who leave quietly."

"And the light?" She nodded upward, toward the tower. "Who keeps it?"

"Some other poor soul. Or nobody. Either way, not your problem."

"Not my problem." She almost laughed. The kettle began its low complaint toward boiling. "I've kept this light eleven years. Through the Meridian winter. Through the year the ice took the pilings off Harrow Neck. You stand in my kitchen and tell me the light is not my problem."

He had the grace to look briefly ashamed, or something shaped like it. Then the shame passed through him like weather and left the clear sky of his purpose behind.

"Sable." He stepped closer, lowered his voice, though there was no one to hear but the storm gathering outside. "Let me say plainly what we're both thinking."

"I doubt that. We've never once thought the same thing."

"The ledger." His eyes went to the ceiling—to the oil room above them, though he couldn't have known exactly, and she made sure her face gave nothing away. "It exists. I know it does. Edric wrote to me before he died. He wasn't subtle, whatever else he was. And if it surfaces at the inquiry, it doesn't just bury him. It buries you. Accomplice by association. It buries *Rosalind*—her name attached to it forever, the daughter of men who sold witnesses to the wreck. Is that the inheritance you want for her?"

"She has no inheritance from any of this," Sable said. "That's rather the point."

"Then burn it." He said it fast now, the words tumbling like scree. "Burn it tonight, before the storm, before anyone comes asking. Ash tells no stories. And when the ferry comes, I take the berth—I go south, I disappear, I am never heard from again, and no one at any inquiry ever hears the name Almeida spoken in connection with Meridian. Silence, Sable. Real silence. The kind that holds."

The kettle boiled. She lifted it, poured two cups of tea she didn't want, and pushed one across the table at him because her mother had raised her to be civil even to men she was measuring for a coffin.

"You're offering me a bargain," she said.

"I'm offering you survival."

"You're offering yourself a berth and calling it my salvation." She sat down across from him, cup untouched, steam rising between them like something escaping. "Let's do the sums, Tobias. You're good at sums—it's most of what you and Edric ever did together, from what the ledger says."

His jaw tightened.

"Sum one. If I burn the ledger, what protects me? Nothing. The letter already came. My name is already written in the magistrate's book. Burning paper doesn't unwrite ink in someone else's office. Sum two. If I burn it and you sail away, I'm alone here with a burned secret and a sealed accusation and no proof of anything except that I destroyed evidence the week before an inquiry. That's not silence. That's a confession with extra steps."

"That's not—"

"Sum three." Her voice stayed level, and she was proud of that, proud the way you're proud of a wall holding against the sea. "If I keep the ledger, I hold the only complete account of who paid whom and why. Every name. Every date. Including, I suspect, some names higher up than yours. That page isn't just evidence against us, Tobias. It's the only currency I have. You don't burn your only currency the day before the market opens."

He stared at her. Outside, the first hard gusts hit the tower and the whole structure hummed, a low note like a struck bell.

"You sound like a merchant," he said finally.

"I sound like a woman who's done the arithmetic you taught my husband." She wrapped her hands around the hot cup, needing the small pain of it. "You think I haven't considered burning it? I stood over the stove with it in my hands the second night. I had the grate open. Do you know what stopped me?"

"Sentiment."

"Fear," she said. "Clean, honest fear. The same fear that's sitting across from me wearing my dead husband's brother's face. If the ledger burns, the only story left is the magistrate's. And the magistrate's story will be written by the Meridian men, who have lawyers and money and friends in the customs house, while I have a lighthouse and a fourteen-year-old girl. They'll make me the villain, Tobias. They'll make Edric the fool who drowned. And Rosalind will grow up believing her father was either a criminal or a victim, depending on which lie sells better. The ledger is the truth's last copy. I am not burning the truth's last copy so you can catch a boat."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The storm found its rhythm against the glass—gust, rattle, gust—and somewhere below them the sea was rearranging itself into something that would try to kill them all within the week.

"You'd gamble your daughter's future on a book," Tobias said softly.

"I'd gamble it on the truth being worth more than your silence. Which, given the price you put on it two winters ago, isn't saying much."

He flinched. Good. Let him flinch.

Then he leaned forward, and all the charm drained out of his face, and what was underneath was tired and scared and—for one unguarded moment—almost human. "Edric meant to confess," he said. "You should know that, whatever you think of me. He told me the autumn before he died. Said he couldn't sleep, said the numbers had started looking like graves. He was going to go to the magistrate after the new year." He looked down at his tea. "And then his boat went out in weather no sane man sails in, and he didn't come back, and I've spent two years not asking myself the question everyone asks."

"Which question?"

"Whether someone decided a confession was worse than a drowning."

The words hung between them. Sable felt them settle into her chest like cold water finding its level. She had asked herself that question in the dark hours, more than once, and buried it each morning under wicks and weather logs. Hearing it aloud—in this kitchen, in this voice—made it real in a way her own midnight versions never were.

"If you believe that," she said quietly, "then you believe the ledger is dangerous to more people than the magistrate. And you still want me to burn it."

"I want us alive."

"No. You want *you* alive, and us quiet, and those are different things, however neatly you stack them." She stood, and took his untouched cup away, and set both cups in the basin with a care that frightened her more than shouting would have. "Here is my counter-offer, since we're bargaining. You stay through the storm. You help me keep the light, because I'll need hands and you owe this family labor if you don't owe it truth. When the ferry comes, the berth goes where I decide it goes—not where precedent points, not where guilt argues. And you touch nothing in this house that has pages."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can walk to the harbor office tomorrow and explain to the ferry master why the brother of Edric Almeida is so eager to leave before the inquiry convenes. I hear they're keeping a list."

He held her gaze for a long moment—the old Almeida stubbornness meeting the newer Ravensworth kind—and then something in him broke sideways, and he laughed, short and bitter, and rubbed his face with both hands.

"Edric always said you were the smart one in the family."

"Edric married me. He knew."

"He also said—" Tobias stopped. Started again. "He said if anything happened to him, you'd find the book, and you'd know what to do. I thought he meant hide it. I think maybe he meant use it." He rose, buttoned his coat out of pure habit, and moved toward the door. At the threshold he paused, not turning around. "The storm's going to be bad, Sable. Worse than the notices say. Bad enough that things wash up on shores that shouldn't be washed anywhere."

"Things," she repeated. "Or people?"

He didn't answer. The door closed behind him, and his footsteps faded up the iron stairs toward the guest bunk, and Sable stood alone in the kitchen with the storm building and the ledger overhead and the arithmetic running on behind her eyes like tide through a channel.

* * *

She climbed to the oil room after dark, lamp in hand, and knelt at the loose board with the familiar mixture of dread and steadiness. The ledger lay where she'd hidden it, wrapped in oilcloth, patient as a seed. She didn't open it. She just rested her hand on the cover for a moment, the way you'd check on a sleeping child.

*Use it,* Tobias had said Edric meant. Not hide it. Use it.

Below her, the great lamp turned its steady circles out over the black water, sweeping the rocks, sweeping the channel, sweeping the road along the coast where—she saw it now through the gallery glass, a bobbing yellow point fighting the wind along the cliff path—a lantern was coming toward the station through the worst weather she'd seen in three years.

Someone was walking to Greywater Point in this. On foot. Deliberately.

Sable watched the little flame struggle closer, and felt the trap she'd been living inside shift another notch tighter, and went down to open the door.
