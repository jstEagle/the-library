## 13. Witness at Greywater

The clerk's name was Emory Voss, and he was twenty-three years old, and until the wind took the coast road out from under him he had believed himself to be a careful man.

He had been walking since noon. The magistrate's office in Harrow Bay had sent him north with a sealed folio and instructions that were simple enough to survive being soaked through twice: deliver the inquiry summons to the keeper at Greywater Point, obtain a signature of receipt, and return before the river road closed. The folio was dry, wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, pressed against his chest like a second heart. Everything else about him had failed. His boots had given up their argument with the water somewhere past the mile marker. His hat was gone. His lantern had guttered at half past four, and after that he had walked by the lighthouse itself—its beam swinging over him every seven seconds, a slow white hand turning him toward the door.

Seven seconds. He would count them for the rest of his life, though he did not know it yet.

When the station door opened, the woman standing in it was backlit by lamplight and looked, for one disorienting moment, like a figure cut from paper: dark hair pinned severely, a wool shawl drawn tight, eyes that took his measure in the space between one swing of the beam and the next.

"You're the runner from Harrow Bay," she said. Not a question. "They sent you into *this*?"

"The road closes tonight," Emory said. His teeth were chattering badly enough that the words came out in pieces. "I'm to—I have papers for—the keeper."

"I'm the keeper." She stepped back. "Get inside before the sea changes its mind about you."

* * *

Here is what Emory Voss knew about Greywater Point, and he knew it precisely, because knowing things was his whole trade:

He knew that the Meridian Shipping wreck two winters past had taken eleven lives and spawned three years of sealed testimony. He knew that the inquiry convening when the ice broke had a list of names attached to it, and that he had copied that list out fair, in his own neat clerk's hand, forty-one times—one for each party summoned. He knew the names the way a man knows a hymn: without effort, without always hearing them.

He knew that Almeida was on the list.

He had not known, stepping into the warm yellow kitchen of the Greywater Point light station, shaking seawater off himself like a dog, that the woman handing him a towel was Sable Almeida. He learned it ninety seconds later, and the learning arrived not as a thought but as a physical event—a cold drop straight down the center of his chest, as if the storm had followed him indoors.

Because she said it herself. She said it easily, ladling something out of a pot, barley and salt fish and something root-grown, the food of a household that had made its peace with distance. "You'll take soup first and questions after. Rosalind! A plate for our guest. My daughter—Rosalind, this is—" and she looked at him then, properly, waiting for his name.

"Voss," he said. "Emory Voss. Clerk to Magistrate Halloway."

Something crossed her face. It was small. It lasted less than a breath. If he had been any less tired, any less cold, any less a boy who still believed the world sorted cleanly into the guilty and the merely wet, he might have missed it entirely. But he was none of those things, and so he saw it: the flicker of a lamp behind a shuttered window. There and gone.

"Magistrate Halloway," Sable Almeida repeated. She set the bowl down in front of him with a care that seemed, suddenly, deliberate. "And what does the magistrate send all the way to Greywater Point in the teeth of a gale?"

"Papers," Emory said, and heard how thin it sounded. "For your signature."

"My signature." She smiled. It was a good smile. It reached her eyes. That was the thing he would remember afterward, lying awake on the pallet she made up in the oil room—that the smile reached her eyes, and her eyes never changed at all. "Well. The law travels far to find me. Eat, Mr. Voss. You look like something the tide returned."

That phrase—he wrote it down later, in the margin of his day-book, because a clerk is a creature of habit and habit is a kind of armor—*something the tide returned*. As if the sea gave things back here. As if the house had experience of it.

* * *

The daughter came in while he ate.

She was fourteen or thereabouts, all elbows and attention, with her mother's dark coloring and none of her mother's discipline; her eyes went everywhere—to his ruined boots by the fire, to the oilcloth shape under his coat where he'd hung it to drip, to the folio itself, which he had, with a professionalism he would later be ashamed of, set on the bench beside him rather than surrendering it to the room.

"You walked the cliff path?" the girl asked. "In this?"

"There wasn't another way."

"Mother says the sea takes walkers every winter." She said it lightly, but Emory noticed she said it looking at her mother, not at him, and he noticed that the mother did not answer, and he noticed that the silence between them had a shape, the way furniture has a shape in a dark room—you don't see it, but you'd bruise on it if you stood up wrong.

"Rosalind," Sable said. "The guest hasn't finished his soup."

It was nothing. It was a mother managing her child at table. Emory told himself it was nothing while he ate, and the fire cracked, and the wind came off the water in long freight-train pushes against the stone walls, and above them all—this was the detail his memory would keep, the one that made the night feel staged, arranged by someone with an appetite for irony—the great lamp turned overhead. Every seven seconds the beam swept the windows and laid a bright bar across the floorboards, across the table, across the folio with the magistrate's seal, and lifted, and swept again.

A house searched by its own light, over and over, all night long.

* * *

He should have slept. She made up a pallet for him in the oil room, warm off the store, with blankets that smelled of lanolin and brass polish, and she said goodnight at the door with the folio still unopened between them, and she said, "Whatever's in there will keep till morning. The ice isn't going anywhere and neither am I."

But Emory Voss was twenty-three years old and had been trusted with a list of forty-one names, and he lay on the pallet doing arithmetic instead of sleeping.

Forty-one summonses. Thirty-eight delivered. Two households gone inland ahead of the storms, untraceable until spring. And the thirty-ninth, the thirty-ninth was the one the magistrate had sent his youngest clerk to carry personally, through a dying gale, up a coast where the roads closed early—not because a signature required it, but because Halloway wanted eyes on the Almeida house before the ice came down and cut Greywater Point off from everything. *Look at them,* the old man had said, tapping the name with one blunt finger. *See how they live. See who visits. An inquiry is paper, boy. Paper tells you what people signed. I want to know what they're afraid of.*

And here was the trouble, the reason sleep kept sliding off him like wet rope: Emory had looked. And what he had seen was a woman whose hands shook only when she thought no one was counting, and a girl who watched the folio the way hungry people watch bread, and a silence shaped like furniture in every room of the house—and now, at some black hour past midnight, footsteps.

Soft ones. Above him. Crossing the floor of the kitchen, pausing, crossing again.

He lay still. The beam swept. Seven seconds. The bar of light traveled the wall over his head, found the seam of the oil-room door—which did not close fully, which had never closed fully, which stood open perhaps two inches, enough to show a slice of the dark kitchen beyond—and moved on.

Through that slice he saw her pass. Sable Almeida, dressed, shawled, carrying no lantern. Walking toward the stairs that led up to the lamp gallery. Then stopping. Then—this was the part that turned his blood to well-water—turning, slowly, her face toward the oil-room door, toward the two inches of open dark, as if she could hear his heartbeat through it.

The beam swung around. For exactly one sweep, the light caught her face full on through the gap.

She was not looking at the door. She was looking at the folio—or where the folio had been. He had brought it to the pallet with him. It lay under the blanket against his ribs, and he understood in that moment that if she asked for it in the morning, he would not be able to say whether he had hidden it out of duty or terror, and that the distinction would not matter to anyone, least of all to him.

Then the light passed, and she was a shadow again, and she climbed the stairs to the lamp, and Emory Voss lay in the smell of brass and whale oil and counted sweeps until dawn.

* * *

Morning came gray and furious. The storm had not broken; it had settled in, the way bad weather and bad news both do, and when he looked out the kitchen window the cliff path was gone under spray and the world ended ten yards out in white confusion.

"No boat will come for you today," Sable said, setting down bread and tea. She said it evenly, factually, a keeper reporting conditions. "Perhaps not tomorrow either. The ferry master won't risk the channel once the ice starts packing." She paused, and the pause was perfectly ordinary, and Emory hated how perfectly ordinary it was. "You'll be comfortable enough. We're used to being cut off."

*I'll bet you are,* he did not say.

He got the folio out instead. Did it deliberately, at the table, watching her face across the steam of the tea. "My instructions, Mrs. Almeida. A receipt of service, and then—" he drew the packet free of the oilcloth, and the magistrate's seal sat red and fat in the lamplight like a wound, "—the summons itself. To be read by you alone, or read aloud, whichever you prefer."

The girl, Rosalind, was in the doorway. Neither of them had seen her arrive. She had that quality, Emory was beginning to understand—the whole house had it—of people appearing in rooms they had already been listening to.

"Read it aloud," Rosalind said.

"Rosalind." Her mother's voice was quiet and carried like a shot.

"Why not?" The girl's chin came up. "If it's nothing. If we're nothing. Read it aloud."

And there it was, the thing Halloway had sent him to find, laid bare on a scarred pine table between bread and tea: not guilt, exactly, not confession—guilt would almost have been a relief—but fear wearing the mask of order. A household holding very still around a secret, the way water holds still around a drowned thing.

Sable Almeida looked at her daughter for a long moment. Then at Emory. Then out the window, where the beam of her own lighthouse swept past, again, again, seven seconds, a slow white search that never stopped and never found anything, because there was nothing left outside to find—it had all come indoors.

"Aloud, then," she said, and her voice did not shake, and Emory understood that this was the most frightening thing he had witnessed yet.

He broke the seal. Unfolded the summons. And began to read the name of her dead husband aloud into the kitchen of the house the dead man had kept.
