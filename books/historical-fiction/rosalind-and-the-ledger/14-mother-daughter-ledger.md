## 14. Mother, Daughter, Ledger

**SABLE**

The clerk slept in the boat room with his boots on, one arm curled around a satchel that held nothing but a change of shirt and an official seal he probably thought she hadn't noticed. Sable had given him the good blanket, the one Edric's mother had woven, because paranoia was no reason to be inhospitable. He'd eaten two bowls of fish stew and asked careful questions about the weather, about the ferry schedule, about whether the family had lived at Greywater long — each question landing like a pebble dropped down a well, waiting for the splash.

Now he was asleep, or pretending to be, and the storm had pulled back just enough to show stars through torn cloud, and Rosalind was standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms crossed, watching her mother bank the fire.

"You're going to tell me," Rosalind said. Not a question.

Sable set the poker down. Outside, the wind worried at the gallery rail like an animal testing a fence. She had rehearsed this speech for eleven days, on the stair, in the oil room, at the sink — eleven days of opening her mouth in empty rooms and saying *Rosalind, there is something you must know* — and now that the moment had arrived, every rehearsal deserted her.

"Yes," she said. "Sit down."

"I've been sitting down my whole life. Sitting down is how you kept me."

The words went in like a splinter, clean and deep. Sable pulled out a chair anyway, and to her surprise Rosalind took it, folding herself into it the way she did when she meant to stay a while — feet hooked around the chair legs, hands flat on the table as if to hold it steady against whatever came next.

Sable sat opposite her. Between them: a candle, a salt dish, the scar where Edric had once set a hot pan down without thinking. The ledger was three rooms away under the floor of the oil room, wrapped in oilcloth, weighing what it had always weighed and more than any paper should.

"Two winters ago," Sable began, "the *Meridian* went down off Halloway Shoal."

"I know. Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows she sank. Nobody asks why she sank with her pumps unmanned and her cargo manifest signed twice." Sable heard her own voice come out flat, official, like the magistrate's letter, and hated it. She stopped. Started again, plainer. "Your father knew things about that sinking. Things some very rich men needed buried. And your father — and your uncle — took money to bury them."

Rosalind didn't move. Her hands stayed flat on the table, but her knuckles changed color.

"There's a book," Sable said. "A ledger. It records the payments. *For silence kept*, the entries say. It's been in this house since before you were born, and I found it four months ago, and I have been carrying it alone ever since because I was afraid."

"Afraid of what?"

"That's the part I owe you honestly." Sable turned the salt dish a quarter turn, needing her hands busy. "I was afraid of the name. If the inquiry came — and it has come — I thought maybe if we were quiet, if we were small, if nobody looked too hard at Greywater Point, we might keep the berth on the ferry. Keep the house. Keep each other. I told myself I was protecting you."

"You were protecting yourself."

The lamp above the table hissed. Sable let the accusation sit there, because it was true, or true enough, and a half-true thing deserved to be seen whole before it could be forgiven.

"Yes," she said. "Both. I don't know the proportions anymore. But I know this: every day I didn't tell you, I chose my fear over you, and I am not going to do it one more hour. So ask me anything. Anything at all. I will answer every question you have until dawn if that's what it takes, and then I will still be here answering."

Rosalind looked at her for a long time. Her eyes were Edric's eyes — grey, quick, always reading two lines ahead — and Sable watched them work now, watching her mother the way she'd learned to watch weather.

"One question first," Rosalind said. "Is that why the clerk is sleeping in our boat room?"

* * *

**ROSALIND**

Her mother had lied beautifully. That was the worst discovery of the night — worse than the ledger, worse even than the entries in Tobias's hand that Rosalind had read by storm-lantern while her mother slept. All those years, all those evenings, her mother's face had been a lit window with the shutters closed behind it, and Rosalind had never once suspected there was anything but glass.

*For silence kept.*

She turned the phrase over in her mind the way she'd turn a stone found on the beach, looking at its underside. Fourteen years of silences came flooding backward through it, rearranging themselves. The year they hadn't gone to the Halloway regatta. The way her mother burned correspondence unopened, feeding the stove with envelopes like they were something venomous. The way Uncle Tobias's visits always ended with him standing at the window, looking out at the water, looking at everything except them.

"He wasn't drowned by accident, was he." It came out of her flat, and she heard her own voice sound like her mother's, and hated that too.

"I don't know," Sable said. "There's a page — his last entry — where he wrote that he meant to testify. It's smudged. I've been trying to read it for weeks. And Tobias said—" Her mother stopped, and Rosalind watched her decide, deliberately, to finish. "Tobias said the sea doesn't choose. Which is a way of saying nothing, which is what he does."

"And you let him sleep under our roof."

"He's your father's brother."

"So was Judas somebody's brother." Rosalind heard how cruel it sounded, and part of her wanted to take it back, and a larger part wanted to say something crueler, and underneath both parts was a smaller, colder voice asking: *if she hid a ledger from me, what else fits under the floorboards?*

She stood up. Walked to the window. The harbor was black water stitched with whitecaps, and far out the last ferry's masthead light swung at anchor like a slow pendulum counting something down.

"When I found it," Rosalind said, without turning around, "I sat on the oil room floor and read the whole thing. Every entry. Do you know what I was looking for?"

"No."

"A mistake. A different Edric Almeida somewhere in those pages. A cousin. A coincidence of handwriting." She pressed her forehead briefly against the cold pane. "He wrote so neatly, Mama. That's what I can't get past. He took their money and he wrote down that he took it, in his neat little hand, with dates, like it was flour or lamp oil. Like it was honest accounting."

Behind her, the chair creaked. Her mother rising, or shifting, or simply unable to keep still either.

"He was keeping evidence," Sable said quietly. "That's how I make sense of it, anyway. A man who means to confess keeps receipts."

"Or a man who means to confess is a story we tell ourselves because the other story is that our husband, our father, sold dead men's silence and banked it." Rosalind turned. Her mother stood by the table with the candle between them, and for the first time since the storms began, Rosalind saw her clearly — not as the fixed point everything else orbited, but as a woman of forty-one who was afraid, who had been afraid for years, who had gotten good at it, the way sailors get good at walking decks.

"Why didn't you burn it?" Rosalind asked. "You could have. Before the letter. Before any of it. One match and we're nobody again, just a keeper and her daughter."

It was, she realized as soon as she said it, the same question Tobias had asked at the supper table with his charming face on. She flinched at the company she was keeping.

But her mother didn't flinch. She considered it, actually considered it, the way she considered weather and tides — seriously, without hurry.

"Because it's the only true thing he left," Sable said. "And because burning it wouldn't un-sink the *Meridian*. It would only make us liars too. And because—" her voice caught, steadied— "because if I ever gave it up, I wanted it to be to someone who would use it right. Not to protect ourselves. To finish what he started."

Rosalind came back to the table. Sat down. The anger was still there — it would be there tomorrow and the day after, she could feel it settling into her like ballast — but beneath it something else had begun, cautiously, like ice forming at the edges of a pond first.

"You should have told me in the beginning," she said. "When the crate washed up. You should have said, *Rosalind, your father left something ugly.*"

"I know."

"You decided for me. Again. You decided what I could carry."

"I know." Her mother's eyes were wet and she didn't wipe them, didn't hide it, and Rosalind understood suddenly that this — the not-hiding — was the apology. Words were what her mother used for weather logs. This was something else. "I will spend a long time being sorry for it. But sorry isn't the same as repaired, and I'm not asking you to be repaired tonight. I'm asking you to know what I know. After that, whatever you decide about me, you'll decide it with open eyes."

* * *

**SABLE**

They talked until the candle burned to a thumb's width and she had to light another from its stub. Rosalind asked her questions in no order at all — jumping from the wreck to the ferry manifest to the smell of the oil room to whether Tobias had known about the ledger all along — and Sable answered each one, and when she didn't know an answer she said *I don't know*, and discovered that saying it aloud cost less than she'd feared. Ignorance, admitted, weighed almost nothing. It was concealment that was heavy. She felt lighter with every truth handed across the table like dishes being dried and stacked.

Near the end, near gray dawn seeping into the east window, Rosalind went quiet for a long stretch, chin on her fists.

"Mama," she said finally. "The berth."

Sable's hands went still on the table. She had known this question was coming; she'd been dreading it since chapter upon chapter of nights ago, since the manifest went up on the ferry office door.

"If it comes to one seat," Rosalind said carefully, "I want you to promise me you won't do anything stupid."

"Define stupid."

"Anything. Giving it to me without asking. Giving it to him out of guilt. Drawing straws like it's a game. I want you to look at me and tell me what you're thinking when the time comes, even if what you're thinking is terrible. Especially then."

And there it was — the whole night folded into one request. Not *save me*. Not even *choose me*. Just: *let me see the ledger of you, too.*

"All right," Sable said. "I promise. No decisions made in the dark. Whatever happens at that dock, we'll both know exactly what was chosen and why. That much I can give you."

Rosalind nodded slowly, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch — not forgiveness, Sable noted, not yet, maybe not for years — but the posture of a person who has been handed the wheel on a rough sea and told she'll never be locked out of the chart room again.

"It's not repaired," Rosalind said, echoing her mother's own words back to her like a receipt.

"No."

"But it's honest."

"For tonight. I'll try for tomorrow."

Rosalind almost smiled. Almost. Then her gaze went past her mother's shoulder to the window, and sharpened.

"Mama." Her voice dropped. "Look."

Out on the black water, the last ferry's anchor light was moving. Rising. The chain coming in, link by link, the great ship waking ahead of schedule — hours ahead of schedule — while the river still ran dark and unfrozen and the storm clouds massed again on the northern horizon like a second tide deciding to come early.

Down the hall, the magistrate's clerk's door opened. His footsteps crossed the boat room, quick and purposeful, and stopped outside the kitchen.

"The ferry master signaled at midnight," he said through the doorway, apologetic, breathless, awake after all. "Ice reports from upstream. She sails at first light. One berth, Mistress Almeida. And you'll need to decide before she clears the harbor mouth."
