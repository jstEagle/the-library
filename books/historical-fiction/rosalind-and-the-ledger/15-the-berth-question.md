## 15. The Berth Question

The ferry master came up from the dock at low tide, boots crunching on the frozen shingle, and he did not take his hat off in the house, which Rosalind knew was bad manners and also meant the news was bad. Men took their hats off for grief or respect. Men kept their hats on when they wanted to be gone before anyone could argue with them.

He stood in the kitchen with the door open behind him, letting the cold pour in like something spilled, and he said it plain: "One berth per household. That's what the manifest says, and that's what I'm bound by. One seat on the *Corvid* when she sails Thursday, if she sails at all. After that, the river's done for the season."

Rosalind watched her mother's face while he said it. Sable Almeida had a face that kept things, like the sea keeps things — you never saw what sank, only the surface going on being water. Her mother's surface went on being water.

"One berth," Tobias said, from his chair by the stove, and there was something almost pleased in the way he repeated it, turning the words over like coins he'd just counted. "One per household."

"That's right, Mr. Almeida."

"And who counts as household?" Tobias asked, mild as milk, and Rosalind felt the question land in her chest like a dropped anchor. Because she knew — of course she knew; she'd known since the manifest went up on the ferry office wall two weeks early, since her mother started sleeping with her boots on — that the answer to *who counts as household* was the whole rest of her life folded small into one word.

"Well," the ferry master said, uncomfortable, shifting his hat from one hand to the other, "keeper of the station, that's the head of the household, that'd be Mrs. Almeida. Then dependents. The girl. And—" he hesitated, and Rosalind saw him glance at Tobias the way people glance at weather they can't read, "—brother-in-law, is it? Family?"

"Family," Tobias agreed warmly. "Blood family. Edric's own brother. I've been here these three weeks keeping my sister-in-law company through the storms, haven't I? Helping with the light."

*Helping with the light.* Rosalind bit the inside of her cheek until it hurt. She thought about the shattered lamp glass, the missing ledger, the night her uncle had come down from the lamp room smelling of cold and oil and lies, and she said nothing, because saying nothing was apparently the family art, practiced by everyone but her.

The ferry master looked at Sable. Everyone looked at Sable.

"It'll be decided," Sable said quietly, "before Thursday."

"Aye." He put his hat back on, which somehow made it worse, made it official, made it over. "I'm sorry for it, truly. Never seen a winter post a manifest like this one. But orders are orders, and the ice doesn't negotiate." He nodded around the room, at all of them, as though they were already portraits of the departed, and let himself out.

The door closed. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind came off the river with a sound like a blade being drawn slowly from a sheath.

Tobias broke the silence first, the way he always did, filling rooms the way smoke fills rooms — everywhere, suffocating, looking for all the world like it belonged there.

"Well," he said. "No sense pretending it isn't what it is. One berth. And there's a natural order to these things, isn't there? Precedent. When a household must choose, the choice goes by blood and by right. Edric would have—"

"Don't," Rosalind said.

Everyone stopped. Even the wind seemed to pause outside to listen.

She hadn't planned to speak. She had spent three weeks learning to speak less, watch more, count everything — the days, the entries in a ledger she'd read by candlelight with shaking hands, the lies accumulating in her house like driftwood after a wreck. But something about *Edric would have* — about her uncle wearing her father's name like a coat he'd stolen — cracked something open in her, and out it came.

"Don't say what Edric would have wanted. You don't get to say that."

Tobias turned in his chair, unhurried, and gave her the smile he used on adults, the one with all the warmth of a shop window. "Rosalind. Child. No one's taking anything from you."

"You're taking everything from me. That's exactly what you're doing. There's one seat and three of us and you've been doing arithmetic since the day your boat touched the rocks." She heard her own voice go high and hated it and couldn't stop it. "You want me to sit here and be polite while you explain why the man should go first? Because he's a man? Because he's older? Because he helped write the thing that's ruined us?"

"*Rosalind.*" Her mother's voice now, quiet, warning, and Rosalind flinched because it wasn't anger in it — it was fear, and fear from her mother was worse than any storm.

"No," Rosalind said, standing now, her chair scraping back. "No, I want to say it. Somebody has to say it out loud in this house or we'll all smother on it." She pointed at Tobias, hand not quite steady, hating that too. "You took money for silence. You took it with my father, and then my father drowned, and then you came here with your questions and your charm and you lost the ledger in the night and now you stand in our kitchen telling us about *precedent*. The precedent is that men like you decide, and everyone else pays. Well, I'm fourteen years old and I've read every page of that ledger, Uncle Tobias, and I am innocent. Not good — innocent. There's a difference, and the difference is that I didn't sign anything, and neither did my mother, and the magistrate will want to know why the guilty ones got warm seats across the ice and the innocent ones froze at Greywater Point."

The room went very still.

It was, Rosalind realized later, the longest speech she had ever made in her life, and possibly the truest, and it changed nothing, which was its own kind of lesson.

Tobias regarded her for a moment with an expression she couldn't name — something moving behind his face like fish under ice — and then he laughed softly, shook his head, and said to Sable, not to Rosalind: "She's Edric's daughter, no mistake. All fire and figures."

And then he rose, straightened his cuffs, and left the kitchen with the unhurried tread of a man who believed time was on his side.

* * *

He wasn't wrong. Time was on his side, and Rosalind knew it, and knowing it was like carrying stones in her pockets.

Because here was the truth of the berth question, the truth nobody would say aloud: her mother held the deciding word, and her mother said nothing. Not cruelly, not coldly — worse. Thoughtfully. As though weighing.

Rosalind spent the next two days being measured. She could feel it happening, feel herself set on scales she hadn't stepped onto. Every time she carried wood in without being asked, she caught her mother watching. Every time she trimmed the spare wicks or recited the tide tables perfectly or ate everything on her plate like a sensible, salvageable child, she felt the invisible pencil moving. *Useful. Steady. Worth saving.*

And beside her on the other scale: Tobias, with his thirty-odd years and his strong back and his blood tie and his terrible, useful knowledge of everything the inquiry would ask. Her mother couldn't testify to what she didn't understand, and Tobias understood everything. He was guilty and therefore valuable, and Rosalind was innocent and therefore — what? Expendable? Was that how the arithmetic ran?

*Found lighter than an uncle's guilt.* She lay awake the first night turning the phrase over, having invented it herself, hating it, unable to put it down. If her mother chose him, it wouldn't mean Rosalind wasn't loved. That was the horror of it. It would mean love wasn't the unit of measurement. It would mean that guilt, knowledge, usefulness, *precedent* — that those were heavier, and a daughter's whole weight wasn't enough to tip the scale against them.

The second night she heard them talking through the wall, low voices, and pressed her ear to the plaster like the eavesdropper she'd become.

"—the inquiry convenes when the ice breaks," Tobias was saying. "Sable. Sister. Listen to me. They'll want a witness who can speak to the Meridian payments, the dates, the amounts. You can give them the ledger. You cannot give them the meaning of half of it. I can. I keep myself alive, I keep that testimony breathing, and I can swear the worst of it onto the dead and the fled. Edric's entry, the confession — I'll bury it properly this time, for both our sakes. You send the girl and you lose your best shield, and the girl grows up an orphan of the state in some inland town where nobody knows her name. Is that the inheritance you want for her?"

A long silence. Then her mother's voice, so quiet Rosalind had to hold her breath to catch it:

"You've given this a great deal of thought, Tobias."

"I've had three weeks and nothing else to do."

Another silence, longer. Then, barely audible, almost tender, which was the most frightening thing yet:

"I think about the light, Tobias. Every night I climb those stairs and light the lamp, and every night I think: this is the only honest thing I do. Everything else in my hands is someone else's mess. And when Thursday comes, whatever I choose, I have to be able to climb those stairs afterward and still see."

Rosalind slid down the wall to the floor and sat in the dark with her knees pulled up.

*Whatever I choose.* Not *whom.* Whatever. Like a sum. Like a ledger entry.

She thought about running to the dock and hiding on the *Corvid* like stowaways did in stories, and dismissed it — the ferry master counted heads twice, and besides, if she hid, her mother would simply fill the berth with Tobias by default, and the choice would be made *for* her, which was somehow the cruelest outcome of all. At least if she were chosen, she'd know she'd been weighed and found worth something. At least then.

That was the terror she couldn't say aloud, even to herself, except now she was saying it to herself at three in the morning staring at the ceiling: that being saved might be worse than being left. That she would spend her whole life knowing she'd been picked the way you pick which books go in the last crate — by weight, by usefulness, by what fits — and that her mother had stood at the shelf a long time, considering, before choosing her.

Or not choosing her.

She got up. She dressed. She went down through the cold house past her uncle's door — silent, but she could feel him behind it awake the way you can feel weather coming — and out into the blue-black morning to the lamp room, where her mother sat in the chair by the light, wrapped in a blanket, watching the beam swing out over the frozen river.

Rosalind stood in the doorway. Her mother didn't turn around.

"If you're measuring me," Rosalind said, "stop it. Weighing me. Whatever you're doing with that look."

Her mother was quiet a long moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the ice boomed — a deep, tectonic groan, the river settling its bones.

"I'm trying to find a way," her mother said finally, still facing the glass, "to tell you something without it breaking either of us. And I haven't found it yet. So I'm sitting here practicing."

"Practice on me. I'm stronger than you think."

That made her mother turn, at last, and look at her — really look, the way she used to when Rosalind was small, before the ledger, before everything, when looking at each other had been simple. And Rosalind saw something in her mother's face that scared her more than Tobias ever had: not doubt, not calculation, but grief arriving early, grief for a thing that hadn't happened yet, the way sailors grieve a storm still two days out at sea.

"Thursday," Sable said. "Before the ferry sails, I'll tell you everything. I promise you that much, and I have learned — God help me — that promises are the only currency left in this house."

"Then promise me one thing more." Rosalind's voice cracked and she let it. "Promise me you're not choosing him."

Her mother closed her eyes. The beam swept the window, once, twice, a slow white wheel of light, and in its passing her mother's face went from lit to dark to lit again, like a page being turned by someone else's hand.

"Rosalind," she said, "there's a page in the ledger I haven't shown anyone. Smudged. Half-legible. I've been reading it every night this week, and I think — I think it changes everything. About your father. About why the berth exists at all. About what your uncle is really afraid of."

The ice boomed again, closer this time, a crack running out under the darkness toward the channel.

"Tomorrow," her mother said, "come to the oil room with me. Before the ferry master arrives. There's something Edric wrote that I need you to see — because whichever way I decide, you deserve to know what your father died trying to say."

Rosalind stood in the doorway of the lamp room, fourteen years old, weighed and waiting, and understood suddenly that the berth was never really the question at all.

The question was what her father had written in his last living hours — and whether her uncle would kill again to keep it unread.
