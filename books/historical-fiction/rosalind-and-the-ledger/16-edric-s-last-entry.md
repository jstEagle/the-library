## 16. Edric's Last Entry

The storm had passed in the night without breaking anything, which felt like an insult. Sable woke to a sky scrubbed clean and hard blue, the kind of cold that made the windowpanes sing when you touched them, and she lay in her narrow bed listening to the house tick and settle and thinking: he wrote it down. He wrote something down. She had been circling the thought all night like a moth against glass.

The ledger sat where it always sat now, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a floorboard under her bed, because after the clerk had come and gone—after that boy with his magistrate's eyes had slept one night under her roof and left on the morning supply boat, glancing back at the tower once, only once—she no longer trusted the oil room. The floorboard creaked when she lifted it. She had learned to lift it silently, the way Rosalind had learned to come down stairs without sound, and the thought that they were learning each other's stealth, mother and daughter, like enemies in a slow war, made her jaw tighten.

But this morning Rosalind was still asleep. Sable could hear the even breathing through the wall, or imagined she could, and she took the ledger to the kitchen table anyway, unwrapped it in the gray early light, and turned past the entries she knew by heart now—the payments, the dates, the neat column of sums that added up to two winters of her widowhood—and went looking for the last page.

She had avoided it before. That was the honest word for it. Avoided. There is a way of not-reading a thing that feels almost like virtue, like tact toward the dead, and Sable had practiced it every time her thumb reached the final leaf of Edric's handwriting. The page was damaged. Water had gotten to it somewhere—in the crate from the tide, or before, in whatever dark place he'd kept it—and the ink had run in long gray weeps so that whole lines dissolved into fog. She had told herself there was nothing there worth the pain of deciphering. A man's last arithmetic. Nothing.

This morning she spread the page flat under the lamp, though the daylight was strong enough, because lamplight came at the paper from below and coaxed out impressions the sun flattened away. She had learned that trick from her father, who had been a customs man on the river for thirty years and could read a bill of lading through three layers of forgery. *Hold it to the flame's angle,* he used to say, tapping the underside of a page with one blunt finger. *Ink lies going down but tells the truth coming up.*

Edric's handwriting was familiar the way a voice heard through a wall is familiar. She knew its habits—the way he crossed his t's with a single impatient stroke, the way his 4s looked like sails. The top half of the page was sums again, the same tidy bookkeeping of silence, dated eleven days before the boat went down. Then the water damage began. A stain like a continent spread across the lower third, and beneath it, half-legible, the writing changed. It was still his hand, but pressed harder, the pen digging into the paper as if he were trying to write through it, through the desk, through the floor of the world.

She worked at it the way she worked at anything—with patience that was really just stubbornness wearing a calmer coat. She held the page over the lamp flame at the angle her father had taught her. She breathed on the paper and watched the fainter strokes darken with the damp. She copied what she could read onto a scrap, leaving gaps, then went back and filled the gaps by habit, by knowing how Edric ended sentences, by knowing the words a guilty man reaches for.

By the time Rosalind's footsteps sounded on the stairs, Sable had the whole of it.

*They know I mean to speak. T. says wait until after the ice. I will not wait. If anything happens to me, this book is the truth of it. Every entry. Let them read what silence cost and who was paid. I go to the magistrate at Greywater Landing on the —*

The date was gone. Eaten by water, by salt, by two years of forgetting. But everything else stood there like bones.

Sable set the scrap down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she would have expected shaking. Instead there was a strange clarity, the clarity of the hour after a storm passes and you walk the gallery rail checking for damage and find the light itself unharmed and think, absurdly, *well, then.*

He had meant to testify. Eleven days before he drowned, Edric Almeida had written down his intention to carry this ledger to the magistrate at Greywater Landing, and he had never arrived, and the boat had been found broken on the shallows off Meridian Point, and everyone—the harbor board, the insurance men, the neighbors who brought covered dishes and said *the sea takes its own*—everyone had agreed it was weather. A squall out of the northeast. Bad luck. The sea takes its own.

And Tobias had said, standing in her kitchen not ten days ago with his charm fraying at the edges: *he meant to confess.* Said it like absolution. Said it like a man handing her grief a softer pillow.

But T. says wait until after the ice.

T. Tobias. And Edric would not wait. And then Edric drowned, and Tobias waited, and waited, and came ashore asking about papers with questions shaped like concern.

Sable sat very still while the kettle began its low complaint on the stove. She did not make tea. She sat with the ledger open in front of her and let the new knowledge move through her body by degrees, the way cold water moves through a wader's boots—not all at once, but finding every low place.

Her first feeling, shamefully, was anger at Edric. Eleven days. He had known for eleven days that he meant to speak, and he had spent them here, at this table, eating the bread she baked, teaching Rosalind knots, laughing at the girl's terrible whittling—and he had said nothing. Not to his wife. Not a word, not a look, not a letter hidden in a boot. He had carried the weight alone and then carried it into the river, and left her to inherit it blind. Two winters she had grieved a good man lost to weather. Now she had to grieve him twice: once for the drowning, once for the silence, and the second grief was worse, because the first had at least been clean.

Her second feeling came slower and settled deeper. It was not anger. It was purpose, arriving like the tide arrives—not suddenly, but absolutely, filling every channel it finds.

Because the sentence wasn't finished. *I go to the magistrate at Greywater Landing on the —* and then nothing. The water took the date and the river took the man. But the intention stood. Edric Almeida had sworn, in ink, in his own hand, to testify. And the sea had stopped him, or a man had, and either way the testimony was still sitting here under her floorboard, patient as ballast, waiting for someone to finish the voyage he started.

It should have been me from the start, she thought, and didn't know if she meant carrying the ledger or carrying the secret, and supposed it was both.

Footsteps on the stairs. Rosalind came into the kitchen with her hair unbrushed and her collar crooked and the particular alertness she wore these days, the look of a girl cataloguing everything, filing it against future need. Fourteen years old and already keeping her own ledger of the household's lies. Sable closed the old ledger and put her palm flat on its cover.

"Tea?" Rosalind asked, already reaching for the kettle, and it was such a domestic little word, such an ordinary offering, that it nearly broke her. Nearly. Sable looked at her daughter—at the line of Edric's jaw on her, at the stubborn set of her own mouth below it—and felt the two griefs and the purpose all pressing together behind her ribs.

"Not yet," Sable said. "Sit down a minute."

Rosalind sat, wary, hands folded, watching the closed book under her mother's hand with eyes that missed nothing and pretended to miss less.

"Your father," Sable began, and stopped, because where do you start? At the beginning costs too much and there isn't time, and besides, some part of her—the part still reading smudged pages by lamplight—had decided that the next person to hear the whole of it would be the magistrate, not the child. "Your father was a better man than some people say lately," she finished, "and a worse one than you remember. Both things are true. I'm going to make sure the true version is the one that survives."

Rosalind's eyes flicked to the book. Back up. "Is that his?"

"It's his unfinished business." Sable stood, wrapping the ledger in its oilcloth with quick, sure movements. "And as of this morning, it's mine to finish."

* * *

She spent the day doing ordinary things with extraordinary attention. She trimmed the wicks. She logged the barometer, which stood high and steady and lied with every reading, because the ice was coming regardless of what the glass said. She walked the gallery rail in the afternoon wind and looked down at the river, which was thickening along the banks now, plates of gray ice riding the current like broken crockery. The ferry master had posted the schedule: last crossing in nine days, weather permitting, one berth per household. Nine days. The inquiry convened when the ice broke, which meant spring, which meant she had a winter to survive first—a winter alone at the light with a daughter's questions and a brother-in-law's appetites and a book that named names.

She caught herself composing the testimony as she climbed the tower stairs. Not deliberately; it assembled itself, sentence by sentence, out of everything she'd read and overheard and deduced. *I am Sable Almeida, keeper at Greywater Point, widow of Edric Almeida, and I hold a ledger in his hand recording payments received from Meridian Shipping for the suppression of witness statements following the wreck of the Coriander's Pride.* Names would be needed. Dates. The Meridian men whose initials dotted the margins—M.H., R.C., the ones Tobias had flinched away from mentioning. She would give them all. She found, testing the thought like a foot on uncertain ice, that it held.

That was the strangest part. All autumn she had treated the ledger as a threat—to her name, to Rosalind's berth, to whatever fragile peace a widow can build on a rock. A thing to hide, or burn, or bargain with. Now it had turned over in her hands like a card and shown its other face, and the other face was Edric's voice saying *let them read what silence cost.* He hadn't kept the book to profit from it. The profits were spent, or buried, or never collected—he'd been paid in fear more than coin, she understood that now. He'd kept it as evidence. As a promise to himself. And he'd died eleven days shy of keeping it.

Or been died, said a colder voice. Or been stopped.

She did not yet believe Tobias capable of murder. She noted that she did not yet believe it, and heard in the phrasing exactly how little the belief weighed. He had been on the water often enough that winter. He had known Edric's routes. He had been the one to identify the wrecked boat, the one who said *squall out of the northeast* before anyone had asked him what he thought. Little kindnesses, all of them, that rearranged themselves in the new light into something else entirely.

At dusk she lit the lamp and stood watching the beam swing out over the freezing river, and she spoke to him aloud, the way she hadn't since the funeral, since before the crate washed up, since before she'd begun to feel like a stranger married to a stranger's ghost.

"You meant to tell the truth," she said to the glass. "So I'll tell it. You're done carrying it, Edric. Do you hear me? You're done."

The beam swept the water, the shore, the blackening ice, and came around again, patient, indifferent, hers.

* * *

Rosalind was waiting in the kitchen when she came down, and Sable knew before she saw the girl's face that something had happened, because Rosalind was doing her imitation of casualness, which had always been terrible—which consisted of sitting very straight and looking extremely interested in nothing.

"There was a boat," Rosalind said. "Past the point. No lights. Rowing quiet, if you ask me, and somebody rowing quiet past a lighthouse in November wants something."

Sable's stomach dropped and steadied. "Which direction?"

"Toward the Landing. Then it hugged the bank north." Rosalind hesitated, and Sable watched her decide whether to spend the rest of what she knew. "There was more than one man. And—" the girl's voice dropped, and for a moment she was fourteen again, purely fourteen, frightened and fierce at once— "Uncle Tobias's skiff is gone from its mooring."

Sable stood in her own kitchen, lamplight on her face, the ledger's weight present under the floorboard above them like a heartbeat in the walls, and understood that the window was closing faster than any schedule had promised. Whatever Tobias had decided—whether to flee, to sell, to bury the book or the woman who kept it—he had decided it tonight.

"Get your boots," Sable said. "And bring the lantern. Don't light it till we're down the path."

Rosalind didn't ask why. That was the thing about her daughter that terrified and proud her in equal measure: she never asked why anymore. She just got her boots.

Outside, the wind had turned. It came down the river from the north now, smelling of iron and deep cold, the wind that makes ice, and far out on the black water, near where the channel markers would be if anyone could see them, something knocked—wood on wood, oar against gunwale, the small human sounds of men moving carefully through a darkness they believed belonged to them.

Sable pulled her coat tight and started down toward the waterline, and behind her the lamp swept its long arm across the river, once, twice, lighting for half a heartbeat a shape on the ice that had no business being there.
