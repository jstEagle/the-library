## 17. Night of the Broken Lamp

The storm had a name before it arrived. The ferry master's runners called it the Widow, because it came up the river the way grief did—slow, then all at once, and taking whatever stood closest to the water.

Sable heard it first in the tower's bones. Greywater Point had been built to speak in weather: a low groan from the foundation stones when the wind swung north, a tick in the iron stair when frost got into the joints, a whistle through the ventilator cap that meant pressure falling fast. By dusk she had heard all three, and she climbed the hundred and six steps with her logbook under her arm and wrote, in her careful keeper's hand:

*Wind NNE, freshening. Barometer 28.6 and dropping. Expect the worst by midnight.*

Below her, the house held its breath. Rosalind was in the kitchen boiling water for tea she wouldn't drink, because boiling was something to do with her hands. Tobias sat by the stove with his boots off, as if he lived there, as if he had always lived there, telling some story about a card game in Harrowgate that Sable was not listening to. And the magistrate's clerk—young Mr. Pell, stranded four days now with his ankle wrapped in linen and his notebook never far from his hand—was in the parlor copying out shipping manifests by lamplight, ostensibly for his own records, actually for reasons nobody said aloud.

Four people in a house built for three. Five, if you counted the ledger, which Sable did. It slept beneath the floor of the oil room, wrapped in oilcloth, under forty pounds of lamp oil and a false board Edric had fitted so well she'd needed two winters to find it. She checked it every night before bed. She had not told anyone that. It felt like praying to a god she didn't believe in.

At seven o'clock she lit the lamp.

This was the part of the day that had never once failed her, not through mourning, not through scandal, not through the slow rot of everything else. You trimmed the wick. You checked the oil feed. You struck the match and touched it to the mantle and the flame took hold with its small sound like an indrawn breath, and you turned the clockwork that turned the lens, and the beam went out over black water and did what it had always done: told the truth about where the rocks were.

She stood in the lantern room a moment longer than she needed to, watching the light sweep. The river below was already wearing its first skin of ice, plates of it grinding against each other out past the point with a sound like crockery dropped in another room. Somewhere downriver, the last ferry would be tied up at Greywater dock until the weather broke—if it broke. One berth. One name on the manifest. That arithmetic still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *I'll decide tomorrow.*

It was the last coherent thought she would have for some hours.

* * *

The watch schedule was hers alone these days; there was no assistant keeper since the cuts, no second pair of hands. But tonight Pell had limped up to offer company—"I can't climb well, but I can sit," he'd said—and she'd let him, because the Widow was coming and even keepers are allowed to be afraid of the dark once a generation.

They sat in the watch room below the lantern, she with her logbook, he with his manifests, while the wind began to lean on the tower. At half past eleven she went up to check the mechanism. The clockwork was running true. The flame burned steady. The glass panes of the lantern room gleamed, each one framed in brass, each one whole.

"I'll take the two o'clock," she told Pell on her way down. "Sleep if you can."

He nodded, already half asleep in the chair, his notebook open on his knee. She remembered thinking, later, how strange it was that she hadn't looked at what he'd written. She looked at everything now. She had become a woman who read other people's handwriting the way sailors read clouds. But exhaustion is its own kind of fog, and she walked down the hundred and six steps, checked the ledger under the oil-room floor—it was there, the oilcloth cold and dry against her fingers—and lay down in her clothes with her boots beside the bed.

She woke to silence.

Not the good kind. Not the settled hush of a storm spending itself. This was the silence of something stopped. Her body knew it before her mind did—the way a mother wakes when a child stops breathing in the next room—and she was out of bed and into her boots and moving before she had finished understanding why.

The tick in the iron stair was gone. The clockwork wasn't turning.

She took the steps two at a time, and the higher she climbed the colder the air came down to meet her, and there was a smell in it that made her stomach drop before her eyes confirmed anything: hot metal, burnt oil, and under it the clean mineral smell of broken glass.

The lantern room door stood open, banging gently against its stop.

The lamp was dark.

And the glass—all around the lantern room, all eight great curved panes that had turned the small flame into a voice that could be heard across twenty miles of black water—the glass was shattered. Not cracked, not starred by a stone flung in malice. Shattered inward, deliberately, pane after pane, the brass frames bent back like ribs pried open, shards strewn across the gallery floor and glinting in the beam of—of nothing, because there was no beam, only moonlight coming through the empty frames in hard white bars, laying stripes across the floor like the bars of a cell.

Sable stood in the doorway and could not move.

There is a particular vertigo that comes to a keeper when the light fails. Every lighthouse book names it obliquely, in the language of duty and vigilance, but no book says what it actually is: the feeling that you have died and not noticed yet. For eleven years the light had been the one true sentence in her life. Edric gone, the light stayed. The money, the lies, the ledger, the letter under seal—the light stayed. And now she stood inside the dead eye of the tower and the wind came straight through the broken panes and hit her face, and somewhere below, out on the river, ice was moving in the dark where ships might be.

"No," she said aloud, to no one. "No, no—"

Then she was moving. Training is a deeper thing than shock. She grabbed the storm shutters' chains and hauled them across the empty frames, one by one, the iron slats screaming in the wind, sealing the room against the gale. She checked the oil reservoir—full. The wick—intact. The mantle—cold. The lamp itself had not been damaged beyond the simple fact that someone had snuffed it and let it go out. Someone had come up here, in the teeth of the rising storm, while she slept thirty feet below, and put out the light, and broken every pane of glass around it, and walked away.

Her hands were shaking so badly she struck the match three times before it caught.

The flame took. Small, naked, unprotected behind the shuttered iron, but burning. She wound the clockwork and the beam swept the closed shutters and threw nothing useful anywhere, and she stood there in the rattling dark with her heart going like a hammer on rock, and she understood, finally and completely, that this was not weather.

This was a message.

* * *

She found them all in the kitchen, because of course they were all in the kitchen; the whole household had been pulled awake by the scream of the shutters or the absence of the light through their windows, the way you wake when a familiar heartbeat stops.

Rosalind stood by the cold stove in her coat, hair unbound, fourteen years old and suddenly looking much younger. "Mama—the light—I saw it go out, I was awake, I saw it just—stop—"

"The glass is broken." Sable heard her own voice come out flat and strange, a voice reporting facts because feelings would drown her. "All of it. Smashed from inside the gallery. The lamp was snuffed."

Tobias was on his feet, and here was the thing Sable would turn over for hours afterward: he was dressed. Fully dressed, coat on, boots on, at two o'clock in the morning, in a house where everyone else had been torn from sleep. He saw her notice. She watched him see her noticing, and watched him decide to meet it head-on, which was either innocence or nerve.

"I couldn't sleep," he said. "Storm coming. I was going to walk out to the point and have a look at the water."

"In this?" Rosalind said.

"A man worries." He spread his hands. "Sable. Who's up there? Who could get in?"

"Nobody gets in." She said it slowly, watching him. "The gallery door locks from the watch room. The watch room door locks from the stair. Both were open tonight."

Pell was sitting very still in the corner, his bad ankle propped on a stool, his face doing an admirable job of being merely concerned. His notebook was in his coat pocket. She could see the corner of it.

"You were in the watch room," she said to him. "Until I relieved myself at two."

"I fell asleep around midnight, ma'am. I'm sorry. I heard nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Nothing." A beat. "Though the wind was up. It would have covered most sounds."

"It would have covered all sounds," Sable said. "That's rather the point, isn't it."

The silence that followed had weight and edges. Rosalind looked from Pell to Tobias to her mother, and Sable watched her daughter do the same arithmetic everyone in the room was doing, and hated every term of the equation. Four people in the house. One light destroyed. One ledger under the oil-room floor—

The ledger.

She left the kitchen without a word. Behind her she heard Rosalind say, sharply, "Mama?" and Tobias say, softly, "Let her go," and she took the stairs down to the oil room with her blood roaring in her ears.

The false board was in place. Her fingers found the seam, lifted it. The oil cans sat where they always sat.

She moved them aside and knew before her hand went in. The space beneath was shaped wrong—too shallow by exactly the thickness of a bound book. She reached in anyway, all the way to the cold stone, sweeping her arm through dust and a single long sliver of lantern glass that had not been there before, and came out with nothing.

Gone.

She knelt there on the oil-room floor in the smell of kerosene and winter, and laughed—one short, awful bark of a laugh with nothing in it—and pressed both hands over her mouth.

Someone had taken the ledger. Someone who knew where it was hidden. Only three people alive knew where it was hidden: herself, the dead man who'd built the hiding place, and whoever had watched her find it—or been told.

*Tobias.* The name arrived without surprise, which frightened her more than the theft. But then, immediately, the counter-thought, cold as river water: *Or the boy who copies manifests. Or the girl who reads everything. Or yourself, Sable—did you move it and forget? Are you so far gone?*

That was the true sabotage, she understood, kneeling in the dark. Not the glass. The glass was only the announcement. The real work was this: the vertigo, the suspicion, the way the house had turned on itself in a single hour. Whoever had done this wanted her to trust no one, including herself. A keeper who cannot trust her own memory cannot testify to anything.

She rose. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She went back upstairs.

* * *

By dawn the Widow had arrived in full, and the world outside the windows was a gray violence of sleet and spume. The river was lost in it. Somewhere out there, ice was building toward the freeze that would close the channel for the season, and the last ferry sat at the dock with its single empty berth, and no boat would move until the storm spent itself.

Sable worked. It was all she had. She and Rosalind carried boards and canvas up the hundred and six steps and sealed the broken frames as best they could from inside, working in the shrieking dark between the storm shutters, their shouts torn away by wind. Tobias appeared halfway through and hauled timber with them, silent, competent, sweating despite the cold, and Sable caught herself watching the way his hands fit the boards and thinking: *those hands have written entries in that ledger. Those hands may have smashed this glass. Both things can be true. Both things are probably true.*

Pell stayed below, and when they came down, frozen and splinter-scraped, he had laid a fire and heated water and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of confession or its own kind of courtesy, and she could no longer tell the difference.

It was Rosalind who finally said what everyone was thinking.

They stood in the kitchen, four people around a fire, steam rising off wet wool. Rosalind's face was raw with cold and something harder than cold.

"The ledger's gone," she said. Not a question. She had read it; she knew what it was; she had known, presumably, the moment her mother's face changed downstairs. "Isn't it."

Sable didn't answer. Answering was confirming, and confirming, in front of Pell, in front of Tobias, was a step she couldn't take back.

"Mama. Isn't it."

"Yes," Sable said.

"And the light." Rosalind's voice cracked and steadied. "Whoever did one did the other. They wanted us blind and they wanted us silenced. In one night. While we slept." She looked around the room, at each of them, her chin up, her eyes bright and furious and afraid. "So one of us is lying. Right now. Standing right here."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Out past the point, the Widow hammered the coast like a fist on a door.

Tobias cleared his throat. "Rosalind—"

"Don't." She said it quietly, and it landed harder than a shout. "Don't say my name like we're family tonight."

Sable moved to stand beside her daughter—not touching, but near, near enough that their shoulders almost met—and looked at the two men across the fire. The clerk with his hidden notebook and his stranded, watchful eyes. The brother-in-law fully dressed at two in the morning, whose charm had worn through sometime in the night like paint through which the grain finally shows.

"Here is what happens now," Sable said. Her voice was steady. Eleven years of speaking over storms had taught her how. "Nobody leaves this house. Nobody goes to the dock, nobody goes to the skiff, nobody 'walks to the point to look at the water.' We keep the light as best we can, we wait out the Widow, and when the weather breaks, the magistrate's inquiry convenes—with or without that ledger, because I gave my account to Mr. Pell days ago, and he has written it down, and paper is patient."

She saw Pell's hand move, involuntarily, toward his coat pocket. Saw Tobias register it. Saw the exact moment Tobias understood that destroying the ledger had bought him nothing—that the words had already escaped into another book, kept by another hand, in daylight.

His face did something complicated and then went smooth again, and he smiled, and the smile was the worst thing she had seen all night, worse than the broken glass.

"Well," he said pleasantly. "Then I suppose we're all witnesses together."

He rose, wished them all a restful morning, and went to his room, closing the door gently, like a man with nothing whatsoever to hide.

Sable stood by the fire with her daughter's shoulder against hers, listening to the storm, and found she was counting footsteps in her head. His room was at the front of the house. The supply skiff was tied at the waterline, forty yards away, on ice-slicked rocks, in the teeth of the Widow.

She counted the minutes. She did not stop counting.
