## 18. Tobias at the Waterline

She counted to four hundred before she moved.

Not because she doubted. Doubt had burned off days ago like sea fog under a hard sun. She counted because she wanted to be wrong, and the counting was the last form of wanting she had left. At three hundred and ninety she heard the front door. It closed with that same terrible gentleness — Tobias Almeida could not close a door loudly if his life depended on it, and perhaps it did — and then his footsteps went down the path toward the water, unhurried, a man out for air.

Sable took the storm lantern from its hook by the kitchen door and did not light it. The Widow threw enough light of her own: white sheets of rain, wind coming in gusts that made the whole station groan on its foundations like a ship working loose from its moorings. She pulled her oilskin over her shoulders, looked once at Rosalind's closed door — the girl slept the way she did everything now, warily, one hand curled near the edge of the bed as if braced for the floor to move — and went out into it.

The path to the waterline was forty yards of rock and rope. She had walked it ten thousand times in weather far worse than this. But tonight every stone was a stranger. The wind hit her sideways at the halfway mark and she put her shoulder into it and thought, absurdly, of trimming wicks, of the small patient economies of the light, of how a flame survives by being tended and dies by being forgotten. *Tend it,* she told herself. *Whatever happens on these rocks tonight, tend it.*

The skiff was still tied where the supply boat's boy had left it, riding at its line in the black churn below the rocks. And Tobias was beside it, bent double against the wind, doing something with his coat.

He heard her boots on the stone and turned, and even in that light — lantern-less, moonless, lit only by the station lamp sweeping past above them in its long blind rotation — she saw him arrange his face. That was the thing about Tobias she would never be able to explain to anyone who hadn't seen it: he didn't lie with his mouth first. He lied with his face. He built the expression before he needed it, the way a mason cuts a stone before he knows which wall it goes in.

"Sable." The wind ate half of it. "God's teeth, what are you doing out—"

"Open your coat."

* * *

For a moment neither of them moved. The beam swung past overhead, and in its wash she saw the ledger plainly — the corner of it, dark leather gone stiff with damp, protruding from the inside pocket of his coat like a tongue he couldn't swallow. Then the light passed and they were two shapes on wet rock again, and he began, of course, to talk.

"It's not what you think."

"Then it will cost you nothing to show me."

He laughed. He actually laughed — short, incredulous, almost admiring. "You always were the stubborn one. Edric used to say you'd argue with the tide if it came up the stairs." He shifted his weight, and the rock beneath him gleamed black with ice, and Sable understood with a lurch that this was the moment — right here, on this slick stone, with forty feet of freezing water below — where everything would be decided. Not at the inquiry. Not at the dock. Here, between the two of them, in the dark.

"Tobias," she said, and made her voice as level as she knew how, "I have kept your secrets for two winters. I fed you. I gave you my husband's brother's bed. I let my daughter call you uncle and mean it warmly, God help us both. So I am asking you once, as family, to open the coat."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I ask you as a keeper of Greywater Point, and you will not like that version of me."

The beam came around again. This time she watched him decide. She watched it happen in his eyes the way you watch ice shift on a river — no single crack, just a general settling, a whole surface giving way at once. He reached into his coat slowly, theatrically, and drew out the ledger, and held it in both hands between them like an offering or a shield.

"There," he said. "Are you happy? I was going to bring it back."

"You were going to row it across the river in a Widow gale and burn it on the far shore, and we both know it."

"I was going to *protect* this family!"

The word tore something loose in her. Maybe it was the word itself — *family*, worn smooth as beach glass from all the handling it had gotten, from all the mouths it had covered. Or maybe it was simply that he shouted it into the wind like a man throwing a rope to a drowning swimmer, when the swimmer was standing six feet away, dry, holding the other end.

"Give it to me," she said.

"No."

"Tobias—"

"*No.*" His voice dropped suddenly, and that was worse. He came a step closer on the treacherous rock, arms out slightly for balance, and the ledger hung from one fist now, and the wind pushed at both of them like a third party trying to get into the argument. "You don't understand what you're holding. You think it's paper. You think it's Edric's sins and mine and some Meridian men you've never met. It isn't. It's a list of everyone who can hurt us, and every reason they'd want to. You hand that to a magistrate, you don't buy justice — you buy attention. You buy men in good coats asking questions in front of your daughter. You think the inquiry stops at the wreck? They'll come upriver, Sable. They'll come to this house. They'll sit where I sat and eat your bread and write it all down."

"They're already coming. The letter named us."

"The letter named us because someone *talked*. Because somewhere along the line somebody decided survival meant confession, and look where it got—" He stopped himself. Too late.

Sable felt the cold go through the oilskin, through the wool, through the skin, all the way down to a place she had been guarding since the day the crate washed ashore. "Look where it got Edric," she finished for him.

* * *

The wind dropped. Not fully — the Widow never dropped fully — but there was a gap between the gusts, a held breath, and in it the words sat on the rocks between them like a third body.

"That's not what I said," Tobias said quietly.

"You said *look where it got* — and you stopped."

"I said nothing."

"You've been saying nothing since you stepped off the supply boat." She took a step toward him and felt her boot slide and corrected, found her line again, kept coming. "You arrived asking about papers before you'd taken your coat off. You asked Rosalind questions dressed up as affection. You offered to destroy the ledger before you'd told me what was in it — how did you know what was in it, Tobias? How does a man know what needs burning unless he helped write it?"

"You read chapter seven," he said. Flat. Defeated. "You already know my handwriting."

"I know more than your handwriting." She was close enough now to see his face without the lamp, close enough to see that the arrangement had failed, that underneath the cut stone of his expression was just a tired man of fifty with rain running off his chin. "I found Edric's last entry. The smudged page. Did you know about it?"

Something moved behind his eyes. "No."

"He wrote that he meant to testify. When the ice broke. He wrote it down, Tobias — the man who taught me that ink is a promise — he wrote down his intention to walk into the magistrate's office and tell the truth about Meridian, about the bribes, about the witness statements you two buried after the *Coriander* went down with nine souls aboard. And two weeks later his boat went out in weather any fisherman on this coast would have refused. Any fisherman. *Edric* would have refused it."

"Edric loved that water more than he loved sense. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Sable agreed. "So answer me one question, and answer it true, because it is the last question I will ever ask you as family." The beam swept past above them, and in its light she watched his hands — the ledger hanging from the right one, knuckles white, and the left opening and closing at his side like a man rehearsing letting go. "Was his drowning chance?"

The gap in the wind closed. The gust hit them both, drove spray up off the black water, and Tobias stood in it and did not answer, and the not-answer was the loudest sound she had ever heard.

"Say it wasn't," she said. "Say it wasn't and I will believe you, because I want to, because I have spent two years building a widow's grief on the foundation you give me, and if you take it away I don't know what's left of me to stand on. Say it."

"You don't know what you're asking."

"I know exactly what I'm asking. I'm asking you to trade one truth for another. It's the only currency either of us has left." She put out her hand. Palm up. Open. "The ledger, Tobias. And the truth. Both. Now."

* * *

He looked at her open hand for a long time.

And then, in a voice so low the wind nearly took it, he said: "There was a man. From Meridian. Came to Edric's mooring the night before he drowned. I didn't see him — I've never seen him, I want that written down somewhere, I never laid eyes on him — but Edric wrote to me after. One letter. He said he'd been warned. He said, *"They know what I mean to do, and they are not men who let ledgers close themselves."*" Tobias's breath shuddered out of him. "I burned that letter. God forgive me, I burned it, because keeping it was keeping evidence, and evidence gets people killed, and I had already lost one brother to this business and I would not lose—"

"You burned the only proof that my husband was murdered."

"I burned it to stay alive!" It came out of him ragged, stripped of all its masonry, all its charm, all the pleasant careful surfaces, and what was underneath was smaller than she'd imagined and older. "You think I haven't done the arithmetic? Every night since the crate came ashore — since before that, every night for two years — I've done it. What do I keep? What do I burn? Who lives? And every answer comes out the same: nobody lives, Sable. Not clean. Not whole. You survive this by being smaller than the truth, and you survive *that* by making sure the truth is smaller than the water. That's all any of us have been doing. Edric too. Edric most of all, at the end."

The beam swung round. In its wash she saw tears on his face, or rain — on these rocks, in this wind, there was no telling, and she suspected he'd chosen the weather deliberately, the way he chose everything.

She held out her hand again. Steadier now. Something in her had gone very quiet and very cold, the way the lamp room went quiet after a storm passes and you stand among the wreckage of the glass and start, methodically, to count what can be repaired.

"Give it to me."

He gave it to her.

She took the ledger out of his hand and held it against her chest under the oilskin, and for a moment they stood there together, brother-in-law and widow, on the ice-slicked rocks above the black water, and the strangest feeling moved through her — not triumph, not grief, but the particular lightness of a debt finally called in. Loyalty died there on the rocks. She felt it go, felt the exact place where it left her, like a tooth coming out: relief and ruin in the same socket.

"Go inside," she said.

"Sable—"

"Go inside, dry off, sleep if you can. Tomorrow the ferry master posts the final manifest and there is one berth and we will stand at the dock like civilized people and pretend none of this happened on the rocks." She turned toward the path. "But hear me, Tobias Almeida, because this is the last kindness I have in me: I am giving the ledger to the clerk tomorrow. All of it. Your entries, Edric's entries, the last page. Whatever the inquiry costs, I pay it in full, in daylight, with my name attached. And if there is a man from Meridian reading this water the way you read it — if there is anyone who thinks a drowning solves a problem —"

She looked back at him, and let him see what was in her face, unarranged, unmortared, the first honest thing she'd shown him since he came ashore.

"—then they should know the keeper of Greywater Point keeps her light burning in any weather. And I see everything on this water."

She walked back up the path with the ledger against her heart and the wind at her back for once, pushing her home, and behind her she heard Tobias's boots on the rocks, following, and above them all the beam went round and round, steady as a pulse, sweeping the black river like a slow searchlight looking for whatever was still out there in the dark.

She did not stop counting his footsteps until the door closed behind them both.

Inside, Rosalind was awake. Sable could see the lamplight under her daughter's door, and the shadow of her sitting up within it, and she stood in the hallway with the wet oilskin dripping off her hem and understood that there would be no more protecting, no more counting, no more doors gently closed. Tomorrow the ferry came. Tomorrow the berth was decided. Tomorrow the ledger changed hands.

Tomorrow, everything she had carried alone would have to be set down in front of witnesses — starting with the one sleeping — or pretending to sleep — behind that door.

Sable climbed the stairs toward the lamp room, ledger in hand, and began to climb.
