## 19. The Last Ferry

The dock at Greywater Point was a black tongue of timber sticking out into a river gone the color of pewter. Ice had begun to lace its edges overnight—thin plates that clicked and shifted against the pilings like a thousand small clocks all telling slightly different hours. Sable stood at the head of the dock with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth under her arm, and she thought: this is what it weighs. Not the paper. The years.

Rosalind walked ahead of her, spine straight in the way she'd learned it from no one, the way some children simply arrive at. Fourteen going on forty. She carried one bag and did not look back, which Sable understood was not stoicism but strategy—if she didn't look back, she couldn't be made to stop.

Tobias was already there.

He stood near the ferry's gangplank with his collar up and his hands buried deep in his pockets, and when he saw them coming he peeled away from the small crowd of evacuees like a man who had been waiting all morning for exactly this. His smile arrived before he did.

"Sable." Warm. Reasonable. A voice built for parlors and back rooms alike. "You've had a night to think on it."

"I have."

"And?"

She looked past him to the ferry—the *Meridian Star* of her own private joke, though she'd never said it aloud, because the real Meridian ships lay at the bottom of the channel with their secrets, and naming anything after them felt like an invitation. The ferry master was checking his manifest. One name per household. He'd said it three times yesterday and would say it again today: *One berth. That's the ice's arithmetic, not mine.*

"Tobias," Sable said, "where is your bag?"

His smile flickered—just at the corners, just for a moment—and then it reassembled itself. "I gave my place some thought," he said lightly. "And I concluded that a man of my... standing... has obligations ashore. Inquiries to answer. Better I stay and face them as a free man than run and be hunted."

"Is that what you concluded." She said it flatly, and watched him hear the flatness. Last night on the rocks, with the ledger clutched against his chest and the sea trying to take it from both of them, Tobias Almeida had confessed things she would carry longer than the bruises now purpling her forearm where he'd gripped her. *Edric meant to testify.* *The drowning may not have been chance.* He'd said the second one quietly, almost gently, the way a man sets down something heavy hoping you won't notice its weight until he's already walking away.

"I concluded," Tobias said now, "that family looks after family. And that the child goes. And that whatever you do with that book—" his eyes dropped to the oilcloth bundle "—is between you and your conscience."

"My conscience," Sable said, "and the magistrate's clerk."

Behind him, at the far end of the dock, the clerk stood waiting with his satchel and his chilblained hands and the patient expression of a young man who had learned that most confessions are delivered late and reluctantly and often at docks. He had been stranded among them for days. He knew the Almeida name. He had seen enough—or guessed enough—that when Sable had found him this morning before dawn and told him what she intended, he had simply nodded and said, "I'll need to open the inquiry record with it, ma'am. Today. Before the ferry sails."

So here they all were, arranged on the dock like pieces on a board neither player fully controlled.

The horn sounded once, low, across the water. Boarding in ten minutes.

* * *

Rosalind heard her mother's boots behind her and pretended she hadn't.

She stood at the rail of the boarding queue, one bag at her feet, watching the ice plates ride the current. She had counted everything since the manifest went up: the days until sailing (four), the berths available (one), the number of times Uncle Tobias had smiled at her mother while meaning something else entirely (she'd stopped counting; it hurt). Counting was what she had instead of power. Her father had taught her numbers were honest even when people weren't. She hadn't known, then, how much he'd meant it.

Her mother stopped beside her. Close enough that Rosalind could smell lamp oil and cold air and the particular soap that never quite washed the salt out.

"You're angry with me," Sable said.

"No," Rosalind said, which was true, and also not the whole truth. She was angry with everyone—her father for lying by omission across two winters and a grave, her uncle for being exactly what he was, the ice for freezing, the ferry for having one seat, the world for arranging itself so neatly around other people's sins. But her mother she was angry at least honestly, and honesty, Rosalind had decided somewhere in the last terrible week, was worth more than comfort. It was the only thing in that house worth more than anything.

"The berth is yours," Sable said.

"I know."

"Rosalind." Her mother turned her, gently, by the shoulder, and Rosalind looked up into a face she had spent fourteen years learning and was still, somehow, only beginning to read. "Look at me. This isn't punishment. It isn't exile. It's a boat, and it goes south, and you'll be warm, and in spring the river opens, and I will still be here, keeping the light, because someone has to, and because—" her voice caught, just once, and steadied "—because I have something left to finish here."

"The trial."

"The truth," Sable said. "The trial is just where it gets read aloud."

Rosalind's throat tightened. She hated crying in public. She hated crying anywhere. But her eyes stung anyway, and she blinked hard, and her mother saw it and did not pretend otherwise, which was, Rosalind supposed, the whole point of the last week. No more pretending. They had burned that currency down to nothing.

"What do I tell them?" Rosalind asked. "When they ask about Father."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. On the water, an ice plate cracked and folded under itself.

"Tell them what's true," she said finally. "That he was a man who did wrong and meant to right it, and the sea didn't give him time. Tell them his daughter keeps honest accounts. That will be enough. That will be more than enough."

"That's a lot of weight for a girl with one bag."

"It's not weight," her mother said. "It's ballast. There's a difference. Weight drags you under. Ballast keeps you upright in weather."

The horn sounded again. Two minutes.

* * *

Sable walked her daughter to the gangplank and handed her over to the crossing like handing over a lung.

The ferry master checked his list. "Almeida, R. Household of Greywater Point station. One berth." He glanced up at Sable, then at Tobias loitering with theatrical unconcern, and something passed over his face—not judgment, exactly. Ferry masters see every kind of parting. "Ma'am. You're certain of the allocation?"

"I'm certain."

"Mum—" Rosalind started, and stopped, and Sable watched her daughter wage the same war she herself was waging, the war between *say it* and *don't make it harder*, and lose it gracefully.

"I know," Sable said. "Go aboard."

Rosalind hugged her then, brief and fierce, arms locked, face pressed into Sable's coat, and Sable held on and memorized it—the pressure, the smell, the exact stubborn architecture of her—and let go first, because if she waited for Rosalind to let go she might never.

At the top of the gangplank Rosalind turned. Of course she turned. She was her mother's daughter; she couldn't help keeping records even with her body.

"Spring," Rosalind called down.

"Spring," Sable called back. "Keep honest books."

And her daughter—her sharp-eyed, restless, counting daughter—smiled through the sting in her eyes and said, "You first."

Then she was gone up the plank, and the deck swallowed her, and Sable stood alone on the dock with an oilcloth bundle and a decision she had made days ago and rehearsed every hour since.

She turned to the clerk.

He came to her without hurrying. Up close he looked very young and very tired and utterly unimpressed by the drama of the morning, which she suspected was the single greatest qualification for his work.

"Magistrate's inquiry, Meridian Shipping matter," he said, producing a ledger of his own—of course he had one; the whole world ran on ledgers, hers was merely better hidden than most. "State your name and station."

"Sable Almeida. Keeper, Greywater Point light."

"Do you surrender materials voluntarily?"

She unwrapped the oilcloth. The ledger lay in her hands for the last time, its cover swollen with damp from the rocks, its pages dense with two men's handwriting—one dead, one soon to answer for it. Payments received. Silence kept. And on the final smudged page, in Edric's failing hand, the sentence he'd never finished: *I mean to go before the inquiry and speak the whole of—*

The whole of what. She would never know. She was leaving it unfinished on purpose, and finishing it another way, and the difference between those two things was her entire life.

"Voluntarily," she said, and put it in his hands. "Every entry. Including the ones in Tobias Almeida's writing. Including the last page. My husband recorded his intent to testify before he drowned. You should begin there."

The clerk wrote it down, line by line, unhurried. Then he closed his book, met her eyes, and said, "Keeper Almeida. For what it's worth—I've read worse men's names than yours. And I've rarely had anyone hand it over standing up."

"It's not standing up," Sable said. "It's just not running."

* * *

From the deck rail, Rosalind watched her mother shrink.

That wasn't how she'd expected to think of it, but the water widened and the dock shrank and her mother shrank with it, becoming a dark figure, then a smaller one, then a shape the eye had to argue with, until the lighthouse itself took over the job of marking her—white tower, black gallery, standing at the end of the world doing the only thing she had ever done without flinching, which was keep the light.

Rosalind counted. She couldn't help it. The distance in fathoms as the ferry cleared the point. The seconds between the horn blasts, lengthening. The number of passengers on deck (thirty-one), the number looking back (most of them), the number who had chosen to stay (one).

Uncle Tobias had stayed too. That was different. Uncle Tobias had stayed because staying was the last move available to a man whose book had been taken from him in front of witnesses, and Rosalind understood—she understood more than any fourteen-year-old should, and less than she pretended—that her mother had engineered even that. Had given the ledger to the clerk on the dock, in the open, where fleeing became pointless and confession became the only road left. Her mother hadn't just surrendered the truth. She'd made sure everyone watched her do it.

*Authorship*, Rosalind thought. Her mother had once said, trimming wicks, that a keeper doesn't make the light, she only decides where it points. Rosalind was beginning to understand that this was the whole of parenting and possibly the whole of justice.

A woman beside her offered a flask. Rosalind shook her head. The shore fell away. The ice plates thinned and scattered and then thickened again toward midriver, white lace turning to white field, and the ferry's bow began to groan and push, and the whole deck leaned forward with the effort of going on.

Her mother did not wave. Rosalind was grateful. Waving would have been a lie, a promise that this was easy. Instead the figure at the dock simply stood, straight-backed as a piling, and got smaller, and kept standing.

Rosalind took out the small notebook she'd packed without letting herself think why—a habit, a reflex, her father's legacy in cheap paper—and opened to the first blank page. Her breath fogged the pencil lead.

*Departed Greywater Point,* she wrote. *Berth: mine. Given, not lost.*

Below it, after a moment:

*Mother stays. Mother chose. Write that down so no one ever says otherwise.*

* * *

The ferry's wake reached the dock in long gray swells, slapping the pilings where ice had begun to weld itself, and Sable stood until the sound changed—until hull met pack ice at the channel mouth and the great groan rolled across the water like a door closing in a house she used to live in.

Then the horn blew one last time, muffled, already distant, and the river went quiet, and the quiet was enormous.

The clerk was still there. He tucked the recovered ledger inside his coat, next to his heart or his paperwork—she couldn't tell which—and said, "Inquiry convenes at the station tomorrow. The magistrate's party crosses once the ice road holds. You'll give testimony?"

"All of it," Sable said. "I've been practicing keeping it. Might as well practice saying it."

He almost smiled. Then he touched his hat and walked back up the dock toward the station, and Sble Almeida turned and climbed the path to the tower alone, her boots crunching frost, her arms empty for the first time in weeks and heavier than they'd been when full.

Inside, she trimmed the wick. Lit the lamp. Set the lens turning.

Out on the water, the ferry was a smudge against the smudge of the sky, and somewhere aboard it was her daughter, carrying nothing but one bag, one notebook, and the only inheritance Sable had ever managed to write clearly.

The beam swung out over the freezing river, steady as a heartbeat.

And far downstream, where the channel bent toward the sea, the ice had begun—quietly, invisibly, the way all the important things happened—to crack.

* * *

Three days later, the ice road held, and a sled came up from the south carrying four men in magistrate's gray and one prisoner wrapped in blankets, and the knock on the station door came just as the lamp needed lighting.

Sable wiped her hands, looked once at the empty hook beneath the oil store where a ledger had slept for two years, and went to answer it.

"Keeper Almeida," said the magistrate, stamping snow off his boots in her doorway, "we're ready to begin whenever you are. We'll start with the night your husband's boat went down."

Outside, the beam swept the frozen river, slow and white and merciless as a page being read aloud.
