## 20. Testimony by Lamplight

They came at first light, three days after the ferry went, when the storm had spent itself and the sky hung grey and rinsed over Greywater Point like a sheet washed too many times.

Sable saw them from the lamp room: two figures on the coast road, one heavyset in a magistrate's greatcoat, the other slighter, moving with the careful, deliberate steps of a man who had learned to walk on ice late in life. The clerk. Wrenley. She had fed him chowder for four nights while the wind screamed and he had said very little, but she had seen him looking—at the ledger's absence from its shelf, at her hands, at the door whenever Tobias passed through it.

She had sent word with the fishing skiff that risked the harbor mouth on Tuesday: *The keeper of Greywater Point has testimony regarding the Meridian affair. Come when you can come safely.*

Now they were coming.

She trimmed the wick anyway. That was the discipline of it—Edric had taught her that much, whatever else he'd kept from her. *The light doesn't care what you're feeling, Sable. It cares that it burns.* So she cleaned the glass, wound the clockwork, logged the weather in the station book with a steady hand, and watched the two men grow larger on the road.

Rosalind was gone. That was the arithmetic she woke to every morning now, the subtraction that made the bed too wide and the porridge pot too big. On the ferry, across the water, in her aunt's house in Kingsport until the inquiry released them all or buried them. Sable had stood on the dock and put her daughter's hand into the clerk's keeping and let the boat take her, and she had not cried until the fog swallowed the sail, and then only once, briefly, standing where Rosalind could not see.

Below her, the door knocker sounded—three blows, official, patient.

* * *

They took her testimony in the kitchen, because the parlor was cold and the kitchen held the smell of bread and coffee and living, and Magistrate Corvane—broad, grey-bearded, with eyes like wet slate—said he preferred kitchens anyway. "Rooms where people tell the truth," he said, settling his bulk onto the bench. "Parlors are for lying."

Wrenley sat at the table's end with fresh paper, his pen poised. The third man was a witness-clerk from the inquiry proper, silent, taking nothing down but watching everything.

Outside the window behind Sable, the lighthouse rose white against the grey, and as the morning turned, the lens inside it turned too, and every ninety seconds the beam swung through the kitchen window and laid a slow bar of light across the table, across the magistrate's folded hands, across her own, and moved on. Sweep. Dark. Sweep again. Like something patient searching the room for what was hidden, finding each thing in turn, passing over it, returning.

"Begin wherever you find it easiest," Corvane said. "Or hardest. They're usually the same place."

Sable folded her hands where the beam would cross them.

"My husband was Edric Almeida," she began, "keeper of this light before me. Two winters past, the *Meridian Star* broke on the shallows off Harrow Neck with the loss of thirty-one souls. My husband drowned the following spring. In the autumn after his death, I found a ledger hidden beneath the oil store."

She told it plainly. She had rehearsed no speech; there had been no time and no appetite for one. She simply spoke the truth in the order she had found it, and found—to her astonishment—that the order held. The waterlogged crate on the tide line. The pages in Edric's hand. The full ledger under the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth beside the spare wicks, hidden so well that she had lived above it for a season without knowing. The entries dated through the months after the wreck. *Received, for silence kept.* Names reduced to initials. Payments recorded in sums that made Wrenley's pen stop moving twice.

"The Meridian Shipping Company paid witnesses not to testify," she said. "My husband was one of the men who carried the money. This ledger records who was paid, how much, and by whom. I did not know any of it while he lived."

"You found it and did not destroy it," Corvane said. Not a question. "Why?"

The beam swept through the window, crossed the table, crossed her hands, moved on. Ninety seconds. She could feel it coming now the way sailors felt weather.

"Because burning it would have been one more silence," she said. "And my family has kept enough of those."

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. "There are those at the inquiry who will say you concealed evidence for a season. That you hid it rather than surrendering it. That your daughter might have been on an earlier ferry if you had acted sooner."

"They can say it," Sable said. "It's true. I hid it because I was afraid—of the name it gave us, of what it would cost, of what I didn't yet understand about my own husband. Fear is not an excuse. It is only a fact, and I am giving you the fact along with everything else." She paused. "I will note that I also did not sell it, bargain with it, or flee with it, though I was offered reasons to do all three."

"We'll come to the reasons," Corvane said dryly. "Tell us about your brother-in-law."

* * *

Tobias Almeida had been taken at the waterline, and Sable described it exactly as it had happened—the skiff, the ledger in his coat, the rocks glazed black with ice, the two of them alone in the wind with the truth between them like a knife neither wanted to hold by the blade.

"He came ashore in November asking about papers," she said. "He stayed. He was charming, and he was frightened, and I mistook the fright for grief. He told me Edric meant to confess. He told me the drowning was chance." Her voice did not change; she made sure of that. "On the ice, three nights ago, he admitted it may not have been."

Wrenley's pen scratched. The beam swept through, lit the ink wet and shining, moved on.

"He said—" and here Sable stopped, because the words were Tobias's and she had carried them long enough and they were heavy and she wanted, finally, to set them down. "He said Edric wrote to Meridian in March threatening to testify unless they released him from the arrangement. He said the letter never reached anyone who could act on it. He said my husband went out in weather no keeper goes out in, and that the sea does not need instructions." She looked at Corvane. "I believe my brother-in-law knows more than he has said. I believe he knows it because he was afraid of it. But I am not the inquiry, and I will not guess beyond what I heard."

"And the ledger itself? He claims he took it to protect it."

"He took it to bury it. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of this affair." She heard her own voice go flat and hard. "Everything wrong that happened on this coast happened because men kept deciding that some things were safer unseen. Witnesses safer silenced. Ledgers safer burned. Drownings safer unexamined. I am done with safer."

Corvane nodded slowly, and something in the nod was almost approval. "Then give us the names."

That was the center of it, and she had known it would be, and still the moment arrived with weight. Because the names were not initials in a dead man's hand—they were men alive, men with houses in Kingsport and seats on the harbor board, men whose money had built half the new warehouses she could see from the lamp room on clear days. Men who would hear her name spoken in rooms she would never enter, and speak hers back.

"The payments came through a broker named Halloran," she said. "Initials in the ledger, but Tobias confirmed the name, and the handwriting changes after February match letters Edric received from the same office—I have those letters still. The orders themselves came from Aldous Meridian's office, specifically his deputy, Casper Vane. Three witnesses are named in full in the later entries, because by then they had stopped bothering with initials: Marta Quill, the harbormaster's widow. Josiah Fenn. And a pilot called Renwick, who took payment twice and testified anyway, which is why the last entry about him reads differently than the rest." She recited it without hurrying. Wrenley wrote it all. The beam swept, swept, swept.

"Aldous Meridian himself?"

"There is no entry naming him directly. But there is no entry in that book that wasn't approved above the men who made it, and I will leave the inquiry to do that arithmetic, which is simpler than anything else in the matter."

Corvane almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

It took until midafternoon. They broke for bread and coffee at noon, and the witness-clerk went out to stand in the cold and look at the sea, and Corvane ate standing up, which struck Sable as the habit of a man who had sat through too many lies comfortably seated.

Near the end, Wrenley looked up from his pages. "Mrs. Almeida. Your daughter. What did she know, and when?"

The question landed harder than any about Tobias or Meridian. Sable set down her cup.

"She knew nothing until eleven nights ago. Then she found the ledger and read it, and came to me in the lamp room, and we fought, and I told her everything. Everything I knew, everything I'd hidden, and why I'd hidden it." She met the young clerk's eyes. "You want to write down whether I failed her. Write it down. I protected her into ignorance because I thought ignorance was lighter to carry than truth. I was wrong. Truth is heavier going down but it weighs nothing after. Ignorance gets lighter going down and crushes you after. That is the whole of what I have learned this winter, and she learned it faster than I did, which shames me and makes me proud in the same breath."

Wrenley wrote it. All of it. She watched him write *weighs nothing* and thought: good. Let it stand in the record in a boy's careful hand. Let someone reading this years hence think the keeper of Greywater Point was a woman who had measured the weight of things.

* * *

At dusk, Corvane rose, joints cracking, and gathered his coat. Tobias was already in custody at Harrow Neck—a constable had collected him the morning after the ice—and tomorrow the inquiry proper would convene across the water, and Sable would be summoned to repeat all of this in a room with windows and lawyers and men whose names she had just given.

"You understand," Corvane said at the door, "that naming those men will cost you. Meridian has friends. There will be talk of the widow who traded her husband's memory for leniency. There will be worse than talk."

"The light costs oil every night," Sable said. "Everything true costs something. I've stopped expecting otherwise."

He studied her a moment longer than courtesy required. "Your husband's last entry," he said. "The intention to testify. You surrendered the page?"

"It's in the record. Smudged, but legible. He meant to speak." Her throat tightened, and she let it. "He ran out of water before he ran out of courage. I intend to finish the sentence for him."

Corvane inclined his head—the closest to a bow she had ever seen him make—and went out into the blue evening, and Wrenley followed with his satchel of pages, and the door closed, and the kitchen was quiet.

* * *

Sable climbed the tower because climbing it was what her body did when it could not decide what else to do. One hundred and twelve steps. At the top, the winter dusk had deepened to violet, and the first stars were out over the frozen river, and far downstream a single light moved—some boat defying the ice, or a signal from the inquiry boats, or nothing at all.

She lit the lamp. Watched the flame catch, steadied, bloom. The clockwork engaged with its familiar click, and the lens began its slow rotation, and the beam went out across the dark water and the dark land, sweeping, sweeping, faithful as breath.

Her testimony was given. The names were spoken. Somewhere across the river, in a warm room, a clerk was transcribing her voice into the permanent record, and tomorrow the machinery of law would begin to grind, slow and enormous, toward the men who had bought thirty-one graves.

And she was alone at the top of her tower with a strange, unbearable lightness, as though someone had opened a valve and let the winter drain out of her. She kept waiting for the fear to arrive—the fear of Meridian's friends, of the talk, of the cost she had just agreed to pay. It did not come. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps it would never come, and that too would be its own kind of lesson.

The beam swept out, touched the horizon, came round again.

Below, on the desk in the oil room, lay the station log, open to the day's page, waiting for her final entry. She climbed down and took up the pen and wrote, in a hand steadier than she expected:

*Keeper's log, 14th. Testimony given in full. Ledger surrendered to inquiry. Names spoken aloud: Halloran, Vane, and others per record. T. Almeida in custody. Weather clearing.*

She stopped. Looked at the page. There should have been more—there was always more—but the old habits of omission died hard, and she made herself sit back down and add the last line, the one that mattered:

*Nothing further concealed.*

She signed it, blotted it, and closed the book.

From the drawer beneath the log, she took out a letter—Rosalind's, brought by the fishing skiff that afternoon, unopened since noon because she had been saving it the way you save the last of anything precious. The paper was thin, travel-worn, addressed in her daughter's sharp, slanting hand: *To my mother, from across the water.*

Sable turned it over twice in her hands. Then she broke the seal.
