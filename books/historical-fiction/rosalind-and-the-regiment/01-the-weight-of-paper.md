## 1. The Weight of Paper

The lamp had burned down to its last finger of oil when Rosalind Whitlock finally lifted her pen from the page and understood, at last, that Master Aldous Vane would never finish this atlas.

Not because his hand had failed him. His hand was everywhere — in the confident sweep of the Varenholt harbor soundings, in the delicate stippling that gave the Saltmarsh Road its texture, in the tiny compass roses he drew with a flourish like a man signing his own name. Forty-one years of cartography sat on her worktable under a skin of dust and lamp-smoke. She was not finishing it so much as keeping it company while it died.

She dipped the pen again and laid the final contour along the eastern cliffs, where the sea had eaten away a whole farm since the survey began. There. The coast was complete. The coast was true. Everything else—

Everything else was the margins.

She had noticed them three nights ago, the first time she'd worked by lamplight instead of gray winter daylight. Faint marks, almost nothing: small crosses inked in the gutters between the map proper and the border rules, each one annotated in Master Vane's cramped cipher-shorthand, the private script he'd used for his field notes. She'd assumed they were survey benchmarks. That was the kind thing to assume, and grief makes a woman generous with her assumptions.

Then she'd found a cross on the map of the Granary Quarter, in the middle of a block marked *burnt — uninhabited*. And another in the drowned lanes below the tide-gates. Places no surveyor would ever stand to take a benchmark.

She turned the leaf now, slowly, as if the paper might feel her hesitation. The cross sat there. Beside it, in the shorthand she had spent six years learning to read: *Kessler, J. — cooper. Taken 14 Brumaire. Not returned.*

A name. A date. A place no living man could be.

Sable — for she had been Sable longer than she had been Rosalind; Master Vane had renamed her himself, laughing, saying her hair in lamplight was the color of dark water — set the pen down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that has become suddenly dangerous.

The atlas was not an atlas. Or it was an atlas, and also something else, the way a river is water and also a road and also a grave. Her master had spent his last months charting the occupation's supply routes — that much he'd told her openly, because the Guild commission demanded it, because even occupied cities must have their maps drawn by their own hands or not at all. But between the routes and the soundings, in the margins, he had been doing other work. Recording. The granaries emptied to feed garrisons while the city starved. The warehouses where wagons went in at night and came out empty. The houses from which men were taken, and the names of the taken, written in a script only two people in Varenholt could read.

One of them was dead. He had been dead eleven days, face-down in the harbor mud near the fish stairs, and the officers who pulled him out had called it an accident of the ice.

Sable pressed her thumb against the margin, against Kessler's name, until the paper dented. *You knew,* she thought. *All those evenings you sent me home early, all those commissions you took alone. You knew what you were carrying, and you let me carry only the safe half.*

Or — colder thought, arriving unbidden — he had been protecting her, and failed anyway, and now whatever killed him would come looking for the other half of the work.

Outside, the wind off the harbor carried the smell of tar and frozen rope. Somewhere a patrol's boots rang on cobbles in that unhurried rhythm soldiers use in cities they do not fear. Curfew was hours away still. The lamps of Varenholt came up one by one along the crescent of the quay, and every lit window was a household that had answered the census honestly, or dishonestly, or had stopped answering at all.

She should burn it. The thought arrived fully formed, sensible, terrible. Burn the margins, keep the coastlines, mourn him properly. An atlas of harbors hurts no one. An atlas of names gets people hanged.

But her hands did not move toward the fire. They moved instead toward the drawer beneath the worktable — the shallow one with the false back Master Vane had built for storing client ledgers too valuable to leave in plain sight — and began, with a steadiness she didn't feel, to separate truth from geography. She cut the marginalia free with a razor-thin blade, sheet by sheet, folding each strip of names into a packet thin as a love letter. The clean maps stayed on the table, blameless, beautiful, nearly finished. The dead stayed hidden in the dark.

She was pressing the last fold flat when someone knocked at the street door.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. The knock of a man who expected doors to open for him.

* * *

Herr Voss stood on her step with snow gathering on the shoulders of his gray coat, and he smiled the way a ledger balances: exactly, without warmth.

"Fräulein Whitlock. Forgive the hour." He did not sound as though he forgave anything. "I am completing the census returns for the Guild district, and your master's premises remain... irregular."

"Master Vane is dead," Sable said. "Eleven days."

"Yes. So I am told. By the harbor office, by the ice wardens, by three separate reports which agree with each other in a way I find restful." He stepped past her without quite being invited, into the workshop, and stood a moment letting his eyes travel over it — the drying racks, the jars of pigments ranked like soldiers, the great worktable with its single lamp and its single chair. "You continue his commissions."

"I finish them. It isn't the same."

"No," Voss agreed mildly. "It is never the same. A finished thing belongs to whoever holds it. An unfinished thing belongs to its maker." He drifted toward the table, hands clasped behind his back, a fat little man moving with the unhurried confidence of a person who has never once needed to hurry. "The final commission. The occupation survey. The Guild has asked after it. The Governor's office has asked after it. Everyone is very eager to know how close it is to done."

"It's close." Keep the voice level. Keep the hands busy — she busied them straightening pens that did not need straightening. "Coastlines are complete. Roads, soundings. Another week for fair-copying."

"And then it goes to the Guild, and the Guild presents it, and everyone applauds." He smiled again. "May I see it?"

Every instinct she owned went quiet at once, the way birds go quiet before weather. Showing him the maps was correct — refusal was worse than suspicion, refusal was confirmation. She turned the leaves for him herself, narrating: the harbor, the cliffs, the Saltmarsh Road, the burned quarter rendered in careful grays.

Voss looked at all of it without really seeing any of it, the way a customs officer looks at cargo. Then, lightly, almost idly: "His working notes. Where does he keep them?"

"The notes go into the fair copy. He kept no separate—"

"He always kept notes." The mildness did not change, but something underneath it shifted, tectonic. "I processed his household registration myself, seven years ago. A cartographer of his standing. He kept field books, day books, cipher memoranda — the Guild catalogued them. I remember the catalogue." His eyes came up from the atlas and rested on her, patient, bottomless. "So you will understand my confusion, Fräulein Whitlock, when I say that a man who kept forty years of notes dies with none. Either they burned with him—" a small pause, delicately cruel, "—or someone is keeping them warm."

The fire crackled. Snow ticked against the window. Sable heard herself say, "There was nothing in his effects but the atlas. You may ask the ice wardens. You may ask anyone."

And there it was — the lie, out in the cold air between them, badly made, badly spoken, her voice climbing half a tone on *anyone* the way voices do when they are defending something. Voss received it the way he seemed to receive everything, folding it away somewhere behind his pleasant gray face.

"Of course," he said. "Grief misplaces things. Grief misplaces everything." He buttoned his coat, one button at a time, unhurried. "The survey will be presented to the Governor's office directly upon completion — not through the Guild. New arrangements for the new year. I will call for it myself." At the door he paused, and did not turn around. "Do finish it, Fräulein. Unfinished things cause such anxiety in everyone."

The door closed. His footsteps faded down the frozen lane, evenly spaced, like knocks.

* * *

Sable stood a long time with her back against the door, listening to the city breathe.

He knew. Not suspected — knew, the way a clerk knows, by pattern, by absence, by the shape of what is missing from a register. He had come tonight not to search but to tell her that he would come again. To give her time to make a mistake.

She crossed to the worktable and touched the fair copy, the beautiful blameless coastlines, and understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that she had gotten it wrong from the beginning. This was not a memorial. It had never been a memorial. It was evidence — forty-one pages of testimony with her master's body lying face-down at the end of it — and she was not its mourner. She was its courier, whether she wished it or not, and couriers were hunted.

In the false-backed drawer, folded thin as a love letter, Kessler the cooper waited with all the others to be delivered.

The question — arriving now, on the far side of midnight, with the lamp finally dead — was: delivered to whom?

She had until the new year to find out. She had, perhaps, until Herr Voss finished balancing his books.

She packed the marginalia into the lining of her coat, banked the cold hearth, and blew out even the memory of light.
