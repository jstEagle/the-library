## 2. Three Names in the Margin

Sable had not slept. The atlas lay open beneath the lamp's yellow circle, and she had been staring at the same square inch of parchment for an hour without seeing it — the estuary rendered in her master Aldous Renne's careful hand, the hatching of the marshes, the little compass rose with its north needle gone brown as tea.

It was the margins that kept her awake now.

She had finished the last plate at dawn, matching his line weight, mixing his particular gray-green for the water, signing nothing because he had signed everything and the forgery would show. The atlas was whole. It should have felt like a resurrection. Instead it felt like standing in a room where someone had died, touching all the furniture.

Because the margins were wrong.

Not badly — no censor or clerk flipping through would notice. But there were marks that belonged to no cartographer's grammar. A cluster of fine dots along the salt road where no village stood. Numbers beside them, small as insect legs: *7. 3. 11.* Dates? Counts? She didn't know. On the map of the northern quarter, a house on Tannery Lane bore a tiny cross above its roof, inked so lightly it might have been an accident, except that Aldous Renne did not make accidents. And twice, in a cipher-hand so compressed it looked like shorthand, phrases she could not read — though one word recurred enough times that she'd begun to recognize its shape the way you recognize a face across a market crowd before you can name it.

*Varenholt,* the word might be. Or *Vernichtung.* Her hands went cold every time she considered the second.

She heard the knock at the street door and knew before she opened it who it would be. Herr Voss had come back. Census takers, in her experience, came to a household once. He had come three times since the funeral.

"Fraulein Whitlock." He removed his hat with the precision of a man folding a letter. Behind him the fog had come up off the harbor, and the gas lamps made haloes like bruises. "Forgive the hour. The Commission requires completion of certain records before the turn of the year."

"The census closed in October."

"The census closes when the occupation says it closes." He smiled without warmth. "I have questions about your late teacher's commissions. You completed his final work?"

"I bound what he left."

"You *completed* it." Voss stepped past her into the hall uninvited, his boots leaving wet prints on the flagstones. His eyes went to the table through the open door — to the lamp, the scattered instruments, the great book lying closed under a linen cloth she had thrown over it only minutes before. "A man dies protecting a manuscript from soldiers, and his student finishes it in six weeks. Devotion. Or instruction. Which was it?"

"He taught me for nine years," Sable said. "He taught me coastlines and river gradients. He never taught me anything worth killing for."

"No?" Voss lifted the cloth by one corner, delicately, as if checking under it for mice. "Then you won't mind if I return tomorrow. With a colleague. To view the completed work properly. The Commission likes to honor its artists."

She said something polite. She did not remember what. After the door shut she stood with her ear against the wood until his footsteps faded into the fog, then blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with her arms around the atlas like a child holding a stolen loaf.

*A memorial,* she had thought for six weeks. It was not a memorial. It was a target, and whatever Aldous had hidden in its edges, someone in the Commission wanted it badly enough to send Voss back a third time. She had until tomorrow. Perhaps less.

She began cutting threads.

* * *

Odile Ferrant had been dismissed from the Bureau of Records eleven days ago for the crime of noticing.

The notice itself had been small — a discrepancy, a rhythm. As a junior clerk she had spent four years copying household registers, and registers have a pulse. Births, deaths, departures; the city breathed in ink. But in the last two years the breathing had changed. Certain households stopped appearing. Not flagged, not marked deceased — simply absent, as if erased mid-sentence. And always the same annotation in the reviewing officer's column, in a hand Odile came to know better than her own: *V*, followed by a number.

Voss. She had said so once, to her supervisor, quietly, over a ledger. By Friday she was dismissed for "irregularities in transcription," and by Saturday she understood that the irregularities were hers: she could read a pattern the occupation preferred unread.

So she did what clerks do. She copied. Before surrendering her keys she spent one long night transcribing the names of forty-one vanished households into the lining of her coat, and for eleven days she carried them around Varenholt trying to find anyone who would not think her mad, or dangerous, or both.

The answer came to her in the form of gossip in a stationer's shop: the old mapmaker's student had finished his atlas, and a census man had been seen at her door three times.

Three times. Odile's blood went quiet and fast.

She found the Whitlock woman's lodging by evening, and found the door ajar, and found the woman herself crouched amid the wreckage of a ransacked room with pages fanned around her like shed feathers, counting them with trembling fingers.

"They took nothing," Sable said, before Odile could speak. "That's what frightens me. They looked at everything and took nothing, which means they're coming back for all of it." She looked up. "Who are you?"

"A clerk. Formerly." Odile closed the door and shot the bolt, an act so domestic it seemed absurd. "I need to see your margins, Fraulein. Your master's margins."

Something crossed Sable's face — fear, recognition, the look of a person handed the last piece of a puzzle they'd been afraid to finish. She pulled the atlas from beneath the floorboard where it had spent the last hour and opened it flat between them on the bare table, and Odile bent over the estuary map, and the world tilted.

"There." Her finger hovered over the cluster of dots along the salt road. "These aren't settlements. There's nothing on the salt road — my own district maps confirm it. These are holdings. And these numbers—" she turned the page to the Tannery Lane cross, then to the ciphered marginal notes, and laid her coat-lining transcription beside them. Forty-one households. She matched names to positions with the speed of four years' practice, her lips moving. "Tannery Lane. The Delacroix house. They were taken in March. Eleven people." Her finger trembled on the ink. "*11.*"

Sable had gone very pale. "Counts."

"Counts. And these—" Odile traced the recurring cipher-word, and this close, with the register-names beside it, the compressed hand resolved. Not *Vernichtung*. Not quite *Varenholt* either. *"Verhoor."* Interrogation. "Your master wasn't charting geography, Fraulein. He was charting where they take people. Every dot is a holding site. Every cross is a household taken. He mapped the disappearances onto the city so precisely that anyone reading this atlas reads a list of prisoners and the addresses of their prisons." She looked up. "This isn't a memorial. This is evidence. It indicts half the officers in this garrison by name and post."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside a patrol's boots rang on wet stone, passed, faded.

"My teacher was murdered for this," Sable said at last, very evenly. "And I finished it. I polished it. I made it beautiful." Her laugh came out cracked. "I made it *legible*."

"Then we don't leave it here." Odile was already moving, already thinking in lists — exits, patrols, the fog thickening, which neighbors could be trusted (none) — and was astonished to find the mapmaker's student moving with her, wrapping plates in oilcloth, working in the wordless tandem of two people who have just become accomplices. "Where does a fugitive atlas go, in this city?"

Sable paused, cloth in hand. "There's a woman," she said slowly. "Maren Ostergaard. Widow. Runs cargo upriver that official manifests never mention. My master trusted her with things he wouldn't trust the church with." A ghost of a smile. "She owes him a debt. Or he owed her one. With Maren it was never clear."

"Then we owe her a visit." Odile shrugged her coat over the lined interior, forty-one names against her ribs. "Tonight. Before your Herr Voss returns with his colleague."

They went out through the fog together, two strangers carrying a dead man's truth between them, and neither said aloud what both were thinking: that somewhere behind them a lamp still burned in a ransacked room, and that they had just become, in the eyes of the occupation, the same kind of people Aldous Renne had been.

The hunted kind.

* * *

Maren Ostergaard's warehouse smelled of tar, wet rope, and eels. Pip — eight years old, small for it, perched on a coil of hawser with a stub of pencil and a scrap of sailcloth — looked up when they came in and assessed them the way her mother assessed cargo: weight, risk, whether they'd spoil in transit.

"You're the mapmaker's girl," Pip said. "You're being followed. Two men, gray coats, they've walked past the door twice since the bell."

Sable froze halfway through her explanation. "How—"

"I watch the window." Pip returned to her sailcloth, on which, in astonishing miniature, she had drawn the canal district — every bridge, every gate, every alley a grown soldier would miss — with arrows marking the patrol routes she'd timed herself over three days. "Also you're going the wrong way to the river. The Lockgate has a checkpoint now. Everyone goes to the Lockgate. Nobody goes under it."

"Under it," Maren repeated from the shadows by the eel tanks, emerging wiping her hands. She was broad, gray-streaked, with the forearms of a woman who had hauled her own nets for twenty years, and she looked at Sable and Odile and the oilcloth bundle the way a surgeon looks at a wound. "Aldous told me once, if ever a girl came to me carrying paper heavier than paper, I was to move it upriver myself." She held out her hand. "Let's see how heavy."

But when Sable unwrapped the first plate and laid it on the barrel head, and Maren bent over the dotted salt road, and Odile began — quietly, precisely — to explain what the dots meant, the widow went still in a way that made even Pip stop drawing.

"Maren?" Sable said softly.

"My husband," Maren said at last, "was taken fourteen months ago. No trial. No grave. I've spent a year telling myself he ran off, drowned, anything but—" Her jaw set. She put one thick finger on the smallest dot of all, a holding site on the marsh edge that Odile's registers confirmed, and her voice came out level as a spirit gauge. "Tell me again, clerk. Slowly. Every site. Every count."

Outside, through the fog, came the double tread of boots passing the door a third time — slower now. Pausing.

Pip slid off the hawser without a sound, blew out the nearest lamp, and whispered into the dark:

"Back way's flooded. But I know another one. Hold my hand, and nobody breathe till I say."
