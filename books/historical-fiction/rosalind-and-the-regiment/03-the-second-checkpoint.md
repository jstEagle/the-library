## 3. The Second Checkpoint

The river checkpoint at Kesselbrug was a wound in the city's throat. Sable had drawn it three times from memory before she understood why the atlas's marginalia circled it like a hawk: every prisoner the occupation took east of the harbor passed through its customs shed, and every name in her master's careful hand had crossed that water without ever coming back.

Now she stood on the near bank in Maren's widow's black, watching lamplight stutter across the ice-edged river, and divided an atlas into pieces small enough to be carried by people who could not afford to be caught carrying anything.

"The sections go separately," Odile had said, spreading the disbound pages across Maren's kitchen table like a surgeon laying out a body. "Never two parts of the same quire together. If one of us is taken, the rest survives." She had said *us* without hesitation, and Sable had felt something crack open in her chest that was not entirely grief.

Maren took the harbor charts, sewn into the false hem of her skirt where a smuggler's wife of twenty years could hide a Bible, a pistol, or a nation. Pip took three pages folded into her primer, between the alphabet and the Lord's Prayer, because no soldier searched a child's schoolwork twice. Sable kept the master index — the names — pressed flat against her ribs under her stays, where her heartbeat could keep them company.

The plan was simple, which meant it would fail.

They went through in pairs, an hour apart, playing the roles the checkpoint expected: a widow visiting her sister's grave in the eastern parish, a clerk delivering year-end tallies, a girl going to her aunt for the holiday. The ferryman, Bram, had poled contraband past this shed since before the occupation; Maren vouched for him with the one currency that mattered, which was that he owed her dead husband his life.

Sable crossed first. The soldier at the plank bridge barely looked up from his brazier — a boy of perhaps seventeen with chilblains on his knuckles — and stamped her pass with the boredom of a man who had checked ten thousand passes and found nothing in any of them worth finding.

She was fifty yards up the far bank when she heard the whistle.

* * *

Pip counted the soldiers because counting was what she did when the world went wrong. Fourteen at the shed, two at the brazier, one walking the ice shelf with a lantern held low, looking for footprints. She knew what footprints meant. Her mother had taught her to read ground the way other children read psalters.

Pip sat very still on the ferry bench, primer open on her knees, and watched Herr Voss step off the customs boat.

He wore no uniform, only a grey coat and a census clerk's satchel, and he walked the plank like a man visiting his own property. He spoke briefly to the sergeant, and then he did a thing Pip had seen him do once before, through a window, in a street in Varenholt: he opened a ledger and began reading names aloud, and soldiers began pulling travelers out of line.

Not searching everyone. Only certain people. Only households that matched his lists.

Her mother was third in the queue.

*Mama will smile,* Pip thought, gripping the primer until the pasteboard dented. *Mama smiles and they never know what's under the smile.* And indeed she saw Maren laugh at something Voss said, saw her spread her hands in the easy gesture of a woman complaining about tolls, saw the soldier reach for her hem anyway because Voss had tapped the ledger twice, twice, the way a man taps a map when he already knows.

They cut the hem open right there on the planks. Pip watched her mother's face do nothing at all while the harbor charts fell into the snow like the wings of a killed bird.

Voss looked up then. Looked down the queue, past the weeping and the stamping boots, and his eyes crossed Pip's bench the way a hand crosses a shelf, touching everything, keeping nothing.

Yet.

* * *

By the time Sable reached the safehouse — a cooper's loft above the tannery reek, lent to them by a man who asked no questions because his brother's name was in the atlas — Maren had been gone four hours and the news had traveled faster than grief.

"Taken east," Odile said. She sat with the census tallies spread around her like fallen leaves, and her hands were steady in the way of hands that had decided to be steady or die. "Through Kesselbrug, on the prison barge. They hold prisoners at Fort Alden over the turn of the year."

"Then we go to Fort Alden."

"No." Odile pulled a printed broadsheet from beneath the tallies and slid it across the table. It was proclamations, official script, the occupation's seal fat as a slug at the bottom. Sable read it twice because the first time the words refused to arrange themselves into meaning.

*"A General Amnesty..."*

"They're ending the war by paperwork," Odile said. "Midnight, the thirty-first of December. Every warrant dissolved. Every register sealed. Every prosecution void — including prosecutions built on seized documents." She tapped the page. "Including evidence. After midnight, your master's atlas isn't testimony anymore, Sable. It's souvenirs."

Sable heard herself say, "Then it doesn't matter if we burn it," and hated how much relief lived in the sentence.

"It matters more than ever." Odile's finger came down on the amnesty clause, on the small merciless print beneath the mercy. "*Save for such matters as are entered of record with the Commissioners of Amnesty prior to the hour of turning.*" She looked up. "There are commissioners sitting in the customhouse on the far side of the city, taking depositions until midnight. If the atlas reaches their table as evidence — sworn, witnessed, entered — it outlives the amnesty. The names become record. Record survives even Voss."

"And if it doesn't reach them—"

"Then in eleven days every name in your master's hand becomes ink. Just ink."

Sable put her hand flat against her ribs, against the paper warmth of the index, and thought about the terrible new arithmetic of her life: eleven days, one gate left, half an atlas, and a woman in Fort Alden whose freedom now weighed against every name she carried.

Because that was the sum, wasn't it? That was the sum Odile was too kind to say aloud. Pages could be copied, split, scattered, replaced. Maren could not. And yet Maren would gut them both with a fish knife if they traded her freedom for the truth — Maren, who had sewn charts into her hem knowing exactly what hems cost.

"We get her back," Sable said, "and we get the pages in. Both. I'm done choosing."

"You haven't chosen anything yet," Odile said gently. "That comes later. It always does."

* * *

Bram came for them at the second crossing two nights later, as promised, and Sable learned on the water that promises have prices.

The river ran black and slow under a sky the color of wet slate. Pip crouched in the bilge with the primer pages, reciting coastlines under her breath like a catechism — *Cape Verre, the old lime quay, the sluice at Millersgate* — because her mother had told her maps were prayers you could act on. Sable held the remaining quires wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and watched Bram's shoulders work the pole and tried to read him the way Pip read ground.

She failed.

At the mid-river bend, where the current slackened and the far lamp of the checkpoint hung like a low star, Bram shipped the pole and turned around.

"I'm sorry," he said, and sounded it. "They have my daughter, Sable. Voss has had her since the widow was taken. A girl of six, in a house with kind people in it." His voice broke and reassembled itself. "I only have to lose you. Not deliver you. Just — lose you. Let the river have what falls."

Sable was moving before the sentence finished. Oilcloth overboard — she threw it toward the eastern bank, the long throw of a woman who had spent a lifetime building muscles for carrying paper — and heard it slap the shallows among the reeds. Half the quires. Gone into black water and winter reeds, retrievable maybe, maybe not, certainly not with a soldier's lantern already swinging toward the splash.

Pip came up off the bilge like a cat, grabbed Sable's wrist, and pointed — not at the lost bundle, but at the sluice at Millersgate, a dark gap in the far embankment where the drainage channel met the river.

"In," Pip said. "Now. Before he poles us to the shed."

They went over the side into water cold enough to stop the heart's argument. The channel took them — narrow, roaring, filthy with tannery runoff — and spat them out behind the coal yards, where Sable lay on her back on the stones with Pip wheezing beside her and the whole eastern half of her master's life's work somewhere behind her in the dark, sinking or snagged or already in Voss's hands.

She counted what remained. Against her ribs, dry inside waxed silk: the index. All the names. In Pip's primer, saved by a child's grip: three pages. In her own soaked memory: every chart she had studied until the lines lived behind her eyelids.

Half the atlas. All the names. One woman in Fort Alden. Six days until the century turned.

Odile found them at dawn, hauled them into the cooper's loft, fed them brandy and bad news: the commissioners had moved up their schedule, the customhouse gate would close at dusk on the final day, and Voss had posted descriptions at every gate in the city — a woman with ink-stained fingers, a dismissed clerk, a child.

Sable sat dripping on the floorboards, holding the index against the firelight, and began, methodically, to make copies of the names in three different hands.

"If they take me," she said, "the names still go in. Promise me, Odile. Swear it on your registers."

Odile didn't answer at once. She was looking past Sable, out the loft window, at the snow coming down over Varenholt like the century itself being buried.

"Sable," she said quietly. "I know how we get into the customhouse. But you're not going to like the cart."
