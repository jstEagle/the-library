## 4. What the Century Keeps

**Sable**

The decoy atlas weighed almost nothing, and that was its cruelty. For eleven months Sable had carried the true one against her ribs like a second heart, its pages softened by lamplight and her own breath, and now the thing in her satchel was paper and paste and nothing — forty blank leaves she had aged with tea and smoke, stitched in a binding she'd copied from memory until her fingers bled at the seams.

It was beautiful. That was the worst part. Voss would want to hold it, to feel the weight of a victory, and it would give him exactly what a forged thing gives: the shape of triumph and none of its substance.

She stood before the final gate as the last afternoon of the century drained out of the sky. The Salt Gate had been built for merchants; now it bristled with occupation bayonets, and above it the amnesty proclamation hung in three languages, gilt letters promising that all crimes of the occupied year would be *forgiven and forgotten* at midnight. Forgotten. As if forgetting were a mercy and not a second disappearance.

Herr Voss came down the causeway with snow on his shoulders and his ledger under his arm like a psalter.

"Fräulein Whitlock." He stopped an arm's length away — close enough for courtesy, far enough for a pistol. "You have made this easy for me. I confess I expected more running."

"I'm tired of running," Sable said, and found it was true. "I'm delivering my master's commission. You asked about it once, in my workshop. You remember."

"I remember everything," Voss said mildly. "It is my profession." He extended his gloved hand for the satchel, and Sable gave it up, and watched him open it, and watched his face do the small, terrible thing it did when he touched her teacher's hand in the ink.

"An atlas," he said. "Of the river country."

"Of the river country," she agreed. "Unfinished. I finished it. It is what he would have wanted delivered."

Behind Voss's shoulder, past the gatehouse, stood a census cart — grey canvas, government stenciling, a bored driver, and beneath the false floor Odile had built from requisition forms, half the atlas of the dead, wrapped in oilcloth, with Pip curled around it like a cat around a warm stone.

Sable did not look at the cart. She looked at Voss, and smiled, because looking away was the only mistake left to make.

* * *

**Odile**

Odile had spent nine years learning that the occupation's greatest weapon was not the gallows but the column of figures — names reduced to entries, entries reduced to totals, totals reduced to the smooth sentence *the city is quiet*. She had been dismissed for noticing that the columns did not add. Now she sat in the dark under the census cart's false floor with her knee against a child's shoulder and half a nation's evidence against her chest, and understood that tonight the arithmetic was going to be read aloud.

Through a knothole she could see the gateyard: lanterns, bayonets, Voss opening the satchel with the reverence of a man handling relics. Let him. Every minute he spent admiring the forgery was a minute bought with Sable's body, and Odile knew the price, and paid attention anyway, because grief was a luxury for after.

"Is she doing it?" Pip whispered.

"Yes."

"Is she scared?"

Odile considered lying, decided the child had earned better. "Yes. And doing it anyway. Remember that part."

Pip's hand found hers in the dark. Small fingers, ink-stained — the girl had been copying map legends by lantern light for three days and knew the marginalia better now than any soldier who'd ever marched by it. Somewhere beyond the wall, the cathedral bells waited for midnight like loaded guns.

A boot crunched near the wheel. A voice: "Cart manifest."

Odile closed her eyes. Requisition 44-C, census materials, authorized — she had written it herself, in the clerk's hand no one questioned anymore because no one believed a dismissed woman would dare forge it. The driver droned. The boot moved on.

Then, from the gateyard, Voss's voice, rising with something new in it — not triumph. Suspicion.

* * *

**Pip**

Pip knew maps the way other children knew prayers, and she knew this one: the gateyard, drawn a hundred times in her head while Maren taught her the tide tables and Odile taught her the registers. There were two ways out of the yard once the bells began. One was the river stair, guarded. The other was through the commissioners' tent, which no one guarded, because no one imagined a child walking into the lion's mouth on purpose.

That was the whole trick, Maren said. Be somewhere they've already decided you can't be.

When the first bell struck — the hour, not yet the year — the cart's floor lifted six inches, enough. Pip slid out into the shadow between the cart and the fodder sacks, the oilcloth bundle strapped flat against her front under her coat, and walked, small and unhurried and utterly unremarkable, toward the tent with the white flag and the word AMNESTY stenciled above the flap.

Inside sat two tired men behind a trestle table stacked with ledgers — commissioners from the capital, sent to make the century end cleanly. One looked up. "No petitions after—"

"This isn't a petition." Pip set the bundle on the table and unwrapped it, and their faces changed the way faces change when a map becomes a grave. "It's proof. My friend says amnesty means forgiven, not forgotten. Those are different words. She says if you burn them or bury them, then next year someone does it all again, and the year after, and nobody will ever know the difference because nobody wrote the names down." She tapped the topmost page, where her own careful transcription of the marginalia ran beside her master-teacher's dying hand. "So don't forget. Read it. Out loud. Before midnight."

The older commissioner picked up a page. His lips moved. His color went grey.

Outside, the bells began to count the old year down.

* * *

**Maren**

They had held Maren since the ferry — three days in a room with a stool and a window, and every day Herr Voss himself had come to ask her questions in his gentle, patient voice, the voice of a man reading a ledger aloud. Where were the pages. Who carried them. What had the widow of a smuggler to do with cartography.

She had given him nothing but weather reports and recipes, and this morning he had run out of patience and ordered her brought to the Salt Gate to watch — that was the phrase — *to watch what happens to those who help thieves of state.*

So she stood in the falling snow between two soldiers and saw Sable in chains, saw Voss holding the decoy atlas up to the lantern like a jeweler with a stone, and understood in one cold flash what Sable had done, and loved her for it, and feared for her with her whole body.

Voss turned a page. Turned another. His brow creased — the tiny arithmetic frown Maren had seen on customs men catching short weights.

"Where is the rest of it?"

"That is the rest of it," Sable said.

"It is blank."

"Much of my master's work was unfinished," Sable said. "I told you I finished it. I never told you what I finished it *into.*"

And that was when the bells began — not the hour-bell but the great tenor, the century turning stroke by stroke over the water — and across the yard, from the commissioners' tent, came a voice amplified by a speaking trumpet, reading. Not proclaiming. *Reading.* Names. Streets. Dates of arrest. The disappeared of Varenholt, itemized in the occupation's own requisition numbers, cross-referenced against Voss's own census registers, which Odile had taught Pip to cite because there is no indictment so damning as a man's own handwriting.

The soldiers did not move. The soldiers listened. Some of them were from here.

Voss stood very still, the forged atlas open and weightless in his hands, and Maren watched him understand that the thing he was holding was the confession — that by seizing it he had seized nothing, and by hunting the pages for a year he had guaranteed they would be delivered loudly, publicly, at the exact moment the law changed sides.

The trumpeter read on. A commissioner emerged from the tent, took Voss's arm, and spoke words too low for the yard. Voss's ledger was taken from under his arm — gently, the way you take a knife from a sleeping man — and opened, and read from.

They released Maren without ceremony, a sergeant unlocking her wrists and not meeting her eye. She walked across the snow to where Sable stood chained and smiling, tears freezing on both their faces, and took her cold hands.

"You gave yourself up," Maren said.

"I gave them up," Sable said. "There's a difference. Look at the yard."

The yard was full of people who had come out for the amnesty and stayed for the names — hundreds of them, saying them back to the trumpeter, learning their dead in their own mouths, the century turning on a city that finally knew whom it had lost and who had lost them for it.

Above the gate, the gilt promise of *forgotten* caught the lantern light, and below it, page by page, the city refused.

The last bell struck. The chains were cold. Sable smiled anyway.

Somewhere in the crowd, Odile was already writing — because an ending, she always said, was just a record no one had checked yet.
