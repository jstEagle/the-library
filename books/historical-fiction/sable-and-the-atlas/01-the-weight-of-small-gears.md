## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Rosalind would later consider the first small mercy of the affair, because Tuesdays were the days Master Havelock took his lunch at the Lamb & Lantern and did not return for two hours, during which the workshop belonged — unofficially, but thoroughly — to her.

She was up a ladder at the time, elbow-deep in the cathedral turret clock's striking train, with a smudge of lampblack on her cheek that she would not discover until evening and a screw held between her teeth like a rose stem. The apprentice boy, Pim, who was eleven and mostly elbows, came clattering up the stairs with the post tray and announced to the room at large that there was *real* paper, the heavy kind, the kind with a seal.

"Don't talk to me about seals," Rosalind said around the screw. "I've been fighting this one since breakfast."

"Not that kind of seal." Pim held the letter up as though it might glow. "It's got a mountain on it."

Rosalind came down the ladder. This was not because she was curious — she was a professional, and professionals did not abandon their work over stationery — but because the striking train could wait four minutes, and her arms had gone numb an hour ago, and if one more person was going to send the workshop something with a seal on it, she preferred to know before Master Havelock came back and made it into a lecture about humility.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, and the wax bore the sigil of the Veymar Observatory: a stylized peak beneath a star. The address was written in a hand so precise it looked typeset.

*To the Guild of Horological Arts, attention of the Master. Re: the master clock.*

"Attention of the Master," Pim read over her shoulder, breathing garlic from his lunch. "That means you have to give it to him."

"It means he has to be here to receive it," Rosalind corrected. "Which is different, though I grant you the difference is lost on most people." She turned the envelope over twice, weighing it. Letters addressed to the Master were usually complaints — a parish clock running fast, a town hall chime that frightened horses — and complaints went into a drawer and died of neglect. But nobody wrote on paper like this to complain about anything. Paper like this was for institutions talking to institutions.

She put it on his desk, squared its corners with the blotter, and went back up her ladder, where she spent the next hour being extremely uninterested in it.

* * *

Master Havelock read the letter standing at the window, which was where he read everything important, because he liked to look out at the city while deciding things about it. He was a long man, folded like a pocket rule, with white hair combed back and hands that were never still — even now they turned the letter over and over, the way he'd turn a watch in his palm.

"The observatory at Veymar," he said at last. "Their master clock is failing."

"Failing how?" Rosalind asked, because someone had to ask practical questions in this establishment.

"Losing time. Irregularly, by the sound of it." He set the letter down and looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable. "They want someone sent up to repair it before winter closes the pass."

"Then we should send our best," said Rosalind, who was twenty-three and had no gift whatsoever for false modesty.

A muscle moved near Havelock's jaw that might, in a less controlled face, have been a smile. "They want someone sent up," he repeated, ignoring her entirely, "who will do the work quietly and come home when it's done. Not someone who will make a project of it."

"I never make projects of things."

"You restored the entire Wexley regulator because its second hand offended you."

"It was *lying*," Rosalind said. "It drifted three seconds a day and everyone pretended it wasn't happening. That isn't restoration, that's courtesy."

"And how long did you spend on courtesy?"

"Six weeks," she admitted. "But it keeps perfect time now, and Mr. Wexley wept, which he has never done in living memory, including at his own mother's funeral."

Havelock turned back to the window. Below them, the guild quarter was going about its afternoon: carts, apprentices, the bell of Saint Aldric marking the half hour with its usual slightly drunken enthusiasm. Somewhere in that sprawl was the capital's new administrative office, flying a flag that hadn't existed five years ago, full of clerks who were very interested in maps, borders, ledgers, and other people's mountains.

"The colonial administration is establishing its survey office in the valley this year," Havelock said, in the tone of a man mentioning weather. "Veymar sits at the head of it. The observatory keeps the region's official time, and official time anchors official surveys. Everything downstream depends on that clock being right."

"So send me."

"I haven't finished."

"You never finish. You circle. Send me while you circle."

He gave her the look he reserved for apprentices who filed gears against the grain — weary, fond, and faintly alarmed — and then he did something unusual. He sat down. Master Havelock sat down the way other men knelt: deliberately, and only under weight.

"Rosalind." He said her name plainly, without title, which he almost never did. "You'll go. You're the finest repair hand this guild has produced in thirty years, and I include myself, and I would thank you not to repeat that. But you will go up that mountain, you will repair their clock, and you will come home. And there is one thing more."

Here he paused, and Rosalind felt the pause settle over the room like dust.

"The observatory has a records room. Old survey plates, chart copies, correspondence — the usual archive. You may find yourself curious. You are constitutionally incapable of not finding yourself curious; I've watched you disassemble a music box to learn why it played flat. But the records room is not your commission. You don't enter it, you don't ask after it, and if anyone mentions it, you develop a sudden and profound interest in escapements. Are we understood?"

"Why?" It escaped before she could dress it up. "What's in it?"

"That," said Havelock, rising, "is precisely the question you are not going to ask."

* * *

That night she packed.

This was, in truth, her favorite part of any job — the ritual of it, the arithmetic of herself. She kept a list pinned inside the lid of her case, revised over six years of apprenticeship, and every item on it had earned its place through some specific failure. The jeweler's screwdrivers, seven sizes, because in her first month she'd stripped a pivot screw in front of a client and wanted to die. The tin of pegwood and oilers. The loupe, her father's, brass-rimmed, with the Nakamura mark stamped inside the bezel — a tiny five-petaled flower her grandfather had cut by hand, back when there had been a family workshop instead of a family story. The burnisher her mother had given her at fifteen, saying, *a good edge forgives many sins.* The spare spectacles, because mountains, presumably, contained weather.

She laid everything on the bench cloth in rows, the way she'd been taught, and looked at it, and thought about what a life was when you measured it by what fit in one case.

Her whole trade fit. Her tools weighed less than a cat. Everything she knew about precision — about the difference between *almost right* and *right*, which was the same distance as between *almost honest* and honest — fit in fourteen inches of calfskin and walnut. It seemed to her both marvelous and slightly insulting, like discovering your soul was portable.

Pim appeared at the door in his nightshirt, ostensibly to say goodnight, actually to inspect the packing.

"You're really going? Up the mountain?"

"Apparently."

"Is it true there's snow up there already?"

"There's snow everywhere at altitude in autumn, Pim, it's not exotic."

"Kestrel says the observatory clock is haunted."

"Kestrel says the bakery cat is haunted. Kestrel is nine and believes everything is haunted."

"He says it *lies*. At midnight. That's why they need a clockmaker." Pim delivered this with the satisfaction of a boy handing over genuine intelligence. "He says his cousin's friend lives in Veymar and the clock strikes wrong and everyone pretends it doesn't."

Rosalind paused, tweezers in hand.

"A clock doesn't lie," she said. "That's the entire point of a clock. People lie. Clocks just report what they've been built to report — or what they've been *made* to report." She heard her own words a beat too late, and something in them snagged on the memory of Havelock's pause, that dust settling over the records room. She shook it off and flicked a rag at him. "Go to bed. If you tell Kestrel I said the clock was haunted, I'll assign you to polish duty until spring."

"You always assign me polish duty anyway."

"Then imagine how much worse spring will be."

He grinned and went, and she sat alone with her rows of tools and the lamplight, and let herself feel it properly at last: not fear exactly, but the particular hum of a mechanism about to engage. A real commission. Her own. A mountain observatory, a master clock, official time anchoring official surveys — her name, eventually, attached to work that mattered, that *held*, the way good work holds. She had waited six years to be trusted with something that would hold.

And underneath the hum, quieter, a small cold gear turning that she couldn't quite locate in the train: *why does a guild master warn a journeyman off a room full of old papers? Why does a warning arrive before the curiosity has any object?*

She decided it was none of her business. She was very good at deciding things were none of her business. She'd been raised on it, more or less — her father had crossed an ocean and rebuilt a life on the principle, and it had served the family well, mostly, except in all the ways nobody talked about.

She packed the last item: her certificate of journeyman status, freshly countersigned, which Havelock had pressed into her hand that afternoon along with travel funds and a second warning, softer than the first, that had frightened her more. *Do the work, Rosalind. Only the work. Some rooms are locked for the protection of what's inside them, and some are locked for the protection of the people outside. From up close, you cannot tell which is which.*

She wound her own traveling watch, set it by the workshop's regulator, and closed the case. The clasps went home with two small, satisfying clicks — the sound, her mother used to say, of a thing prepared.

In the morning she would take the coach north. In three days she would stand at the foot of a switchback road climbing into cloud, toward an observatory full of strangers, a failing clock, a magistrate's valley, and one forbidden room whose contents she had promised, twice, formally, on her certificate and her word, not to wonder about.

She wondered about it the whole way down the stairs, and all through dinner, and again as she blew out her candle.

The clock downstairs struck ten — accurately, obediently, reporting exactly what it had been built to report — and Rosalind lay in the dark listening to it, thinking: *I wonder what a clock sounds like when it lies.*

She fell asleep before she found an answer, which was the last easy sleep she would get for a very long time.
