## 2. Ascent by Switchback

The mule was named Biscuit, which Rosalind considered a slander against biscuits everywhere.

He had refused the road, refused the bridge, and refused — with a flatness of ear that suggested long practice — every one of the switchbacks for the first hour of the climb. Now he stood at the second turning, breathing like a bellows with opinions, while Rosalind adjusted the straps of her tool case and consulted the letter of summons for the fourth time.

*The master clock at the Veymar Observatory fails. The Guild will send its apprentice. Come before the snows.*

No signature she recognized. No mention of what was wrong with it. No word, either, about payment, though Master Aldous had waved that concern away with the particular briskness he reserved for subjects he did not wish to discuss.

"Guild work is guild work," he'd said, pressing the summons into her hands. "You'll go up, you'll repair the clock, you'll come down before first snow. And Rosalind—" He had paused at the door of the workshop, and in the pause she had heard something she had never once heard from him in nine years: hesitation. "The records room on the third floor. It's locked. Whatever anyone tells you, whatever they offer to show you — you keep your nose out of it. You're a clockmaker's apprentice, not an archivist. Clocks only."

She had asked him why. He had turned his back and begun winding the shop regulator, which was already wound, and said nothing at all.

So now she climbed, measuring her life in switchbacks, and tried not to think about the way her master's hands had trembled — very slightly, very briefly — around a letter that wasn't his to be nervous about.

* * *

Veymar announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet stone and, unexpectedly, cardamom. Rosalind crested the shoulder of the mountain where the road gave up pretending to be a road and became a street, and there below her lay the town, folded into the valley's throat like a coin in a purse.

It was not at all what the maps in the guild library had shown her. The guild maps showed Veymar as a dot with a church and a customs house, which was technically true the way a skeleton is technically a man. What the maps did not show was that the town spoke three languages in the space of two streets — the low singsong of the valley families, the clipped colonial tongue of the survey offices, and something older still, carried up from the southern trade roads, that came drifting out of a bakery window along with the smell of cardamom bread and made her stomach announce itself without permission.

The bakery belonged to a broad woman named Ilsa Fen, or so said the sign, though the woman herself corrected this within seconds of Rosalind entering.

"Fen," she said. "Ilsa *and* Tomas Fen. He's the quiet one. You'll want the plum bread, and don't argue, you've been walking since dawn and it shows." She wrapped the loaf, took Rosalind's coin, and studied her over the counter with the frank appraisal of someone who had weighed travelers for thirty years. "Up to the observatory, are you? For the clock."

"The clock," Rosalind agreed, mouth full.

"Hm." Ilsa wrapped a second bun unprompted and pushed it across. "For the walk. The last part's steep." She said it lightly enough, but her eyes went past Rosalind's shoulder, up through the window, toward the white crown of the mountain where the observatory sat, and stayed there a moment too long. "Mind how you go."

"Is it dangerous?"

"No," said Ilsa, too quickly, and then, as if paying a debt: "The town's fine. The people are fine. It's only—" She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture that bought her time. "We're a mixed town, girl. Valley folk, southern families, the survey men, the garrison. Everyone gets along by not asking certain questions. You'll learn which ones."

"I'm very good at questions," said Rosalind.

"I can see that," said Ilsa Fen, and something in her face — not unkind, but tired in the way stone is tired — suggested that this was precisely the problem.

* * *

She meant to go straight up. The light was good, the weather held, and every hour of delay was an hour borrowed from winter.

Instead she found herself stopping in front of a house.

It stood apart from its neighbors at the bend where the town street began its climb, a solid stone cottage with a good roof and a garden gone wild — not neglected, exactly, but *unattended*, weeds growing with the particular confidence of plants that know no one is coming. The windows were boarded. Not shuttered — boarded, plank over glass, and the planks were new, pale and raw against the old grey of the house, nailed up recently and thoroughly.

Rosalind stood looking at it the way she looked at a mechanism that didn't match its description. Everything about the house was in order except its emptiness. Curtains behind the boards. A coat still on its hook inside the porch, visible through a gap in the planking — a heavy surveyor's coat, oiled wool, brass buttons gone green. Someone had left in haste, or been left in haste, or—

"You're staring at the dead man's house."

Rosalind turned. A child stood on the low wall beside the gate, perhaps ten years old, sitting with the boneless balance of youth, eating an apple with total commitment. She wore a man's coat cut down badly and boots that had belonged to two larger children before her.

"I wasn't aware anyone had died," Rosalind said carefully.

"Everyone's aware. That's the whole trouble." The child took another bite. "Anselm. He surveyed things. He fell off the crag above the lake in spring. Slipped, they say." She pronounced *they say* with a precision that did not belong to a ten-year-old, or perhaps belonged entirely to one; children, Rosalind knew, learned irony from watching adults fail at sincerity.

"That's very sad."

"It's very *convenient*," said the child, and swung her legs. "I'm Petra. Who are you?"

"Rosalind Nakamura. I'm going up to repair the observatory clock."

Petra stopped chewing.

For a moment the apple hung forgotten, and the child looked at her with an expression Rosalind would spend a long time trying to classify afterward — not fear, not excitement, but the sharp bright attention of someone who has just seen a key fit a lock everyone pretended had no door.

"The master clock," Petra said.

"So I'm told."

"It lies at midnight."

Rosalind blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"The big clock. Up top. When it strikes twelve at night, it strikes wrong." Petra resumed eating, casual again, as if delivering meteorological intelligence. "Four minutes late. Everybody knows. Mama says don't say it loud because of the magistrate, but everybody knows. Papa says a clock doesn't lie unless somebody taught it to." She slid off the wall, landed, and looked up at Rosalind with the frank blue stare of the very young and the very dangerous. "Are you going to fix it?"

"That is literally my entire purpose here."

"Oh." Petra considered this, chewing. "Good," she said finally, with such weight that it sounded less like approval and more like an instruction. Then she pointed the half-eaten apple at the boarded windows. "Nobody goes in there. Magistrate's orders. But the boards have a loose one, second floor, back corner, if you're the type who looks."

"I'm the type who repairs clocks," said Rosalind.

"Mm," said Petra, in a tone that suggested she had heard finer distinctions drawn to worse effect. She walked three steps away, then turned back. "The clockmaker's mark on your case. Is that the guild?"

"Yes."

"My mama says Anselm trusted clocks more than people. Said a clock couldn't be promoted or threatened." She shrugged, a small sharp gesture far older than her years. "Maybe that's why they broke it instead."

And she was gone, around the corner, quick as a dropped stitch, leaving Rosalind standing in the lane with a plum loaf under her arm and a sentence lodged in her chest like a splinter.

*A clock doesn't lie unless somebody taught it to.*

* * *

She should have gone straight up. She wants it noted, in whatever internal ledger she kept — and she kept one, always, hours and tasks and small debts — that she genuinely intended to go straight up.

But the loose board was, after all, loose. And she told herself — honestly, mostly — that it was professional habit, the same instinct that made her open the backs of watches, that could not walk past a mechanism without wanting to see its works. Houses were mechanisms too, of a crude sort. Doors were escapements. Windows were dials.

The gap between the second-floor boards showed her a room frozen mid-sentence: a desk with papers squared neatly, waiting; a bed made with military corners; a pair of boots by the hearth, dried mud still on them, pointing toward the door as if their owner had stepped out of them mid-thought. On the desk, a candle stub burned down to nothing and never replaced. On the wall above the desk, rectangles of paler paint where charts or maps had hung and been taken down — taken down, note, not fallen, for the nails remained, four to each rectangle, tidy as punctuation.

Nobody falls off a crag and takes their maps down first.

Rosalind withdrew, replaced the board with the discretion of long practice — she had, in her apprenticeship, resealed many a watch case she had no business opening — and stood a moment in the lane with her heart going quicker than the climb accounted for.

Unease had arrived ahead of her, like weather coming down the valley. She could feel it settling on the town, on the bakery, on the child with the apple, on the empty house with its tidy desk: a pressure change, a storm somewhere beyond the ridge that hadn't broken yet but intended to.

Master Aldous had said *clocks only*. The magistrate's boarded windows said *nothing to see*. A dead surveyor's boots pointed at a door he'd never walk through again.

And overhead, at the top of the last and steepest switchback, white against the deepening blue, the observatory dome caught the afternoon sun and flashed it down the mountain like a signal — and beneath the dome, she knew, ticking away in the dark of its tower, waited the largest mechanism she had ever been asked to mend.

A clock that lied at midnight. Four minutes, every night, faithful as sin.

Rosalind shifted her tool case higher on her shoulder, took the first step of the final climb, and found she was no longer certain whether she was ascending toward a repair — or into a trap that had been set, patiently, gear by gear, long before she was ever summoned.

At the top of the switchback, a figure stood waiting by the observatory door, silhouetted against the sky, watching her come.

He did not look friendly.
