## 3. The Astronomer Who Keeps Hours No One Asked For

The observatory door was the first thing in Veymar that had been built by someone who cared.

Rosalind noticed this because she had spent the entire climb cataloguing things built by people who didn't — the sagging fence posts along the switchback road, the bridge planks nailed at angles that suggested a wager rather than a plan, the shuttered house with its windows boarded like eyes closed against bad news. But the observatory door was iron-banded oak, hung on hinges whose knuckles were oiled, and fitted into its frame with a tolerance so fine that when Rosalind pressed her palm against it, it neither rattled nor breathed.

Whoever kept this place kept it *properly*.

She raised her hand to knock and hesitated, which was unlike her. Her master used to say she knocked like an auditor — three raps, evenly spaced, each one a small accusation. She tried to soften it and failed, and the sound went through the door and down some corridor inside like a coin dropped in a well.

Footsteps. Unhurried. Then a pause behind the door itself, long enough that Rosalind imagined a man on the other side deciding whether she existed.

The door opened.

The man standing in it was younger than she'd expected and more rumpled than any portrait of an astronomer she had ever seen. He wore a coat that had once been good and now served mainly as a place to keep chalk dust, and his hair looked as though he'd been arguing with the wind on the dome above and losing. His eyes did a thing she would come to know very well: they went from her face to her instrument case to her boots and back again, weighing, in about two seconds, exactly what kind of trouble she was.

"You're the clockmaker," he said. Not a question, but not entirely a greeting either.

"Apprentice clockmaker," said Rosalind. "Rosalind Nakamura. The guild sent me."

"The guild," he repeated, the way another man might have said *the plague*. "Sent you. In October. To repair a clock that has been failing, by their accounting, for eleven months." He stepped back from the doorway without inviting her in, which was a particular kind of arithmetic. "Did they tell you what's wrong with it?"

"They told me it runs slow."

"Runs slow." He smiled, and it was a strange smile, thin at one corner. "Yes. It does that."

There was a silence. Rosalind had been raised in a workshop where silence was considered a tool, to be picked up and set down deliberately, and she recognized a fellow practitioner when she met one. This man could hold a silence like a jeweler holds a stone.

"May I come in?" she asked finally. "It's beginning to snow, and my case weighs as much as I do."

He looked past her shoulder at the sky, where the first flakes were indeed coming down in that tentative way snow has before it commits to anything, and something in his face shifted — not warmth, exactly, but the acknowledgment of a shared fact. Snow was a fact. Facts he could deal with.

"Third door on the left is the clock room," he said. "You'll find everything you need there. The kitchen is at the end of the corridor; help yourself to tea, the kettle's always on. Your room is the second on the right, made up already." He began to close the door — then stopped, as if remembering a formality owed to civilization. "I'm Sable. Elias Sable. I keep the observatory."

"Keep it?"

"I keep its hours." He said it plainly, without irony, and then, apparently satisfied that the necessary words had been exchanged, added: "There are rooms you're not to enter. The dome. The east archive. The room at the end of the west corridor — the locked one, you'll know it by the lock. Everything else is yours to use."

"That's a generous portion of a building to forbid," said Rosalind.

"It's a precise portion," said Sable. "I measured."

And he shut the door — no. He didn't. He turned and walked away down the entrance hall, leaving the door open behind him, which Rosalind slowly realized was not rudeness but a test, or perhaps simply the manner of a man who had lived alone so long that hospitality had worn down to its load-bearing members. She stood a moment in the cold with her case, feeling the snow settle on her shoulders, and then she carried herself and her twenty-six pounds of tools across the threshold.

* * *

The clock room was magnificent, and it was wrong, and it took her less than four minutes to know both things.

The master clock stood against the north wall — a regulator of serious pedigree, walnut case gone silver-grey with age, brass face polished by decades of careful hands. Below the dial, glass panels let into the case showed the movement, and Rosalind stood before those panels the way other women stood before altars.

It was clean. That was the first wrongness. A working clock accumulates dust the way a working kitchen accumulates flour; this movement was immaculate, every wheel bright, every pivot oiled. Someone serviced it constantly. And yet the guild's letter had spoken of a mechanism "degraded by altitude and neglect."

The second wrongness took longer, because it required her to open the case, and opening the case required permission she technically hadn't been given — but Sable had said *everything else is yours to use*, and Rosalind had learned long ago that precision in language was a gift meant to be used.

She got the panels off, set out her tools on a cloth in strict order — screwdrivers by size, tweezers, the loupe on its cord around her neck — and put her eye to the escapement.

An escapement, for those who have never loved one, is the heart of a clock: the little gate that lets time through in equal measures, tick and tock, releasing the wheels one tooth at a time so that all that coiled violence in the mainspring becomes, instead, an orderly procession of seconds. A good escapement is honest. It cannot flatter. It gives you exactly what the geometry says it will give you, forever, or it fails loudly and everyone knows.

This escapement had been *adjusted*. Not broken — adjusted. Someone with real skill had altered the impulse angle, eased the pallets, done half a dozen small things that together would produce a single elegant result: the clock would lose time. Steadily. Predictably. About four minutes a night, if her eye was worth anything, and her eye was worth something.

Rosalind sat back on her heels and stared at the beautiful, lying machine.

"Well," she said aloud, to nobody. "Hello, you."

"You talk to clocks."

Sable was leaning in the doorway. She hadn't heard him approach, which annoyed her professionally — a man that large should not move like weather.

"Only when they say something interesting," she said.

"And this one?" He came into the room, and she watched his eyes go to the open case, to the exposed escapement, and she watched — carefully, the way she watched everything in this building — the exact moment his expression closed like a ledger.

"This one has a great deal to say," said Rosalind. "Most of it contradictory. The guild told me neglect. This movement has been maintained within a tenth of a millimeter of its life. The guild told me degradation. These pallets have been re-cut — recently, and beautifully. Whoever did this work should be employed by the guild." She paused. "Instead of whatever it is you do here."

"I chart stars."

"Do you." She stood, brushing off her knees. "Then you'll want to explain to me why your star charts are filed by date and your clock room is heated to a temperature that keeps the oil at ideal viscosity, while the corridor outside is cold enough to hang meat. Astronomers care about the sky, Mr. Sable. Only clockmakers care this much about a clock."

Something flickered behind his eyes — surprise, she thought, quickly converted to caution. "I keep the hours," he said again. "That's all keeping hours means. You'd know."

"I'd know," Rosalind agreed pleasantly. "Which is why I'm going to ask you directly, since we seem to be two people who prefer direct instruments: do you want me to fix this clock or not?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended. Or perhaps exactly as hard as she'd intended. Sable crossed to the window — snow now, properly committed, whitening the valley below — and stood with his hands behind his back like a man addressing a parliament only he could see.

"When I was a boy," he said, "there was a watchmaker in the capital who repaired my mother's watch. Free of charge. He opened it, looked at the movement, and closed it again without touching anything, and told her it was fixed. She wound it faithfully for six years. It never ran right, but she trusted him, so it never ran wrong either, if you follow me." He turned. "Some repairs are about what gets changed. Some are about what gets noticed. Before you touch anything in this room, Miss Nakamura, I'd like to know which kind of repairer the guild sent."

Rosalind considered him. Up close he had ink on his fingers and a smear of chalk at his jaw, and beneath the suspicion there was something else, something tired and vigilant, like a man who had been holding a door shut for years and had just heard footsteps on the far side. She recognized the look. She had seen it in her master's face, twice, when certain clients' names were mentioned.

"My master sent a letter with me," she said. "Instructions."

"May I see them?"

"No," said Rosalind, surprising herself. "They concern the records room, and the records room is none of my business, and I intend to keep it that way. My business is this clock. And this clock—" she laid two fingers gently on the case, "—is lying. Four minutes a night, give or take. Somebody taught it to lie, very carefully, and I would bet my best screwdriver that whoever did it knew this movement better than the man who built it."

The silence that followed had texture. Outside, the snow thickened against the glass. Somewhere below them, muffled by walls, Veymar was closing its shutters for the season.

"You haven't touched it," Sable said at last.

"I've looked at it. Looking isn't touching."

"What will you do?"

"That," said Rosalind, "depends on whether the person who altered it tells me why before I decide, or after I've already decided and made it awkward for everyone."

For the first time, Elias Sable laughed. It was a rusty sound, an unpracticed sound, the laugh of a hinge that had gone too long without oiling, and it transformed his face so completely that Rosalind had to look away and pretend to be interested in the pendulum.

"You realize," he said, recovering, "that you've just threatened a man you've known for a quarter of an hour."

"I've threatened a *clock warden* I've known for a quarter of an hour. It's practically the same as knowing him." She began, methodically, to lay out her tools again. "I'll need the light until dusk. Is there a lamp in here that doesn't smoke?"

"There's a lamp that smokes *beautifully*," said Sable. "And one that behaves. I'll bring the behaving one."

He paused at the door. Rosalind felt it, the pause, the way you feel a clock hesitate between ticks.

"Miss Nakamura. Why did the guild really send you? An apprentice. In October. For a slow clock."

She kept her eyes on her tools. "Because I'm cheap, I'm thorough, and I don't ask questions." A beat. "Two of those three are still true."

When she glanced up he was gone, and she could hear him on the stairs, unhurried, and she found she was smiling at a pendulum, which was undignified, and she stopped, and then started again, because there was no one to see.

* * *

He brought the lamp, and tea, and — this was the part she noted most carefully — answers.

Not freely. Nothing in this building was free. But over the next hour, as she worked and he sat in the corner ostensibly reading star tables, a conversation developed between them with the structure of a duel and the rhythm of a dance, and both of them pretended it wasn't happening.

"The remontoire's original," she said, head under the movement. "Unusual, this far from the capital."

"The founder insisted. He distrusted long trains."

"A man after my own heart. And the gravity arm — someone's re-machined it."

"Someone improved it."

"Someone *altered* it. Improvement leaves the maker's intent intact. Alteration replaces it." She emerged, wiping her hands. "You know an extraordinary amount about horology for a stargazer, Mr. Sable. Most astronomers treat clocks the way farmers treat roosters — gratefully, and without curiosity."

"Time is my raw material," said Sable mildly. "Star positions mean nothing without precise time. Every coordinate I record is a negotiation between the sky and that clock. If the clock lies, the stars lie. So yes. I keep the hours." He turned a page. "Why does an apprentice carry a loupe worth more than her contract?"

Rosalind's hands went briefly still. It was a good strike; she gave him the point. "My master's loupe. On loan. He says I'll lose it or earn it."

"Which do you believe?"

"I believe," she said, "that my master says a lot of things designed to be understood later. Like instructions written in water-soluble ink." She narrowed her eyes at him across the lamplit room. "Your turn. Who re-cut these pallets?"

"Previous keeper."

"He died eight years ago, according to the plaque downstairs, and this oil is six months old."

"His ghost was very diligent."

"Mr. Sable."

"Miss Nakamura." He set down the star tables, and for a moment neither of them performed anything at all, and what remained underneath was two people of roughly the same stubbornness regarding each other across a room full of gears. "Here is what I can tell you. The clock is slow. The slowness matters to someone. You were sent by people who may be those someones, or may be worried about them, or may merely be punctilious about their property. Until I know which—"

"—the clock stays slow," Rosalind finished. "And I stay a guest. And the dome stays closed, and the east archive stays closed, and the room at the end of the west corridor stays locked."

"You were listening."

"I'm always listening. It's how I found the click in your striking train from the corridor. You have a cracked tooth on the hour wheel. Third bell of noon, there's a hitch — you've trained yourself not to hear it, but it's there."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "You diagnosed my clock by ear through a wall."

"I diagnosed your *clock*, Mr. Sable. You remain under observation."

Another rusted-out laugh escaped him before he could stop it. He looked almost angry about it, the way men do when a thing escapes that they've kept caged a long while, and he stood abruptly and gathered his star tables as though the conversation had become a room he needed to leave.

"Dinner is at seven," he said from the doorway. "I cook adequately. You'll find I'm better company after dark, when there's work to look at instead of questions."

"Is that when the questions stop working?"

"That's when," said Sable, "I stop pretending I mind them."

He went. Rosalind turned back to the movement, to the beautiful lying escapement with its four stolen minutes, and found her own reflection warped in the polished bell of the pendulum bob — a small dark figure in a lamplit room, smiling faintly at nothing, in a building full of locked doors.

She should report the tampering. That was procedure. That was the guild's whole catechism: observe, record, certify, return. Her master's voice recited it in her head even now, patient as a metronome.

But her master also said *ask nothing about the records room*, and a man doesn't warn you away from a room unless he knows what's in it, and Rosalind Nakamura had spent her whole life learning mechanisms by asking one question over and over: *what is this supposed to do, and who benefits from it doing something else?*

The lamp hissed. The clock ticked, steady and false.

At midnight, she thought, watching it, I'll sit with it when it strikes. A clock that lies on schedule tells you something too. Even liars keep appointments — especially liars. Especially, she suspected, in Veymar, where the whole town seemed to hold its breath between the ticks.

She packed her tools in reverse order and went down to dinner, to adequate cooking, and to the company of a man who knew more about her clock than she'd yet managed to pry out of him — and who, she was beginning to suspect with a sensation she couldn't quite file anywhere, knew more about her summons than she did herself.
