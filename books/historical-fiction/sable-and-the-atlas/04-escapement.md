## 4. Escapement

The master clock lived in a room of its own at the base of the observatory tower, and the room had opinions.

Rosalind felt them the moment she unlocked the door that first morning — a draft with intentions, a smell of cold stone and old oil, a silence so deliberate it could only have been rehearsed. Guild workshops were never silent. They muttered, clattered, swore in three languages when a mainspring slipped. This room simply waited, the way a patient waits for a diagnosis.

"Very well," she told it, setting her case down on the workbench Elias had cleared — grudgingly, she suspected, and only after checking twice that she hadn't touched anything else. "Let's see what you've been hiding."

She lit both lamps because one seemed presumptuous and three seemed theatrical, then stood before the clock itself and did what her master had drilled into her across nine years: she looked before she opened anything.

It was a beautiful instrument. That was the first surprise. She had expected something municipal, all function and no vanity, the sort of clock a colonial survey office would order from a catalogue. Instead this was hand-built, case of local walnut darkened to the color of strong tea, dial enamel fine enough to show brush marks under lamplight if you knew to look. The maker's mark near the six o'clock position was worn nearly smooth — someone had rubbed it, deliberately or not — but the *work* couldn't be rubbed out. The proportions sang. The pendulum's suspension was overengineered by half, which was the signature of a craftsman who expected his work to outlive him and wanted it to do so gracefully.

"You didn't build this," she murmured. "You inherited it. And then—"

And then what? That was the question, wasn't it. The child in the lane had said the clock lied at midnight, with the flat certainty children use for facts they shouldn't know. The magistrate's clerk had been very polite about her arrival, and politeness like that always had a bill attached. Her master's letter had contained exactly two instructions separated by an ominous amount of blank paper: *repair the clock; ask nothing about the records room.*

Rosalind Nakamura had spent nine years learning to read mechanisms, and roughly four of those years learning to read people, mostly by watching guild customers lie about how their grandfather clocks had come to be stopped at suspicious hours. People who wanted you not to look at something always arranged the not-looking too carefully. The blank space in the letter was a boarded window. The forbidden rooms list was a boarded window. Even Sable's suspicion, she suspected, was a kind of boarding — planks nailed over whatever he couldn't afford to have seen.

Fine. She was good at windows.

She opened the case.

* * *

Inside, the movement was even finer than the promise of the case — brass wheels bright where they should be bright, dark where decades of honest oil had done honest work, every pivot polished, every jewel seated true. She went through it the way she'd been taught: train by train, wheel by wheel, hands moving on their own while her mind made its inventory. Great wheel, sound. Center wheel, sound. Third wheel, escape wheel —

She stopped.

Her breath fogged in the cold room, a small ghost between her and the mechanism, and she watched it drift aside as though even the air was making room for what she'd found.

Someone had altered the escapement.

Not broken it. Not neglected it. *Altered* it — and recently, within the last year at most, because the screw heads on the pallet assembly showed fresh tool marks under her loupe, bright scratches crossing older ones like handwriting over handwriting. The crutch had been bent a degree and a half off true. A tiny weight — a sliver of lead, no bigger than a grain of barley — had been fixed to one pallet arm with wax. Wax. Not solder, not rivets. Something meant to look like an accident to anyone who glanced, and meant to be removable by anyone who knew.

Rosalind sat back on the work stool and looked at it for a long moment, the way you look at a locked door you've just realized was never really locked.

Then she got out her notebook and did the arithmetic.

A bent crutch of that angle, plus a weight of that mass at that distance from the pivot, would retard the beat — slightly, consistently, invisibly to anyone who wasn't standing inside the case with a loupe and a grudge against imprecision. She ran the numbers twice, then a third time because the answer kept being the same and the same answer three times running stops feeling like coincidence.

Four minutes a night. Give or take a few seconds, depending on temperature and humidity, but four minutes, night after night, week after week. The clock wasn't failing. The clock was being *steered*. Someone had taken a precision instrument and taught it to drift, gently, reliably, toward a falsehood — and had done it with wax and a bent wire, the clockmaker's equivalent of a whispered lie, deniable, invisible, cheap to undo.

Her hands, she noticed, were steady. That surprised her more than the discovery. Somewhere beneath her sternum a small hot coal had settled, the particular heat of a craftsperson confronting vandalism, and it burned away everything shakier.

"Who," she said quietly to the empty room, "did this?"

The draft moved through the room and said nothing, which was, she was beginning to understand, the observatory's native language.

* * *

She worked through the morning, because working was thinking and thinking was dangerous to do standing still. She cleaned what needed cleaning — genuinely needed it; whoever maintained the clock did so lovingly apart from the tampering, oil fresh on every bearing but the ones the alteration had disturbed, as though the saboteur couldn't bring himself to touch more than he must. That detail snagged in her like a splinter. You didn't sabotage a clock you loved. You sabotaged a clock you *needed* to misbehave, and hated yourself for it, apparently, one oiled bearing at a time.

At noon there was a knock — two raps, precise, unmistakably Sable — and the door opened before she answered, because precise men did not wait on invitations they had already decided to grant.

"Progress," he said. It wasn't quite a question. He stood in the doorway with a tray — bread, cheese, tea in a dented tin pot — and set it on the bench with the air of a man performing an obligation he resented finding in his own character. "You've been in there since dawn."

"Clocks don't inspect themselves."

"No," he agreed. "That's rather the problem with them."

He lingered. She felt him hovering at the edge of her peripheral vision the way weather hovers at the edge of a horizon, and she understood that he wanted to ask something and would die before asking it. So she gave him nothing — just lifted the center wheel into the light, checked its teeth against her loupe, hummed four bars of something tuneless, and let the silence do her work for her. Nine years of workshop apprenticeship had taught her that silence was a tool with a fine edge; most people couldn't bear more than a minute of it.

Sable lasted ninety seconds, which she noted with respect.

"The pallets," he said finally. "Are they — original?"

There it was. Delivered flat, eyes on the middle distance, voice level as a spirit gauge. An astronomer asking about escapements with that tone was a man asking whether his crime was visible.

"Original design," Rosalind said, and watched his shoulders drop a quarter inch. "Worn, of course. Everything's worn. That's what time does." She turned and looked at him directly, and let a small, pleasant, professional smile settle onto her face — the smile she used on customers whose grandfather clocks had stopped at suspicious hours. "I'll have it keeping proper time within the week."

Something crossed his face too fast to name. Relief and dread, sharing a coat, pretending they were strangers.

"Proper time," he repeated.

"True time." She tapped the great wheel with one fingernail, a bright little *tink* in the cold air. "A clock has one job, Herr Sable. It can fail at it honestly or succeed at it dishonestly, but it only has the one job. I intend to see it done."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she had the distinct sensation of a man recalculating an orbit — discovering the body he'd been charting had a companion star, hidden, pulling things off course.

"Tea," he said at last, gesturing at the pot as if it were evidence. "It goes bitter otherwise."

"Everything does," said Rosalind, "given long enough."

He left. She counted to a hundred, listening to his footsteps climb the tower stairs, fading into the geometry of the building. Then she took off her coat, rolled her sleeves, and got to work.

* * *

Undoing the alteration took less than an hour. Doing it *right* took until well past dark.

She straightened the crutch by eye and feel alone, no tools that would leave new marks — warming the metal in her hands, easing it back a degree and a half with the patience of a woman persuading rather than forcing. She picked the lead sliver free of the wax, weighed it on her smallest scale (4.2 grains; she wrote it down), and held it in her palm for a while afterward, this tiny gray seed of a lie, wondering whose hand had placed it there. Steady hands, whoever they were. Clockmaker's hands. Or close enough to fool one.

Because that was the other thing, the thing she turned over and over while she worked: the alteration was competent. More than competent. Whoever bent that crutch understood beats and amplitudes well enough to hit four minutes a night — not five, not two, *four*, a chosen number, a designed error. You didn't stumble into that. You calculated it. Which meant somewhere in this frozen town was a person who knew movements the way she did, and had aimed that knowledge at a clock instead of at truth.

She restored the last of the wax to a clean tin, labeled it in her neat hand — *removed from pallet arm, left side, 14 November* — and then sat for a long moment with the tin in her hands, understanding, gradually and completely, what she was holding.

Evidence. Physical, portable, damning evidence of deliberate falsification. And the man upstairs — the man who brought tea and asked about pallets in the voice of a drowning man asking about the waterline — had flinched when she said *proper time.*

If she reported the tampering to the magistrate, the way the guild rules said, the way the summons implied, the way every dutiful apprentice in the empire would — the tin would go into a report, the report would go to the colonial office, and the colonial office, judging by its clerk's silk-smooth courtesy, would lose the tin between desks with the efficiency of long practice. The record would show: clock repaired, cause attributed to wear, matter closed. And whatever the four minutes a night had been *buying* someone — some map, some verdict, some border — the purchase would stand forever, notarized by her own signature.

Or she could say nothing. Keep the tin. Keep the knowledge. Let the clock run true and let whoever had bent the crutch spend their nights wondering why it wouldn't stay bent.

Her master's voice arrived unbidden, dry as pumice: *You are paid to repair instruments, not to judge them.* But her master had also once spent three days rebuilding a church clock that the parish council wanted declared beyond saving, because — he'd said, tightening the last screw — *the council lies, the minutes lie, but the clock keeps God's time and I keep the clock.* She had thought it was a joke. Standing here, in a cold room full of someone else's careful sin, she understood it had been a creed.

"Well," Rosalind said to the movement, to the room, to the small gray seed of lead in its labeled tin, "I suppose I'm complicit now."

The word sat strangely in her mouth. Complicity was supposed to feel like dirt. This felt like a door opening onto a staircase going down — thrilling, dark, and absolutely requiring of her to find out where it led.

She reassembled the clock with a craftsman's tenderness. She set the pendulum swinging and listened to the beat come right — tick-tock, tick-tock, even as a pulse, honest as bread — and the sound filled the stone room like water filling a dry well. On the test dial she let it run an hour and measured its loss: under two seconds. Within tolerance for any instrument alive. Within tolerance for truth.

Then she cleaned her tools, ate the abandoned cheese, poured herself the abandoned tea (cold, bitter, excellent), and wrote the day's entry in her log:

*Movement found in fair condition. Escapement adjusted. Clock restored to standard. Cause of deviation: wear.*

Two words of it were true. The rest was hers to carry.

She blew out one lamp and sat in the half-dark a moment longer, listening to the tick travel up through the tower like a heartbeat in a wall. Four minutes a night, stolen and returned. Somewhere above her, Elias Sable was keeping hours no one had asked for, guarding rooms no one was allowed to enter, in a building full of boarded windows — and now she was inside the conspiracy of silence whether she liked it or not, a co-conspirator with a labeled tin.

Tomorrow night, she thought, the clock would strike midnight on the true hour for the first time in however many months. And everyone in Veymar who had grown used to the lie would hear the difference.

She wondered, snuffing the second lamp, how many of them would flinch — and what it would cost her, and the man upstairs, when they did.
