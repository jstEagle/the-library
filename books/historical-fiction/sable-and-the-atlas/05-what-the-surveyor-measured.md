## 5. What the Surveyor Measured

The clockmaker had been at the observatory for three days when Elias Sable caught himself lying to her.

It was a small lie, the kind that fit in a pocket — he told her the east corridor was closed for damp, which was even true in the technical sense, since everything on this mountain was damp and the east corridor led to the dome stairs where Anselm's plates slept in their oilcloth shroud. But it was still a lie, and Elias had built his whole adult life on the principle that an astronomer who lied about small things would eventually lie about the sky, and then there was no point to him at all.

He stood in the instrument room that evening, listening to her work below. The master clock's case was open, its guts exposed to lamplight, and from two floors up he could hear the small sounds of her craft: the tick of a pick against brass, the soft scrape of a file, once — startlingly — a hummed fragment of some valley song, cut off as if she'd surprised herself with it.

*She'll find it,* he thought. *Of course she'll find it. That's why they sent her.*

Or perhaps not. Perhaps they'd sent her because she was simply the best apprentice the guild had produced in twenty years, and the clock was failing, and the capital wanted its observatory time clean before the annexation papers were signed. Both things could be true. In Elias's experience both things were almost always true, and the truth was less a single line than a bundle of wires, and someone always got shocked sorting them.

He took down the tea tin — the good one, the one Marta had filled before winter — and went downstairs to make his nightly inspection of a man who no longer needed inspecting, because he was dead.

* * *

Anselm Veymar had been the sort of man who whistled while he worked and apologized to instruments when he dropped them. He had arrived in the valley eleven years ago with a government commission, a theodolite older than Elias, and a laugh that made the observatory's thin walls feel briefly like a tavern. The commission said: survey the valley's borders for the new administration's maps. What Anselm actually did, over six patient years, was measure the borders *correctly* — and discover that the annexation maps drawn in the capital were wrong by a margin that mattered. A river bend here. A ridge line there. Fourteen thousand hectares of valley that belonged, by every honest measurement, to the people who had farmed it for three hundred years, and not to the colony that intended to take it.

Elias remembered the night Anselm first showed him the figures. They had been on the dome catwalk, because Anselm liked heights and Elias liked being where Anselm was, and the surveyor had spread his field sheets across the telescope mount like a man laying out cards he already knew were winning.

"Look," Anselm said, tapping the numbers. "Just look. I've checked it four ways. Stellar azimuths, triangulation off the north peak, chained distance. The maps are wrong, Elias. Not mistaken — *wrong*. Someone drew those lines in an office in the capital without ever getting his boots wet."

"Then you'll send your findings and they'll redraw them."

Anselm had looked at him with an expression Elias had never learned to read until much later, after it was too late to need to. It was the expression of a man watching a friend walk toward a cliff in perfect good spirits.

"You're an astronomer," Anselm said gently. "You think the universe wants to be corrected. It doesn't. It just wants to be observed. Men are different. Men want very much *not* to be corrected, and they have soldiers."

"They can't falsify a triangulation."

"No," Anselm agreed. "But they can falsify a surveyor."

Elias had laughed at that. He could still hear his own laugh, bright and stupid, bouncing around the dome under the stars. Eleven months later Anselm fell from the switchback above the mill race, and the magistrate's inquiry ruled it a slip on wet stone, and the same magistrate signed the finding with the same pen he used for fishing licenses, and Elias stopped laughing at things for approximately four years.

* * *

In the present, Rosalind Nakamura accepted the tea with both hands, which struck Elias as either very polite or very cold, and he hadn't yet determined which. She sat on the work stool with her spine straight as a plumb line and blew across the cup in small, economical sips, as though steam were something to be managed rather than enjoyed.

"The third wheel is worn," she said. "And the escapement has been adjusted by someone who didn't know what they were doing."

Elias kept his face doing nothing at all. This was a skill he had practiced for eleven months and was, he suspected, still visibly a skill — like watching a man balance a chair on his chin and pretending it was furniture arrangement.

"Mountain clocks suffer," he said. "Temperature swings. Damp."

"Mm." She sipped. "Damp doesn't file a pallet stone. Damp doesn't move a counterweight by exactly the amount required to lose four minutes a night." She looked at him over the rim of the cup. Her eyes were dark and extremely awake. "Four minutes, Herr Sable. Whoever did it was precise. Precise people don't do things by accident, and they don't do them for free."

"What do you intend to report?"

"That the mechanism was worn and I repaired it." She set the cup down. "That's what I was sent to do. Repair it. My master was very clear about the limits of my commission." Something crossed her face then — quickly, like a bird past a window — and Elias thought: *she knows more than she's saying too.* The mountain was turning into an aviary of secrets, everyone with a bird behind their teeth.

"Good," he said, and heard how the word came out too fast, too relieved, and watched her file that away alongside everything else she filed away. She was going to be a formidable old woman someday. He hoped to be alive to see it, which was a strange hope to have about a stranger, and stranger still given what he was about to do.

Because that was the thing about grief calcifying into vigilance: eventually the vigilance itself became the shape of your days, and one evening you found yourself standing in front of a girl of maybe twenty-two, holding a tea tin, deciding whether to hand her the weight of a dead man's life's work on the strength of a humming habit and steady hands.

"Come with me," Elias said. "There's something you should see before you finish the repair. If you finish the repair wrong, it won't matter how well you do it."

Her eyebrows performed a small, skeptical ascent. "Is this the part where you tell me another room is forbidden?"

"This is the part where I un-forbid one."

* * *

They climbed the dome stairs with lamps, and Elias felt each step the way he always did — the way you feel stairs you've climbed carrying something heavy, even on nights when your hands are empty. At the top, under the great shuttered eye of the roof, he knelt at the base of the pier that held the transit telescope, worked loose two panels of the mahogany cladding, and drew out the oilcloth bundle.

It was heavier than memory. It was always heavier than memory.

Inside were forty-one glass photographic plates, each wrapped in waxed paper, each bearing in silvered emulsion the sky as Anselm had recorded it — and along the margins, in the surveyor's cramped, cheerful hand, the coordinates that undid an empire's paperwork. Star positions measured to fractions of a second of arc, tied to ground stations, tied to boundary stones, tied to the stubborn physical fact of where the valley ended. You could burn paper. You could bury a notebook. But Anselm had written his testimony into the sky itself, fixed against stars that no magistrate could subpoena and no soldier could relocate, and then he had hidden the plates inside the one building in the valley whose purpose was looking upward, entrusted to the one man whose profession was noticing what was true.

"He hid them here," Elias said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "For six years I've checked them twice a season for damp and delamination. I've re-wrapped them eleven times. I have, at various points, talked to them." He paused. "You may write that down if you're keeping notes on the mad astronomer."

Rosalind didn't smile, quite. But something in her shoulders came down half an inch, which from her seemed equivalent to laughter.

"Why show me?" she asked. "Three days ago you gave me a list of rooms I wasn't allowed to enter. Now you're showing me the reason for the list."

"Because tomorrow or the day after, you're going to open the escapement fully, and you're going to find something in it, and I need you to have seen these first. I need you to understand that the clock isn't just a clock." He set the plates between them on the catwalk grating, lamplight swimming in the silvered skies. "Anselm Veymar surveyed this valley honestly, and the honesty killed him, and the plates are all that's left of him except a widow and a boarded house. And the clock—" His throat tightened around it, the way it did every time, grief gone to scar tissue but scar tissue still tears. "The clock was his idea. The losing of four minutes a night. It was a message. As long as the master clock ran slow, the observatory time was wrong, and wrong time meant every official record anchored to it — land transfers, boundary registrations, the annexation schedule itself — was legally questionable. Sloppy. Challengeable. He built doubt into the gears, and he told me: *as long as it loses time, nothing they sign here is solid.*"

"And then he died," Rosalind said quietly.

"And then he died, and I kept it running slow myself, every winding, every adjustment, for eleven months. Lying in brass instead of words. I told myself it was what he wanted." Elias looked up at the shutters, beyond them at the darkness where the stars were keeping their own records regardless. "Then the guild sent you, and I understood the capital had noticed the four minutes and wanted them erased before the paperwork went through. So now you know what your repair means, Fraulein Nakamura. When you correct that clock, you aren't fixing an instrument. You're signing something."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the damaged clock ticked on, arrhythmic, like a heart remembering what it was for.

"I haven't corrected it yet," Rosalind said at last.

Elias turned. "You said—"

"I said I'd found the tampering. I didn't say what I'd done about it." She picked up her teacup, which she had apparently carried all the way up the dome stairs without his noticing, and drank the last cold swallow with the composure of a woman closing a ledger. "I'm an apprentice clockmaker, Herr Sable. I don't sign things. I only repair them. Whether a mechanism runs true or false is a question for whoever owns the truth it keeps." She stood, brushed off her skirt, and looked down at the plates with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life — the look of a precise person measuring precisely how large a thing was. "Forty-one plates. Is there an index?"

"There's a notebook. Half of it burned. His widow has it."

"Then we'll need the other half." She started toward the stairs, then stopped, and without turning around added, in a tone of tremendous casualness that fooled neither of them: "Your tea is terrible, by the way. Tomorrow I'll make it."

She went down into the dark of the stairwell, lamp bobbing, and Elias stood alone under the dome with a dead man's sky in his arms and the unfamiliar, dangerous sensation of not being the only one keeping watch.

Below him, faint through the floorboards, the master clock ticked its crooked count toward midnight — and for the first time in eleven months, Elias Sable realized with a lurch that he had no idea whether he wanted it to strike true.
