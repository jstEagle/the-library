## 6. Four Minutes Past Midnight

Rosalind had told herself she would sleep.

She had gone through all the proper motions of a person intending to sleep. She banked the fire in the little guest room off the clock chamber, set her boots side by side with the toes pointing toward the door — a guild habit, so you could dress in the dark and be running in four seconds — and lay down beneath a quilt that smelled of cedar and someone else's life. She closed her eyes. She counted the seconds between the master clock's ticks as they came up faintly through the floorboards, the way she had done every night since arriving at the observatory, the way other people counted sheep.

Four minutes slow. Every night, four minutes, lost somewhere in the brass like water seeping from a cracked cistern.

Except it wasn't seeping anymore. She had fixed it that evening. She knew exactly what she'd done — she could draw the altered escapement from memory down to the last filed tooth, could tell you which surfaces had been cut by a careful hand and which had been left rough on purpose, rough in the specific way a person roughs something when they want it to look worn rather than tampered. Someone had built that error into the clock deliberately, patiently, the way a forger ages paper. And she had taken it out.

She had not reported it. That was the part that kept her awake. Guild law said every anomaly went into the service ledger, initialled and dated, because an instrument's history was part of its accuracy. Instead she had written *escapement cleaned; minor wear corrected* and pressed her seal into the wax with a steadiness she did not feel.

Below her, through the boards, the tick changed.

Not much. A layperson would have heard nothing at all. But Rosalind had spent eleven nights learning this clock's voice the way one learns a singer's — its slight drag on the fourth beat, its hesitation at the top of the hour, the tiny asthmatic catch where the altered pallet met the altered wheel. The catch was gone now. The count ran clean and even, each second landing on the next like coins into a purse, and lying there in the dark Rosalind thought: *it sounds honest.*

Then she looked at the window, where the first hard stars were out over the valley, and thought: *what have I just said, and to whom?*

She got up. There was no point pretending. She dressed, took the lamp, and went down to sit with the clock, because a clockmaker should be present when her work speaks for the first time — and because, if she was entirely honest with herself, which was a professional habit she couldn't switch off, she wanted to see Elias Sable's face when it struck midnight true for the first time in nearly a year.

* * *

The clock chamber was cold enough that her breath showed. The great case stood against the east wall, dark walnut gone silver at the edges, its face a pale moon behind glass. Elias was already there.

He was sitting on the workbench with his coat on and his hat, absurdly, still on his head, as though he had come in intending only to pass through and had been caught by gravity. He didn't startle when her lamp found him. He'd known she was coming down; she suspected he'd heard her counting seconds through the floorboards the way she'd heard his pacing overhead, two insomniacs keeping separate watches of the same night.

"You fixed it," he said. Not a question.

"Minor wear," Rosalind said. "Corrected."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

He took off the hat finally, turned it in his hands. In lamplight she could see the tiredness carved under his eyes, the particular fatigue of a man who has been standing guard over something alone for so long that company feels like an intrusion and a mercy at once.

"Rosalind." He used her name rarely enough that when he did, it landed. "Do you know what happens tonight at twelve o'clock?"

"The clock strikes. On time. That's rather the point of clocks."

"And everyone in Veymar will know it." He slid off the bench and stood, and she saw that his hands weren't quite steady either, though his voice was. "Eleven months, this town has lived four minutes adrift and been told it was weather. Wear. Altitude. Every train schedule, every market bell, every birth registered and death recorded since last winter happened four minutes inside a lie, and people adjusted, because people adjust. They set their watches back without asking why. And tomorrow morning they'll discover their watches are wrong again — or rather, right again — and they will ask why, and the answer is a girl from the guild with a screwdriver and a clean conscience."

"I have a very good conscience," Rosalind said. "It's my ledger that's dirty."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the place where one kept trying to form and kept being suppressed, like frost on a warm window. "You didn't report it."

"You told me not to."

"I told you to repair the clock. I never told you to lie for me."

"You didn't have to." She set her lamp on the bench beside his and looked up at the pale face of the clock, at the minute hand creeping through the last quarter hour toward the twelve it hadn't touched honestly in eleven months. "Whoever cut that escapement did beautiful work. Whoever ordered it done wanted the town's official record anchored to a falsified time — births, deaths, land surveys, everything timestamped four minutes inside something untrue. I don't know what yet. But I know that when I restored it, I didn't restore a clock. I restored a witness." She turned to him. "So before it starts talking, astronomer — what is it going to say?"

Elias looked at her for a long moment. Outside, wind moved across the dome above them with a sound like a hand brushing velvet.

"Ask me again in the spring," he said quietly. "If we're both still here."

The clock began to gather itself.

They both felt it before they heard it — the weight climbing the train, gear after gear engaging, the whole instrument leaning forward into its purpose. Rosalind's hand found the edge of the workbench without her deciding to put it there. Elias had gone very still, the way he did at the eyepiece, the way she imagined he'd stood the night Anselm died, waiting to see what the sky would do.

Midnight.

The first stroke went through the floorboards and out into the stone of the observatory and down the mountain itself. Twelve strokes, evenly spaced, unhurried, each one landing on the second it belonged to, and Rosalind realized she was holding her breath and made herself breathe, because a clockmaker does not flinch at her own work.

The town flinched for her.

She felt it happen — felt it as surely as she felt the vibration in the floor. Somewhere below, dogs began to bark in a ragged wave, street by street, as the sound rolled down through Veymar. Windows lit. A shutter banged somewhere, then another. And underneath the fading twelfth stroke, thin on the frozen air, came voices — startled, calling house to house, the particular pitch of a town waking to find the ground has shifted under it by exactly four minutes.

"They're frightened," Rosalind said.

"They're remembering," Elias said. "There's a difference. Give them until morning."

* * *

Morning gave them the magistrate.

He arrived at ten, by which time the story had already grown teeth in the town below — Rosalind could hear fragments of it from the courtyard, brought up on the wind with the smell of woodsmoke: the clock had struck true, the ghost of Anselm Brandt walked, no, it was the guild, no, it was a sign, no, it was the government finally telling the truth, which was received with the loudest laughter. By ten the story had three endings and none of them were safe.

Magistrate Corvin Hessler was not what she expected. She had pictured hardness; she got silk. He was a compact, grey man in a good coat, with the courteous, exhausted manner of a functionary who has learned that courtesy is cheaper than force and twice as effective. His gloves matched his hat. His smile arrived precisely on cue and departed precisely on schedule, and Rosalind, who read mechanisms for a living, placed him within seconds: here was a man built like a governor — designed to regulate, to smooth, to deliver power at a constant, non-alarming rate.

"Fraulein Nakamura." He bowed slightly. "The guild sends us its best, I see. Master Aldous spoke highly of your work in his letter."

"My master is generous with words he hasn't verified," Rosalind said, and watched the smile hold perfectly while something behind it recalculated.

Hessler turned the smile on Elias, who had come down to receive him with the wary formality of a host expecting a search. "Herr Sable. You're well? The mountain agrees with you, I think — you've colour."

"The mountain agrees with everyone," Elias said. "That's why it's a mountain."

"Quite." Hessler removed his gloves finger by finger, laying them over his arm like a man settling in, though he settled nowhere. "I won't take much of your time. I've come about the clock."

"It keeps excellent time," Rosalind said. "As of last night."

"So I'm told." He examined the ceiling, the windows, the instruments, with the idle thoroughness of a buyer. "Veymar was alarmed. Grown men, Fraulein, respectable men, telling me their watches lied to them all winter. One fellow threw his grandfather clock into the square." He sighed at the folly of provincials. "Naturally I reassured them. Errors of maintenance, I said. The altitude, I said. But then I thought — I ought to come and ask the expert directly. So." He folded his hands. "Does the master clock now keep official time?"

And there it was — the question laid out flat between them, polite as an invitation and weighted like a trapdoor. *Official time.* Not accurate time. Not true time. Official. As though the hours themselves required a signature to be legal.

Rosalind felt Elias go still beside her, the way the air goes still before snow slides.

She considered the question the way she'd consider a movement that wouldn't run: not fighting it, understanding it. Hessler wasn't really asking whether the clock was correct. He was asking whether she would certify the correction — whether the guild's authority now stood behind the true hour, publicly, on the record, where it could be held accountable, or punished, or quietly reversed. Her repair had already been heard all through the valley as a declaration. Now its author was being asked to sign it.

"Official time," Rosalind said, "is whatever the master clock says. That's what makes it official. That has been the arrangement between the guild and every settlement on this line since before either of us was born." She smiled at him with perfect craftsmanship. "The clock doesn't take instructions from me, Herr Magistrate. It takes them from the earth's rotation. If you have a complaint about that, you're welcome to write to the sky. I believe they have offices everywhere."

Beside her, Elias produced a small cough that fooled nobody.

Hessler's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Charming. But you'll understand my position. A clock that changes its mind unsettles people. Records depend on consistency. Births, deaths, transfers of title—" the pause was a hair too long, "—surveys. If the clock is to strike differently than it did through the winter, the administration will want to know the change was authorized."

"Authorized by whom?"

"By whoever is competent to authorize it."

"Then you'll want the guild," Rosalind said. "Which is me, until spring. And my authorization is entered in the service ledger, dated and sealed, available for inspection any time you like." Let him come and read *minor wear corrected*. Let him try to make a crime out of a clean escarpment of brass. "Would you like to see it?"

For the first time, Hessler's composure moved — a fractional delay before his answer, a blink held a moment past its natural term. She had offered him the one thing he didn't want: paperwork. Paperwork was evidence, and evidence had a way of surviving the people it inconvenienced.

"Not necessary," he said smoothly. "I trust the guild's bookkeeping." He retrieved his gloves and worked them on, finger by finger, and paused at the door with the light behind him so that his face was harder to read than any dial. "One more thing, purely as a neighbour. Mountain winters close the bridge early some years. Earlier than travellers expect." He let that sit. "I should hate for the guild's apprentice to be stranded far from her masters. Do give it thought, Fraulein. Winter gives one time to reconsider one's... repairs."

The door closed behind him with the soft click of well-oiled hinges.

Rosalind stood listening to the hoofbeats fade down the switchbacks, and understood, with the cold clarity of a balance wheel settling, exactly what had just happened. She had thought she'd repaired a clock. She had thought she'd kept a secret. But the town had heard the twelve strokes and read them as a message, and Hessler had climbed the mountain to read it back to her — and now she was not a clockmaker who had fixed a mechanism. She was a signatory. Whatever the four minutes had been hiding, her name was now on the other side of it, in wax, dated.

"Well," she said. "That went well."

Elias was looking at her with an expression she hadn't seen on him before — stripped of suspicion, stripped even of caution, just a bare and startled gratitude, the look of a man who has been holding a door shut alone for a year and turns to find someone else's shoulder against it.

"You didn't have to do that," he said.

"Yes, I did." She picked up Hessler's abandoned teacup, sniffed it out of professional habit, and grimaced. "Your tea is worse than mine, by the way. We'll fix that too." She carried the cup to the window. Below, Veymar smoked peacefully in the morning light, chimneys straight, streets quiet, a town wearing its alarm like a coat buttoned wrong. "How long until the bridge closes?"

Elias came to stand beside her. When he answered, his voice had gone quiet and flat, the voice he used for readings he didn't like.

"The first real snow usually comes within the fortnight. After that, nothing crosses until thaw." He paused. "Whatever Hessler plans, he'll want it done before the bridge closes. Or after it opens."

Rosalind watched the road down the mountain, empty and brown and temporary, and thought about ledgers and seals and the weight of a signature in wax, and about how a person could cross a bridge in one direction thinking she was carrying tools, and find, too late, that the bridge had opinions about return journeys.

"Then we have a fortnight," she said, "to decide what we're going to be when the snow lands."

Above them, muffled by floors and stone, the master clock ticked on — steady, even, honest — keeping the only kind of time that needed no one's authorization, and waiting, with the patience of all good instruments, to see what its keepers would do next.
