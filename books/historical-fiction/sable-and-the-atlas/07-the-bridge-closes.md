## 7. The Bridge Closes

The first snow did not fall so much as arrive — Rosalind woke to a silence she recognized from her childhood in the mountains above the guild town, the particular hush of air that has decided to hold something back. She lay in her narrow bed in the observatory's guest room and listened to it, the way one listens to a movement that has stopped ticking for no apparent reason. Nothing was wrong. Everything was about to be.

She dressed quickly and went to the east window, where the valley opened below Veymar like a palm uncurling. The river ran grey between its banks, and the bridge — the slender iron span that connected the observatory road to everything else in the world — wore a thin white pelt of snow along its rails. Above it, clouds the color of wet slate were stacking themselves against the pass like plates on a shelf.

"Snow," said a voice behind her, and she turned to find Elias Sable in the doorway holding two cups of tea, as though he had been standing there long enough to know what she would want before she wanted it. "I thought you should see it come down."

"I've seen snow," Rosalind said, taking the cup anyway.

"Not this snow." He set his own cup on the sill and nodded at the bridge. "That's the closing snow. It'll fall for three days, maybe four, and when it stops, the bridge will be shut until thaw. The courier will make one last run tomorrow morning, if he can, and then nothing goes up or down that road until April. No post. No wagons. No letters." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes that she could not read — relief or dread, or both wearing the same coat. "You may still catch him out, if you mean to go."

Rosalind looked at the bridge. She had been at the observatory eleven days. The master clock ran true now, kept true by her hands twice daily; the escapement she had mended sat inside its case like a secret she carried under her ribs. She had not reported the tampering. She had not told anyone why four minutes a night had been shaved off the town's time, or by whom, or how neatly it had been done — by someone who understood gears the way she did, which was a very short list indeed.

She thought of the guild workshop, of Master Aldous's warning: *repair the clock, ask nothing about the records room*. She thought of the letter she would have to write him. She thought — and was surprised by how easily the thought came — of tea at the edge of the dome, of star charts annotated in two different hands now, of the way Elias said her name as though checking it against an instrument reading.

"I don't mean to go," she heard herself say.

Elias did not smile, but something in his shoulders loosened, a tension released by a quarter-turn, the way a mainspring settles. "Then you'd better bring your coat to breakfast," he said. "The courier leaves at seven, and he'll want to take a good look at the woman choosing winter over the lowlands. He'll tell the whole valley by noon."

* * *

Tomas the courier was sixty years old and built like a man who had argued with weather his entire life and won most rounds on points. He stood in the observatory courtyard stamping his boots, mule loaded, while the first fat flakes came down around him with the unhurried confidence of a thing that has all season to finish its work.

"So you're staying," he said, looking Rosalind up and down. "Smart. Or foolish. Hard to say which till spring." He grinned, showing teeth worn round as old coins. "Last chance, miss. Once I'm across, the mountain keeps you."

"The mountain is welcome to me," Rosalind said, "for the duration."

"Mm." He dug into his satchel — a battered leather thing that had clearly been repaired more times than it had been replaced — and drew out a bundle of sealed papers tied with official cord. "Then do me a kindness and give these to the astronomer. And don't ask me what they are, because I don't know, and the man who gave them to me made a face like a closed door when he handed them over."

Rosalind took the bundle. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. The wax seals were the magistrate's — she had seen the impression often enough these past days, on invitations that were not quite invitations — but the handwriting on the outer wrapping was unfamiliar, cramped and official, the hand of someone paid by the page.

"Orders?" she asked.

"Sealed ones," Tomas said cheerfully. "Which is a polite way of saying none of my business, and if I'm wise, none of yours either. Though between us" — he leaned closer, lowering his voice beneath the rising wind — "there are men in the valley who'd pay good money to know what crosses that bridge in winter. And men who'd pay more to make sure certain things never cross back."

"That sounds ominous."

"That sounds like Veymar," he said. "Ominous is just weather here. You get used to dressing for it." He swung himself onto the mule with a creak of joints and old leather, gathered the reins, and then paused. "Miss Nakamura. Clockmaker."

"Yes?"

"You fixed the big clock, they say. Made it keep honest time again." His eyes, small and bright as a bird's, held hers. "There's folk down-valley who liked it better the old way. Four minutes slow. Four minutes is a long time, if you're using them to bury something." He touched his cap. "Keep well. Keep warm. Keep — " he glanced once at the clock tower's face, pale through the falling snow — "keep counting."

And he was gone, mule and satchel and sealed orders, down the switchbacks into the white, the last thread connecting the observatory to the world paying itself out behind him until the snow took even that.

* * *

From the dome, Elias watched the courier descend until the road swallowed him, and felt the peculiar double sensation he always felt at the closing of the bridge: grief and safety arriving together, like twins who could not be separated without killing both.

Below, Veymar was putting itself away for winter. He could see smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless cold, see the dark rectangles of shuttered windows multiplying street by street. Somewhere down there was Anselm's house with its boarded windows. Somewhere down there Marta Anselm lived alone with a grief the whole town had agreed, in that silent cowardly way towns have, not to look at directly. And somewhere down there, Elias knew, men whose names appeared on the sealed orders in his hand were warming their hands over their stoves and congratulating themselves that the mountain — and its inconveniently honest clock — would be inaccessible until spring.

He broke the seals in the warmth of the instrument room, because a seal is only ever a lock for people who intend to obey it.

The first order was administrative: the observatory's winter allotment, reduced. Fuel, reduced. The survey instruments requisitioned for "inventory in the capital" — which meant they would sit in a warehouse in the capital until the annexation maps were beyond dispute, and then be quietly lost.

The second order was shorter, and colder.

*Regarding the master clock: the Guild has been petitioned to certify the mechanism 'as found' upon completion of repairs, per standard practice. Ensure the visiting technician completes her certification without irregularity. Her term of residence concludes with the bridge reopening.*

*As found,* Elias read, and almost laughed. *As found* — meaning four minutes slow, meaning the falsified time, meaning every map drawn against that falsified time anchored and approved. They wanted Rosalind's signature on the lie. That was the elegance of it, he thought, with the cold admiration he reserved for well-made terrible things: they didn't need to touch the wheel. They only needed the guild's own apprentice to bless it, and then no one could ever claim the record had been coerced.

He fed the orders to the stove and watched the seals blister and go black.

Upstairs, faintly, through two floors of timber and stone, he could hear the master clock striking the hour — striking truly now, each note landing exactly where it belonged. Eleven strokes. In the old corrupted time, it would have struck eleven four minutes late, and the town would have gone on setting its appointments, its court sessions, its land registrations, to a lie so gentle nobody noticed it was one.

Four minutes. Anselm had died for less, Elias thought. Had died for exactly that, in fact — for measurements that disagreed with maps by margins smaller than four minutes, and been called accidental for it.

The snow thickened against the dome glass. Elias stood a long while in the instrument room, listening to the house settle around him, feeling the walls draw in — not with menace, exactly. With intention. A case around a movement. He had taken clocks apart enough times, in the years since Anselm, to know the feeling from the inside: everything enclosed, everything positioned, every gear held precisely where the design required it to be.

The difference, he reminded himself, was that in a clock, the escapement chose when things moved.

He went upstairs to find out whether the new clockmaker had understood that yet.

* * *

Magistrate Corven read the confirmation of the courier's departure at his desk by lamplight, snow ticking softly against his study window like a patient clerk.

Across the valley, the bridge was already invisible — a white absence where iron used to be. He had stood on his own porch an hour ago and looked at it, and felt the sensation he had learned, over eleven years in Veymar, to call prudence rather than relief: the mountain sealed. The witnesses contained. The clockmaker — and Corven had read her file twice, a guild-trained girl, precise, ambitious, recommended by Aldous himself — safely snowed in among people who understood discretion.

It was tidy. Corven valued tidiness the way other men valued courage.

And yet.

He poured himself the small evening glass of plum brandy he permitted himself and did not drink it, and considered the feeling he could not name. It had arrived with the courier's departure and refused to leave. It was not guilt — he had arranged his conscience years ago, the way one arranges a room: certain doors kept shut, certain windows curtained, nothing examined too closely in direct light. He had signed the ruling on Anselm's death because the alternative was ruin, and ruin had a wife and children in it, and a man could live with a shut door far longer than his family could live without bread. He knew exactly what he was. He had inventoried himself thoroughly and found the total acceptable.

No, the feeling was not guilt. It was the feeling of a man who has locked a door for the eleventh time and hears, for the first time, a faint sound on the other side of it.

The clockmaker. She had repaired the clock without reporting the tampering — oh, yes, Corven knew about the tampering; Corven had signed the order authorizing it, four years ago, in the same cramped official hand that had crossed the bridge today. Four minutes a night, a drift so slow no farmer would notice, no clerk would question, and every survey benchmark in the valley quietly re-anchored to a time that agreed with the annexation maps. It had been beautifully done. It had been done, in fact, by the same craftsman who had built the tampering into the escapement itself, and Corven had sometimes wondered, in the sleepless arithmetic of three in the morning, whether the man who did such elegant work might not also have done something else with it — some second purpose hidden in the brass, the way a smuggler hides papers in a false bottom —

But that was three-in-the-morning thinking, and it was barely nine.

He drank the brandy. Outside, the snow fell on the bridge, on the boarded house, on the graves, on everything equally, which was the closest thing to justice the valley offered.

In spring, the clockmaker would certify the mechanism *as found*, would go down the mountain with her signature drying on the lie, and the file on Veymar would close forever. He told himself this the way he told himself many things, firmly and in complete sentences.

The sound on the other side of the door continued anyway.

* * *

By evening the snow had erased the world down to the observatory's own dimensions, and Rosalind discovered what a small universe felt like from the inside.

The building contracted around her pleasantly at first — firelight, tea, Elias explaining the meridian circle with chalk-dusted fingers, the storm booming against the dome like a giant testing the lid of a box. But by the third day she began to feel it differently. Every corridor led somewhere she already knew. Every conversation circled back to subjects they had nearly finished. The guest room, the kitchen, the instrument room, the dome: four chambers, and herself the balance wheel swinging between them, over and over, the same arc, the same arc.

On the fourth evening she found Elias in the library attempting to mend a chair with more conviction than skill, and stood in the doorway watching him fight the joinery.

"You're going to split that," she said.

"It's already split."

"You're going to split it *more*, which is somehow worse." She crossed the room, took the chisel from him, and set it aside. "Hold the rail flush. Let the glue do the work. Glue is patient; you're not."

"I'm extremely patient," Elias said. "I watch stars form. It takes millions of years and I don't complain once."

"You complain about the tea."

"The tea is a separate grievance." But he held the rail flush as instructed, and after a moment the joint came together with a satisfying whisper, and they stood with their hands overlapping on the wood for slightly longer than joinery required.

"Elias," Rosalind said, to the chair, because it was easier than saying it to his face. "What happens in spring?"

He was quiet long enough that she looked up. Snow-light came through the window and laid a pale stripe across half his face, leaving the other half unreadable.

"In spring," he said carefully, "the inspectors come. Officially to verify the clock. Unofficially—" He stopped. Started again. "There are records in this observatory that contradict the annexation maps. If those records survive the winter, the maps fail, and certain men in the capital lose a great deal of money and standing. So in spring, the records will be inspected very thoroughly, and found, I expect, to have been damaged or lost, and everyone will express regret."

"And the clock?"

"The clock will be certified *as found*." His voice had gone flat and precise, a man reading coordinates. "Four minutes slow. Anchoring the maps. With your signature."

Rosalind let go of the chair. She walked to the window, where the valley had become a single unbroken white field, featureless, directionless — a page with nothing written on it yet, or a page with everything written on it and covered over.

"They sent me here to sign that," she said slowly. "Master Aldous. My own guild."

"Or they sent you here believing the job was honest, and someone above Aldous intends to use you." Elias came to stand beside her. Their reflections floated in the dark glass, ghostly and doubled. "There's a difference. I've watched enough men in this valley to know that the machinery of a cover-up doesn't require villains. It only requires people doing their precise, competent jobs without asking what the whole mechanism is *for*."

"That's what my master warned me not to do. Ask."

"And did you?"

Rosalind was silent. In her mind's eye the escapement lay open under her loupe, the altered pallets gleaming, the four stolen minutes ticking away night after night — and beneath the alteration, so fine she had almost missed it, a pattern in the wheel's engraving that she had told herself was decorative. She had not told Elias about the tampering. She had not told him about the pattern. She was carrying both secrets the way one carries a full cup across a crowded room, and the room was getting smaller every day the snow fell.

"I asked the clock," she said finally. "The clock answered. I'm still translating."

Elias turned to look at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly, the way one acknowledges a reading confirmed by a second instrument.

"Then we have all winter," he said.

Outside, the snow went on falling with the patience of something that intended to keep them exactly where they were — and Rosalind, pressing her palm flat against the cold glass, felt for the first time that perhaps the mountain had not trapped her at all.

Perhaps it had *kept* her, the way one keeps a thing that must not be lost.

And in the tower overhead, unwitnessed, the master clock counted out the hour — true, stubborn, and waiting.
