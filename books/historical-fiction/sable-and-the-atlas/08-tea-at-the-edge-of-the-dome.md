## 8. Tea at the Edge of the Dome

### Rosalind

The snow began on a Thursday, which Rosalind knew because she had wound her traveling watch against the master clock that morning and written the discrepancy in her log. By Friday the bridge was a rumor. By Saturday it was a white scar across a black gorge, and the courier who might have carried her back down the mountain had gone without her, leaving nothing but boot-prints filling up like small graves.

She stood at the observatory's east window with her tea going cold and watched the valley disappear.

"Well," said Elias Sable behind her, "I hope you like barley tea."

She turned. He was holding two cups with the careful awkwardness of a man who had not offered anyone anything in years and was out of practice. The astronomer kept his beard trimmed but his cuffs frayed, and he had the particular posture of someone perpetually listening for a sound no one else could hear yet.

"I've survived worse," Rosalind said, taking the cup. "The guild canteen served something in my second year we were told was tea. We were never told what else."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It was a warning. I'm passing it on."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where one might be considering construction. He nodded toward the spiral stair. "The dome is warmest after sundown. The stone holds the day. If you're going to be trapped here until the thaw, you may as well be trapped somewhere with a view."

She should have declined. She was, after all, an employee of an institution whose interests and his were — she suspected — not aligned, and a prudent clockmaker would have taken her tea in her room behind a locked door, as her master would certainly have advised.

Rosalind Nakamura followed him up the stairs.

* * *

### Elias

He had prepared the dome for company the way a man prepares a lie: thoroughly, and then over-thoroughly, and then with such completeness that the effort itself became conspicuous. Anselm's plates were under the floor plate beneath the meridian circle, beneath three months of dust he had been careful not to disturb too evenly. The field notebook — Marta's, still hidden where it had always been — was in the wall cavity behind the star catalogues. Everything incriminating was invisible.

Which was why it unnerved him that within ten minutes of sitting down, the clockmaker looked straight at the shelf of catalogues and said, "That third volume sticks out by the width of a fingernail. Has done all week."

"The building settles," Elias said.

"In winter?"

"Especially in winter."

She accepted this with a nod that fooled neither of them, and he understood then that the next four months would be a long, polite war conducted over tea, and that some traitorous part of him had already begun looking forward to it.

He set up the telescope instead of answering further questions, because a man with his eye to an eyepiece cannot be interrogated, and aimed it at the first stars coming out over the eastern ridge.

"Clockmaker," he said. "What do you know about latitude?"

"How to find it from a clock and the sun. It's in the examinations."

"And longitude?"

"That one they teach last, and badly." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "Why? Planning a voyage? The nearest sea is nine days away and frozen."

"I'm planning accuracy." He stepped back from the eyepiece. "Look."

She bent to the scope with the economy of motion he'd already come to associate with her — no wasted gestures, hands never hovering, always arriving. He watched her face while she watched the sky. Most people looking through a telescope performed wonder. She did arithmetic.

"Polaris," she said. "You're checking the polar alignment."

"I check it every clear night. Have done for six years."

"That's either devotion or insomnia."

"Both run in my family."

* * *

### Rosalind

The dome smelled of cold iron and lamp oil and, faintly, of barley tea reheated too many times. It was, Rosalind decided, the most honest room she had ever stood in. Every instrument in it admitted what it was for.

They had fallen into a rhythm over the past week — if a thing repeated three times it became a rhythm, and this was its fourth evening. He would work the telescope; she would sit on the observing chair with her mending or her logbook; they would talk about everything except the two subjects that mattered, which were the records room and the four minutes, and by talking around them with great skill they managed to say a great deal about both.

Tonight he abandoned the eyepiece entirely and unrolled a chart across the worktable, weighting the corners with four brass objects that she recognized, professionally, as parts of a transit instrument that would never function again in that configuration.

"Anselm Voss made this," he said. "The surveyor. Before the accident."

So they were saying *it* tonight. Rosalind kept her voice level. "I know the name. His house has boarded windows."

"He measured the valley. Properly — triangulated off the peaks, verified against stellar observations, the way the old school taught it. Not the way the new maps do it." Elias's finger moved along the chart's edge, where a border line ran with the confidence of official ink. "The annexation maps put the boundary at the river bend. Anselm's coordinates put it eleven kilometers east."

"Eleven kilometers is a great deal of valley."

"It's a great deal of mine. Timber. A mill. Forty households who woke up one morning inside someone else's empire because a map said so." He smoothed the chart with his palm, once, as though it could feel it. "His measurements were correct. I checked them myself, night after night, against these stars. The same stars he used. That's the beauty of it — the sky doesn't take sides. Polaris doesn't care whose flag is on the church."

Rosalind looked at the chart, and at the man, and felt the familiar guild-trained caution war with something less disciplined. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you repaired my clock."

"Because I repaired your clock?"

"Four minutes a night, for two years, and you fixed it without asking me why, and without telling anyone." He met her eyes. "A person does that for one of two reasons. Either she didn't notice, which is impossible, because you're the sort of person who notices a book sticking out a fingernail's width. Or she noticed and chose silence."

Rosalind drank her tea to buy a moment. It had gone lukewarm and bitter. "Or," she said finally, "she judged it wasn't her business. Clocks are my business. What they're used to time is yours."

"That's a very clean line."

"I draw clean lines. It's the trade."

He almost laughed — she saw it caught and swallowed, a fish released back into the pond. "Then let me muddy the water a little." He pulled the sticking catalogue off the shelf, and from the gap behind it drew out a slim notebook, its edges charred, its cover warped by heat.

Her heart did something unscheduled. "Where—"

"Not here. And not tonight." He put it back. "But you should know it exists, and that someday soon I'll need someone who can read a mechanism the way I can read a sky. When that day comes, I'd rather ask a friend than a stranger."

"We're not friends," Rosalind said. "We're a hostage and a jailer."

"You have the roles backwards."

"Do I?" She gathered her mending. At the stair door she paused, because fair was fair. "The tea's better than the guild canteen's. That's not friendship. But it's a start."

* * *

### Elias

After she left, Elias sat in the dome for a long time with the charts and the cooling lamp, doing the astronomy of his own position.

He had shown her the chart. Not the notebook, not the plates, not the escapement — but the chart, which was itself enough to hang them both if the magistrate's ear were bent the right way. He turned the decision over and found, to his irritation, that he could not locate the moment he had made it. Somewhere between the barley tea and her noticing the book, the question *can I trust her* had quietly become *when*, and he resented the sleight of hand.

Six years alone on this mountain teaches a man his own habits the way frost teaches a windowpane its patterns. Elias Sable knew himself to be suspicious, methodical, and — since Anselm died — armored to the teeth. He had planned to remain so until the thaw carried him or the truth out, whichever came first.

And now there was a woman on his observing chair who held her teacup like a precision instrument and drew lines so clean they cut, and he had handed her a map worth killing for because she'd fixed a clock and kept quiet about it.

*Standards are slipping,* he told himself sternly.

But when she came back the next evening — and she came back, fourth night becoming fifth — he found he had saved her the chair by the lamp, and the good tea, and had even, in a fit of weakness he chose not to examine, baked bread.

* * *

### Rosalind

On the ninth evening, snowed-in and restless, Rosalind brought her tools up to the dome and found Elias trying to fix the shutter gear with a wrench and a prayer.

"Stop," she said. "You're stripping it."

"It's stuck."

"It's *timid*. There's a difference." She knelt, took the smallest driver from her case, and had the gear free in four minutes flat. "Everything in this building is either over-tightened or neglected. You astronomers are extremists."

"We work nights. Extremism comes with the hours."

She held the gear up to the lamplight, and he watched her read it — really read it, the wear patterns, the old repairs, the story of a machine's life told in brass — and thought, not for the first time, that she was wasted on clocks. Or perhaps that clocks were elevated by her. He hadn't decided.

"This was replaced," she said. "Different alloy, maybe eight years ago. Cheap work. Guild rejects' bench, by the file marks."

"The previous director was a man of economy."

"Your previous director was a man of theft," she said mildly, fitting the gear back. "This cost half what it should and will fail twice as soon. Economy is just theft with good manners."

Elias laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled them both. Rosalind sat back on her heels looking pleased and slightly alarmed, the way one does upon provoking a landslide.

"You should do that more often," she said.

"What, laugh?"

"Maintain. It's the same principle. Parts seize up."

"I'll add it to the maintenance schedule." He fetched the tea. "Between the polar alignment and the insomnia."

They sat shoulder to shoulder at the worktable then, not quite touching, the chart between them and the notebook's existence between them and the four minutes between them, none of it spoken of. Outside, the wind rearranged the snow against the dome. Inside, she showed him how to true a bent arbor using nothing but a lamp flame and patience, and he showed her how to find due south from any star in the winter sky, and both of them pretended, with tremendous discipline, that they were not learning each other instead.

At midnight the master clock struck below them, twelve clean notes rolling down through the floor.

Rosalind counted them with her eyes closed. "It's keeping perfect time now."

"Yes," Elias said quietly. "The whole valley can hear it again."

"That's what frightens me," she said, before she could stop herself.

He didn't ask. That was the thing about him — he never pried, he only left doors open, and waited to see if you'd walk through. She picked up her mending and did not walk through, and he poured more tea, and the storm sealed another layer onto the world outside.

* * *

### Elias

The fifteenth evening, she asked about Anselm directly.

Not the death — he could see her deciding, visibly, not to ask about the death, and he loved her a little for the mercy of it, though he filed that particular word away in a drawer marked *do not open until spring.* She asked instead: "Was he any good? As an observer?"

"One of the best I ever saw. He'd hold a star field in his head overnight and come back at dawn knowing which point of light had moved. No notes. Just memory." Elias turned his cup slowly. "He used to say instruments forget, but observers remember. I argued with him. Said it was backwards."

"And now?"

"Now I think we were both right, and the argument was the best part."

She smiled into her tea. The lamp between them had burned low, and in that light the sharpness in her face went soft at the edges, and Elias thought: *you are in a great deal of trouble, astronomer.*

"Tomorrow," she said, standing, brushing biscuit crumbs from her skirt with two efficient strokes, "I want to see the transit instrument properly assembled. If I'm to be snowbound with your observatory, I intend to inspect it."

"If you find fault—"

"I'll find forty. I'll tell you five. A clockmaker learns mercy the way everyone does — by practicing."

She went down the stairs, and her footsteps faded into the tick of the house, and Elias sat alone under the dome with the stars wheeling overhead in their ancient, incorruptible order.

Then he heard her stop halfway down, and her voice came back up the stairwell, light and casual and not casual at all:

"Oh — Elias. A sled came up from the village this afternoon while we were working. Mail pouch. There's a letter in it with the guild seal, addressed to me."

The words landed in the quiet dome like a dropped weight on a delicate movement.

"I haven't opened it yet," Rosalind called up. "I thought — tomorrow. With daylight."

Elias looked at the chart on the table, at the border drawn in confident ink eleven kilometers from the truth, and thought of everything a letter with a guild seal might instruct a clockmaker to certify, and sign, and carry back down the mountain when the thaw came.

"Daylight," he called back, steady as a level bubble. "It keeps."
