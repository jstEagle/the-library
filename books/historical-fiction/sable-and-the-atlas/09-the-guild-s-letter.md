## 9. The Guild's Letter

The last sled came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing in particular except that Tuesdays were the days the observatory's cook, Frau Brenner, made plum dumplings, and so the whole mountain smelled of sugar and butter when the courier shouted up from the switchback that the bridge would close behind him by nightfall.

Rosalind heard it from the workshop, where she had spent three weeks now rebuilding the strike train to a tolerance her master, old Aldric Voss, would have called adequate and then silently improved upon if he'd been standing behind her. She set down her tweezers. She listened to the bells of the sled team, the crunch of runners, the voice of a man who was already halfway to being a rumor — the last link between Veymar and the world until spring.

Then she ran. She was not a girl who ran; she was a girl who walked precisely, having been taught that haste was the enemy of fine work. But she took the stairs two at a time and arrived in the entrance hall with her breath clouding and her collar askew, just as Frau Brenner pressed a canvas-wrapped packet into the courier's gloved hands for the return journey.

"Anything come?" Rosalind asked.

The courier looked at her. He was young, wind-burned, with the particular blankness of a man carrying sealed things he has been paid not to wonder about. "For the magistrate, mostly," he said. "One for the astronomer." He dug into his satchel and produced a smaller envelope, stiff and gray, bearing a wax seal she knew better than her own heartbeat: the interlocked gears of the Horological Guild of Vell, and beneath them, in Master Voss's cramped hand, *Nakamura*.

Her name. Her whole name, not *apprentice*, which meant he was angry, or afraid, or both.

"Thank you," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her.

She did not open it in the hall. She carried it upstairs like a coal she didn't want to drop, past the corridor of forbidden doors Elias had enumerated on her first day — records room, survey archive, the magistrate's office wing — and into her small room under the eaves where the window framed the white shoulder of the pass. The pass. Even as she sat on the narrow bed, even as she turned the envelope over twice, three times, she could see the weather stacking itself against the peaks like plates on a tray.

The last sled. The last post. Whatever the letter said, there would be no answer sent back this season. Whatever the letter asked, she would have all winter to live inside the asking.

She broke the seal.

*Apprentice Nakamura,* it began — ah, so it was apprentice after all; the full name on the outside had merely been formality for the postal clerks — *you are instructed to complete your assessment of the observatory's master clock and certify it as found. Note well: as found. No alterations, corrections, or improvements are to be entered on the certificate. The Guild requires confirmation only that the instrument is serviceable and its timekeeping consistent with official record.*

*You will recall that the clock's regulation was adjusted by Guild authority prior to your departure. Any deviation you may observe from standard practice is therefore authorized and must not be reported, corrected, or discussed.*

*Complete the certification before the thaw. Return with the first reliable crossing.*

*V.*

Rosalind read it four times. The fourth time was not because the words were difficult — they were perfectly clear, insultingly clear — but because she kept hoping the letters would rearrange themselves into something else.

*As found.*

As found meant: with the escapement tampered. As found meant: losing four minutes every night, four minutes bleeding out of the valley's official time like a slow leak in a pipe, and every document timestamped by that clock — births registered late, deaths registered early, boundary surveys anchored to observations logged at hours that never truly occurred — quietly endorsed by her signature. Certifying the clock as found was not a statement about brass and steel. It was a statement that the maps were true. That Anselm's measurements, wherever they disagreed with the annexation lines, were the errors. That a dead man's life's work was a rounding mistake.

She thought of what Elias had told her, carefully, in the dome, with the telescope ticking softly overhead as it tracked some patient star. *Anselm charted the valley the way it actually is. The new administration needs the valley to be something else. A clock that lies makes the lying legal.* He hadn't said it that neatly. He'd said it the way astronomers say everything, one coordinate at a time, letting her assemble the shape herself. But that was the shape.

And now the Guild — her Guild, the guild that had taken in a half-orphaned girl with quick fingers and no family name worth pronouncing, that had fed her and trained her and taught her that an honest mechanism was the nearest thing to an honest soul — wanted her signature on the lie.

*Adjusted by Guild authority prior to your departure.* She almost laughed. She had been in the workshop when the order came through, had watched Master Voss read it, had seen him go gray around the mouth and say only, "Pack your tools, child. You're going up the mountain." So the instruction to falsify had come down from above him too. Someone in the capital wanted this certificate, and had reached all the way down the chain to reach her hands.

That was the part that frightened her most, when she let herself think it plainly: not that the letter lied, but how far up the lie went, and how small she was at the bottom of it.

* * *

She did not go down to dinner. She told herself she was being industrious, that there was cleaning to do on the second wheel, that plum dumplings could wait, and she sat at her bench under the lamp and cleaned parts that were already clean, which any guildsman would tell you is what a clockmaker does instead of praying.

At half past eight — true half past eight, the pocket watch she trusted more than the building around her — someone knocked.

"If you've brought dumplings," she said, "the answer is yes."

"It's tea." Elias came in without waiting, because by three weeks' acquaintance they had arrived at the stage where knocking was ceremonial. He had a way of entering rooms like a man stepping onto a ledge he'd already measured — no wasted motion, no false confidence either. He set two cups on the corner of her bench, having cleared the corner first, having noticed which tools were laid out in working order and arranged around them rather than across them. She had stopped being startled by this somewhere in week two. She had not stopped noticing it.

"You missed dinner," he said.

"I wasn't hungry."

"You're always hungry. You ate four rolls yesterday and apologized to none of them." He poured the tea. "Frau Brenner says you received post."

Word traveled fast in a building with forty residents and one corridor. Rosalind wrapped her hands around the cup. The warmth felt borrowed, temporary, like everything on this mountain. "From my master."

"And?"

"He wants me to certify the clock as found."

Elias went very still. It was a different stillness than other people's; most people froze all at once, like struck animals. He stilled in sequence, she had learned — hands first, then shoulders, then face — as though he were shutting a house down room by room. "As found," he repeated.

"As found. No corrections. No improvements. Nothing 'reported, corrected, or discussed.'" She recited it flatly, the way one reads a specification sheet. "He says the regulation was authorized by the Guild."

"Was it?"

"I don't know. I was there when the summons came and nobody said anything about any adjustment. But I'm an apprentice. Apprentices aren't told why. Apprentices are told *how* and *when* and thanked occasionally." She stared into the tea. "Maybe it was authorized. Maybe someone above him decided the clock needed to lose four minutes and my master obeyed, the way we all obey. And maybe—" She stopped.

"Maybe the same people who needed the clock to lose four minutes also need a certificate saying it doesn't," Elias finished quietly.

"Yes."

They sat with that. Outside, the wind had begun its evening work on the eaves, a low persistent worrying, like a tongue finding a loose tooth. Somewhere below them the town of Veymar was lighting its lamps, all those mixed households — valley families and settler families and the few remaining survey families, living folded together along streets whose names had recently been changed to names nobody used.

"What will you do?" Elias asked.

"That depends on what you'll think of me."

"Try me."

Rosalind set down the cup. "My first instinct — my honest first instinct, before anything noble — was relief." She made herself look at him while she said it. "Certify the thing, sign the form, go home in spring, and never think about this mountain again. That's what a sensible person does. That's what I was sent here to do. I'm an instrument, Sable. My master said it himself once, not unkindly: *a good apprentice is a tool with good habits.* Tools don't ask what the work is for. They hold their edge and they don't rust and they go home when the job's done."

"Do you believe that?"

"No," she said, surprising herself with the speed of it. "No. I believed it when I was fifteen and believing it got me praised. But I opened that escapement, Elias. Somebody built that tampering with skill — real skill, deliberate, patient — and I sat there and I *fixed* it, and I didn't report it, and that was the first secret I ever kept in brass. And now they want me to put my name on paper swearing the secret isn't there." She flexed her fingers, which had begun, faintly, treacherously, to ache. "I keep thinking about Anselm. About Marta Anselm, who still hangs washing on a line facing a valley that officially belongs to someone else. If I sign, I'm not signing for a clock. I'm signing that her husband measured wrong. I'm signing that the last year of his life was a bookkeeping error."

Elias said nothing for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register she privately thought of as his observatory voice — the one he used for facts too large to say loudly. "You should know that if you refuse, they won't punish you. They'll simply replace you. Another clockmaker comes up in spring, certifies the clock, and you've lost everything and changed nothing. That's how these machines work. I've watched it happen to braver men than either of us."

"I know."

"And if you comply—"

"I know that too." She laughed, short and without much humor in it. "That's the trap, isn't it? Both roads are paved. One leads off a cliff slowly and one does immediately."

"There's a third road."

"Which is?"

"The one you haven't let yourself look at yet. The one where you don't choose between the Guild and the truth, because you've realized the Guild's own creed is on your side." He tapped the edge of her bench, right beside her loupe, her mark, the tiny stamped spiral every certified guildsman wore on their instruments. "*An instrument serves the measure, not the measurer.* First article. Your masters made you memorize it."

"They also made me memorize obedience."

"Then you have a contradiction to resolve," Elias said, "and I'm given to understand clockmakers enjoy those."

It was gentle, and it was teasing, and it was neither of those things at all. She looked at him — this precise, guarded man who had spent years keeping vigil over a dead friend's coordinates, who drank his tea at exactly the temperature of patience — and felt the ground shift under her in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain.

Because the terrible truth, the truth she had been circling since the escapement, was this: she was not staying for the clock anymore. Some nights, listening to the wind, she caught herself calculating how many weeks remained of winter and feeling — briefly, shamefully, unmistakably — glad of them. Glad of the snowbound evenings, the shared lamp, the arguments about gear ratios that were never really about gear ratios. Glad of a man who cleared her bench corner before setting down a cup.

Duty had brought her up the mountain. Something else was trying very hard to make her stay.

"Elias," she said. "If I refuse the certification — if I find some third road — it won't just cost me the Guild. It could cost you. Everything you're protecting in that dome. My name will be on the refusal. They'll look at everyone near me."

"They already look at everyone near me. I'm the astronomer who keeps hours no one asked for." He smiled, thin and real. "But thank you. It's been a long time since anyone worried about the danger to me instead of the danger from me."

"Someone has to. You're terrible at it."

"So I'm told."

* * *

He left at nine, taking the cups, pausing at the door to say, "Don't decide tonight. Decisions made at midnight belong to the midnight version of us, and she's exhausted." Which was so exactly the sort of thing she'd have said to an apprentice that she laughed despite everything, alone in her cold little room with the letter lying on the bed like a fallen bird.

But she did not sleep. She lit her lamp again and laid the certificate forms out on the bench — the Guild's standard assessment sheets, blank, waiting, innocent-looking as fresh snow — and beside them the letter, and beside the letter, her tools.

Her hands, she discovered, were shaking.

She held them up to the lamplight and watched them tremble, these hands that had not shaken since she was nine years old and broke her first balance spring, these hands trained to steadiness through ten thousand repetitions, and she understood with a kind of clinical fascination that the shaking was not fear. Fear shook differently — she'd seen it in men's hands on the mountain, quick and animal. This was slower. Deeper. This was the tremor of a mechanism bearing a load it had never been rated for.

Love against duty, she thought. People wrote about it in novels as though it were a crossroads, a dramatic fork, one road clearly love and one clearly duty. Nobody wrote about how it actually felt: like a form. Like a simple guild certificate, one page, six fields, a line for a signature. Like discovering that the heaviest weight you will ever lift weighs less than an ounce.

She could sign. Six fields, thirty seconds, done. Or she could refuse, and lose the guild that raised her, or she could stall, and stall, until spring delivered someone else to do the signing for her — the coward's road, smooth and downhill all the way.

Or.

Or there was the third road Elias had pointed at, the one she hadn't let herself look at. The first article of the guild she loved: *an instrument serves the measure, not the measurer.* If the Guild had become the measurer — if men in the capital were bending the measure to their maps — then serving the measure faithfully might mean disobeying the men. Not betrayal of the creed. Its fulfillment, at whatever cost fulfillment demanded.

Her hands were still shaking. She watched them shake, and then, deliberately, she picked up her pen.

She did not sign. She did not refuse. She wrote, in her best formal guildhand, a single line at the bottom of the draft certificate:

*Assessment incomplete. Escapement requires further study. — R. Nakamura*

A stalling answer. A technically true answer. An answer that would buy her the winter — and, she noted as she blotted it, an answer her master would read and understand instantly, because Master Voss had taught her that a clockmaker's reports should be exact, and exactness cuts both ways. *Further study.* He would hear what she was really saying: *I have seen what was done to this clock, and I am not done looking at it.*

Whether he'd protect her or report her, she couldn't yet know. That uncertainty sat in her chest like a stone in a movement — wrong weight, wrong place, throwing everything off beat.

She folded the reply, addressed it to a bridge that would be closed by morning, and set it by the door for whoever might carry it down whenever the world reopened. Then she blew out the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the wind and to the muffled, faithful heartbeat of the master clock two floors below — striking, now, the true hour, thanks to her own hands.

Somewhere in the town below, a widow named Marta Anselm was sleeping in a house with boarded windows down the lane, guarding whatever her husband had left behind.

In the morning, Rosalind decided, she would pay her a visit.
