## 10. Anselm's Widow

The letter from the guild had come on the last sled before the bridge closed — no. I am getting ahead of myself, the way my hands do when they are frightened. Let me begin where a clockmaker should begin: at the moment of contact.

The morning I walked down to Marta Anselm's house, Veymar wore its first true snow like a borrowed coat, not yet fitted to its shoulders. The roofs were white but the streets were still dark stone, and every footstep rang out clearly enough that I felt observed by the whole town, which was absurd. Nobody watched me. Everybody watched me. In a town of four hundred souls, a stranger is a bell rung in an empty church; even after six weeks I had not stopped echoing.

I told myself I was going to buy thread.

This was even partly true. My coat lining had begun to give way along one seam — a fact that would matter more later than I could then have imagined, though at the time it concerned me only as a nuisance and a small professional embarrassment. A clockmaker who cannot keep her own coat sewn is like a surgeon with a splinter. I had asked Frau Brenner at the dry goods shop where a person might purchase good linen thread, and Frau Brenner had said, without looking up from her ledger, "Marta Anselm spins her own. Finest in the valley. The blue door past the chapel."

Then she had looked up.

Frau Brenner was perhaps sixty, with the face of a woman who had spent decades deciding how much to say. She looked at me for the length of two ticks of the shop's wall clock — a cheap thing, losing a minute a day, and it took everything in me not to say so — and then she said, "Tell her Rosalind sent you."

"Rosalind sent me," I repeated. "That's my name."

"I know whose name it is." And she went back to her ledger, and I understood that something had been arranged, or revealed, or risked, and that I would only find out which at the blue door.

* * *

Marta Anselm lived in a narrow house that leaned companionably against its neighbor, as old buildings do when they have stood together through enough winters. The blue door was faded almost to gray, the color of a memory of a color, and the knocker was a small brass hand holding a ring, worn smooth by thumbs.

She opened the door before I finished knocking. Either she had seen me coming up the street or she had been standing behind it for some other reason entirely. She was tall and straight-backed, maybe fifty-five, with white threading through dark hair she had pinned with what I recognized, with a lurch, as a broken clock hand — the minute hand off some small carriage clock, repurposed, its brass gone dull.

"You're the clockmaker," she said. Not a question.

"Rosalind Nakamura. Frau Brenner said—"

"I know what Frau Brenner said." But she stepped back from the doorway, which in Veymar's grammar of gestures constituted an invitation of considerable warmth. "Come in before you freeze. And wipe your boots. Anselm hated snow on his floors."

*Anselm.* Not *my husband*. I wiped my boots very thoroughly.

Her front room was small and fiercely tidy, the tidiness of someone who has nothing left to be careless about. A loom stood in the corner with half a length of blue-gray cloth on it. On every horizontal surface sat clocks — mantel clocks, a bracket clock, a regulator with a mercury pendulum that made my palms itch with wanting — and every single one of them was stopped.

Not broken. Stopped. Their hands set at different hours, frozen mid-sentence.

"You've noticed," Marta said, following my eyes.

"They're all—"

"Wound down. Deliberately." She said it the way you'd report weather. "After Anselm died I couldn't bear them striking. Every hour, the house announced it. So I let them run down, one by one, and now they just... hold their hours." She looked at the regulator. "That one stopped at 11:47. He came home at 11:47 the last night. I know because he wound it, and it ran three days, and I did the arithmetic afterward, the way you do."

I did not trust myself to speak. Grief does arithmetic. Of course it does. It is the cruelest kind, done over and over, always arriving at the same sum.

"Sit," she said. "Tea?"

"Please."

She poured from a pot kept warm on the stove, and the tea was black and strong enough to stand a spoon in, and we drank in silence for a while, two women in a room full of stopped clocks, which is either the saddest or the most honest company in the world.

Finally she said: "Show me your loupe."

"My—" My hand went to my collar before I could decide whether to obey. Clockmakers wear their loupes on a ribbon at the neck, tucked away when not in use, and mine had been my master's before it was mine, and his master's before that. Brass, with the guild mark stamped on the barrel: a wheel within a wheel, and beneath it the maker's initials, cut small.

I passed it across the table. Marta turned it in her fingers with surprising competence — she found the focus ring immediately, thumbed it open and shut twice — and then held it up to the window light and read the stamp.

"Voss and daughter," she said quietly.

"The founder had no daughters. The name stayed anyway. Sentimental people, clockmakers."

"Mm." She set the loupe down between us, precisely in the center of the table, like a piece on a board. "Anselm carried one exactly like it. He wasn't a clockmaker — he was a surveyor, God knows he couldn't fix anything, I mended his watch myself twice a year — but he bought it at auction in the capital, years ago. He said a man measuring land ought to carry the tools of men who measure smaller things. He said everything was surveying if you got close enough to it." Her mouth did something complicated. "He used to read the etchings under a loupe like this. For hours. In this room."

"Etchings on—"

"On whatever he was reading. Margins. Foredges. He trusted print the way other men trust God, but he trusted *small print* most of all. Anything important, he wrote too small to read casually." She refilled my cup without asking. "So when Frau Brenner told me a clockmaker had come up the mountain, and that the observatory's clock had been repaired in November, and that since then the town keeps hearing midnight strike at *midnight* — well."

I put my cup down. My hands were steady. I noted this the way you note a strange calm in the middle of a rockfall.

"Madam—"

"Marta."

"Marta. I repair clocks. What the clock strikes is a matter of the escapement, and the escapement was worn. That is the entire story."

"That is the story you've been telling the magistrate," she said, "and telling him well, from what I hear. You looked him in the eye at the winter fair and said the word 'escapement' until he went cross-eyed. Very nicely done. Anselm would have admired it." She folded her hands. "But I watched you just now when I said *etchings*. Your breath changed. Clockmakers breathe like metronomes, girl, I've known three of you in my life and you all do it — in, out, perfectly even, except when something matters. Your breath stopped."

I thought about lying to her. I want to record, honestly, that I considered it — that the instinct toward safety rose in me like water and I stood there weighing it. But there was something in that room: forty stopped clocks holding forty different hours, and a woman who had done grief's arithmetic every day for a year, and a loupe sitting in the center of the table like a small brass heart.

"He was murdered," Marta said. Not asking again. "You know it. Whoever altered your escapement knew it. The magistrate knows it. Half this town knows it and the other half suspects, and everyone is waiting to see who says it first, because saying it first is dangerous." She leaned forward. "I am fifty-seven years old, Rosalind Nakamura. I have buried a husband and a son and my patience. I don't intend to die wondering whether anyone ever said it."

* * *

I told her. Not everything — not the wheel, not Elias, not the specific shape of what I suspected the mechanism held — but I told her about the four minutes. About the deliberate, patient tampering, the work of skilled hands over months. About the certificate the guild wanted signed. Her face never moved while I spoke, except once, when I described how neatly the alteration had been done, and something crossed it that was not surprise but recognition, as if she were hearing a voice she knew describe a room she knew.

When I finished, she stood, went to the loom, and knelt beside it.

I assumed she was checking the warp. Then she reached underneath, into the frame's shadow, and drew out a oilcloth packet tied with waxed string, and my heart performed a maneuver no pendulum should attempt.

"Anselm's field notebook," she said. "From the last season. The official copies went to the survey office in the autumn, and in December the survey office burned, and in January the colonial administration issued its maps based on 'the surviving records.'" She untied the string with fingers that shook only slightly, and I pretended not to see, because I would have wanted the courtesy myself. "The surviving records. Yes. This one survived because I stole it back from his office the night after the funeral, while the magistrate's men were still deciding what to confiscate. I hid it in a bread oven for four months. Then I burned the edges off, so that if anyone ever searched here they'd find a damaged notebook of no value and stop looking."

"You *burned* it—"

"Partially. Deliberately. The pages that mattered, I protected." She opened it on the table between the tea things, and I saw at once what she had done: the outer leaves scorched to charcoal lace, and inside them, nested, a core of intact pages — dense, precise, covered in Anselm's small, upright hand. Survey coordinates. Bearing calculations. Dates. And in the margins, in that same hand but *smaller*, so small I could not read it across the table: columns of figures, tiny as insect script.

My breath stopped again. She noticed. This time she smiled.

"There it is," she said softly. "There's the metronome breaking."

"Marta, why are you showing me this? You don't know me. You know nothing about me except—"

"Except that you repaired a clock you were told not to look inside, and didn't report what you found, and have been walking around this town for weeks carrying a secret in your face like a coal in a closed fist." She pushed the notebook an inch toward me. "And except that you wear Voss's mark, and Anselm trusted Voss's mark, and I am running out of people to trust and out of winters to wait."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Outside, the snow had thickened, pressing softly at the windows like something trying to get in, or like the world leaning closer to listen.

"Why give it to me?" I asked. "Why not keep it? Why not give it to Sable?"

"Elias has the plates. Elias has the dome and the stars and his own stubbornness." She said Elias's name carefully, the way you handle a tool with a cracked handle — still useful, requiring caution. "But plates can be seized. Domes can be locked. Stars can be argued with by men paid to argue." Her hand flattened on the notebook. "What can't be argued with is a document in the right hands. Anselm believed that. He built his whole method around it — truth too small to burn, hidden in plain instruments, waiting for the one person trained to read it." She looked up at me, and her eyes were dry and terrible and steady. "He just never guessed the person would take two years to arrive."

"I'm an apprentice," I said. "I'm nobody. The guild will disown me if—"

"The guild sent you a letter on the last sled." I flinched; she saw it. "Veymar is small, child. The postmaster reads everything worth reading. Certify it as found and come home, yes?" She nodded slowly. "They want your signature. Your *guild* wants your signature on the lie. Which means your signature is worth more than you think, and so are you." She turned the notebook fully toward me. "Take it."

"I can't—"

"You can and you will. Listen to me." For the first time her voice roughened, and forty stopped clocks seemed to lean in. "A widow who hides a notebook is a woman with a grievance, and grievances can be dismissed. But a *clockmaker of the Voss line* who certifies, in writing, on guild paper, the true state of that clock — that is a record. Institutions eat individuals. They choke on records." She pressed the notebook into my hands. It was lighter than I expected and heavier than anything I had ever held. "I kept it safe for two years. Keeping isn't enough anymore. It needs to move. It needs hands that know what the small writing means."

I looked down at the scorched edges, at the tiny marginal script I already knew — knew with sick certainty — matched the etchings on an escapement wheel turning overhead in a dome above the town.

"What happens if they search me?" I asked. "If the inspector comes—"

"Then you'll have to be cleverer than a bread oven." Almost a smile. "Clockmakers usually are."

* * *

I stayed another hour. We did not speak of notebooks or wheels; we spoke of thread, and she sold me a spool of her linen at a price she refused to discount, and she showed me the loom and complained cheerfully about the damp and its effect on wool, and for a little while we were simply two women in a kitchen, and I understood that this too was part of the inheritance she was handing me: the ordinary hours that have to keep running underneath everything else, or none of the rest means anything.

At the door, she pinned her coat closed against the cold and studied me one last time.

"One more thing," she said. "You'll want to read those margins tonight, alone, with your best glass. When you do — and I know you, girl, you'll do it tonight, you won't sleep first — remember that the man who wrote them checked every figure three times and laughed at himself for checking a fourth." She pulled her scarf up. "Don't check a fourth time. Trust him. He earned it."

I walked home through deepening snow with the notebook sewn — temporarily, badly, I would improve the stitching that night with her excellent thread — into my coat lining, and with each step the town's clocks struck four in their staggered, drifting voices, none of them agreeing, all of them wrong.

Except one. High above the rooftops, faint with distance, the observatory's master clock struck four exactly, cleanly, once.

Behind me, at the edge of hearing, I thought I heard Marta's door close. Ahead of me, at the observatory, a lamp was already lit in Elias's window — though it was hours yet until dark, and he had told me himself that astronomers waste no light on daytime.

Unless, I thought, climbing the hill with my heart keeping strange, quick time, unless he had heard that I had gone down the mountain. Unless he had spent the whole afternoon watching the road, waiting to see what I carried back.

I put my hand flat against my coat, against the small scorched weight riding over my ribs.

Some things, once carried, cannot be set down again.
