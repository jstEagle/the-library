## 12. Inspection

The thaw was still six weeks away by every calculation I could make, and I had made many, because calculations were the one comfort I trusted absolutely. Ice on the bridge measured four fingers thick at the rail. The river beneath it moved in its slow black way, patient as a clerk waiting for a signature. Nothing crossed that bridge in February. Nothing had ever crossed it in February.

Which is why, when the dogs began barking on the morning of the ninth of February, my first thought was not *visitors*. My first thought was *wolves*, and even that felt generous. Wolves, at least, would have had the decency to be impossible too.

I was at the bench in the clock room, filing the teeth of the strike train's third wheel — the decoy work had begun quietly, in hours when no one watched, though "quietly" is a generous word for anything Elias Sable attempts, because the man hums when he concentrates, tunelessly, like a kettle with opinions. He stopped humming. That was how I knew something was wrong before I heard it myself: Elias only ever fell silent for weather, magistrates, and me, in roughly that order of frequency.

"Listen," he said.

"I am listening."

"No. Listen to what's *missing*."

I set down my file. The observatory has its own grammar of sound — the tick of the master clock, the groan of the dome when the wind turned, the creak of frost working its way through the roof beams like a slow reader going through a long book. What was missing was the sound of the valley waking up. No sled bells from Veymar below. No children shouting on the path. Just the dogs, and under the dogs' barking, a lower sound I took another moment to place.

Boots. Many boots. And the particular crunch-squeak of men walking in step who have been trained to do it without thinking about it.

We went out onto the gallery together, and there, absurdly, impossibly, coming up the switchback road in single file through the snow-haze, came eleven figures and a sledge. They had come over the pass. Not across the bridge — *over* — which meant they had climbed above the tree line in winter, which meant they had been sent by someone who did not accept *impossible* as an answer, or who had never been told the question.

Elias said nothing for a long time. His breath came out in small white flags, surrendering one after another.

"That will be Herr Vogel," he said finally. "The inspector. He wrote in December that he would come when the roads allowed."

"The roads don't allow."

"Yes," said Elias. "I had rather counted on that."

* * *

Herr Inspector Vogel was not what I expected, and I have learned to distrust that sentence, because expectation is just prejudice wearing its good coat. I expected a large man, loud, someone whose authority arrived ahead of him like cavalry. Instead he was small and dry and precise, with spectacles that caught the light so thoroughly you could watch yourself distorted in them — a face split into two small curved mirrors, each showing me a slightly different version of myself, both of them nervous.

He removed his gloves finger by finger in the entrance hall while his escort stood dripping in ranks behind him, and he looked around the observatory with the expression of a man appraising a house he intended to repossess.

"You are the clockmaker," he said. It was not a question, but it was also not quite a greeting.

"Rosalind Nakamura. Guild-certified." I gave him the bow the guild teaches, shallow and correct, and hated how automatically my body performed deference even as my mind filed objections.

"Nakamura." He tried the name the way one tries a key that may or may not fit. "You were sent by the guild in autumn. To repair the master clock."

"To service it. It was losing time."

"And does it lose time now?"

Here it was. The whole season balanced inside that question like a coin on its edge. Behind me I felt Elias go very still — the stillness of a man watching someone else handle his loaded instrument.

"It keeps true time," I said. "That is what I was engaged to ensure."

"Mm," said Vogel. He produced a notebook — leather, government-issue, a stub of pencil attached by string — and wrote something in it. I would have given a great deal to know what. I settled for knowing nothing, which was cheaper.

The magistrate had come up from Veymar behind the party, moving slower on the ice, wrapped in a coat with a fur collar that made him look like a bear attempting respectability. He caught my eye over Vogel's shoulder and gave me a look I could not read — warning, apology, or arithmetic. With Magistrate Brandt those three often traveled together.

"Lunch," announced Vogel, as if declaring a military objective. "And then I should like to see everything."

* * *

Lunch was soup, bread, and pickled cabbage, eaten by eleven exhausted soldiers with the silent gratitude of men who had crossed a pass and were only now allowing themselves to be hungry. Vogel ate precisely, wiping his spoon between mouthfuls, and asked questions between bites in the tone of a man making conversation who is actually taking minutes.

How long had I been at the observatory? Since October. Had the bridge closure inconvenienced me? Considerably. Did I correspond with the guild? Only by courier. When had the last courier come? January, with the sled. What had it carried?

"A letter from my master," I said, which was true, and left out everything else, which was survival.

Vogel nodded and wrote. I noticed that his notebook had a habit of absorbing even cheerful answers, as though joy itself were evidence.

Afterward he inspected the instruments. He looked through the transit telescope without adjusting it properly and pronounced the view satisfactory, which any astronomer will tell you is like pronouncing a book satisfactory after reading the spine. He examined Anselm's old survey office — boarded windows, dust sheets, the chair where a dead man had once sat and drawn lines that powerful people wished were different — and spent four minutes in there alone, and came out writing.

And then he said, pleasantly, the way one says *shall we take some air*: "Now the clock."

* * *

I want to record, because it matters, exactly what happened in the clock room that afternoon, because afterward everyone remembered it differently and memory is the first casualty of any inspection.

Vogel walked around the case twice. He opened the door himself — nobody stopped him; stopping him would have required a reason, and reasons are ammunition you spend carefully — and he looked at the movement the way I look at a movement, actually looking, which frightened me more than ignorance would have. A ignorant man you can manage. An attentive man is a different instrument entirely.

"This is the regulator," he said. "The master. All the town clocks answer to it."

"They do."

"Then whoever controls this controls the official hour of Veymar." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way one remarks on weather, and I understood suddenly why they had sent *him* over a mountain in February instead of sending soldiers to simply take the thing: because the taking had to look like paperwork. Violence leaves witnesses. Procedure leaves signatures.

"There was an irregularity last autumn," Vogel continued. "A discrepancy in the recorded times of certain observations. The commission in the capital requires the instrument itself for examination. As evidence."

"Evidence of what?" Elias asked. He had appeared in the doorway, and his voice had that flat calm I had learned to hear danger underneath, like bedrock under snow.

"Of whatever the examination finds," said Vogel, with the serenity of a man who has never once been required to finish a syllogism in public. "The order is countersigned. You are welcome to read it." He held it out. We both read it. It was beautifully done — references to statute, to the commission, to irregularities unnamed, to cooperation requested and expected, all in a hand so orderly it might have been printed. Nowhere did it say *the truth*. Nowhere did it need to. Documents like this don't accuse; they confiscate.

"When?" Elias asked.

"Tomorrow. Your clockmaker will supervise the dismantling — she is qualified, and it would be unfortunate if the mechanism were damaged in transport. Damaged mechanisms raise questions." He smiled at me then, and it was almost a kind smile, which made it worse. "You understand, Fräulein Nakamura. Evidence must be handled by professionals."

* * *

They quartered the escort in the lower rooms and the stable block, and the observatory, which all winter had been ours — mine and Elias's and the clock's and the stars', a small sovereign nation of four — was suddenly occupied territory. Soldiers' boots on stairs built for socked feet. The smell of tobacco in the corridor where there had only ever been lamp oil and cold brass. One of them whistled while he chopped wood, badly, off-key, and I found I minded the whistling more than the rifles.

I found Marta Anselm in the kitchen that evening, because she had come up with the supply sled that followed the escort — she'd have come up anyway, she told the soldiers, and they'd believed her, because everyone believes Marta, that is her genius and her armor. She was bullying the cook into making proper dumplings, and she pulled me aside by the elbow with a grip that had raised a surveyor and buried one and feared nothing since.

"So," she said. "They've come for it."

"You know?"

"I've known since October that they'd come. I only didn't know when, or whether God would grant us one more decent blizzard." She looked at me then, really looked, the way she had the day she put her husband's half-burned notebook in my hands. "Rosalind. Tell me plainly. Is it still in there?"

I couldn't answer with words. Words travel. So I did the thing clockmakers do: I tapped my own wrist, twice, where a watch would sit, and then pointed upward — toward the clock room, toward the escapement, toward the little wheel of etched brass turning overhead in the dark, carrying a dead man's measurements through its teeth sixty times a minute, patient as scripture.

Marta closed her eyes. For a moment she was not the formidable widow of Veymar but a woman standing in a kitchen hearing that her husband's voice still existed somewhere, faint but legible, if only the machine could be kept from their hands.

"Then they can't have it," she said, opening her eyes.

"Marta—"

"*They can't have it.*" She smoothed her apron, and the formidable returned like a tide. "I fed these men dumplings. I know their sergeant's mother's village. I will find out which room they sleep in and which ones snore. You find out the rest. That's how it works now — I do the dumplings, you do the wheels."

It should not have made me smile. It did. Fear and laughter share a pulse rate; I learned that that winter.

* * *

Elias and I did not speak of it until midnight. We could not — the corridors had ears now, and ears change a house more than locks do. But at midnight the escort slept, the magistrate had gone back down the hill, and the two of us stood in the clock room with the lamp turned low and the great pendulum breathing its slow breath above us, and everything we needed to say fit into the silence like gears into a train.

"He'll ship it in pieces," Elias said. "Crated. By the time it reaches the capital it will be 'examined,' and what examination means, in that city, is *disassembled until inconvenient parts disappear.*"

"The wheel is hardened steel with micro-etching. If they scrub it, polish it, treat it—" I stopped, because finishing the sentence was unbearable. "One cleaning with the wrong solvent and Anselm is gone. Not killed again — *unwritten*. There'd be nothing to testify to. No wheel, no data, no proof it ever existed."

"Then it cannot go."

"Elias. There are eleven armed men downstairs and an order with three signatures."

"There are eleven armed men," Elias agreed, "and one road, and one thaw, and six weeks. I have spent five years learning what one determined person can hide inside an institution." He turned to me, and in the lamplight his face had none of its usual guarded irony; it was bare, and young, and terrified, and resolved, all at once, which is a combination I have seen only on faces in love or in war, and I have come to believe they are frequently the same face. "But I'm an astronomer. Hiding things in institutions is my craft. Wheels are yours."

I looked up at the movement. At the escape wheel turning in the dark, tooth by tooth, second by second, carrying its cargo of truth past all our heads, faithful, oblivious, doomed. Somewhere in the capital there was a crate with its name on it already, and somewhere in my chest there was a plan beginning to assemble itself, gear by gear, the way plans do — not chosen so much as *meshed*.

"They inspect tomorrow," I said. "So tonight we start building."

"Building what?"

I put my loupe to my eye and looked at him through it, which made him appear magnified and slightly ridiculous and entirely beloved.

"A forgery worth making," I said.

Above us, unhurried, indifferent to empires, the clock struck midnight — the true midnight, our midnight — twelve times, and not one of the eleven sleeping soldiers stirred.
