## 13. A Forgery Worth Making

The plan was Elias's idea, which surprised her, because she had assumed astronomers were constitutionally incapable of lying.

"A forgery," he said, laying it out over the workbench in the observatory's lower room, where the lamplight pooled yellow and the smell of machine oil mixed with the pine smoke from the stove. "A decoy wheel. Perfect. Plausible. Wrong."

Rosalind set down her tweezers and looked at him for a long moment.

"You want me to make a lie out of brass."

"I want you to make a *very good* lie out of brass." He had the particular earnestness of a man who had rehearsed this argument all night and lost to himself twice before dawn. "The inspector will take the wheel apart. He'll see etchings. He'll see Anselm's mark. He'll carry his prize to the capital and crow about it, and by the time anyone reads what's actually on it—" he paused— "it will say nothing at all. Or worse. Something harmless."

"Something harmless," Rosalind repeated. "Like what?"

"Star positions. Boring ones. Coordinates of nothing." A ghost of a smile crossed his face, the first she'd seen there since the inspector's arrival had turned him grey. "The kind of thing an astronomer might etch into a wheel if he were showing off."

"And meanwhile I decode the real one."

"You've already started. Don't pretend you haven't. You've been squinting at that magnifier like it insulted your mother."

She almost laughed — caught it, held it, let a little escape anyway. It felt dangerous to laugh in this building now. The inspector had taken rooms at the magistrate's house; two of his clerks walked the town with notebooks; even the church bell seemed to ring more quietly, as though it too had been asked to keep official time.

But here, below the dome, under the great silent weight of the master clock, they were still allowed one small conspiracy.

"All right," Rosalind said. "I'll build your lie. But I have conditions."

"Name them."

"One: the forgery must be flawless. If it fails inspection because of a sloppy tooth profile, we don't just lose the wheel — we lose everything. Marta. The magistrate's nerve. Possibly Elias Sable's continued liberty."

"Agreed. And two?"

"Two: you stop hovering. You are going to stand at my elbow breathing astronomy at me while I file brass, and I will file crooked, and then we'll both be hanged for a crooked tooth."

Elias considered this with the gravity of a man weighing orbital mechanics. "I could breathe at you from across the room."

"That would be acceptable."

He moved his stool three feet away. Then, after a moment, three feet back. "Acceptable," he said, "within tolerances."

* * *

The first problem was material. A forged escapement wheel could not be made of new brass; new brass shone differently, aged differently, smelled — Rosalind insisted — different under a loupe. They needed metal that had lived in the clock's world for years.

The answer came, as answers sometimes did, from the junk drawer.

Every workshop has one: the drawer of broken pendulums, snapped arbors, weights whose faces had been dented by careless hands across decades. The observatory's drawer was deep and full of the accumulated failures of sixty years of timekeeping. Rosalind spent an evening elbow-deep in it while Elias held the lamp and narrated, unhelpfully, the provenance of each piece.

"That arbor broke in 1904. I know because the logbook blames it for a transit error that got my predecessor a very stern letter from Vienna."

"This weight fell on someone's foot."

"The same someone who broke the arbor?"

"No, that was a different catastrophe. This place is a museum of disasters."

"Then it's perfect," Rosalind said, holding up a scrap of old brass plate, greened at the edges, scratched with the honest wear of decades. "Old metal lies convincingly. New metal confesses immediately."

Elias looked at her sideways. "You speak of forgery with the fluency of a much guiltier woman."

"I'm a clockmaker. We spend our whole lives making machines that pretend the sun is exactly overhead when it isn't. Timekeeping is organized lying, Master Sable. I'm merely changing which lie we tell."

She said it lightly. She didn't feel light. Every stroke of the file that night felt like a small treason against the guild that had raised her — the guild whose letter lay folded in her trunk, demanding her signature certifying the clock *as found*, meaning as falsified, meaning the maps, meaning the annexation, meaning Anselm dead in a ravine and called an accident. She was choosing sides with every grain of brass dust. There was no neutral bench anymore.

And yet her hands had never been steadier.

That frightened her a little, when she let herself think of it. That deceit should fit her so well. That the work of lying should feel, in her fingers, exactly like the work of truth.

* * *

They worked in shifts, because the inspector's clerks kept irregular hours and the observatory had to appear ordinary — an astronomer doing astronomy, a clockmaker doing repairs, nothing worth a notebook entry.

Days, Rosalind decoded. She worked in the dome itself, high above the town, with the true wheel mounted in its jeweled frame and winter light coming through the shutter-slit in a blade so thin it seemed cut from paper. Anselm's etchings were invisible to the naked eye — lines finer than a hair, laid onto the wheel's spokes in a code that was not quite a code, because codes could be broken by clever men with time. Instead the surveyor had disguised his data as *tooling marks*: the scratches any craftsman leaves in manufacture, meaningless unless you knew the hand that left them.

Rosalind knew hands. She had spent ten years learning to read the fingerprints of makers long dead — the hesitation of a novice's graver, the confidence of a master's. Anselm had been a surveyor, not a clockmaker, but he'd had a clockmaker teach him, and once Rosalind found the rhythm of his marks, the ledger opened like a book.

Numbers. Bearings. Elevations. Page after page of the valley written in brass — the true borders, the true river forks, the boundary stones that had been moved in the night and moved back again by morning. All of it ticking away overhead in Veymar for years, while men downstairs rewrote the maps.

"Some nights," she told Elias, "I can hear it. Not the etchings. Just — the wheel turning. Knowing things."

"It's brass. It doesn't know anything."

"Neither do stars, and you talk to them constantly."

"That's different. Stars answer."

Nights, they built the forgery.

It was slow, exacting, oddly joyful work. Rosalind cut and filed the blank wheel from the salvaged plate; Elias — who turned out to have surprisingly capable hands, the hands of a man who had ground his own lenses — polished, measured, checked her work against the true wheel's dimensions with calipers borrowed from her case. They aged the new brass with vinegar and woodsmoke and, at Rosalind's instruction, a week of being carried around in Elias's coat pocket, absorbing warmth and skin oils until it stopped smelling of the bench and started smelling of history.

"You're aging it like cheese," Elias observed one midnight, holding the wheel up to the lamp.

"Everything old is just something young that survived. Now hold still, I need to check the tooth spacing against the original again."

He held still. He was very good at holding still, she was discovering — a quality she had initially mistaken for coldness and was beginning to understand was simply the shape his care took. He watched her measure with the total attention most people gave only to eclipses, and it made the back of her neck warm in a way that had nothing to do with the stove.

"There," she said finally. "Tooth forty-one is within a tenth of the original. If the inspector can find a flaw better than a tenth, he deserves to win."

"He won't find it. Inspectors look for lies. They never imagine a lie built to their own specifications." Elias paused. "That's rather bleak, isn't it."

"It's Tuesday," Rosalind said. "Bleakness keeps regular hours here."

* * *

The nights grew longer, and the tea stronger, and somewhere in the third week the work stopped being two people conspiring and became something neither of them named.

They developed rituals without deciding to. At two in the morning she would find a cup of tea at her elbow, sweetened the way she liked it, though she had never told him how she liked it — he had simply watched her reach for the sugar twice and drawn his conclusion with astronomical precision. At four, he would begin to flag, his sentences growing shorter, his star charts drifting, and she would wordlessly take the polishing cloth from his hand and finish his section while he dozed upright in his chair with his collar undone, looking younger than a man carrying a murdered friend's secret had any right to look.

One night she woke him gently and he came up out of sleep saying, "Anselm, wait, the reading's wrong—" and then blinked at her, at the lamp, at the half-forged wheel between them, and went quiet.

"He trusted the wrong instrument," Elias said finally. "Me. I told him the ravine path was safe. I'd walked it a hundred times."

"You couldn't have known."

"No. But knowing doesn't help. That's the thing nobody tells you about grief. Knowledge is supposed to be the cure for everything. I've spent my life believing that. Measure it, understand it, and it stops hurting." He turned the teacup slowly on its saucer. "Anselm is dead and I understood every step of why. It didn't save him and it doesn't help me sleep."

Rosalind thought of her guildhall, of her master's kind, careful voice: *repair the clock, ask nothing.* She had obeyed for years, telling herself precision was neutrality. Standing in this lamplit room, watching a man try to drink his way past a confession, she understood that she had never been neutral. She had merely been far enough away not to hear the sound the lie made.

"My master sent me here as an instrument," she said. "A tool that fixes tools. And I came because it was easier than asking what I was being used for." She looked at the wheel. "I think maybe everyone in this story did the easy thing until it became impossible."

"Yes," Elias said softly. "And then?"

"Then we file brass and commit treason, apparently."

His laugh was quiet and cracked in the middle, and it was — she admitted it to herself, filing it alongside the other precise measurements she'd been taking all month — the best sound in the building.

* * *

On the ninth night of forging, disaster arrived wearing wool socks.

Rosalind heard the stair creak a half-second before Elias did — clockmaker's ears — and they moved without speaking: the true wheel into its hiding slot behind the dome's meridian wall, the decoy onto the bench under a repair cloth, Elias into his chair with a star chart, Rosalind bent over the clock's case with her loupe as though she had never in her life done anything more scandalous than clean a pivot.

Boots on the stairs. Then the clerk — the younger one, the one with the notebook and the habit of appearing exactly when the tea was freshest.

"Working late, Fräulein Nakamura?"

"Clocks keep hours whether or not anyone approves of them," Rosalind said pleasantly, not lifting her eye from the loupe. Her heart was performing a rhythm no escapement should produce. Under the cloth, six inches from the clerk's sleeve, sat a forged wheel aged in vinegar and Elias's coat pocket.

"Mm." The clerk wrote something. He wrote something every time; Rosalind had begun to suspect the notebook contained nothing but the word *clocks* in increasingly exasperated handwriting. "The inspector asks when the mechanism will be ready for transport."

"When it's correct," she said. "Not before. Your inspector may rush the maps and the records and the graves, but he will not rush my clock."

Silence. Then the boots retreated down the stairs, unhurried, notebook presumably updated.

When the door closed, Elias exhaled for both of them. "You lied beautifully."

"I know." Her hands were shaking now, delayed, the tremor arriving after the danger like thunder after lightning. She stared at them, annoyed. "That's the problem. I lie beautifully and shake afterward. Someday the order of operations is going to reverse at the worst possible moment."

He crossed the room without ceremony and put his hands over hers, warming them, steadying them, the way you'd steady a balance wheel. Neither of them said anything about it. Neither of them moved away. The lamp burned; the snow ticked against the dome above; somewhere in the walls the master clock counted seconds toward a dawn none of them were sure of.

"Your hands are cold," Elias said.

"They're always cold."

"I know. I've noticed for weeks." Which was, in the language they had been building between them — a language of tolerances and adjustments and unspoken measurements — very nearly a declaration.

She looked up at him. He looked down at her. The space between duty and memory, between the guild and the record, between the woman sent as an instrument and the woman choosing to be an author — all of it narrowed to the width of a lamplit room.

"We shouldn't," she said, without conviction.

"No," he agreed, equally unconvincingly.

They stood there, hands joined over a forged future, and did not kiss, because the clerk might come back, and because some things deserve better than interrupted timing. But something passed between them regardless — an agreement, a setting of the hands, the quiet click of a mechanism engaging.

* * *

By the twelfth night the decoy was finished, and it was beautiful.

Rosalind turned it under the lamp one last time — the false etchings crisp, the false wear convincing, the false data elegant enough to make an inspector weep with bureaucratic joy. Star positions of nothing in particular. Coordinates of nowhere. A surveyor's vanity, harmless as fog.

"It's the best lie I've ever made," she said. "I hate it and I'm proud of it."

"Welcome to espionage," said Elias. "The hours are terrible but the craftsmanship is excellent."

They hid the true wheel deeper — behind the meridian wall, wrapped in oilcloth, in a cavity Rosalind had chosen and Elias had widened with tools borrowed from her own case, so that the hiding place was, like everything else they'd built that winter, half his and half hers. When it was done they stood in the cold of the dome and looked at the blank stretch of wall that held the whole valley's truth, and Rosalind felt the strange vertigo of it: everything that mattered reduced to nothing visible at all.

Below them, in the town, lights were going out one by one. Except one.

"Whose lamp is that?" she asked. Near the bridge road. Burning at two in the morning, steady as a planet.

Elias followed her gaze and went still in a way she had learned to read.

"That's the boathouse," he said slowly. "Marta's late husband leased it. Nobody works there in winter." He paused. "Nobody *should* be working there in winter."

The distant lamp flickered — or perhaps it was only the wind moving the glass, or the snow thickening between them and the shore. But Rosalind, who had spent her life trusting instruments, felt the needle of her unease swing hard over, the way it does a moment before the weather changes.

"Elias," she said quietly, "who else knows we've been working nights?"
