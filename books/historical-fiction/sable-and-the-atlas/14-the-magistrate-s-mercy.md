## 14. The Magistrate's Mercy

Magistrate Aldous Renner woke, as he had woken every morning for eleven years, at the exact moment the observatory clock finished striking seven — and every morning for eleven years, the clock had been four minutes slow, and so Aldous Renner had begun his days four minutes behind his own conscience and had learned to call that arrangement *order*.

He lay in bed a while this particular morning, listening to his wife breathing beside him and to the kettle his housekeeper would be setting on downstairs, and he performed the small daily arithmetic that was the true labor of his office. Not budgets. Not petitions. Arithmetic: how many lies could stand upright on one another before the tower swayed.

Eleven years ago the answer had been two. A signature on an accident report. A seal on a set of maps that were, as the colonial surveyor from the capital had put it with the breezy confidence of a man describing weather, "close enough." Close enough. Anselm Voss had measured the valley to the width of a hair, and the capital had wanted it measured to the width of a signature, and when Anselm declined to be close enough, Anselm had gone over the falls in a season of high water, and Aldous had signed the paper saying so.

Two lies. They had stood up fine for eleven years.

Then a clockmaker came up the mountain, and now the tower swayed.

* * *

Aldous dressed carefully, as he did everything carefully, choosing the grey coat rather than the black because grey suggested a man who was neither mourning nor celebrating, which was the precise impression he had spent a decade cultivating. In the kitchen his wife, Ilsa, looked at him over her tea and said nothing, which from Ilsa was a full paragraph.

"You've got your thinking face," she said finally.

"I have my magistrate face."

"They're the same face. That's what worries me."

He kissed the top of her head, because eleven years ago she had watched him sign that report too, and she had not left him, and some debts you repay in small installments forever.

His first appointment of the day was a dispute over a fence line between the Hessler brothers, which meant forty minutes of two grown men shouting measurements at each other with the passion other men reserved for religion. Aldous listened, as he always listened, with his hands folded and his face arranged into patient gravity, but underneath the gravity his mind kept returning to the thing his clerk had told him at dawn, breathless, hat in hand.

The clock was keeping time again.

Four minutes, returned overnight, without ceremony, without explanation. The town had noticed the way a body notices its own heartbeat changing. The baker had nearly burned a batch. The schoolmaster had arrived early to an empty room and stood there blinking like a man translated suddenly into a foreign country. And the magistrate himself had woken at seven exactly, and lain in the dark feeling strangely, unbearably *on time*, and could not say why that felt less like relief than like exposure.

"Officially," his clerk had said, "the guild woman repaired it. That's all anyone will say officially."

And unofficially? He hadn't asked. Eleven years ago he'd stopped asking unofficial questions. It was remarkable, really, how much of a man's integrity consisted simply of declining to ask things.

* * *

The Hasslers were resolved by noon with a compromise fence both brothers hated equally, which Aldous considered the highest form of justice available to him. He ate lunch standing at his office window, looking up at the observatory dome catching the thin autumn sun, and it was there that Deputy Krail found him — the deputy sent by the colonial administration, a young man with a soldier's posture and a clerk's eyes, who never removed his gloves indoors and whose courtesy had the temperature of a blade laid flat against the skin.

"Magistrate. A pleasant day."

"Adequate," said Aldous.

"The inspector sends word. He'll come before the thaw, over the pass, ahead of schedule. He wants the records inventoried and the master clock inspected upon arrival." Krail consulted a paper he did not need; it was a habit of men performing authority. "He asks whether the town's instruments can be relied upon. There have been... irregularities reported. In the timekeeping."

"Has there," said Aldous.

"The clock ran slow for months. Conveniently slow. Now it runs true again, also conveniently. Someone has been adjusting it, Magistrate. Adjustments are statements. The inspector reads statements."

Aldous turned from the window. Here it was, then — the familiar machinery engaging, gears he knew by feel, the great patient apparatus of the administration converting a question into a threat with no visible seam between them. He had fed this machine once, eleven years ago. One document. One signature. It had been so easy, and the ease had been the horror of it ever since — that the whole weight of a dead man's truth could be buried under the light pressure of a pen held by a frightened man on a Tuesday afternoon.

"And what does the inspector wish me to do about a clock?"

"Nothing yet. Only observe. You know the town, Magistrate. You know who talks to whom. If the clockmaker or the astronomer is doing anything more than oiling bearings—" Krail smiled, briefly, without warmth reaching anywhere near his eyes, "—you'll be the first to know. Won't you."

It was not a question, and they both let it remain one.

After Krail left, Aldous sat down at his desk and looked for a long time at the drawer where he kept the seal. Eleven years ago he had opened that drawer on a Tuesday afternoon. He could still smell the ink.

* * *

It was the following evening that he discovered the plan, though discovered was perhaps too grand a word for what actually happened, which was that he went to the observatory to deliver a routine writ concerning the observatory's fuel allotment, and the door was answered by the clockmaker herself, and behind her, through the open door of the workroom, he saw the thing he was not supposed to see.

She had been quick — he'd give her that. She stepped sideways with the naturalness of a woman closing a cupboard, and the view was gone, and her face was pleasant and empty as a fresh ledger page. But Aldous Renner had spent thirty years reading faces across a magistrate's desk, and he had seen, in the half second allowed to him, a workbench lit by three lamps, and on that bench a wheel — a brass escapement wheel — mounted in a holding jig beside tools arranged with the loving precision of surgical instruments, and beside *that*, a second wheel, half-finished, gleaming.

Two wheels. No clock needed two escape wheels. No honest repair produced a duplicate part in secret lamplight at dusk.

"Magistrate Renner," she said. "The fuel writ?"

"Yes." He handed it over and made himself smile the grey-coat smile. "Cold coming, they say. First snows early this year."

"So they say."

"You'll want to finish any repairs before the bridge closes."

Something moved behind her eyes — not guilt exactly; more like a door pulled shut very gently so as not to wake the house. "I always finish," she said.

He believed her. That was the trouble. Walking back down the switchbacks in the blue dusk, coat collar up against a wind that smelled of coming snow, Aldous understood that he believed her completely, and that this belief placed him in a position he had not occupied in eleven years: the position of a man who knows something the administration does not, and must decide what to do about it.

* * *

That night he could not sleep, and so he did what he always did instead of sleeping, which was to sit at his desk and review documents, because paperwork was the only prayer he knew. But the papers blurred, and around midnight he heard the distant sound of the observatory clock striking twelve — striking *true* twelve, the corrected twelve, four minutes later than his body expected — and he put down his pen and covered his face with both hands.

Anselm Voss had been a nuisance. That was how Aldous had framed it at the time, framing being the first skill of cowardice. A nuisance with his chains and his theodolite, measuring borders the capital had already drawn to its own satisfaction, writing letters full of coordinates that contradicted the annexation maps, polite letters, relentless letters, each one another small stone in the boot of empire. When the order had come down — informal, verbal, deniable — to see that the survey concluded, Aldous had told himself the words *see that the survey concludes* meant something administrative. A reassignment. A funding cut. Anything but the falls.

And then Anselm had drowned, and the report had been drafted for him, and the pen had been in his hand, and Ilsa had been asleep upstairs, and Krail's predecessor had been watching him with that same flat courteous patience, and Aldous had signed.

The terrible thing — the thing he had never told anyone, the thing that lived in him like a splinter working always deeper — was that his hand had not shaken. He had signed cleanly, legibly, in his best administrative script, the script they taught you so that history could read your name. And afterward he had walked home through the town he had just helped bury alive and had bought bread, and the baker had joked about the weather, and Aldous had joked back, and the world had continued, and he had learned that night the central secret of ordinary wickedness: it does not require monsters. It requires only tired men with steady hands.

Now there was a girl from the guild with lamp-oil stains on her fingers building a duplicate wheel in secret, and an astronomer who had loved Anselm Voss — everyone had known that much, even if no one had said it — and somewhere in the brass of that old clock, Aldous would have bet his office, was the truth he had signed away. And above them all, descending through the pass before the thaw, came an inspector who read adjustments as statements.

If he reported what he had seen, the machine would do what machines did: crush the girl, crush Sable, seize the clock, grind the truth into a capital archive and lose it among ten thousand tidy lies. And Aldous Renner would go on waking at seven, four minutes behind his conscience, until he died of respectable old age and was buried with his best handwriting on his stone.

If he said nothing—

What? What did silence buy? A chance, that was all. A narrow, improbable chance that a clockmaker's hands were steadier than a magistrate's had been.

Ilsa found him there past one o'clock, still at the desk, the pen uncapped and dry in his fingers.

"You look like your father," she said from the doorway, "the winter before he died. Sitting up with papers he couldn't read."

"There's a thing I might do," Aldous said. "Or not do. And either way, I don't know yet if I'm doing it out of courage or out of wanting to stop feeling sick."

Ilsa came and stood behind his chair and rested her hands on his shoulders — the same shoulders that had held the pen steady eleven years ago. "Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. Courage that's only nausea is worth nothing when the nausea stops."

"Then here's a test," she said. "Ask yourself what Anselm's widow deserves. Not what you deserve, not what keeps you comfortable. What Marta Voss deserves. Answer that, and do it, whatever it costs your stomach."

She kissed his hair and went back to bed, and Aldous sat alone with the dry pen, and outside the window the snow began — fat, silent flakes, the first of the season, closing the pass road inch by inch, sealing the valley, sealing him in with his decision.

* * *

In the morning he summoned Deputy Krail and gave him the report the administration required: the clockmaker was conducting routine repairs; the astronomer kept to his instruments; the town was quiet; nothing warranted the inspector's early attention.

Krail wrote it all down, gloved and punctilious, and at the door he paused. "You're certain, Magistrate? About the workroom? Nothing... duplicated?"

"Duplicate what?" said Aldous mildly. "Clock parts? The guild sends spare springs with every apprentice. It's standard practice. I can fetch you the guild handbook, if you'd like to check."

Something flickered across Krail's face — the irritation of a man whose blade, laid flat against the table, has been lifted and set down elsewhere. "No need," he said. "For now."

For now. Aldous watched him go down the street, and he felt the moral vertigo take him fully at last, the sick swinging drop of a man who understands he has just chosen sides — not the right side, necessarily, not the safe side, but *a* side, after eleven years of standing on the seam between them pretending the seam was solid ground.

He walked home slowly through the falling snow. At the corner by the bakery he passed Marta Voss, wrapped in grey, carrying her market basket, and she nodded to him the way she nodded to everyone — politely, evenly, with eleven years of grief worn smooth as river stone — and Aldous Renner stopped in the middle of the street like a man struck.

Because she had paused too. Just slightly. Just long enough to look at him — not the way the town looked at their magistrate, but the way one looks at a locked door, wondering.

"Marta," he said, and his voice came out wrong, thinner than he intended. "How are you keeping, this winter?"

"As well as widows keep," she said.

And then she was past him, and he stood in the snow among the ordinary sounds of the town — the baker calling his prices, children throwing the first slushballs of the year — and Aldous understood with sudden, total clarity what the next weeks would demand, and that he was not ready, and that readiness had nothing to do with anything.

Behind him, faint on the cold air, the observatory clock began to strike nine. True nine. On time.

He counted every stroke, and did not move until it finished.
