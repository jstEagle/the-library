## 15. Betrayal at the Boathouse

The morning the world ended began, as so many of its worst days do, with something small and beautiful: sunlight on snow.

Rosalind noticed it from the workshop window while she held a burnisher against the decoy wheel's final tooth — the last of ninety-six, each one filed and polished and aged by hand until it looked as though it had been turning faithfully for forty years. The light came off the drifts in a hard white sheet, and somewhere below in the town a dog was barking at nothing, and Elias was asleep for the first time in three days with his coat still on, and she thought — she actually thought — *we have done it.*

The forgery was perfect. She knew because she had tried to catch it lying. She had examined it under the loupe the way the inspector's own assayers would, looking for tool marks too fresh, brass too bright, the telltale absence of wear along the pivot seats. She had even, one giddy midnight, dusted it with graphite and run it against a test train of gears to give it honest-looking scuffing. It was plausible. It was *good*. Somewhere beneath her dread, a small guild-trained voice kept whispering that this was the finest piece of dishonest craftsmanship of her career, and wasn't that something to be proud of.

She gave the last tooth its final pass, blew away the swarf, and set the wheel down beside its true twin — Anselm's wheel, the real ledger, micro-etched with coordinates and dates and the surveyor's stubborn, patient hand. Two wheels, identical to the eye. One true, one false. By Friday one of them would be in an inspector's crate bound for the capital, and if all went well it would be the wrong one.

She allowed herself exactly four minutes of pride. She timed it. Old habit.

Then Marta's knock came at the workshop door, three quick strikes, wrong rhythm, and Rosalind knew before she opened it.

* * *

"Boathouse," Marta said. Her face was the color of the ice on the lake. "They've taken Sable. There are men at the dome. Rosalind — there were men watching my house yesterday. I thought they were checking on the wood delivery."

"How many men?"

"At the boathouse? Six. Maybe more. They came over the pass two days ago — I told you the mail sled brought strangers." Marta caught her arm. Her grip was strong; grief had made her hands into tools years ago. "Child, listen to me. They didn't come up here for the view."

The questions arrived all at once and cancelled each other out. Who informed? How much did they know? Was it the decoy or the real wheel? Had someone read her notes, followed her to Marta's cellar, watched the lamplight in the dome? Rosalind stood in the doorway with burnishing compound still on her fingers and understood, with the cold clarity she usually reserved for diagnosing a broken movement, that the answer to almost every question was already walking toward them across the snow, wearing the magistrate's borrowed authority like a coat that fit him badly.

"We split the risk," she said. "That was the whole point. Decoy in the clock, original in the dome, notes on my body. No single seizure kills us."

"No single seizure," Marta said grimly, "unless they take everything at once, which appears to be what they're doing. Come away from the window."

But Rosalind was already calculating. It was what her hands did when her mind refused to feel anything: they worked the problem. The inspector's party would secure the observatory first — the clock, the dome, any papers. Then the town. Then, methodically, the people. Which meant she had perhaps an hour before anyone remembered the apprentice clockmaker was not merely furniture.

"Elias is at the boathouse," she said. "They took him at the boathouse. Why was he at the boathouse?"

Marta's silence answered before she could.

"He went to check the boat," Rosalind said flatly. "For spring. He was going to tell me tonight, when the work was done. He wanted—" Her voice did something humiliating and small. She stopped it. "He wanted to have one piece of good news ready. In case the rest went badly."

* * *

She went anyway. Of course she went. Every sensible voice in her head — her master's, the guild's, Marta's, her own, the one that sounded increasingly like Elias saying *the clever move is to survive* — every one of them told her to stay hidden, to sit tight in the workshop among the innocent-smelling shavings and let events pass her by. But she had learned something about herself in the past weeks, something she suspected Anselm had known about himself too: she could not bear to let an instrument lie unattended while it was being broken.

She took the back path, the one along the lake shore where the willows grew thick and bare, moving fast enough to sweat inside her coat. The boathouse sat at the end of a short pier, grey timber, green door, a building so ordinary it hurt. Through the gap in its shutters she could see lantern light and boots, and hear voices — the inspector's clipped capital accent, the magistrate's softer one, both talking at once, and between them a third voice she knew better than her own heartbeat, level and courteous and absolutely refusing to confirm anything.

"—a private boat, gentlemen, used for soundings on the lake. I'm surprised it requires six men—"

"Dr. Sable." The inspector's voice was almost gentle. Rosalind had heard that gentleness once before, from a guild examiner informing a failed apprentice of his results. "You've been observed making nocturnal visits to this structure for weeks. You'll forgive us if we don't take your word regarding its contents."

A pause. The scrape of something heavy being dragged.

"You understand," Elias said, and his voice had changed — gone quiet in the way it went quiet when he talked about Anselm, "that whatever you believe you've found, you're carrying it down the mountain on ice that won't hold your weight after Thursday. I'd hate for the empire to lose its evidence to the weather."

"Your concern is touching. Bind his hands."

Rosalind pressed herself into the willow shadow and watched, through the shutter gap no wider than a gear tooth, as they walked Elias Sable up the pier. He moved without resistance, which frightened her more than struggling would have. Struggling meant hope. This was a man conserving himself, rationing his strength the way she rationed file strokes — for later, for when it mattered. He looked thin. He looked tired. He looked, as the soldiers turned him toward the path up to the observatory, briefly and directly at the willows.

He looked at nothing at all, actually. His eyes passed over her hiding place with the bland courtesy of a stranger, and only the smallest thing — a blink held half a second long, the ghost of their old argument about whether a second could be felt — told her he had seen her, and was telling her, with everything he could not say: *don't.*

Then the trees took him, and the lanterns, and the six pairs of boots.

* * *

She ran for the dome. It was stupid and she knew it was stupid, but there was a version of this afternoon in which the soldiers went to the boathouse first precisely because the dome was already handled — because someone had already been inside, already found the plates, already left a man waiting. And there was another version in which the dome stood untouched, snow unbroken on the north stair, and she could still reach the wheel before they did.

It was the second version. For nine more minutes, it was the second version.

She came through the dome's lower door at a dead run, breath sawing, and found the observatory silent except for the master clock ticking away below with the decoy in its heart, keeping official time, lying beautifully. Up the iron stairs, two at a time. The dome chamber smelled of cold brass and lamp oil and, faintly, of the tea they'd drunk here three nights ago while Elias traced Orion's belt with one finger and she pretended not to watch his hand instead of the stars.

The floor panel was undisturbed. The plates were there. The wheel—

The wheel was there.

For one entire second she let herself believe in miracles. Then she heard the boot on the stair behind her, deliberate and unhurried, and understood that the miracle was bait.

"Apprentice Nakamura." The inspector filled the dome hatchway, flanked by two soldiers, and inclined his head with genuine, chilling politeness. "We wondered where you'd got to. Your colleague has been remarkably uninformative, but then, he would be. He's protecting someone." He smiled. "I confess I hoped it might be you. Clockmakers are such useful witnesses. You can tell authentic from forged at a glance, can't you? That's rather your whole profession."

Rosalind's mind went very quiet and very fast, the way it did at the bench when a spring threatened to slip. Behind the inspector's shoulder: two soldiers. Below in the observatory: more. Between her and the windows: ten feet of open dome floor, and beyond the glass, the north roof slope, and beyond that —

Beyond that, nothing but a two-story drop into a snowdrift and a very long walk to Marta's cellar.

"The wheel," the inspector said, nodding at her hands, at the panel, at the truth lying open between them. "You'll want to know that we found the etchings within an hour of arriving. Your astronomer's friend was clever, but cleverness leaves marks. Micro-engraved coordinates, dates, annotations — a complete refutation of the annexation surveys, hidden in a clock part. Treason in brass." He said it lightly, almost fondly, and stepped aside to let the soldiers past him. "Wrap it. Carefully — it's evidence now, and evidence is worth more than the man who made it."

*They found the etchings.* Not the decoy — the real one. Because of course they had; the decoy was clean, that had been the entire point, and a clean wheel next to a documented etched one only proved which was which. Their plan had a flaw she hadn't seen because she'd built the forgery too well: perfection itself was a confession.

The soldier reached for the wheel. Rosalind's body made the decision before her mind ratified it.

"I wouldn't touch it like that," she said, stepping forward with all the crisp authority of the guild hall, every lesson her master ever barked at her arriving in her mouth at once. "Micro-etching on hardened brass? You scratch that surface with a gauntlet seam and your evidence becomes scrap metal. Do you want to explain to your superiors why the record of Anselm's coordinates arrived unreadable?" She held out both hands, palms up, the universal gesture of the tradesman. "Cloth. Cotton wool. A case lined with felt. If you want this wheel to testify, Inspector, it travels the way instruments travel — or it doesn't travel at all."

The inspector studied her for a long moment. Snow-light moved across his face.

"You really do love these things," he said, and there was something almost wistful in it. "Very well. Fetch her materials. And post a man on her — Nakamura, you will assist with the packing, and afterward you'll come downstairs and answer some questions about the master clock. Slowly."

*Slowly.* She packed the wheel with exquisite care, feeling its true weight settle into the felt-lined case — feeling, beneath her grief, the terrible professional satisfaction of knowing her handling instructions were correct — and all the while her other mind, the cold one, was measuring. The soldier posted at her elbow was young and bored and standing with his weight on his heels. The dome hatch was blocked. The windows were not. The drop to the drift was maybe eighteen feet, onto snow that February had piled deep against the foundation, and Marta had told her once, laughing, that she'd fallen off that roof as a girl and it was the softest landing in Veymar.

The case left with the soldiers. Rosalind followed it to the head of the stairs, stumbled — clumsily, extravagantly, a novice's stumble, dropping her loupe and swearing at herself — and in the three seconds the young soldier bent to help her pick it up, she turned and went through the window.

Glass broke the way glass breaks in clocks: all at once, everywhere, irreversibly.

* * *

The drift caught her the way Marta promised, soft and suffocating, and for a moment she simply lay in it, gasping, blood trickling warm from her cheekbone, listening to the shouting above. Then the training took over — *move, girl, grief is for later* — and she ran, low, along the foundation line, through the willows, up the frozen creek bed, following a route she had mapped in her head weeks ago without ever admitting to herself she was doing it. Behind her the observatory shouted itself into chaos. Ahead of her the snow held no tracks but her own.

Marta opened the cellar door before she knocked. Word had travelled; word always travelled in Veymar, faster than couriers, faster than soldiers.

"They took him," Rosalind said. Her voice came out in pieces. "They took the wheel. Everything. Marta, everything—"

"Not everything." Marta pushed her down the steps into candlelight and coal smell and the particular safety of underground places. "Show me your coat."

Rosalind stared at her.

"The lining," Marta said impatiently. "You told me yourself, weeks ago, that you sewed the decoded pages into the lining. You sew like a clockmaker — nobody unpicks your stitches without knowing they're there." She knelt, produced a knife, and slit the seam with three efficient strokes, and there it was: the record. Forty-one pages of Rosalind's smallest, steadiest handwriting. Coordinates. Dates. The border Anselm measured. The truth, folded flat against her ribs through every mile of this winter, through every cup of tea, through every hour at the bench beside a man who was at this moment in a cell because he had loved a record enough to die for it.

"It isn't enough," Rosalind whispered. She heard herself sounding young and hated it. "It's paper. Paper burns. Paper gets disbelieved. We built a machine to make time itself testify, and they carried it off in a box, and I'm sitting in a root cellar with — with notes—"

Marta took her face in both hands, the way she must once have taken Anselm's.

"My husband wrote those numbers first in a notebook that burned," she said, "and then on a wheel he hid inside a clock, because he believed only a clockmaker could read them and no one would think to look. He was right twice. Now they've looked, and taken the wheel — and they'll take it to the capital, where the victors' historians will examine it, publicly, on the record, because inspectors love nothing more than displaying what they've seized." A terrible, fierce light kindled in her eyes. "You told the inspector yourself, child: evidence wants to travel. Let it travel. And you — you know those etchings by heart, and you carry the guild's own certificate, and there is a hearing coming in that city that nobody in Veymar can stop you from reaching."

Rosalind looked down at the pages in her lap, at her own careful stitches gaping open, at the small sharp knife still in Marta's hand.

Outside, far off across the valley, the master clock began to strike four — the decoy wheel turning, keeping official time, telling the town the hour everyone agreed upon. She counted the strokes out of habit. All four, evenly spaced, perfectly false.

"Marta," she said quietly, "tell me everything you know about the road to the capital."
