## 16. What Can Be Carried

The cellar smelled of turnips, lamp oil, and the particular cold of earth that has decided to keep a secret.

Rosalind had been in it three days. She knew this not by any clock — there was no clock down here, which she found herself taking as a personal insult — but by the rhythm of Marta Anselm's feet overhead: four steps to the stove, a pause, four steps back. The widow walked her kitchen like a sentry, and Rosalind had learned to read her days by the percussion of her grief.

She sat on an upturned crate with her coat across her knees, the lining slit open with Marta's sewing scissors, her decoded notes spread out before her on a flour sack like the world's smallest and most dangerous alphabet.

Four hundred and eleven figures. Latitudes, longitudes, declinations, corrections for refraction — Anselm's true survey of the valley, micro-etched onto the escapement wheel of a master clock, readable only by someone who knew how to count teeth and trust nothing. She had spent eleven nights decoding it by lamplight while Elias ground a decoy wheel two feet away, and now the wheel was gone, seized from the dome, and Elias was gone with it, and all that remained of the truth was what fit inside a clockmaker's memory and a coat's worth of stolen linen thread.

*What can be carried.*

The question had been following her since the boathouse. It had followed her through the hedge, down the frozen lane, over the wall into Marta's yard while a whistle shrilled behind her and someone shouted a word she refused to repeat even to herself. It sat down beside her in the cellar and refused to leave, the way grief does, the way guilt does, the way a good apprentice sits when the master is angry and waits to be given work.

So she gave it work.

She took out the guild certificate.

It lay at the bottom of her tool case, where she'd put it the day the letter came — Master Aldous Vey's letter, carried by the last sled before the bridge closed, instructing her to certify the master clock *as found* and return to the workshop. The certificate itself was a handsome thing: heavy rag paper, the guild's seal stamped in wax the color of dried blood, blank lines awaiting her hand. *I, the undersigned journeyman, do attest that the master timepiece of the Veymar Observatory has been examined and found—*

Found what? That was the trap in the wording, and she'd seen it too late. *As found.* If she signed it, she attested that the clock kept official time — the falsified time, the four-minutes-slow time that anchored the annexation maps, the lie ticking away overhead in brass while a surveyor rotted under a verdict of *accident*. Her signature would be the last rivet in the cover-up. The guild didn't want her hands because they were skilled. They wanted them because they were trusted.

She had carried the certificate down the mountain in her case like a stone she couldn't put down. And now, in a cellar full of turnips, she turned it over and looked at it properly for the first time — not as an order, but as an object. As material.

Heavy rag paper. Fine tooth. Strong enough to take ink on both sides without bleeding through.

Strong enough to take very small ink.

Rosalind held it up to the lamp and felt something shift in her chest — not warmth exactly, more like the click of a detent engaging, the small positive *snap* of a mechanism arriving at its intended position.

"You've got that face," said Marta, coming down the stairs with a covered bowl. "My husband used to get that face. The one where you can hear gears turning."

"Can I borrow your pen, Mrs. Anselm? And your finest needle?"

* * *

Marta Anselm did not ask questions, which Rosalind had come to understand was its own form of eloquence. She brought the pen — a stubby steel-nibbed thing that had signed a widow's pension forms and a burial notice and, once, according to Marta, a complaint to the magistrate about the price of coal — and she brought her darning needles, and then she sat on the stairs and watched with the particular patience of a woman who had waited out worse winters than this one.

"The paper takes ink well," Rosalind murmured, testing on a corner of the backing sheet. "But my notes are pencil. Pencil smudges. Pencil can be argued with."

"And ink can't?"

"Ink can be forged," Rosalind said. "But not easily, not in quantity, and not in a hand they don't know." She flexed the steel nib between her fingers, considering. "The problem isn't making it legible. The problem is making it deniable."

Marta frowned. "Deniable?"

"If they find coordinates written on a sheet of paper, they burn the paper and ask who wrote it. But if they find—" She stopped, and looked at the certificate again, and smiled in a way that surprised her, because it wasn't a nice smile. "Mrs. Anselm, do you know what a guild certificate is, legally?"

"A piece of paper that says you're allowed to touch clocks."

"It says I'm allowed to touch clocks *anywhere in the colonial administration's territory*. It's a warrant, of a kind. It travels under seal. It can't be opened or inspected except by another certified member of the guild — that's in the charter, article nine, we memorized it as apprentices mostly so we could complain about it." Her heart was going fast now, the way it did when a mechanism first began to move under her fingers. "If I fill in the certificate — sign it, date it, certify the clock *as found*, exactly as ordered — it becomes an official document. Protected. And official documents have margins."

"Margins," Marta repeated.

"Margins, borders, the space around the attestation clause. Every certificate carries the standard endorsement block — chain of examination, instruments used, conditions observed. Dozens of lines. Numbers upon numbers upon numbers." Rosalind laid the certificate flat on the flour sack and smoothed it with both palms, the way she'd smooth a dial face. "Nobody reads the endorsement block, Mrs. Anselm. Not the inspectors, not the historians, nobody. It's the part of the document that exists so the rest of it looks finished. Which means it is the one place in the entire colonial administration where four hundred and eleven figures can hide in plain sight — disguised as calibration records."

Marta stared at her. Then, slowly, she began to laugh — a low, cracked sound, half sob, the laugh of a woman who had spent two years being polite to murderers. "Anselm hid his truth in a clock," she said. "And you mean to hide yours in the paperwork *about* the clock."

"Anselm understood something," Rosalind said quietly. "He understood that the people rewriting history would never bother to learn how anything actually works. They'd seize the wheel and ship it to the capital and lock it in a drawer, and it would sit there keeping its secret because no historian alive can read an escapement. So I'll do the same. I'll write the truth where only a clockmaker would think to look — and where only a clockmaker can prove it means anything. Calibration figures. Refraction corrections. To anyone else it's noise. To me it's a map of the valley as it truly is, line for line against Anselm's plates."

"And if they take the certificate from you?"

"They can take it," Rosalind said. "That's rather the point. Let them file it. Let them archive it under seal, in the capital, in the guild vaults, in whatever iron room they keep inconvenient papers in. A document under guild seal cannot be destroyed without a hearing — article twelve. They'll have preserved the evidence themselves, catalogued and dusted, waiting for the day someone comes who can read it." She looked up. "I'm not trying to carry the truth past them, Mrs. Anselm. I'm trying to make *them* carry it for me."

The lamp flame stood very still. Somewhere above, snow slid off the eaves with a soft whump, and Marta's kitchen clock — a cheap thing, thirty years old, kept running out of stubbornness — ticked on, indifferent.

"Well," said Marta at last, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Anselm would have liked you. He'd have pretended not to, he was terrible at compliments, but he'd have liked you." She stood and gathered the empty bowl. "I'll bring more lamps. You'll ruin your eyes, and then who'll read your clever margins?"

* * *

The work took four nights.

Rosalind worked the way she'd been taught to work — Master Aldous's voice in her head the whole time, *measure twice, cut once, and never let the work know you're afraid* — though she suspected that if Aldous could see what she was measuring twice and cutting once into, he would have taken the nib away and sent her to bed without supper.

First she drafted. On the back of Marta's grocery lists, in columns, she converted her decoded notes into plausible calibration language: *escapement amplitude, degrees; rate deviation, seconds per day; temperature coefficient, observed at—* Each of Anselm's true figures became a reading; each reading slotted into the endorsement block in strict numerical order, so that the whole ran continuously, unbroken, boring as ditchwater. She checked each figure three times against her notes, and her notes three times against her memory of the wheel, because memory was now the only other copy in the world and memories, unlike wheels, could not be seized.

Then she wrote. Small — smaller than she'd ever written, the letters scarce taller than the teeth of a watch pinion, the nib re-sharpened nightly on a whetstone Marta produced from a drawer of implements whose purposes Rosalind chose not to inquire after. Her hand, which had shaken the day the guild letter came, was steady now. She noticed this on the second night and found it strange: fear had made her tremble, but purpose had stilled her, the way a running engine smooths out the vibration of a standing one.

On the third night she reached the middle section — the border coordinates themselves, the ones that contradicted the annexation maps, the ones that proved Anselm had measured the valley honestly and died for the arithmetic — and she had to stop.

Not because her hand shook. Because her throat closed, and she sat in the lamplight with the nib poised, looking at the little figures waiting in their column, and thought: *these are the numbers he climbed for.* Every one of these was a morning on a mountainside, a plumb line in the wind, a man squinting through a theodolite at a horizon he believed should belong to everyone living in it. He had etched them into brass with a graver finer than hope. And Elias had guarded them. And now Elias was in a cell somewhere in the magistrate's back rooms, giving nothing, saying nothing, and the last thing she'd said to him through the boathouse door was his name, just his name, as if a name were enough to hold a man together.

"Rosalind." Marta's voice, quiet, from the stairs. "You've stopped."

"I'm carrying him wrong," Rosalind said, before she could stop herself. "That's the trouble. I keep trying to carry everything. The wheel, the dome, the clock, Elias, the whole ridiculous winter — and you can't, can you. You can't carry a person in a coat lining. You can only carry what they made. What they *meant*." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Anselm knew that. He didn't try to run down the mountain with his charts when they came for him. He put them where no one would think to look and trusted the future to send someone who could read. He carried the record, and let go of everything else, and it cost him—" Her voice caught. "It cost him everything, and it still might not be enough."

Marta came down the last two stairs and sat beside the crate. For a while she said nothing, which was her gift.

"My mother knitted," she said finally. "All through the war, through the bad years. And when soldiers came through, she'd unravel the sweaters and re-knit the wool with messages worked into the pattern — purl where the stitches should be plain. Anyone could see the sweaters. Only our people could read them." She touched the certificate, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. "Wool wears out. Paper burns. But while it lasts, it lasts *on purpose*. Finish your stitching, girl. Grief keeps. Truth doesn't, unless you sew it down."

So Rosalind bent back over the work, and on the fourth night she finished.

At the bottom of the endorsement block, beneath the final calibration figure, she added the closing lines every certificate required — *examined and attested under guild seal, this—* — and signed her name in her best round hand: *Rosalind Nakamura, journeyman, Clockmakers' Guild.* Then the date. Then, because the form demanded it, the certification itself:

*I attest that the master timepiece of the Veymar Observatory has been examined and found as found.*

As found. Exactly as ordered. Every word true, every word a trap for whoever read it carelessly — because the clock *had* been found as found: found tampered, found lying, found keeping four false minutes a night by deliberate design. Let the guild lawyers chew on that phrase until spring. Let them discover, someday, in some hearing room, that the truth had been sitting in their own archives all along, filed and sealed and stamped, wearing the disguise of their own paperwork.

She blew on the ink. She held the certificate up to the lamp, and the light came through the rag paper, and for one moment she could see both sides at once — the official text shadowed behind, the hidden figures glowing faintly in front — a document that was two documents, a lie wrapped around a map, an order obeyed so thoroughly it became treason.

"There," she said softly. "Carried."

* * *

She slept, for the first time in four nights, without dreaming of the boathouse.

She woke to Marta's boots on the cellar stairs — quick, wrong, four steps instead of the sentry's steady eight — and sat up with her heart already counting.

"Girl." Marta crouched, breathless, holding a scrap of paper. "Sled tracks on the north road this morning. Fresh ones. And Kessler's boy came by the back fence — the inspector's party is moving again. They're bringing prisoners down to the bridge." Her old hands closed hard around Rosalind's wrist. "They're bringing *him* down to the bridge, Rosalind. With the evidence wagon. First transport over the pass since the thaw started — tomorrow, maybe the day after."

Rosalind was already reaching for her coat, sliding the certificate into its inner pocket, feeling the small stiff weight of four hundred and eleven truths settle against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Then I haven't got until spring," she said. "I've got until the ice goes out of the river."
