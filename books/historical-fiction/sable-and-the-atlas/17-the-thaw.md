## 17. The Thaw

The cell had one window, and the window had one view: the north wall of the guardhouse and a slice of sky above it, which was, at least, honest sky. Elias Sable had spent thirty years learning to read the sky through instruments, and now he read it naked, without even a quadrant, the way a man reads a letter he once wrote himself.

Snow. Then sun. Then snow again. The thaw would come when it came. He could do nothing about that, and so he did what he had always done with things beyond his power: he measured them.

They had taken his watch on the first night. Of course they had. But they had not taken his pulse, and a man's heart is a clock of tolerable quality if you don't ask too much of it. He counted beats against his breath — four to an inhale, four to an exhale, sixty to the minute if he stayed calm, which he intended to, professionally.

*She got out.*

He held that thought the way he had once held a hot coal in tongs: carefully, at arm's length from despair, but never letting go.

* * *

The interrogations came twice a day at first, then once, then irregularly, which was worse. Captain Voss of the colonial constabulary was a patient man in the way that glaciers are patient, and he had a notebook of his own, though Elias doubted its contents would survive a century.

"Dr. Sable," Voss said on the ninth morning, setting down his tea with the click of a man who wanted you to notice he was comfortable. "Let's review. On the night of the twelfth, you were seen leaving the dome after midnight."

"I'm an astronomer," Elias said. "We're seen leaving domes after midnight the way fishermen are seen leaving rivers wet."

"You were seen carrying a case."

"Astronomers carry cases. It's practically our national costume."

Voss turned a page. "You were seen with the clockmaker."

That one landed, though Elias kept his face still. He had gotten good at stillness. Stillness was most of astronomy, really — standing in the cold, watching something enormous happen very slowly.

"The clockmaker was repairing the master clock," he said. "Under guild commission. You'll find the paperwork in order. I signed it myself."

"And then she vanished."

"She left. There is a difference, though I understand the distinction requires a conscience."

Voss smiled thinly and made a note. Elias watched him write and thought, not for the first time, that handwriting was a kind of clockwork too — every loop and stroke a small escapement releasing intention onto paper. Voss's hand was cramped and slanted forward, like a man perpetually leaning into a wind only he felt.

*She got out.* The bridge had been closed for weeks when Rosalind disappeared from the town like a note struck and sustained somewhere out of hearing. No one would tell him anything. That was the design, he understood — the not-knowing was part of the instrument, a weight applied precisely where it would hurt. So he did the arithmetic instead. Marta Anselm's cellar had a back wall that backed onto the drainage cut. The drainage cut reached the river path. The river path reached the old shepherd stairs below the bridge footings, passable in winter by anyone young, determined, and carrying less than forty pounds. Rosalind was all three. He had watched her climb the observatory ladder one-handed while holding a tray of tea, because putting the tea down first would have been admitting the tray was heavy.

*She got out.* He repeated it nightly like an observatory log entry. *Position: unknown. Velocity: sufficient. Condition: preferred.*

* * *

In the second week they moved him from the guardhouse cell to the magistrate's old wine cellar, which had been converted with the particular efficiency of administrations that expect short wars and long occupations. A cot. A bucket. A lamp that burned until ten o'clock exactly, because even cruelty keeps office hours here.

It was colder than the guardhouse, and damp, and the ceiling wept.

Elias discovered this last fact on his third night there: a seam in the stone overhead where meltwater from the slope above found its way through, gathered along a hairline crack, and fell — plip — into a puddle in the corner. Once every few seconds. Irregularly at first, as the ice above shifted, then settling into something almost steady.

He lay awake listening to it and realized he was smiling.

Because this was the thing no one had thought to take from him: time. They had his watch, his charts, his instruments, his wheel — seized from the dome in a crate stenciled EVIDENCE in letters so large they were practically a boast. But the drip was still running, and a drip, given patience, is a clock.

He calibrated it against his pulse. Roughly one drop per second and a half, drifting slower as the nights warmed and the melt above quickened — no, wait. Slower meant more water. He corrected himself, lying in the dark like a man correcting a student gently: faster flow, longer intervals between drops as the channel widened? Or shorter? He worked it both ways, argued with himself, lost, started over. It was the best conversation he'd had in weeks.

By the end of the first week in the cellar he could tell midnight within five minutes. By the end of the second, within two. The guards thought he was sleeping. Let them. A man asleep is the least dangerous thing in any building, and Elias had decided to be underestimated with the same rigor he brought to everything else.

He began keeping a calendar in his head, anchored to the drips. Day nine: bread fresher, meaning supply sleds had come up. Day eleven: new guard, talkative, mentioned the pass road being cleared "section by section." Day thirteen: the drip slowed dramatically, then stopped for six hours, then resumed — a freeze-thaw cycle, which meant the temperature had crossed zero and come back. Spring was negotiating.

Day fourteen, he named the drip. He called it Rosalind.

Not aloud. Aloud he said nothing at all, having learned in week one that silence was the only document they couldn't confiscate. But privately, in the ledger of his head, the drip was hers: steady, precise, slightly stubborn about its intervals, and utterly indifferent to the authority of the men who wanted the ceiling sealed.

* * *

Voss came on day sixteen with the crate.

Two guards carried it in and set it against the wall, and Elias's chest went tight before his mind caught up — because he knew the crate, knew the stencil, knew the weight distribution of a box built for the escapement assembly. They set it down and opened it, and there it was: the great wheel, Anselm's wheel, the truth of the valley etched in lines finer than a spider's ethics, sitting in straw under lamplight like a saint's fingerbone in a reliquary.

"Do you know what this is?" Voss asked.

"A gear," Elias said. "A very well-made gear. The guild does fine work."

"We believe it contains encoded survey data. Coordinates. Border measurements." Voss watched him the way a man watches a lock. "The capital's cryptographers will open it regardless. But it would go easier on everyone — on the town, on the clockmaker's guild, on certain widows — if you simply confirmed it."

There it was. Not a question about the wheel at all. A question about Marta, about the guild masters, about every name Voss had written in his cramped, forward-leaning hand.

"I'm an astronomer," Elias said. "I can confirm the moon's phase. Beyond that, you want a clockmaker, and your clockmaker has eloped with your evidence, which must be embarrassing."

Something flickered across Voss's face — not anger; calculation. He closed the crate himself, which Elias noted: the man didn't trust the guards near it either. Paranoia, in an administration, was a form of flattery. It meant they understood exactly what they were stealing.

"They're transporting it down with the first convoy," Voss said at the door. "With you, incidentally. The magistrate's office has requested the prisoners travel with the evidence. Chain of custody." He smiled. "You'll be reunited with your gear. All of it."

The door shut. The lamp burned down toward ten o'clock. And Elias lay in the dark doing the thing he did best, which was taking a statement and finding the error in it.

*Chain of custody.* Men who believed they had won did not bother with chains. Chains were for men who suspected the verdict might someday be appealed. Which meant someone above Voss — someone in the capital, some historian or minister with a longer horizon — was already nervous. Already covering the cover-up with a second cover-up, the way you paint over a crack and the paint cracks too.

And the transport would cross the bridge. The bridge that was, at this very moment, being tested section by section by the thaw.

Elias listened to the drip — one, one-and-a-half, one — and permitted himself, for the first time since the boathouse, a whole and unguarded feeling, which was this:

*Rosalind got out. And if she got out, she is coming back.*

He knew her the way you know a mechanism you've rebuilt with your own hands. He knew how she held her loupe between her teeth when both hands were busy. He knew she apologized to gears. He knew she had crossed a flooded river once already — he'd heard it from the talkative guard, gossip traveling faster than official dispatches as gossip always does — a woman matching the clockmaker's description, alone, at dawn, against every warning, going *up* the valley road when everyone sensible was going down.

Everyone sensible. Yes. She had never once been sensible in his presence, and he had come to understand that this was not a flaw in her construction but the entire point of her design.

* * *

Day nineteen, the drip changed key.

It had been falling into the puddle with a flat, watery knock. Now, suddenly, it rang — a bright, bell-like *tink*, the sound of water striking stone instead of standing water. The puddle had drained through some new fissure below, or the floor had shifted, or spring had put its shoulder to the mountain and the mountain had shrugged.

Elias sat up on the cot in the dark and laughed — actually laughed, out loud, alone, in a wine cellar, and did not care who heard.

Because he knew that sound. Every astronomer knows it. It is the sound of an instrument coming alive after a long cold night — the tick of metal expanding, the creak of a dome rotating, the first drop of condensation falling from the shutter. It is the sound the world makes when it stops holding its breath.

The thaw was not coming. The thaw was *here*. He could hear it working in the walls, patient as a craftsman, undoing in a fortnight what winter had spent three months building. Ice was a magnificent material and a terrible archivist: it kept everything, and then it gave everything back.

Which meant the convoy was days away. Which meant the bridge would bear weight soon — first the light sleds, then the wagons, then a wagon heavy enough to carry a crate stenciled in letters too proud by half, and a prisoner walking beside it in irons, trying to look like a man whose life was over.

Elias stretched out on the cot, folded his hands on his chest, and began, methodically, to plan.

He had no tools. He had no allies among the guards — the talkative one had been rotated out, replaced by a silent one who smelled of the magistrate's kitchen, interestingly. He had no idea whether Rosalind was fifty miles away or already in Veymar, whether Marta was safe, whether the guild had sold them all or was merely waiting, the way guilds wait, for the price to be right.

What he had was this: a body that still worked, a mind that refused to stop measuring, and a certainty he would defend to whatever passed in this country for a court.

They thought they were transporting evidence to the capital to bury it. They were wrong. Evidence, properly handled, is testimony in transit. Every mile that crate traveled toward the victors' historians was a mile it traveled toward people whose entire profession was *asking questions*. Voss feared the cryptographers would open the wheel. Let them try. Etchings a quarter-millimeter deep, laid in a cipher keyed to star positions only an astronomer and a clockmaker could reconcile — let the capital's finest men bend over it with their lenses and their certainty, and find nothing but beautiful, obedient, meaningless geometry.

Unless someone read it aloud first. Unless someone stood in a room where the record mattered and spoke the numbers against the striking of a true clock, so that the etchings and the hour confirmed each other like two witnesses who had never met.

For that, he would need to reach the hearing alive. For that, he would need the bridge to hold. For that, he needed—

*Tink.*

—her.

The drip rang on in the dark, steady as a promise, and Elias Sable closed his eyes and counted the intervals, and waited for the ice to finish giving everything back.

Three days later, they came for him with leg irons and a warrant, and told him the convoy left at dawn.

At dawn, on the river road below the bridge, a figure stood waiting in the flood mist — small, mud-splashed, unmistakable — and the guards hadn't seen her yet.

But Elias, lifting his eyes to the bridge as they marched him out into the grey morning, saw her first.
