## 18. Crossing at Flood

There is a moment in every repair when the work stops being reversible.

Rosalind knew it intimately. The instant the solder flows, the instant the old wheel leaves its arbor, the instant you commit — after that, there is only forward and whatever skill you carry with you. Her master had called it *the point of no apology*. You could undo almost anything in a clock if you caught it early enough. Past that point, undoing became a different act entirely: not repair, but confession.

She had passed her own point of no apology somewhere on the mountain road above Veymar, walking south through melting snow with Marta's notebook against her ribs and the guild certificate sewn into her coat lining, and she had known it, and she had kept walking.

Now, three days later, standing at the southern abutment of the Veymar bridge while the river beneath it roared like something with an opinion, she understood that she had not actually finished crossing anything yet.

The bridge was open. Officially. A courier had come up from the valley two days ago with the thaw's first post, and with it the news Rosalind had been waiting for and dreading: the transport would cross within the week, prisoners and seized evidence together, bound for the capital and the victors' historians who were already sharpening their pens. Marta had read the notice aloud in her kitchen, her voice flat as slate, and then had looked at Rosalind over the rim of her teacup with the expression of a woman watching someone decide something.

"You'll want to go down to the bridge," Marta had said. Not a question.

"I'm instructed to stay hidden until—"

"Yes," Marta said. "I imagine you are."

That was the trouble with Marta. She never argued. She simply laid out the truth like a tool roll and let you see which instruments you'd failed to pack.

* * *

The bridge itself was a thing of iron and stubbornness, built in her grandfather's era by engineers who had trusted rivets more than rivers, and for forty years the river had lost every argument with it. But this spring the river had brought reinforcements. The melt had come fast off the high passes, three weeks of snow giving way in one warm week of rain, and the water below the spans ran brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways, shouldering ice floes the size of cart wheels against the piers with a sound like slow drumming.

The engineer's notice, posted at the toll house, was admirably honest: *BRIDGE OPEN FOR OFFICIAL TRAFFIC ONLY. NO PEDESTRIAN CROSSING UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.*

Official traffic. Which meant the transport. Which meant Elias, in leg irons, crossing over water that wanted very much to swallow him before he ever reached the capital and the hearing where his testimony might matter.

Rosalind stood in the flood mist reading the notice twice, because the first time her eyes had refused to cooperate, sliding off the words the way they slid off a badly cut gear.

Against every instruction. That was the phrase she kept returning to, turning it over like a wheel looking for the flaw. Her master's letter had been instructions. Marta's caution was instructions. Even her own good sense — the part of her that measured twice and cut once, the part that had carried her through the guild's apprenticeship without a single broken movement — even that was instructing her, in its dry precise voice: *Wait. Let them bring him to you. Intercept the convoy on the valley road. Do not stand on a flooded bridge for any reason whatsoever.*

All reasonable. All correct. All beside the point.

Because here was what none of the instructions accounted for: the transport would not wait for the river to calm. The magistrate's replacement — a colonial administrator named Herr Falk, who smiled the way winter smiles — had moved the departure up twice already, and every day of delay was a day for the evidence in the wagon to be *reorganized*, a day for witnesses to be interviewed into silence, a day closer to the capital where documents could be misplaced with institutional efficiency. And here was the other thing the instructions didn't account for, the thing Rosalind had been circling for days the way a fly circles glass:

If she waited on the valley road, she saved the record. If she crossed now, she might save the man.

And she had spent an entire winter learning — in lamplight, over tea, across star charts spread between two cups going cold — that the choice was false. That the whole architecture of her life, every rule the guild had poured into her, rested on a lie of convenience: that duty and love were separate movements, requiring separate hands, and a person must choose which clock to serve. Anselm had refused the false choice, and they had killed him for it, but they had not managed to make him wrong. The data was still etched in brass. The record had survived because a surveyor had loved the valley enough to hide the truth inside the instrument of his own craft.

*Rosalind,* Elias had told her once, in the dome, his breath fogging in the cold, *the stars don't ask us to choose between precision and devotion. That's the whole lesson of astronomy. You can't chart a sky you don't love.*

So. The bridge.

* * *

She spent the evening before at Marta's kitchen table doing what she did instead of praying: preparing.

The notebook went into an oilcloth packet, and the packet went against her spine where sweat and rain couldn't reach it. The guild certificate — the forged-and-true document, her master's seal above her transcription of Anselm's coordinates, the most dangerous piece of paper in the province — she split. Half stayed sewn in her coat lining. Half she copied out in her smallest hand onto the back of her journeyman's card, which she tucked into the ribbon of her hat, because a girl crossing a bridge was unremarkable and a girl with a bulging coat was not, but no guard alive searched a hatband.

Marta watched all this with her arms folded, saying nothing, which was its own kind of eloquence. At last, near midnight, she spoke.

"My husband used to say a measurement isn't finished until somebody's willing to defend it." She set a flask on the table between them — brandy, by the smell, and strong enough to strip varnish. "He defended his for six years and it got him a boarded-up house and me a cellar full of turnips. I've regretted it exactly never."

"Marta—"

"I'm not advising you." Her chin came up, and for a moment she looked so like Anselm that Rosalind's throat closed. "I promised myself I'd never again advise anyone about courage. I'm telling you the ferryman at the north landing owes me for twenty years of clock repairs, and he'll look away for ten minutes at dawn if a young woman happens to need the walkway under the western rail. It's narrow. It's wet. Don't look down, or do, I don't care, just don't stop in the middle."

Rosalind looked at her. "Why? Truly. You owe me nothing."

Marta turned her wedding ring once around her finger, a small worn motion, like a hand winding a clock.

"Because my husband believed records survive people," she said. "I intend to prove him right, and I can't do it alone, and you, Miss Nakamura, are the only person I have ever met who treats the truth like it has moving parts. Somebody has to keep it running." She pushed the flask across the table. "Take this. You'll shake. Shaking is allowed. Stopping is not."

* * *

Dawn came grey and dripping, the mist so thick the far bank was a rumor. Rosalind stood at the south abutment in her muddiest coat and least remarkable hat, and listened to the river, and made her last measurements.

The transport was due at seven. She could hear it before she saw it — wheels on wet stone coming up the valley road, the clop of horses, the occasional flat order of a voice that enjoyed giving them. Six guards, by the sound. One wagon, heavy-laden, the kind of weight that meant seized equipment packed in straw.

Under the western rail, the maintenance walkway ran the length of the span, a plank path meant for painters and rivet-men, hung a body's width above the water. The ferryman, true to twenty years of debt, had unlocked the gate and then developed a sudden fascination with the inside of his shed.

Rosalind stepped through.

The planks were slick with spray. Below her, close enough to touch, the river went by in brown torrents, whole continents of foam, a pine trunk rolling past like a drowned giant turning in its sleep. She fixed her eyes on the next plank. Then the next. Then the next. This, at least, was familiar territory: one tooth of the escapement at a time, no skipping ahead, no looking at the whole terrifying mechanism at once. Just the next engagement. Just the next release.

Halfway across, she permitted herself one glance up — and found the wagon stopped at the far abutment, guards clustered around it, arguing with the toll keeper about the weight limit. And among them, leg irons clinking, coatless in the cold, head lifted into the mist like a man listening for a bell —

Elias.

Their eyes met across sixty feet of flooded bridge, and everything in Rosalind that had been steady for three days of preparation went suddenly, gloriously unsteady, and she understood why Marta had given her the brandy.

He started forward. A guard's hand landed on his shoulder. Voices rose; the mist swallowed words but not their shapes — anger, confusion, a question being asked that nobody wanted answered. And then the toll keeper's voice cut through, pitched to carry, performing outrage beautifully:

"Weight limit! I told you! Bridge won't take wagon and escort together — send the men across first, wagon after!"

Which was, of course, nonsense. The bridge had carried loaded ore carts. But the guard commander looked at the water, and the water looked back at him, and fear makes excellent arithmetic, and so the order came: prisoners across on foot, wagon to follow, single file, *move*.

They marched Elias onto the bridge. And Rosalind did the thing she would think about for the rest of her life, the thing she would tell her apprentice years later by the light of the restored clock: she stepped up out of the walkway, through the gap in the railing that the ferryman had oiled open for her, and planted herself in the middle of the span directly in the convoy's path, mud-splashed, hat-ribboned, five feet nothing of deliberate obstruction.

"Halt," she said, in her best guild voice, the one calibrated to stop machinery and men alike.

The guards halted, mostly because the world contains very few things more confusing than a small woman standing calmly in the middle of a flooded bridge at dawn.

"Miss Nakamura." The commander recognized her — of course he did; half the province had seen her face on the inspection warrant. His hand moved toward his baton. "You're under orders of detention. Step aside."

"I'm under no such orders," Rosalind said. "I'm under orders to deliver evidence to the historians' hearing in the capital, per the writ of the Commission on Records — signed, dated, and sealed." She drew the certificate's twin from her hatband with two fingers, unhurried, letting the morning light catch the wax. "You may verify it with Herr Falk. I'd suggest verifying quickly. Your wagon exceeds the posted load limit, and if the bridge fails under seized evidence, I imagine the Commission will want to know whose signature authorized the loss."

It was magnificent nonsense. The Commission writ covered papers, not wagons; her presence proved nothing; and the guards had batons and she had a hatband. She knew all of it. She also knew — the way she knew a trainwheel's true diameter by its song — that the commander did not know it, that men raised on official paper cannot tell real authority from confident forgery, and that every second she held them on this bridge was a second the mist thickened and the ferryman's cousin, conveniently hired yesterday as a toll hand, found fresh reasons to dispute the wagon's manifest.

Beside her, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers, Elias had gone very still. When he spoke, his voice was low, roughened by a month of interrogation, and threaded with something that was trying very hard to be wry and failing into wonder.

"You came back across the bridge."

"You counted the drips," she said. "We're even."

"I gave you instructions."

"You gave me a coordinate fix and a decoded ledger," Rosalind said. "Instructions were never part of it." She kept her eyes forward, on the guards, on the mist, on the far bank where the road bent toward the capital and the hearing and whatever waited there. "Anselm hid the truth inside the clock because he knew instruments remember what people are made to forget. Well. Here's mine." She tapped the hatband where the certificate lay against her temple like a second heartbeat. "Everything we carried out of that winter is on my person and in three other places Marta arranged, and it reaches the historians today, with or without your cooperation, gentlemen. So the only question left is whether the prisoner crosses as a suspect or as a witness. I'd choose witness. Historians remember those more kindly."

Silence, except the river, hammering its slow drums against the piers.

And then, upstream, around the bend, came a sound that made every man on the bridge go rigid: a deep official horn, and the chuff of engines — the Commission's own launch, fighting upriver through the flood, flying the victors' colors, sent ahead to meet the transport. Sent ahead, Rosalind realized with a lurch that was half terror and half laughter, by whoever Marta's third arrangement had reached. The current swung the boat toward the span, and a voice hailed the bridge through a speaking trumpet:

"Convoy from Veymar! Prepare to transfer prisoner and evidence for direct conveyance to the Guildhall! All prior routing revoked!"

All prior routing revoked. The commander stared at the launch, at the woman in the mud-splashed coat, at the prisoner who was smiling — actually smiling, cracked lips and all — and made the expression of a man discovering that the mechanism he'd been winding all along had belonged to someone else.

"Very well," he said at last, heavily, and gestured Elias forward. "Cross. Both of you. Before the river changes its mind."

They crossed side by side, the planks singing underfoot, the mist closing behind them like a door. At the far rail, where the bank began, Elias paused and looked back once at the bridge — at the brown water, at the iron that had held, at the whole improbable span of it.

"It held," he said.

"Bridges usually do," said Rosalind Nakamura, keeper of hours, "when someone's willing to stand on them."

Ahead, the launch bumped against the landing, and an officer in Commission grey came up the steps with a leather folio under his arm, calling out a name — *her* name — and asking whether she was the clockmaker who wished to enter evidence at the hearing.

Rosalind squared her shoulders, felt the notebook against her spine and the certificate against her heart, and said:

"Yes. And I have quite a lot to enter."
