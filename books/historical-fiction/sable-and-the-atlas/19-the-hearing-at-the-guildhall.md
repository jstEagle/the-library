## 19. The Hearing at the Guildhall

### Rosalind

The Guildhall in the capital had been built by men who believed that marble could make a lie sound true. Rosalind felt it the moment she crossed the threshold — the hush of it, the way voices fell half a tone softer and rose half a tone more carefully, as though the building itself were listening for anything that might need correcting later.

She stood in the anteroom with her coat still buttoned to the throat, travel dust on her hem, and the certificate pressed flat inside its oilcloth sleeve against her ribs. Sewn into the lining of that same coat were her notes: Anselm's coordinates transcribed in her smallest hand, the etchings decoded line by line across four nights in Marta's cellar by candlelight, her eyes burning, her fingers cramping around the pencil until Marta pried it away and made her drink tea.

"You look," Marta had said, threading the last stitch through the lining, "like a woman carrying a bomb made of paper."

"It's only accurate," Rosalind had answered.

Marta had smiled for the first time since Rosalind had known her — small, tired, real. "Yes. That's what I said."

Now, in the anteroom, an under-secretary with a clipboard was explaining the procedure to her as one explains weather to a child. The historians of the Commission would convene at ten. She would be given twenty minutes. Testimony would be entered on the record or struck from it at their discretion. Questions would be put to her afterward, if time allowed.

"And the transport?" Rosalind asked.

The under-secretary consulted his clipboard. "From Veymar? Delayed at the river crossing. Flood water. They may arrive this afternoon. Or tomorrow." He said it lightly, the way one says a shipment of coal is delayed, and something in Rosalind's chest went cold and hard as a bearing seized without oil.

*They may arrive this afternoon.* Elias, locked in a prison wagon with the thaw turning every road to soup. The wheel — *the wheel*, the whole truth of the valley etched into brass fine as spider silk — rattling in a crate marked EVIDENCE between him and the armed escort.

"Then I'll speak anyway," she said. "I'll begin when you're ready."

"That isn't quite—"

"I walked three days ahead of your schedule," Rosalind said, in the voice she used on apprentices who claimed a mainspring couldn't possibly be dirty, *look, I cleaned it last month*. "Your schedule has already been flooded. Mine hasn't."

He blinked at her, decided she was mad, and opened the doors.

* * *

The hearing chamber was a half-circle of desks beneath tall windows, and behind those desks sat seven historians of the victors' commission — five men, two women, inkwells aligned like soldiers. At the center sat the senior historian, a woman named Corvane with grey at her temples and the particular patience of someone who had heard a thousand testimonies and believed perhaps nine of them.

"State your name and trade," Corvane said.

"Rosalind Nakamura. Journeyman clockmaker, third bench of the Veymar guild chapter." Third bench — she'd earned that rank at nineteen, youngest ever, and had never once been permitted to forget it either.

"A clockmaker." One of the men at the far end — Hessler, by his nameplate — did not bother hiding his smile. "And you've come to testify about borders?"

"About time," Rosalind said. "Which is where the borders are written."

Corvane lifted one finger, and the murmur along the desks stopped. "Proceed."

Rosalind unwrapped the oilcloth and laid the guild certificate on the table before them. The document she had been ordered to sign — *certify the clock as found* — and had signed, in fair round letters, exactly as instructed. Her master's letter lay beside it, with its careful instruction. Both authentic. Both damning, if you knew where to look.

"This is my certification of the master clock at the Veymar observatory," she said. "Dated the twelfth of March. You will notice the figures recorded here — the daily rate, the amplitude, the error per day." She turned the page toward them. "Four minutes slow. Every night. Precisely four minutes, not drifting, not wandering — *maintained*. A clock does not lose exactly four minutes a night unless something makes it. Wear scatters. Deliberate alteration holds its line."

Hessler leaned back. "A faulty mechanism proves nothing but a faulty mechanism."

"No," Rosalind agreed. "It proves nothing. It *records* everything. Because the correction applied to the escapement was keyed to the observatory's survey chronometers — the instruments used to fix the coordinates on the annexation maps. Every boundary stone in that valley was set by a time signal passed through that clock. If the clock ran four minutes slow, then every longitude derived from it shifted. Not enough to notice with the eye. Enough to move a border." She looked down the row of faces. "Enough to move it onto land that didn't belong to the Crown. Enough to make a dead man's charts wrong by law, so no one would ever have to ask why he died measuring the difference."

The chamber had gone very quiet. Somewhere above, a pigeon shuffled on a windowsill, indifferent to history.

Corvane's pen had not moved in some time. "You claim the maps of record are falsified."

"I claim the maps of record are *derived*," Rosalind said, "from a clock that was made to lie. And I can prove which version is true, because the man who found out hid the proof where he knew only one kind of person would ever think to look." She drew a breath. "In the clock itself."

* * *

### Elias

The prison wagon smelled of wet canvas, horse, and the particular sourness of fear that had been living too long in a small space. Elias had catalogued all three smells over eleven days the way he once catalogued variable stars: precisely, because precision kept the mind from doing anything worse than counting.

Across from him, wedged among the crates, sat the evidence box with its official seal — and beneath the seal, packed in straw, the escapement wheel of the Veymar master clock. Anselm's wheel. Elias had spent six weeks watching inspectors photograph it, weigh it, argue about whether micro-etched lines on a gear tooth could constitute anything but scratches. Six weeks of saying nothing, because the man who understood the etchings was currently sewing secrets into her coat lining in a widow's cellar, and his silence was the wall that bought her time.

The wagon lurched through floodwater. Water came over the floorboards in a thin sheet, and one of the escorts swore and shifted his rifle higher.

"You're staring at the box again," said the young guard opposite — Teller, the talkative one, who had decided somewhere outside Aldenford that the prisoner was less frightening than boredom.

"I'm an astronomer," Elias said. "I stare at things. It's most of the profession."

"They say there's a hearing today. In the capital. Some clockwoman testifying." Teller shrugged. "About your valley. About the surveyor." He lowered his voice with the delicious caution of a man passing rumor. "They say she's reading numbers off a gear."

Elias went very still. Outside, the river roared past the wheels, brown and swollen, carrying winter downhill in pieces.

Reading numbers off a gear.

Only one person alive could do that. Only one person had spent the nights learning Anselm's notation — the surveyor's private shorthand, the coordinates hidden in plain sight on the rim of the escape wheel, each tooth a digit, each digit a degree and minute that matched the stars Elias himself had verified from the dome while Anselm was still alive and laughing about how no administrator in history had ever learned to read brass.

"She'd have to be certain," Elias said carefully. "To read them aloud. In front of historians. If she read them wrong, they'd take it apart as forgery and bury it deeper than before."

Teller grinned. "Then let's hope she counts well."

The wagon hit a rut. Water sloshed. Elias put his hand flat against the evidence crate, felt the cold of the brass through the wood, and for the first time in eleven days let himself believe the sentence he had refused to finish:

*She got out. She's already there.*

"Hold together," he murmured to the wheel, to Anselm, to the road ahead. "Almost home."

### The Magistrate

Voss had dressed in his second-best coat, which told anyone who understood such things everything. His best coat was for occasions where he intended to be seen; the second-best was for occasions where he intended, finally, after years, to be *heard*.

He had traveled ahead of the transport on the magistrate's warrant — his own signature, the first document he had signed in nine years without first calculating whom it might offend. The clerk at the Commission office had raised an eyebrow at the wording: *Magistrate Voss of Veymar district requests to appear before the historians' hearing as witness, voluntarily, on matters touching the records of his jurisdiction.*

Voluntarily. As though there were any other kind left to him now. As though he had not spent nine years signing the other kind.

He sat in the public gallery of the Guildhall chamber, hat on his knees, and watched the Nakamura girl stand alone before the desks with her certificate spread out like a surgical diagram, and he thought: *she is twenty-three years old, and she is doing what I should have done at thirty-five, with a dead man's blood on my signature and nothing but my own cowardice between me and this room.*

When she reached the part about the surveyor — "Anselm Brandt, whose death was ruled accidental on a date I have since confirmed falls three days *after* his final entry in his field notebook, a notebook I hold here" — Voss felt the old fear rise in him, the familiar cold, the voice that had whispered for nine years: *you have a family, you have a position, you signed what was put in front of you, everyone signs what is put in front of them.*

And then he looked at the empty chair reserved for the transported witnesses, and at the girl who would not sit down, and he thought of his daughter asking, just last winter, why the church clock and the observatory clock never agreed, and him telling her clocks disagree all the time, little one, it means nothing.

It meant everything. It had always meant everything.

Corvane's voice cut the air. "Madam Nakamura, your testimony rests substantially on etched markings you say exist on a wheel currently in transit. We cannot enter testimony on evidence we cannot examine. Until that wheel arrives—"

"It's arriving now," said someone from the doorway. "Or drowning in the attempt."

Every head turned. Voss stood up before he had decided to, his heart hammering like a schoolboy's, and said, into the hush, with a voice that shook and did not stop:

"The transport is at the river gate. I rode ahead to say so. And I request — formally, on the record — to amend my prior certifications regarding the Veymar district." He swallowed. All nine years came up his throat at once. "They were signed under duress of a kind that leaves no marks. I would like the record to show that I am the one who ordered the clock altered. Four minutes a night. On instructions from the administration. And that I am prepared to say so in whatever words this Commission requires."

The silence that followed had weight, mass, gravity. Somewhere in it, Voss thought distantly: *so that's what absolution feels like. Like falling.*

### Rosalind

They brought the transport through the river gate at half past two, and Rosalind heard it before she saw it — the groan of wheels in floodwater, the shouts of drivers, and beneath it all, faintly, absurdly, the chime of the capital's cathedral clock striking the quarter hour, late by nearly a minute, because even capitals drift when nobody tends them.

They brought Elias in first, manacled, thinner, a bruise gone yellow-green along his jaw. When his eyes found hers across the chamber, he did not smile — there were guards, and historians, and nine years of caution in the set of his shoulders. But he inclined his head a fraction, the way he did when a star he'd predicted appeared exactly on schedule, and Rosalind's breath went ragged and came back level.

Then the crate. They set it on the trestle table before the Commission and broke the official seal with a ceremonial knife, and Hessler said something about theatricals, and Corvane said, "Open it."

Straw. Packing cloth. And the wheel.

Even now, even seized and boxed and handled by strangers, it was beautiful — the escape wheel of a great regulator, brass gone soft-gold with age, teeth machined to tolerances Anselm Brandt had coaxed out of a mountain workshop with hand tools and stubbornness. To the room it was a gear. To Rosalind it was a page.

"Examine it however you wish," she said. "But you will want it moving. The etchings sit along the tooth flanks, and they resolve under raking light at a specific angle — and they mean nothing standing still. Anselm designed them to be read the way a clock reads them: *in motion, against time.*"

Corvane studied her for a long moment. "Explain."

"Mount the wheel in a movement running at standard seconds beat," Rosalind said. "Sixty teeth, one tooth passing the pallet per second — that's how an escape wheel works, gentlemen, it is the one gear in any clock whose job is to count time aloud. As each tooth passes, the light catches its flank, and the etching on that flank becomes legible for exactly one second. Read the teeth in order, in real time, and the etchings spell out coordinates. Anselm hid the valley's true border points in the very gear that counts the seconds — so that proving the map false and proving the clock true would be *the same act.*"

"And the demonstration?" Corvane asked. "What would convince this Commission?"

Rosalind took a breath. This was the part she had rehearsed in a cellar, in a wagon, walking the frozen road, and it came out steady because her hands, at least, had finally stopped shaking.

"At six o'clock this evening, the cathedral clock strikes — if someone climbs its tower and corrects it, which I have requested, and your own horological section has verified against the naval chronometer. At that same hour, we mount Anselm's wheel in the demonstration regulator your instrument-makers have kindly provided. Tooth by tooth, second by second, I will read the etchings aloud as they pass the pallet. Each coordinate I read will correspond to a benchmark stone your own survey office still holds on file — stones placed by Anselm Brandt in the year before his death. If the wheel is forged, the readings will fail against the stones. If it is genuine, then when the corrected clock strikes six, every number leaving my mouth will match the ground your own engineers once walked." She looked at the seven of them, one by one. "Time itself will testify. I am only its interpreter."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the Commission's instrument room, and it was the happiest afternoon of Rosalind's life, which she found ridiculous and refused to apologize for. Elias, manacles struck off at Corvane's order ("we cannot have a witness vibrating"), bent over the mounting arbor beside her, calling tolerances while she fitted the wheel to the regulator's train. Their hands overlapped on the same small screws. Neither moved away.

"Fourth wheel's a touch fast," Elias murmured.

"It's within half a second a day."

"We're establishing a border, Rosalind. Half a second is forty meters."

She tightened the screw and did not look up. "Forty meters, then. Say it again."

He laughed — quietly, under his breath, the first laugh she'd heard from him since the boathouse, since the snow, since everything — and the sound did more to steady her than all the certainties in the world.

At ten minutes to six, the historians filed in. At five minutes to, the magistrate Voss took his place at the witness table, grey-faced and upright. At two minutes to, the door at the back of the chamber opened and an exhausted courier from the cathedral tower delivered a slip of paper: *Cathedral clock corrected. Will strike six true. God help us all if it doesn't.*

Rosalind stood beside the regulator with Anselm's field notebook open on the stand before her, its burned edges curled like autumn leaves. Elias stood at her shoulder. The room held its breath.

The first stroke of six rolled through the Guildhall like a verdict.

"Tooth one," Rosalind said, as the pallet caught and released and the flank swung into the lamplight. "Latitude forty-six degrees, eighteen minutes, twelve seconds north. Longitude—" the second stroke "—eleven degrees, oh-four minutes, fifty-one seconds east. Benchmark stone, Anselm Brandt survey, upper ford of the Vey." She checked the notebook. "Matches."

Second tooth. Second coordinate. Stroke by stroke, second by second, the clock counted and she read, and the two sounds braided together in the marble air — the bell overhead, the tick below, her voice between them, steady as a pendulum. The historians' pens scratched. Someone in the gallery was crying quietly. Voss, when his turn came, corroborated each point in a voice that grew steadier with every number, a man setting down a weight he had carried so long his spine had forgotten its shape without it.

At the twelfth stroke, the final tooth passed the pallet, and the last etched figure resolved in the light, and Rosalind read it aloud, and it matched, and the chamber erupted — not cheering, nothing so simple, but the deep shifting roar of a record being rewritten in real time, clerks rising, Corvane's gavel coming down and being ignored, Hessler sitting with his head in his hands like a man watching his house settle into the sea.

Through it all, Elias's hand found Rosalind's at the edge of the table, and closed around it, and neither of them watched the historians at all.

"It held," he said, near her ear, under the noise. "All this time. It held."

"Of course it did," Rosalind said. "We built it properly."

Above them the bell finished its last echo. Below them the regulator ticked on into the new minute, unhurried, unashamed, keeping — at last, in front of everyone — the true time.

But Corvane was already leaning forward, pen poised, her eyes sharp as a loupe, and when the room quieted enough to hear her, she said the sentence that turned Rosalind's triumph cold:

"One matter remains before this Commission can close the record. Madam Nakamura — your certification of the clock *as found*. You signed it. We have it here." She tapped the oilcloth-wrapped paper. "Either you certified a falsified instrument to your guild, or you did not. Which is it?"
