## 20. Verdicts and Repairs

The guildhall smelled of beeswax and old paper and, faintly, of the lilies someone had thought appropriate for the occasion. I had never trusted lilies at official functions. They were flowers that attended funerals and hearings with equal enthusiasm, which told you everything about how institutions regarded both.

Three days had passed since the hearing. Three days since the master clock's wheel had been carried into the chamber in a padded crate like a relic — which, in a sense, it was — and I had stood before the victors' historians with my voice steadier than I had any right to expect, reading Anselm Voss's coordinates aloud while the clock struck the true hour behind me. Three days since the magistrate, Herr Aldous Brandt, had risen from his seat in the gallery with the expression of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten his own shoulders, and said: *I countersigned the annexation maps. I knew what Anselm measured. Ask me anything.*

They asked him everything.

Now I sat in the guildhall's side chamber, waiting to be summoned again — there was always another summons; bureaucracy, I had learned, was simply grief processed through carbon paper — and watched the light move across the floor. It moved at the correct rate. That was something. After months of a town living four minutes displaced from itself, the light in the capital arrived when it was supposed to.

A clerk came in, young, earnest, ink on his cuff. "Frau Nakamura? The historians will see you now."

"They saw me three days ago."

"There are matters of record," he said apologetically, as if the phrase explained anything. It did, actually. I had come to understand that *matters of record* was the guild's way of saying *we have decided something and now require your signature to make it look inevitable*.

I followed him down the corridor.

* * *

The hearing room had been rearranged since the climax. Where the crate with the wheel had sat, there was now a long table bearing Anselm's charts under glass, weighted at the corners with brass. His handwriting marched across them in that surveyor's script — patient, exact, unimpressed by the politics of borders. *The valley line runs here,* his pen had said, over and over, in numbers no one could argue with because they were not opinions. They were measurements. Instruments remember what people are made to forget. Anselm had known that. He had built his testimony out of angles and elevations, and then he had built it again inside a gear wheel, trusting a clockmaker would someday read it.

I looked at those charts for a long moment before anyone spoke.

"Remarkable work," said the senior historian, a broad woman named Frau Ostermann whose spectacles made her eyes enormous and kind. "The committee has voted to enter them into the permanent record. All of them." She paused. "You understand this will be contested. The families of the men who signed the original annexation orders are already petitioning. There will be appeals. There may be years of appeals."

"I understand," I said.

"It does not discourage you?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to a historian seemed professionally rude. "Frau Ostermann, I spent a winter trapped in a town where everyone politely pretended a man hadn't been murdered and a clock hadn't been tampered with. Appeals do not frighten me. I have seen what silence costs. It is much more expensive than argument."

Something warmed in her enormous eyes. "Your master trained you well."

"My master trained me to repair clocks," I said. "The rest I learned from a man who kept hours nobody asked for." I did not say Elias's name. Not because I was protecting him — the whole capital knew his name by now; the newspapers had made him *The Astronomer Who Refused to Lie*, which he found mortifying and I found hilarious — but because some things belonged to me alone, and the way his hand had found mine under the table when the vote was announced was one of them.

"Then let us turn to less pleasant business," Ostermann said, and slid a paper across the table.

I recognized the guild's letterhead. My stomach performed a small, precise maneuver it had learned during the inspection crisis.

"The Clockmakers' Guild," she read, "wishes to offer you full partnership. Effective immediately. Workshop of your own, first refusal on observatory contracts, apprentices assigned at your discretion." She tapped the page. "There is a condition."

"There is always a condition."

"*Discretion.*" She pronounced it carefully, the way one handles a cracked movement. "The guild's council feels that given the... publicity... surrounding the case, a partner should refrain from public statements, publications, or testimony regarding the Veymar affair going forward. Let the historians handle history. Let the clockmakers handle clocks."

I looked at the paper. Fine vellum, beautiful engraving, the guild seal pressed deep into the wax. Everything about it was excellent except its meaning.

It was, I reflected, the same document in a different key as the letter my master had sent up the mountain on the last sled. *Certify the clock as found. Return. Do not make trouble.* He had not written that letter out of wickedness. He had written it out of fear, which is the same material cowardice is forged from, hammered thin and polished until it shines like duty. The guild had been making that alloy for two hundred years.

And I had spent a winter learning exactly what it weighed.

"May I ask," I said, "who on the council drafted this?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to me whether it was the same hands that sent me to Veymar knowing what the clock had been altered to hide." Because I had worked that out too, somewhere between Marta's cellar and the crossing at flood. The guild had known. Perhaps not all of it, perhaps not the murder, but enough. They had sent an apprentice — an apprentice, expendable, deniable — to certify the falsification, expecting either compliance or irrelevance. Instead they had received testimony.

Ostermann's face did something complicated. "Rosalind. You are twenty-three years old. This partnership is worth more than most clockmakers earn in a lifetime."

"Yes," I agreed pleasantly. "How much is discretion worth?"

Silence. Somewhere down the hall a door closed with the soft authority of institutional architecture.

"You could take it," she said quietly. "Take the partnership and ignore the clause. Everyone does. Clauses are not enforced until they're inconvenient, and by then you'll have a workshop, standing, students—"

"Students." I turned the word over. "That's the part that tempts me, you know. Not the workshop. The idea of teaching. But I cannot teach children to keep true time and also teach them to keep quiet about it. Those lessons cancel each other out like opposing forces on a balance wheel. Everything stops."

I pushed the paper back across the table. Gently. I had learned gentleness from watching what rough handling did to delicate things.

"Tell the council thank you," I said, "and tell them I am returning to Veymar, where there is a clock that needs recalibrating after everything done to it, and an observatory that needs its astronomer back, and a valley full of people who deserve to know what hour it is. When the guild can offer partnership without a gag stitched into the wax seal, I will consider it. I suspect," I added, rising, "that day is coming. Fear doesn't hold its calibration forever either."

At the door I paused. "And Frau Ostermann — when the appeals come, and they will — cite the escapement. Cite the etchings. Make them argue with a dead surveyor's engraving tools. It is very hard to win an argument against a machine that was only ever telling the truth."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly suppressed, historian to the bone — but her eyes said she would.

* * *

The magistrate resigned on Thursday.

I know because it was in the papers, and because Marta Voss wrote to tell me, her letters arriving now with the regularity of something finally unobstructed. *He came to the house,* she wrote. *Stood in the doorway where they stood the night they told me my husband fell off the pass road. He said, "I signed it, Marta. I want you to hear it from me and not the newspapers." I offered him tea. He cried into it. I have decided to forgive him, but slowly, the way ice goes off a window — you cannot rush it or the glass cracks.*

I read that passage four times. *Forgive him, but slowly, the way ice goes off a window.* Marta had a novelist in her that tragedy had never permitted an outlet. I copied the sentence into my notebook, where I kept sentences that were better than anything I could build myself.

Brandt's resignation letter was printed in full beneath the article. It was, everyone agreed, an extraordinary document — plain, unsparing, naming names including his own. *I was afraid,* he wrote, near the end. *I tell you this not to excuse myself but to warn you: fear is the cheapest ink in the world and the most expensive to erase. Every compromise I made seemed reasonable at the scale of a single evening. Added together, they were the death of a good man and twenty years of a lie. Do not measure your courage by what you refuse in one dramatic moment. Measure it by what you sign on an ordinary Tuesday.*

I clipped that column and kept it. Someday, I thought, an apprentice of mine might need it more than a lesson on jeweling pivots.

Not everyone in Veymar forgave him at all, of course. The town's opinion divided along lines older than the cover-up — the families who had benefited from the annexation contracts, the families who had lost their land claims when Anselm's true charts were buried. There were shouting matches outside the church that Pastor Lindqvist handled with the weary skill of a man who had spent decades mediating disputes over fence posts and was now mediating disputes over justice. Some people wanted Brandt tried. Some wanted him hanged. A few — mostly the widows, in my observation, the ones who understood grief's arithmetic — wanted exactly what he had done: to stand in the doorway, say the truth plainly, and accept whatever came.

Justice entered, I wrote in my journal that night. Not completed. There is a difference, and the difference is the rest of everyone's life.

* * *

Elias was released on a grey Tuesday with nothing but the clothes he'd been taken in, a folder of papers the interrogators had not managed to break him with (they had tried; the marks of their trying were on him in ways I catalogued helplessly and he refused to discuss), and a cough he had developed in the holding cells that he insisted was nothing.

It was not nothing. I was a clockmaker; I diagnosed mechanisms for a living, and I could hear the new rattle in him, the way his breathing caught on cold mornings like a train engaging a grade it used to take at speed.

We stayed in the capital a week longer than we wanted to — there were affidavits to sign, copies of the decoded ledger to deposit with the historical commission ("in triplicate," said the clerk, and Elias muttered, "of course in triplicate, why would truth ever travel light"), and a ceremony I begged my way out of attending, in which the commission presented me with a framed certificate of commendation that I accepted on behalf of a dead surveyor and then gave to Marta, because it belonged to her if it belonged to anyone.

But the real work of that week was quieter, and harder, and it happened in the evenings in the rented room above the chandler's shop, where Elias sat by the window and sometimes did not speak for an hour at a stretch.

I learned not to fill the silences. This cost me considerably; I am a talker when nervous, a habit the guild never managed to file off me. But I had watched him with the stars long enough to understand that his mind worked in long exposures — that things developed in him slowly, and that crowding the frame produced nothing but blur. So I sat across the room repairing the small traveling clock I carried everywhere, and I let the quiet be a shared instrument rather than an absence.

On the fourth evening he said, without preamble: "They asked me, in there, over and over — the same question, different words. Whether it was worth it. Whether the charts, the wheel, all of it, was worth what it cost." He turned his cup in his hands. It had gone cold some time ago; he drank cold tea the way other men smoked, absently, continuously. "Anselm's widow lost him. Marta lost twenty years. You nearly drowned at the crossing. I—" He stopped. Started again. "I lost pieces of myself in those cells that I don't think grow back. And the honest answer, Rosalind, the answer I would not give them because they had no right to it, is *I don't know yet*. Maybe it was worth it. Maybe worth isn't the right unit of measurement for any of this. Maybe some things are simply correct, the way a pendulum's length is correct for its period — not good, not profitable, just *true*, and everything else must arrange itself around that."

I set down my tweezers.

"Elias Sable," I said, "you have just described why I love you, using astronomy, horology, and moral philosophy, in one sentence, and I want it noted that you did this instead of saying the actual words."

He looked up. For the first time all week something moved behind his eyes that was not calculation or endurance — something lighter, surprised out of hiding.

"The actual words," he said slowly, as though approaching a delicate adjustment, "are these: I love you. I loved you over tea at the edge of the dome while we were both pretending we weren't. I loved you when you lied to the inspector with a straight face about the wheel's provenance, which remains the most alarming thing I have ever witnessed, because you did it better than I could have. And I loved you when you crossed the flood against every instruction, because I knew — sitting in that cell, knowing nothing else — that if anyone alive would refuse a false choice, it would be you."

"Well," I said, when I could speak, which took a moment. "That's entered on the record, then. No taking it back. Historians were present." I gestured vaguely at the empty room. "Metaphorically."

"Metaphorically," he agreed, and smiled — the real one, the crooked one I had missed through all the grey weeks, and for a little while the room held nothing but warmth and the tick of the small traveling clock, keeping honest time for the two of us.

Then the cough came back, doubled him over, and held on far too long, and I put my hand flat between his shoulder blades and felt the tremor in the mechanism and thought: *victory with a limp.* We had won. The charts were in the record. Anselm's name was being engraved onto a memorial stone in Veymar even now. And the man I loved breathed like a bellows with a cracked seam, and laughed too rarely, and woke some nights calling a name that was neither mine nor his, and there was no affidavit for any of that.

Some repairs, I understood, were not done with tweezers. They were done with time, patience, and daily oiling. Fortunately, time was rather my specialty.

* * *

We took the spring coach north, the slow one, the one that stopped in every village and took on farmers and chickens and one memorable accordion player whom Elias paid, with visible desperation, to stop playing. The mountains rose ahead of us the whole way, white-capped still, familiar as a signature.

At the last ridge before Veymar the driver reined in — the road was blocked by sheep, an occurrence Elias assured me was seasonal, civic, and eternal — and we stepped down to wait, and there it was: the valley, the town, the bridge reopened and swagged with new timber, and above it all the observatory dome catching the afternoon sun like a bright coin balanced on the mountain's thumb.

And beneath it, faint on the wind at this distance but unmistakable to a trained ear —

the master clock, striking the hour.

Four o'clock. Exactly four o'clock. I counted the strokes the way other people count heartbeats, and when the last one faded I found I was gripping Elias's arm hard enough to leave a mark.

"It kept time," I said. "All winter. Without me."

"You left instructions," he said. "You left eleven pages of instructions. Your apprentice — that boy, Tomas, the one who polishes things within an inch of their lives — followed them like scripture."

"Tomas." I blinked. "Tomas kept it running?"

"Tomas kept it running. Marta brought him soup. Pastor Lindqvist mediated two arguments about it. Apparently it has become a civic institution, the winding of the clock. People come to watch. There is a rota."

I stared at him. "A rota."

"A rota, Rosalind. Your clock has a congregation." He was laughing — really laughing, the sound slightly rusted from disuse but unmistakably genuine — when the sheep cleared the road and the driver called us back.

As we climbed aboard, I looked once more at the dome, and felt the strange double weight of homecoming: joy at what waited, and the knowledge of the work waiting underneath the joy. The clock needed recalibrating properly — a new escapement wheel, one bearing Anselm's data etched openly where any eye could read it, no more secrets in brass. Elias needed spring air and slow mornings and someone to sit with him through the nights the cells still visited. And I needed — I was only beginning to learn to name this — I needed to find out who I was when I was not anyone's instrument. Not the guild's. Not the cause's. Not even the record's.

The coach lurched forward. Veymar came toward us at the pace of a patient spring.

"First order of business," Elias said, settling beside me, "is tea. Second is sleep. Third—"

"Third is the clock," I said. "I know. Third is always the clock."

He smiled and closed his eyes against the sunlight, and the valley rolled up to meet us, keeping, at last, its true time.
