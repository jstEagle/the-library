## 21. Spring Calibration

### Rosalind

The thaw came to Veymar the way a confession comes to a guilty man — slowly, in drips, with long pauses where nothing seemed to happen at all.

Rosalind heard it first in the eaves of the observatory. For four months the mountain had been silent except for wind and the master clock, and now, all at once, the roof began to speak: *drip, pause, drip-drip, pause.* She lay in her narrow bed in the observatory's east room and listened to it the way other people listened to music.

*Instruments remember what people are made to forget,* she thought, and then laughed at herself for thinking in creeds before breakfast.

She had been back at the observatory eleven days. The hearing in the capital was three weeks behind them — the certificate entered on the record, Anselm's charts read aloud into the official transcript, the magistrate's resignation letter still being copied and recopied by clerks who could not believe their luck at having something interesting to do. Elias had walked out of the transport wagon under his own power, which everyone agreed was the important part, and had said almost nothing for two days afterward, which nobody said aloud at all.

Now he was in the dome, and she was going to join him, and between her bed and the dome lay forty steps of stone stair and one decision she had already made but kept making anyway, each morning, like winding a clock.

The decision was this: to stay.

Not as the guild's apprentice. She had declined the partnership — politely, in ink, with the word *discretion* underlined twice in their letter and crossed out once in her reply. Not as a guest, either; the season of guests was over. As clockkeeper. The title had been invented for her by a committee that had never invented anything before and found the experience dizzying. The valley needed its true time kept. Someone had to keep it.

She filled the kettle, carried it up the stairs, and pushed open the dome door with her shoulder because her hands were full.

Elias was standing on the ladder beneath the great clock, sleeves rolled, looking up into the movement's opened heart the way he looked at the sky — with the particular hunger of a man reading something written just past his ability to see.

"You're staring at it again," she said.

"I'm admiring it."

"You're staring. There's a difference. Admiration has an ending."

He glanced down at her, and there it was — the thing his face did now, the small unguarded movement at the corner of his mouth that had taken the whole winter and a trial to teach him. "Tea?"

"Tea," she confirmed, and set the kettle on the brazier they'd moved up into the dome in March, officially for warming the mechanism's oil, actually because neither of them wanted to climb down for meals anymore.

The new escapement wheel sat on the workbench between them, wrapped in flannel like something newborn. Which, Rosalind supposed, it was. She had cut and filed it herself over six weeks — brass from the capital, good stock, no secrets in the metal. And onto its face, before the teeth were finished, she had etched Anselm's data openly: coordinates, dates, the surveyor's own notation system, rendered large enough to read with the naked eye if you took the wheel down and held it to the light.

Anyone could read it. That was the entire point. It had taken her a full day to stop flinching at the idea.

"Today?" Elias asked.

"Today," she said. "If the weather holds."

"It's spring. The weather never holds in spring."

"Then we'll calibrate in the rain like our ancestors did."

"Our ancestors calibrated in temples with slave labor and excellent funding."

"Speak for yourself. My ancestors calibrated in a guild workshop with a leaky roof and Master Havel's opinions." She handed him his cup. "Drink. Then hold the light."

* * *

### Elias

He held the light.

It occurred to him, somewhere around the third hour of the work, that he had spent two years holding things in the dark — plates, records, his tongue — and that this was the first time holding a light had felt like the same vocation. The lamp threw Rosalind's shadow enormous against the dome's curved wall, hands moving with a steadiness he had watched for a winter and still hadn't gotten used to.

"Third tooth," she said. "Depth gauge."

He passed it. Their fingers overlapped on the handle for a moment longer than the transaction required, and neither of them pretended otherwise, which was new, and which he was trying very hard not to make a ceremony of. He had a suspicion that if he made a ceremony of it she would assign him a task involving the outhouse.

"The depth is true," he reported.

"Mm." She seated the tooth, checked it, breathed on it, checked it again. Watching her work was like watching weather decide. "You know what I keep thinking about? The decoy wheel. The forgery we made. Perfect, plausible, wrong."

"What about it?"

"It was the best work I've ever done, and I'm ashamed of it, and I'm proud of it, and I don't know where to put those two feelings."

Elias considered this. Below them, through the slit windows, Veymar was emerging from snowmelt in patches — brown fields first, then the dark seam of the road, then, yesterday, the first stubborn green along the river. Somewhere down there Marta Anselm was planting. He had visited her once since the trial. They had drunk tea and said Anselm's name without lowering their voices, and he had walked home feeling lighter and heavier in equal measure, which he was learning was simply what grief did when it started healing: it balanced.

"Put them in the same drawer," he said finally. "Labeled honestly. That's all a record is, isn't it? Feelings you can't throw away, kept where someone can find them later."

Rosalind looked up from the wheel. "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said and it was about filing."

"I contain multitudes. Fourth tooth."

They worked through the afternoon. At some point the rain arrived, exactly as forecast, drumming softly on the dome above them like an audience that couldn't quite applaud yet. At some point the tea went cold and neither of them mentioned it. At some point — he noted the time, because noting times was his whole character and he had made peace with it — she began to hum.

It was tuneless. It was possibly not even intentional. It rose out of her the way the dripping had risen out of the eaves, and Elias realized he had never heard her hum before, because humming required a person who believed no one was listening, and Rosalind had spent her whole life being listened to by instruments.

He said nothing. He held the light.

* * *

### Rosalind

The trouble with being off-duty, Rosalind discovered over the following week, was that she was terrible at it.

On Tuesday she reorganized the tool cabinet by function, then by size, then back by function, then caught herself doing it a third time and went outside instead. On Wednesday she walked down to Veymar proper, bought bread, and stood in the square for ten minutes watching the town clock — the little sister clock, the one that answered to the master above — strike noon truly for the first time in two years. A woman beside her remarked to her neighbor that the clock sounded different lately. *Cleaner,* she said. Nobody looked at Rosalind. Nobody knew. The truth was running underneath the town like water under ice, and only three people alive knew how it got there.

On Thursday she tried to be idle and lasted until two o'clock, at which point she climbed back up to the dome, where Elias was cataloguing star plates with the focused serenity of a man who had also failed at idleness and retreated to the thing he knew.

"We should practice," she announced.

"Practice what?"

"Being off-duty. We're both dreadful at it. You alphabetized your star charts last night. By constellation *and* by date. That's two systems, Elias. Pick a lane."

"I find the dual system more—"

"You fell asleep at the table. I put a blanket on you and you apologized to me in your sleep. To me, specifically. 'Forgive me, Miss Nakamura, the plates are misfiled.'"

He had gone slightly pink, which she noted with satisfaction and intended to bring up at intervals for the rest of their natural lives. "What would practicing look like, then?"

"Tea," she said. "At the edge of the dome. No charts, no wheels, no calibration logs. Just tea."

"That's what we did all winter."

"No. All winter we did tea *adjacent to* confession-adjacent conversations. Neither of us ever finished one. I'd like to finish one."

He set down the plate he was holding. Outside, the meltwater ran loud off the dome, and the light through the slit windows had gone gold the way it did at the end of April afternoons, and Rosalind understood suddenly that she had engineered this conversation on purpose and was now standing in it with nowhere to hide, which was, she supposed, how he had felt all winter.

"Ask me something," Elias said quietly. "Anything. I'll answer it completely. That's my offer."

She had prepared questions. She had, in fact, prepared them in a list, ranked, in her notebook, because she was who she was. But standing there, every question on the list seemed too small — *why didn't you trust me sooner, what was the interrogation like, do you regret any of it* — and the real question wasn't on any list, because the real question wasn't a question at all. It was a statement wearing a question's coat.

"Why did you keep the hours no one asked for?" she asked instead. "All those years. Before Anselm died, even. Everyone else in this valley let the clock lie and got on with their lives. Why couldn't you?"

He was quiet for a while. The rain ticked on the dome.

"Because my mother was a translator," he said at last. "Did I ever tell you that? Back home, before the annexation. She translated documents — land deeds, mostly. And when the new administration came, they brought their own translators, and hers were declared inaccurate, and then she was declared unreliable, and then she was declared nothing at all. Just a woman who used to translate." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "I was fourteen. I watched an entire language of record get erased in about eighteen months, and everyone around me behaved as though it were weather. Natural. Unarguable. And I swore — childishly, furiously, the way children swear things — that I would always know what time it actually was. Even if knowing was all I could do. Even if knowing changed nothing."

"Did it change nothing?"

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he looked at stars, which was to say with the understanding that what he saw was old light, evidence, a message that had traveled a long way to arrive.

"No," he said. "It waited. That's what knowing does. It waits for a clockmaker."

Rosalind discovered that her eyes were stinging and decided, with some dignity, to blame the lamp smoke. "That's the first confession-adjacent conversation we've ever finished."

"How does it feel?"

"Like a tooth that finally stopped aching. You keep touching the spot anyway."

* * *

### Elias

They installed the wheel on a morning in early May, when the bridge had been open a month and the road up the mountain was mud and wildflowers in roughly equal proportion.

It was, formally speaking, a two-person job and practically speaking a five-hour job, because Rosalind refused to hurry and Elias had learned — was learning, was in daily remedial education on the subject — that her refusals to hurry were load-bearing. She cleaned each pivot twice. She tested the drop of each pallet three times. When the wheel finally went in and the movement closed around it like a hand closing around a heart, she stepped back and wiped her hands on her apron and did not say anything at all for a full minute, and Elias, who had once been a man who filled silences with facts, let the silence stand there with them, all three of them, comfortably.

"Well?" he said finally.

"Listen."

They listened. Above them the pendulum swung, and the escapement released — tick — and caught — tock — and the sound it made was the sound it had always been meant to make, which was no sound in particular, which was the sound of a machine keeping honest time without commentary. Anselm's coordinates rode on the wheel's face in plain etched brass, turning slowly, visible to anyone who opened the case, hidden from no one, needing no decoding, no courage, no coat linings sewn with notes. Just a clock, telling the truth, in public, forever.

"Anyone can read it now," Elias said.

"Anyone who opens the case."

"Should we leave the case open?"

Rosalind considered this with the seriousness she gave all engineering questions. "No. Dust gets in. Truth shouldn't have to fight dust." She smiled — the small crooked one, the one he privately thought of as the escapement smile, the release-and-catch of her whole face. "Let them open it. Opening is the point. A record you don't have to open is a monument. A record you do have to open is a *conversation.*"

He wrote that down. She caught him writing it down and objected, and he informed her that astronomers documented everything, and she informed him that she had just made it up, and he replied that the best observations were made-up until verified, and she threw a cleaning cloth at him, and he caught it, and neither of them mentioned that catching it meant stepping close enough that the throwing had been, arguably, an invitation.

The bell struck noon while they stood there. True noon. Solar noon, verified against his transit observations, verified against her regulator, verified against the sun itself, which had never once been asked to falsify its position and had never once obliged anyone who tried.

Down in the valley, the town clock struck in answer — twelve clean strokes rolling up the mountainside through air that smelled of mud and pine and the green, impatient smell of everything about to grow.

"There," Rosalind said softly. "You hear that? The whole valley keeping true time. Every kitchen, every schoolroom, every court date. They'll never know how much arguing went into it."

"They'll know eventually. It's on the record."

"On the record," she agreed, and leaned her head against his shoulder, and he stood very still, the way he did with things he was afraid of disturbing — though he was done, he hoped, with being afraid of good things.

They stayed like that while the last stroke faded.

It was nearly a week later, on a bright windy afternoon with the door of the observatory propped open for the first time since autumn, that the knock came — hesitant, small-knuckled, the knock of someone young. Elias was below, restacking the woodpile with the methodical contentment of a man who had discovered chores; Rosalind answered the door herself.

A girl stood on the step, perhaps twelve, windblown, with a canvas satchel and the particular expression Rosalind recognized from mirrors and guild benches everywhere: the look of someone who had taken apart something at home and put it back together with pieces left over, and could not rest until she knew why.

"Please," the girl said. "Are you the clockkeeper? My mother says you might take an apprentice, come summer. If — if you think I'm worth teaching."

Rosalind looked at her for a long moment. Behind her, down the mountain, the valley glittered, keeping its true time. Ahead of her, somewhere in the unwritten years, a story waited to be told properly, from the beginning, to someone who would need it.

"Come in," said Rosalind Nakamura, clockkeeper. "Tell me your name. And tell me — what did you take apart?"
