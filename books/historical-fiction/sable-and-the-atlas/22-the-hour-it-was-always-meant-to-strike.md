## 22. The Hour It Was Always Meant to Strike

The girl's name was Ilsa Brenner, and she had taken apart her father's pocket watch with a fish bone.

"A fish bone," Rosalind repeated, turning the ruined movement over in her palm three days later, in the workshop beneath the observatory, where the light came down slanted and gold through windows that had not been clean since before the war and never would be, because Elias said the dust gave the light something to do.

"It was all I had," Ilsa said. "There was no screwdriver small enough."

"And so you improvised."

"My mother says improvising is how things get broken."

"Your mother," Rosalind said, "is right about most things and wrong about that one." She set the watch down on the bench, next to the oiler, the tweezers, the loupe on its cord — the same loupe, its brass worn to a soft shine at the grip where two generations of hands had held it now, hers and her master's before her, and one day, if she judged rightly, Ilsa's after. "Every tool ever made was an improvisation first. A gear is just a wheel that got tired of rolling away."

Ilsa laughed — a quick, startled sound, as if laughter were also something she'd taken apart without permission.

Rosalind liked her immediately, which frightened her a little, because liking was the beginning of obligation, and obligation was the whole weight of the world balanced on a pallet stone.

* * *

The workshop smelled of oil and cedar and, faintly, of tea, because Elias had long ago abandoned any pretense that the observatory's lower floor belonged to astronomy. He came down twice a day like a tide — once mid-morning, once at dusk — carrying a tray he refused to let anyone else touch, on the grounds that he made better tea than either of them and saw no reason to be modest about his one domestic virtue.

"Is she settling?" he asked, setting the tray on the corner of the bench that had been cleared for it years ago, a square of space no tool was permitted to occupy. The rule was his. He enforced it with the mild ferocity of a man who had learned, late, what small ceremonies were worth.

"She took apart a pocket watch with a fish bone," Rosalind said.

Elias considered this. He was greyer now at the temples, and there were mornings when his left hand needed a moment before it agreed to hold anything steady — mornings Rosalind did not mention and he did not explain, the two of them keeping that particular account between them the way they kept all their truest accounts: silently, accurately, without interest accruing. "With or against the grain?"

"With," said Ilsa, from her stool by the window, where she had spent the morning cataloguing screws into a wooden egg-tray with the solemnity of a bishop arranging relics.

"Then she'll do." He poured three cups. "Against the grain is how you get splinters in the mainspring. I've seen it. Ruined a perfectly good month of my life."

"You've never touched a mainspring," Rosalind said.

"I've watched you touch one for twenty-two years. That counts."

It did count. That was the thing about Elias Sable that the histories would eventually get wrong, Rosalind suspected — the historians were already circling, polite and hungry, wanting him to be a tragic figure, a martyr-adjacent astronomer, a man defined by the year the soldiers came over the pass. And he cooperated, mostly, because cooperation cost less than argument. But the man himself was mostly this: tea at ten, tea at dusk, jokes about mainsprings, and a stubborn, unglamorous devotion to the idea that a measurement was a promise you made to people who weren't born yet.

He carried his cup to the window, where the afternoon lay across the valley in long blue shadows. Down there, Veymar went about its business — the market square, the new school with its too-large windows, the bridge rebuilt wider than before, wide enough that no season could close it again. The town had voted on the width. Rosalind had spoken at the meeting, briefly. *Bridges are instruments too,* she'd said, and then stopped talking, because some sentences only need saying once.

"Ask her," Elias said suddenly, to Ilsa, nodding toward Rosalind. "You've been circling the question all week. I can hear it circling. It sounds like a bird in the chimney."

Ilsa went pink. "I haven't—"

"You have. You look at the master clock every time we go up through the dome, and you don't ask. That's a question wearing a coat." He sat down on the high stool that had been his since the year of the flood, the one with the wobbly leg he refused to let Rosalind fix, on the grounds that a man should keep one imperfection within arm's reach. "Go on. Ask."

Rosalind wiped her hands on the rag and waited. This, too, was part of teaching: the waiting. Her master had waited for her the same way, thirty years ago, in a guild workshop smelling of different oil but the same patience.

Ilsa set down her cup. She looked, for a moment, very young — twelve going on thirteen, all elbows and serious eyes, her hair escaping its braid in the way hair does when its owner has more important things to think about.

"Why does the clock matter so much?" she asked. "Truly. I mean—" she gestured upward, toward the dome, toward the great case of walnut and brass that stood above them all, keeping the hour whether anyone listened or not. "There are clocks everywhere now. The school has one. The station has one. Father says the capital ones are more accurate even. So why does *this* one matter? Why do *you* matter? Why did—" and here she faltered, because she was twelve and not cruel, but she was also twelve and honest, which is nearly the same thing at that age, "why did people die over it? We learned about Anselm in school. About the hearing. But nobody explains why a *clock*. Clocks just tell time. Time happens anyway."

The workshop was quiet. Somewhere above them, through two floors of timber and stone, they could all three of them hear it: the tick. Not loud. Never loud. But constant, the way breathing is constant — unnoticed until you listen for it, and then impossible to stop hearing.

Rosalind picked up her tea and held it without drinking.

"When I was your age," she said, "my master told me that a clockmaker's work is not to make time. Time makes itself; we're not needed for that. Our work is to make time *honest*. To build a machine that cannot be flattered, cannot be bribed, cannot be told what to say by a magistrate or a minister or a man with a sealed order and a cold smile. A good clock is the one witness that cannot be threatened."

"But people can," Ilsa said. "People were threatened. People signed things."

"People signed things," Rosalind agreed. "I nearly did myself. There was a form, once — a certificate. My own guild wanted my name on a lie, four lines of ink, and my hand shook over it like a leaf, and do you know what saved me? Not courage. I want to be honest with you, because you will meet courage in books and think it arrives dressed for battle. What saved me was arithmetic. Four minutes a night. Someone had bent the escapement to steal four minutes a night, every night, quietly, so that the maps drawn from the survey would sit inside a fog of wrong time and no one could prove the borders false. And I sat with the numbers, and the numbers did not care who I was afraid of. Four minutes is four minutes. Truth is often just arithmetic that refuses to apologize."

She turned her cup slowly on its saucer.

"The men who altered the clock thought they were altering time itself. That's the arrogance of power — it believes the instrument serves it. But an instrument doesn't serve. It *records*. And here is the creed, Ilsa, the whole of it, the thing I was given by my master and he by his, all the way back into the dark where names wear out: **instruments remember what people are made to forget.**"

"Instruments remember," Ilsa repeated.

"Anselm Voss etched his measurements into the teeth of an escapement wheel smaller than your palm, because he knew — he *knew*, dying, with the snow coming — that paper burns, that witnesses recant, that archives have fires both accidental and otherwise. But a wheel of hardened brass, turning faithfully inside a clock everyone believed was merely telling the time? No one looks at the heart of a working thing. They look at the face. They read the hands. The truth ticked overhead in that dome for years while men walked beneath it planning lies, and it never once lost its place." Rosalind smiled. "I have known many brave people. Some of them were gears."

Elias made a low sound from the window — the laugh he kept for moments when laughing fully would break something open that he preferred to keep closed. Rosalind glanced at him. He raised his cup an inch: acknowledgment, apology, love, the shorthand of three decades.

"So the clock matters," she went on, "because the clock is where the valley kept its conscience while the valley couldn't afford one. Because when I testified — you've read the transcripts, they make the schoolchildren read them — the proof wasn't my voice. Voices get argued with. The proof was the wheel, reading true against the strike of the hour it was always meant to strike. The clock spoke first. I only translated."

* * *

They climbed to the dome together at dusk, as they always did, the three of them now, though Ilsa still moved through the observatory like a guest at a shrine — careful on the iron stairs, hushed under the great ribs of the roof, her eyes going up and up to the slit of darkening sky where the first star was pricking through.

The master clock stood in its alcove, taller than two men, its case polished by decades of sleeves. The wheel inside was not Anselm's wheel — that one rested in the guildhall archive in the capital now, behind glass, visited by scholars and school parties, read aloud once a year on the anniversary of the hearing by whoever drew the short straw among the historians. The wheel in the clock was the second one, the one cut and etched in the spring after everything, its data engraved openly, legibly, in characters any clockmaker could read with a loupe and any child could read with a teacher. *For anyone to read.* That had been Rosalind's condition, and the commission had grumbled and the guild had huffed, and then the commission had done it, because by then it was politically wise to agree with Rosalind Nakamura, which she found funny in a way she never explained to anyone but Elias, usually at night, usually until they both had to stop and breathe.

"Wind it with me," she said to Ilsa. "Slowly. Feel for the point where it resists. The resistance isn't the machine being difficult. It's the machine telling you where it ends and you begin. Every honest instrument has that boundary. Learn to feel it and you'll never be fooled by a dishonest one."

Ilsa wound. Her hands were steady. Rosalind remembered her own first winding, the terror of it, the sense that the whole world's accuracy hung off her fingertips — and smiled to see the girl take it as simply as drawing water, which is what mastery looks like from the outside, and never from within.

Above them, Elias had opened the dome shutter. Cold air poured in, clean as rinsed glass. The valley below was lighting its lamps one by one, the way it did, the way it had, the way it would — Veymar keeping its evening the way a heart keeps its beat, without asking anyone's permission.

"Star check," Elias said, to no one and everyone, and bent to the eyepiece out of habit older than grief.

Rosalind stood beside the clock and rested her palm flat against the case, feeling the vibration come up through the wood into her bones, that patient metronome under everything. Twenty-two years since the flood-crossing. Twenty-two since the hearing, since the wheel read aloud, since the magistrate stood in the guildhall with his confession in his hands like something heavy he had finally set down. The magistrate was dead now, three winters gone; they had buried him in Veymar, and Marta Anselmsdatter — old, sharp, unappeased and appeasing in equal measure — had sent a single white flower, which Rosalind had understood completely and never tried to explain to anyone.

Marta was gone too now, two years past, and the notebook — half-burned, whole-hearted — lived in the archive beside the wheel, its scorched edges preserved exactly, because Rosalind had insisted that the burning stay visible. *Let them see what almost happened to it.*

"Rosalind." Elias straightened from the eyepiece. His voice had the particular quality it took on for observations worth noting. "Come look. The seeing is perfect."

She went. Of course she went. Through the eyepiece, a star sat precisely on the crosshair where Anselm's coordinates said it would sit, where it had sat for centuries, indifferent and exact, the oldest witness of all.

"There," Elias said softly. "Right where he measured it. Right where it will be when neither of us—" He stopped, the way he sometimes stopped, and Rosalind put her hand over his on the telescope's barrel, and they let the sentence finish itself in silence, because some endings don't need speaking, only company.

Below them, the minute hand climbed the last of its arc.

"Miss Nakamura?" Ilsa whispered. "Should we—"

"Watch," Rosalind said. "Always watch the strikes. Anyone can wind a clock. Watching is how you learn what it's saying."

The great clock gathered itself — they could feel it through the floor, the weight descending, the escapement counting the final seconds with the same indifference it gave to Tuesdays and to history — and struck.

Nine strokes, clear and unhurried, each one falling into the valley below like a stone into still water, ringing outward over the rooftops, over the school and the station and the wide new bridge, over the graves and the gardens and the market square where children who had never heard of annexation maps were being called in to supper by mothers who checked the time against the sound coming down off the mountain.

The true hour. As always. As intended. As kept.

Ilsa stood with her head tipped back, listening, and Rosalind watched her instead of the clock — watched the girl's lips move slightly, counting, watched her brow furrow and smooth, watched understanding arrive the way dawn arrives: not all at once, but completely.

"It remembers," Ilsa said at last, quietly. "Doesn't it. Even when we're not listening. It just — keeps remembering."

"All day," Rosalind said. "All night. All the years we're given, and all the ones after." She took the loupe from around her neck — the worn one, the inherited one — and held it out. "Which is why tomorrow we start with the escapement. Hands washed, mind empty, questions allowed. And Ilsa—"

The girl turned back at the stair, the loupe already glinting at her throat like a small bright oath.

"When someone asks you why it matters — and someone will, someone always does — you tell them what I told you. Then you show them the wheel. Words convince the convinced. Brass convinces everyone else."

Ilsa grinned — sudden, unguarded, twelve years old with a lifetime arriving — and clattered down the iron stairs two steps at a time, toward the workshop, toward the tools, toward everything that would be hers.

Elias came to stand beside Rosalind at the rail. Below, the valley kept its true time. Above, the stars kept theirs. Between the two, ticking steadily in the dark, the clock went on doing the only thing it had ever been asked to do, and the only thing it knew:

remembering.

"Same time tomorrow?" Elias said.

"Every time," said Rosalind. "That's rather the point."
