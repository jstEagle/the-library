## 1. The Weight of Water

The river took my mother on a Tuesday, and I have spent every day since learning how to be forgotten.

That is not a complaint. In Sablemouth, being forgotten is a trade skill, like rope-work or tanning. I learned it the way other children learned letters. By six I could stand in a doorway for an hour and no one would later swear anyone had stood there at all. By ten I could walk the length of Harrow & Vane's main wharf carrying forty pounds of cargo and be seen by no one worth remembering. It was not magic. It was posture. It was the flattening of the eyes. It was learning to move the way water moves around a piling — present everywhere, noted nowhere.

My name, in those days, belonged to the company.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have left of her. When I was indentured, the clerk at the Harrow & Vane counting house wrote *Ansel Kavanagh* into his ledger with a nib that needed cutting, so the K came out ragged, like something chewed. Then he drew a line through it — not to strike me out, but to fold me in. Above my name he wrote: *Harrow & Vane Trading Co., Indenture No. 114.* Below it: *Term, fourteen years or majority.* And that was that. I existed thereafter as a line item. A cost of doing business. When men spoke over my head about freight and tariffs, they spoke as if I were the crates themselves — weight without voice.

Fourteen years. I served nine of them before the winter everything changed, which means I had gotten very good at nothing happening to me.

* * *

February in Sablemouth is a gray thing. The river runs brown-green under skin-thin ice at the edges, thickening toward the middle where the current slows, and the sky sits so low you feel you could reach up and press your palm against its belly. The town huddles on the north bank like a man warming one hand at a fire he doesn't trust. Everything smells of tar and brine and the particular sweetness of rotting grain spilled between the planks.

Harrow & Vane's wharf jutted out from the warehouse district like a splinter driven into the river's palm. Three berths, two cranes, a forest of pilings furred with ice at the waterline. The warehouse behind it rose four stories of black brick, and above the loading doors hung the company sign — HARROW & VANE — TRADING IN ALL THINGS — which everyone in Sablemouth understood to mean *trading in anything you couldn't afford to lose.*

I was seventeen that February, though I carried it small. Hauling cargo on the morning shift meant burlap and timber and barrels of whale oil bound upriver, and it meant keeping my head down in both senses. Foreman Grieve ran the dock with a voice like a cleaver hitting bone, and he had a habit of looking through workers rather than at them, cataloguing only their usefulness. I made myself useful the way furniture is useful. Present when needed. Unremarked otherwise.

There were perhaps sixty of us on the regular shift. I knew the names the company used for them and almost none of the names their mothers had given them. That was deliberate too, though I didn't understand then what the deliberateness was costing me.

One of them was Tam Osgood.

Tam worked the second berth, big through the shoulders and soft everywhere else, twenty-two years old and already talking about saving enough to buy a share in a boat. He talked about that boat the way some men talk about a woman — endlessly, tenderly, with no evidence it would ever have him back. He was the closest thing I had to a friend, which is to say he talked at me while I listened, and once a week he bought me a meat pie from the cart by the Tollgate because, he said, someone ought to get fat off wages besides the directors.

"You're going downriver this spring," he told me, the third of February, hauling a crate beside me. "First thaw. My boat's going to run cotton out of Merle's Landing, and I need a hand who doesn't chatter."

"I don't own a boat's worth of silence."

"You own more than you think." He grinned. He had bad teeth and never minded them. "You're like one of them deep wells, Ansel. You drop a coin in and you don't hear it hit."

I said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't true.

The near-drowning happened eleven days later, on a morning so cold the ropes had gone stiff as wire.

We were taking on the last of a grain shipment off the brig *Cormorant*, and the wind had picked up out of the southeast, shoving chop against the pilings so the whole wharf breathed and groaned like something sleeping badly. Tam was on the gangplank with a barrel on his shoulder — against procedure, against sense, but Tam had been showing off for a stevedore's daughter all week — and the plank was iced where spray had frozen across it in the night.

I saw him go. Everyone saw him go, in that slow terrible way you see things happen to other people: the foot sliding, the barrel swinging loose, the body turning in the air with an odd gentleness, as if the river had reached up and simply taken him the way it takes everything in Sablemouth eventually. He hit the water between two pilings and the brown-green closed over him and did not even splash much.

What happened next happened in pieces, and I have assembled the pieces so many times since that I can no longer tell memory from construction. But here is what I know.

Grieve shouted. Men ran to the edge. Someone threw a coil of rope that fell short and was instantly dragged under the ice shelf forming along the piling shadow. Tam surfaced once — I saw his hand, just the hand, slapping the water — and went down again, snagged, most likely, on the chains and debris around the piling footings. The water there was eight feet of black cold over ten more feet of muck and chain, and every man on that wharf knew what the river did to a body in February. It kept them. The river in Sablemouth has always been less a road than a landlord, and it collected.

And I went in.

I did not decide to go in. I want that on the record, whoever's record ends up holding this. There was no moment of courage, no thought at all. One instant I was standing at the edge with my heart hammering like a fist on a door; the next, the cold had me whole and the world above the surface became a ceiling of silver noise.

Here is the thing I had never told another living soul, the thing I am setting down now only because everything else got set down too, eventually, whether I willed it or not:

The cold did not take my breath away.

It gave it to me.

I went under and the shock should have emptied my lungs — that's what cold does, that's what it did to Tam, panic drowning him faster than water could. But instead something older than panic opened in my chest, something I had no name for because I'd never had occasion to use it, and my heartbeat slowed, and my body went easy in the water, and I sank.

I sank on purpose.

Underneath, the river was quiet in a way the surface never is. Gray light came down in slanted columns. Debris drifted past — a plank, a dead gull, a woman's glove — all of it moving with the unhurried patience of things that have nowhere to be. And below me, tangled in the chain footing of the middle piling, Tam Osgood thrashed in a slow-motion fury, his coat hooked fast, bubbles leaving him in silver strings.

I reached him in four strokes. I remember counting them, which I still cannot explain. Four strokes and my hands were in his collar, and I got my knife out and cut him free of the hooking chain-link, and then we came up together, and men's hands dragged us both onto the ice-slick planks, and I lay there vomiting river water and listening to Tam cough his soul back into his body, and above me Grieve's face appeared, wearing an expression I had never seen on it before.

Not gratitude. I want to be clear about that. Grieve was not capable of gratitude any more than the warehouse was. What he wore was *recalculation*. He looked at me the way a clerk looks at a ledger line that doesn't add up.

"Kavanagh," he said. "You were under near two minutes."

Someone must have counted. Someone always counts.

"Longer than any diver should," said a voice behind him, and I twisted around and saw Mr. Vane himself standing at the head of the wharf.

I had seen Augustus Vane perhaps a dozen times in nine years, always at distance, always in the context of inspection. He was a narrow man, dry as August paper, with the kind of stillness that comes from never having needed to hurry for anything in his life. He wore a greatcoat worth more than my indenture contract's full value, and he stood now at the wharf's edge looking down at me — dripping, half-drowned, seventeen years old and suddenly, horribly, *visible*.

"How long can you hold your breath, boy?" he asked.

I didn't answer. I didn't know. I had never done it on purpose before today. All I knew was what my body had done, unasked, in the dark of the river — that old opening in the chest, that slowing, that ease, as if some part of me had been waiting underwater all along for permission to begin.

"She was a diver, wasn't she," Vane said. Not a question. "Your mother. Pearl beds, down south. Company records list it under your mother's trade. Drowned." He said the word the way he said *freight*. "Drowned nine years ago this month, isn't that right?"

"Yes, sir." My voice came out flat and small. I hated how small.

Vane looked at me a moment longer. Whatever moved behind his eyes, he filed it away the way he filed everything — carefully, in the dark. Then he turned to Grieve. "See he gets dry clothes. Dock pay doubled for the rescue." And then, already turning away, in the tone of a man noting a figure in a margin: "Might be use for a boy who can hold his breath."

They wrote it up, of course. *Worker rescued from drowning by fellow indenture, Ansel K., No. 114.* It went into the daily record, and the daily record went into the archive, and the archive sat in the low stone rooms beneath the counting house where the company kept everything it might someday need — including, as I would learn too late, the reasons certain people had to stop existing.

Tam recovered. He bought me three meat pies that week instead of one, and cried once, briefly, when he was drunk, and swore he'd put my name on his boat's registry when she launched. *"The Ansel Kavanagh,"* he said. "Has a ring."

"Don't," I said.

"Why not? It's a good name."

It was a good name. It had been my mother's name too, in a way — she'd married a Kavanagh who lasted one season and a letter, and after he went she'd kept the name the way she kept everything: close, uncomplaining, hers. Mara Kavanagh. Pearl diver. She had held her breath for minutes at a time in the southern beds, walking the bottom in weighted shoes, coming up with shells in her net and light in her lungs. She taught me the rhythm before I could write — *breathe low, breathe slow, let the water do the work* — sitting with me in the shallows of the summer river when I was four and five years old, my cheek against her wet shoulder, learning to be still inside my own chest.

Then she took a contract with a company boat working the northern beds, because the money was better and I needed feeding, and the boat came back in November without her, and the company paid out a diver's death benefit of four pounds ten, and a clerk entered *Mara Kavanagh, drowned* into a ledger, and that was the whole of her in writing. One line. Ragged K.

They never found the body. They said the cold keeps them.

* * *

After the rescue, something changed in how the wharf treated me, and it was worse than being ignored. Being ignored is a kind of shelter. Being *noticed* is exposure. Men nodded to me now. Grieve assigned me to water work — checking piling footings, recovering dropped cargo — and each time I went down, in the gray cold, with that old rhythm opening in my chest like a door I couldn't shut again, I felt the company's attention settle on me a little heavier. Vane sent for me twice that month. Asked questions. About my mother. About how long, exactly. About whether I'd ever considered that a talent might be *indentured property.*

"Company property works for the company," he said the second time, smiling with the bottom half of his face only. "Even talents. Especially talents."

I said yes, sir. I had said yes, sir for nine years. It was the safest word I owned.

But at night, in the bunkhouse, lying in the dark listening to sixty men breathe, I found myself doing the thing my mother taught me. Breathing low. Breathing slow. Letting myself sink into that stillness where the heartbeat drops and the edges go soft. And in that stillness I could almost hear her voice, not in words but in pressure, the way sound carries underwater — telling me what she never got to tell me in life, which was simply this: *you come from people who go under and come back up.*

She never came back up. That was the grief I carried, quiet as ballast, all those years. Not that she died — death is honest, death at least admits what it's doing — but that the river kept her, and the company recorded her, and between the two of them there was nothing left that was actually *her.* No face in any file. No voice on any page. She had been erased so thoroughly that even her son had learned to erase himself in sympathy, folding smaller every year, until by seventeen I was mostly a shadow with strong hands.

I thought that was survival. I thought being nothing was the price of staying safe, and I paid it gladly, and I would have paid it my whole life.

Then, eleven nights after Tam Osgood coughed the river out of his lungs on Harrow & Vane's wharf — eleven nights, I counted, because someone always counts — the storm came down off the hills and shook the warehouse like a dog shaking a rat, and in the white roaring middle of that night, somebody left a bundle on the company doorstep.

I was the one who found it. Of course I was. Invisible by design, awake by accident, the only soul in that black brick building still moving when the world should have been empty.

And the note pinned to the blanket, ink bleeding in the wet, read five words that ended the person I had been:

*Forget this face.*
