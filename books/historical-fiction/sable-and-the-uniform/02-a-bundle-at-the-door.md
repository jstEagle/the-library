## 2. A Bundle at the Door

The storm came up the river at dusk on the ninth of February, riding a wind that smelled of broken ice and something older underneath. Ansel felt it in their teeth before they heard it — a pressure change, a hush in the gulls, the water going flat and dark as poured ink between the pilings. Their mother had called that look *the river holding its breath*, and she had taught them to respect it the way you respect a sleeping dog with an uncertain temper.

By full dark the respect was due. Rain came sideways off the harbor in sheets that hit the wharf planks like thrown gravel, and the Harrow & Vane warehouse groaned on its timbers, a ship that had forgotten it wasn't one. The night crew had been sent home early. Ansel had volunteered to stay — volunteering was a thing unremarkable people did, and it kept them in the company's good graces, which was the same as keeping them fed.

They were re-tying the last of the tarpaulins over the cotton bales when they heard the knock.

It was a strange sound to hear at all. The company door — the front one, the one with the brass plate and the lion knocker that had gone green with salt — was for gentlemen and magistrates and men with ledgers under their arms. Dock workers used the side gate. Nobody knocked at the front door of Harrow & Vane in a February storm unless they wanted something, and nobody wanted something from Harrow & Vane without paying more than it was worth.

Ansel stood in the rain and listened.

The knock came again. Fainter. Wrong, somehow — not the fist of a grown man. More like a hand too weak to make the sound it intended.

They crossed the yard with the wind shoving at their back like a hand between the shoulder blades, and pulled the front door open on its swollen hinges.

No one stood there.

The lamp above the lintel had been lit for the watchman and it threw a cone of greasy yellow down onto the step, and in that cone, on the wet stone, sat a basket.

Not even a good basket. A laundry basket, the kind Odile Brannock's girls carried down the alley to the wash-house, one handle cracked and bound with wire. Inside it, wrapped in a shawl that might once have been white, a bundle. The bundle moved. A small fist had escaped the wrapping and was clenched against the cold, and the sound the bundle made was the knock — a cry worn down to almost nothing, the cry of something that had been crying a long time and had learned it did no good.

Ansel looked up and down the street. The storm had emptied it. Water ran in the gutters like a river deciding its course. Somewhere a shutter banged, and banged, and banged.

They should have gone for the watchman. That was procedure. Found property at the company door was logged, reported, and disposed of, and Ansel Kavanagh had survived nine years at Harrow & Vane by never once being the person who found things. But the wind was driving rain straight into the basket, and the small fist was blue at the knuckles, and Ansel's hands moved before any thought of procedure arrived — lifting the bundle out, tucking it inside their coat, against the chest, where the heat of a body could actually do some work.

The baby weighed almost nothing. Less than a coil of rope. Less than the ledger Ansel had once carried across the yard for a clerk who hadn't wanted to wet his boots. It made a sound against their collarbone, half sigh, half hiccup, and then went still, and Ansel stood in the storm holding it and felt something crack open in their chest that they had spent nine years keeping boarded shut.

*Don't,* they told themselves. *You know what this is. You know what the company does with things nobody claims.*

They carried the basket inside anyway.

* * *

The counting house was dark, but the stove in the clerks' room still held heat, and Ansel built it up carefully, feeding it splinters first, then offcuts, the way you'd approach a frightened animal. They sat on the floor by the stove — not the chair, the chair was for clerks — and unwrapped the shawl.

A girl. Newborn, or near enough; the cord-stump still healing, the face still swollen and red with the effort of having been born. Dark hair, plastered flat. She had stopped crying and was watching Ansel with eyes the color of river water, and there was a ferocity in that watching that Ansel recognized from the pearl beds, from the divers who came up bleeding from the ears and went back down anyway because the shells below were worth more than the blood.

Tucked into the wrappings, a note. One line, written in a fine hand, the ink slightly smeared by rain but perfectly legible:

*Forget this face.*

Ansel read it three times. It was an odd note, once you got past the strangeness of it. Not *take care of her.* Not *her name is.* Not even *please.* Just an instruction, cool as a shipping order. Forget this face. As if the face were a debt, a scandal, a shipment to be mislaid on purpose. As if whoever wrote it knew that forgetting was a thing you could order done, the way Director Vane ordered cargo destroyed when it suited the insurance.

Ansel looked down at the face in question.

And the world went very quiet, the way it did underwater.

There was a birthmark behind the child's left ear. Small, wine-dark, shaped — Ansel's mother would have said — like a mussel shell. Ansel had seen that mark before. Once. Only once, and from a distance, and for less than a minute, and they had spent three years afterward telling themselves they'd imagined the rest.

It had been the summer of the heiress's funeral. Miss Isolde Vane — no, not Vane, that was the thing, that was the whole buried thing — Miss Isolde *Marchmont*, the shipping heiress whose marriage into the Vane family had been the talk of the wharf for a season, and whose death had been the talk of the wharf for exactly as long as Director Vane's men allowed it to be. Drowned, they said. An accident on her own husband's yacht, they said. Ansel had been fourteen, hauling crates on the day the funeral cortege came through, and the coffin had been closed, and the company had closed with it — flags at half-mast for one day, then business as usual, then a strange and total silence, as if the name Marchmont had been scrubbed from the ledgers and the walls and the memory of the town.

But before the silence, there had been the viewing. Ansel hadn't meant to see it. They'd been sent to carry a message to the Vane house on the hill, and had been left standing in the entrance hall among the ferns and the hired mourners, and the family had come down the staircase, and Director Vane had had his hand on the shoulder of a young woman in black — his wife's sister, someone said, or his wife's niece, the relations were never clear — and the young woman had turned her head to speak to him, and her hair had lifted from her neck.

Mussel-shaped. Wine-dark. Behind the left ear.

Ansel had thought nothing of it at the time. A mark like a hundred other marks. What they had thought about, later, for three years, in the dark of the bunkhouse, was the look on the young woman's face as Vane's hand rested on her shoulder. Not grief. Something smaller and worse. The look of a person standing very still on a river that was already moving them toward the falls.

She had gone into the water eight months later. The yacht, the accident, the closed coffin. And now, on the floor of the counting house, in the shawl of a stranger, the same mark looked up at Ansel from the neck of a child too new to have enemies.

*Forget this face.*

The stove ticked. Rain hammered the high windows. Ansel sat very still and let the arithmetic do itself, because it would do itself whether they consented or not.

The heiress had been with child when she died. Ansel remembered that too — a whisper in the laundry, Odile Brannock's voice, low and certain over the washboards: *six months gone, and no announcement made, and Vane's man asking odd questions about midwives.* The whisper had died the same week the whisperer's sister lost her position at the Vane house. Ansel had filed it away the way they filed everything — under *things that are not mine to know.*

But the child in their arms was not six months old. The child was weeks old. The dates did not sit neatly together, and Ansel, who had learned to read ledgers by lamplight out of pure self-preservation, knew that dates were the one thing a ledger could not lie about for long. Somewhere in that building — in the counting house above them, in the iron cabinets that Vane kept locked and the clerks kept quiet — there was a line of ink that explained this child. Who she was. Who had written the note. What *disposition* had been planned for her, and by whom, and for what.

The baby yawned. It was an outrageously large yawn for so small a face, and it undid something in Ansel that had been holding since they were six years old, since the day a company man had written *Ansel* in a book without asking what they'd been called before, since the day their name had gone into a ledger and their self had gone out of the world.

*Forget this face.*

"No," Ansel said aloud. Their voice sounded strange in the empty room — rusty, unused to declaring things. "I don't think I will."

The baby blinked at them, unimpressed, and fell asleep.

* * *

Director Vane came down at seven, as he always did, before the clerks, to review the night book in private. Ansel heard him from the clerks' room — the front door, the wet footprints, the cane he didn't need but carried anyway, its ferrule striking the floorboards like a slow metronome. Ansel had the baby wrapped and hidden in the laundry basket behind the coal scuttle, and had arranged themselves on the floor beside it, mending a tarpaulin seam with sail twine, a picture of a person doing nothing that could interest anyone.

Vane appeared in the doorway. He was a tall man built like a crane — all leverage and no warmth — with a face that had been handsome once and had since been repurposed. His eyes went around the room the way a customs man's hands went through a hold: fast, thorough, assuming guilt.

"Kavanagh." He never used a first name. First names were for people who existed. "You were on the gate last night."

"I was on the wharf, sir."

"The gate, the wharf, the door. You were on the company's property, and things that happen on the company's property are the company's business." He came further in, and Ansel saw that behind him, at the edge of the lamplight, stood one of Vane's quiet men — Mr. Quill, they called him, though that was not a name, it was a function. Quill stood the way furniture stood. "There was a delivery in the night."

Ansel kept their eyes on the twine. "I found a basket on the step, sir. Empty. I brought it in out of the rain."

"Empty."

"Empty, sir."

Vane crossed the room. Ansel did not look up. Behind the coal scuttle, wrapped in a shawl and dosed into sleep with a fingertip of warmed milk, the baby did not make a sound — and Ansel learned in that moment that a held breath could be inherited, that their mother's gift had passed down to them in a form no one had warned them about: the ability to be still, entirely still, inside and out, while the thing they loved sat three feet from a man who would destroy it.

Vane looked into the laundry basket. Ansel watched his shoes.

"Hm," said Vane.

He straightened. "If a basket should become un-empty, Kavanagh, you will bring it to me. Not to the watchman. Not to the constabulary. To me. The company does not care for entanglements. Foundlings are entanglements. The workhouse is equipped for them, and the workhouse is where they go — by morning, before the town wakes up and starts making stories. Do you understand me?"

"Yes, sir."

"Stories are like water, Kavanagh. They get into everything and rot it from inside. The company is built of wood." He turned to go, and paused, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse. "You of all people should understand the value of being forgotten. It's kept you alive, hasn't it? Nine years, and no trouble, no name worth writing down. There's a wisdom in that. Don't lose it now."

His cane ticked away down the hall. The front door closed. Quill unfolded from the doorway and followed, and Ansel sat with the sail twine in their hands and their heart going like a drum in a parade, and waited until the yard was empty before they let the breath out.

*By morning.*

That was the order. That was the whole of the law, delivered in a voice as dry as the note itself: the child went to the workhouse by morning, and the workhouse, everyone knew, was where the river's foundlings went to become statistics. Two in five lived out the year. The rest were forgotten — which was, Ansel understood now, not a failure of the system. It was the system working exactly as designed.

They pulled the basket out. The baby slept on, her small face slack with trust she had no grounds for. Behind her left ear, the mussel-shaped mark, wine-dark in the stove light.

Ansel looked at the door to the hall. Then at the stairs. Then up, toward the dark of the counting house above, where the iron cabinets stood in rows like coffins, and somewhere inside one of them a line of ink waited with the child's true name in it — the name someone had paid to have forgotten.

The clock over the clerks' door said four hours until morning.

Ansel had been invisible for nine years. Nobody watched a person like that. Nobody counted the seconds they were alone in a building full of paper.

They picked up the baby, and stood, and looked at the stairs.
