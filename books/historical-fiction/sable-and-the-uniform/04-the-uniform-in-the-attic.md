## 4. The Uniform in the Attic

The laundress's shop sat where Sablemouth always smelled of lye and river rot — Brannock's Steam & Mangle, wedged between a chandler's and a shuttered pawnbroker on Cordage Lane. Ansel had passed it a thousand times hauling crates for Harrow & Vane. They had never once gone inside.

They went inside now because there was nowhere else. Because the ledger page burned against their ribs like a coal sewn into their coat lining, and because every other door in Sablemouth belonged to Vane or to Vane's money.

Odile Brannock was mending when the bell over the door clanked — a constable's greatcoat spread across her knees, her needle moving with the unhurried precision of someone who had been stitching other people's authority back together for thirty years. She was small and square as an anvil, gray hair pinned so tight it looked structural. She didn't look up.

"Company collar buttons go on the left rack," she said. "If you've come about mending."

"I haven't come about mending."

That made her look up. Her eyes were pale, nearly colorless, the kind of eyes that had spent decades reading men by their stains — what they'd spilled, where they'd sweated, whether they lied. She took Ansel in the same way: the company drab, the rope-burned palms, the way they stood half-turned toward the door.

"No," she said. "You haven't. You've got the walk of someone carrying something heavy that isn't cargo." She set down the greatcoat. "Shut the door. The bell sticks; give it your hip."

Ansel gave it their hip. The bell clanked again and went silent.

"Brannock," she said, though of course Ansel knew her name. Everyone at the wharf knew Odile Brannock, who washed for the constabulary barracks three streets up and asked no questions and told no tales, which made her the most trusted and least known woman on the riverfront. "And you're the Kavanagh one. Harrow & Vane's ghost."

Ansel flinched before they could stop themselves.

"Oh, don't take offense. I mean it kindly." She threaded a fresh needle without looking at it. "I mend for half the men on the wharf payroll. You'd be surprised what falls out of pockets into my wash. You'd be more surprised how many of them I couldn't pick out of a crowd afterward. You're like them. Unremarkable on purpose. It's a skill, that. Most folk waste years trying to be seen."

"The child," Ansel said. Their voice came out lower than intended. "The one left at the company door in February."

Something shifted behind those pale eyes. Not surprise. Confirmation.

"Sit down," said Odile Brannock.

* * *

She poured tea from a pot black with use, and Ansel held the cup and did not drink, because drinking would mean staying still, and staying still would mean feeling everything they were refusing to feel.

"They sent it to the workhouse?" Odile asked.

"They were going to. By morning. Vane gave the order himself." Ansel stared into the tea. "It's not there anymore."

Silence stretched. Outside, a cart groaned past on the cobbles, and somewhere a gull screamed its one note of complaint.

"You took it," Odile said. It wasn't a question either, but it landed heavier than one.

"There's a page in the counting-house ledger. Recording whose child it is. And a second entry underneath authorizing its—" Ansel stopped. Swallowed. "Its disposition. That's the word they used. *Disposition.* Like it was spoiled grain."

"And you have this page."

Ansel reached into their coat and drew out the oilskin packet. Their hands were steady until they tried to open it, and then they weren't, and Odile watched the tremor with something that was not quite pity. Pity would have been easier to bear.

Inside the oilskin, the ledger page lay flat, the ink sharp as the day it was dried. Odile read it twice. Ansel watched her face change — not dramatically, nothing theatrical, just a slow settling, like ballast shifting in a hull.

"Merrivale," she said at last. "The heiress who drowned last autumn. This says the child is hers. Born in June, registered under a false name at St. Aubin's parish." Her finger hovered over the second entry without touching it. "And this says Director Vane authorized payment to a Mrs. Quill at the workhouse to see it 'quietly placed beyond retrieval.'"

"That's the word again. Retrieval. Like it was luggage."

"It's worse than you think it is, if that's possible." Odile laid the page down with exaggerated care, the way one handles something already broken. "Merrivale's fortune sits in trust. If the heir dies unregistered, the trust dissolves into — guess who holds the administration."

"Harrow & Vane."

"Harrow & Vane." She looked up. "Why are you here, Kavanagh? Truly. You could burn this page tomorrow and nobody alive would ever know you'd read it. You could keep your head down another twenty years and collect your freedom papers and vanish upriver like all the others. Instead you're standing in my laundry at dawn with stolen evidence and a stolen baby. So why?"

Ansel opened their mouth to say *because it was right* and found the words weren't there. What was there was older and stranger.

"When I was six," they heard themselves say, "they took my name. Company indenture. My mother had just drowned off the pearl beds and there was no one to object, so the clerk wrote *Kavanagh, given name unknown* and after that nobody used it, and after a while I stopped using it myself. I know what it is to be erased on paper. I know how easy it is. One line of ink." They looked at the ledger page. "This is one line of ink too. Going the other direction."

Odile studied them for a long moment.

"You'll need help," she said. "Not mine, specifically. But you'll need it, and you're too smart to ask Vane's people and too honest for the smugglers, so here we are." She rose, joints cracking like kindling, and crossed to the narrow stair at the back of the shop. "Come up. And mind the third step. It lies."

* * *

The attic was low-ceilinged and smelled of camphor and cold cedar. Odile lit a single lamp and its light caught rows of hanging garments — constables' tunics, a sergeant's cloak, shirts with collar rings worn shiny — the whole wardrobe of borrowed law, waiting to be washed and mended and returned to the barracks.

But against the far wall stood a trunk. Plain pine, iron-banded, locked. Odile knelt before it with the lamp and produced a key from around her neck, where it hung on the same cord as a small wooden cross, and unlocked it, and lifted the lid, and did not move for a moment.

"My brother's," she said.

Ansel said nothing. Some silences invite speech; this one forbade it.

"Callum Brannock. Twelve years on the force. Sergeant's stripes two years before they took them back." She lifted out the uniform piece by piece, laying each on the trunk lid as if dressing a body. Tunic, dark blue, brass buttons dulled but intact. Trousers with a sergeant's stripe. The belt. The peaked cap with its band of braid. Last, folded alone, the greatcoat — the same style as the one downstairs, except this one had a tear at the shoulder that had been mended so finely the repair was invisible unless you knew where to look.

"He refused to falsify a report," Odile said. "A drowning, out past the icehouse, autumn before last. He was told what to write and he wouldn't write it, and so he was dismissed — not charged, mind, dismissed, quietly, with his character reference torn up and his name whispered on. He drank after that. Drinks still." Her voice stayed level the whole time, which somehow made it worse. "He wanted to throw this in the river. I wouldn't let him. I told him cloth doesn't disgrace. Men do."

"Why are you showing me?"

"Because you came to me knowing I wash for the constabulary, which means you already had the thought. You just hadn't dared think it all the way through." She turned and faced Ansel fully, and the lamplight made her shadow climb the rafters like a hanged man's. "So let's think it through together, and then you can decide whether you still want to."

Ansel nodded.

"A uniform is not a disguise," Odile said. "It's a key. There are three places between here and the low archive downriver where papers matter more than faces — the Tollgate, the wharf inspection, the Icehouse Bridge. At each one, a man in constable's colors walks past where a man with papers gets searched. That's what the uniform can do for you." She picked up the tunic and held it out. "Here's what it can do *to* you. Wearing this, when Callum Brannock is a discharged man whose discharge was public, is impersonation of the Crown's authority. If you're caught in it and recognized for what you are, it's not a fine. It's not the workhouse. It's the hangman's yard, Kavanagh, and the crowd that gathers will include everyone who ever saw you haul a crate and thought you were nobody."

The attic was very quiet. Somewhere below, water dripped from the mangle into its trough, counting out seconds.

"And it's not only you," Odile went on. "Anyone who helps you becomes an accessory. Anyone who hides you, feeds you, mends that tunic's lining to fit your shoulders — accessory. Me. My brother, if he ever learns of it. We hang alongside you. That's the arithmetic. I want you to carry it out of this room weighing exactly what it weighs."

Ansel looked at the uniform lying across the trunk lid. Up close, they could see the wear at the elbows, the salt-fade along the collar, the faint smell of a man's sweat worked deep into the wool even after years in camphor. A garment that had been lived in. Defended in. Refused in.

"If I'm caught without it," Ansel said slowly, "Vane's men find me, and the page burns, and the child grows up nameless in a workhouse until the workhouse finds a use for her. If I'm caught wearing it—" 

"—you die faster but better," Odile finished. "Yes. That's the trade."

"That's not a hard trade."

"Don't say that." For the first time, Odile's voice cracked its level surface. "Don't you dare say that like it's simple. I've mended the collars of men who said it wasn't a hard trade, and I've washed the blood out of the ones who learned otherwise. Nothing about this is simple. You're a diver's child with a stolen ledger page and no legal existence, proposing to walk through armed checkpoints dressed in a dead career, carrying the proof of an inheritance that four directors want buried under spring flood. Every part of that can fail separately. Plan for all of them failing at once."

Ansel absorbed that. Then: "Will you help me?"

Odile looked at the uniform for a long moment. When she answered, she was looking at it still, as if addressing it rather than Ansel.

"My brother lost everything because he told the truth once, at the worst possible moment. I've spent two years being angry with him for it. Two years thinking, *if only he'd written what they told him, we'd still have the house, the pension, the name.*" She reached out and straightened the cap's brim, a small futile gesture of care. "But the truth didn't ruin him, Kavanagh. Vane's kind ruined him. The truth just showed where the floor was rotten." She turned back. "So yes. I'll help. But we do it my way, and my way starts with measurements, because that tunic was cut for a broader man than you, and a badly fitted constable fools nobody."

* * *

While Odile fetched pins and chalk, Ansel drifted to the dormer window. Below, Cordage Lane ran gray and empty toward the river, and beyond the rooftops the Sable lay wide and white-locked, ice sheets grinding softly against the pilings in the pre-dawn dark. Somewhere downriver, under that ice, in the low archive at Marsh Cut, sat the registry pages that would prove everything — sitting in water that would rise and smash and scatter them within weeks, when the break-up came.

Weeks. Maybe less.

"Your brother," Ansel said, without turning. "Does he know where the uniform is?"

Odile paused, chalk in hand.

"He knows I kept it. He doesn't come here. He drinks at the Anchor & Chain and tells anyone who'll listen that the force was rotten before he joined it and rottener since." A beat. "Why?"

"Because he refused to falsify a drowning report." Ansel turned from the window. "Autumn before last. Out past the icehouse."

The chalk stilled in Odile's fingers.

"Kavanagh," she said carefully, "what drowning?"

"The one they wrote anyway." Ansel held her pale gaze. "The heiress. Merrivale. The mother of the child sleeping in a basket above the chandler's two doors down."

For a moment Odile Brannock, who had spent thirty years unmoved by blood and lye and the confessions of strangers' laundry, stood absolutely motionless in the middle of her attic. Then she set down the chalk, deliberately, the way a woman sets down a tool before picking up a weapon.

"Then we need to talk," she said, "about whether my brother is going to be the most dangerous man in Sablemouth — or the most useful one."
