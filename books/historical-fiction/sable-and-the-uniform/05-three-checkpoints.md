## 5. Three Checkpoints

Odile Brannock had spent thirty years reading people the way she read linen—by what the stains said and where they tried to hide them. A collar told her whether a man drank alone or in company. A shirtcuff told her whether he counted his money or merely carried it. And a woman who came to her door at dusk, holding a stolen ledger page inside her coat like a hot coal, told Odile everything she needed to know about how the winter was going to go.

"Sit," she said to Ansel Kavanagh, and shut the door behind them both.

The laundry room smelled of lye and wet wool and the ghost of every secret that had ever passed through Sablemouth's better houses. Odile washed for magistrates and merchants, for the constabulary, for Director Vane himself, and she had learned long ago that a woman with a washboard in her hands was furniture. Nobody confessed to furniture. Everybody forgot it was listening.

She spread a sheet of brown paper across the mangle table, took up a stub of charcoal, and drew the river.

It came out of her hand without thinking—the Sable as she'd known it since she was small enough to fall in: a fat grey question mark bending through the town, wharf above wharf stacked along its inner curve like crooked teeth. She marked three places on it before Ansel had even finished sitting down.

"You want to move paper downriver," she said. "Past the town, past the company ground, out to where it can reach a magistrate who isn't bought. There are exactly three doors between here and open water, and I'm going to tell you about all of them, and then you're going to understand why we have weeks and not months."

Ansel said nothing. That was one of the things Odile had decided to like about them. They listened the way their mother must have listened to the water—with the whole body, waiting for the lie underneath.

* * *

"The first is the Tollgate." She tapped the charcoal against the upper bend of the river, where the coast road squeezed between the customs house and the old granary wall. "Every wagon, every cart, every man on foot going downriver passes under Sergeant Meech's nose, and Meech has a memory like a bailiff's ledger. He doesn't care what you're carrying. He cares whether your story matches your face."

"Papers?" Ansel asked.

"Papers for goods. Faces for people." Odile drew a little square at the gate. "A grain shipment from Harrow & Vane goes through twice a week, and nobody looks hard at a company wagon because the company owns half the men who might look. But you—" she looked up at Ansel properly, taking in the flat grey nothing of them, the way they seemed to occupy space without claiming it— "you've spent your whole life being nobody, haven't you? That'll get you through most doors. It won't get you through Meech's, because Meech remembers nobodies. It's the somebodies he waves through."

"So I need to be somebody."

"You need to be somebody boring." Odile allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "A carter. A clerk. Somebody whose face promises a receipt and delivers one. Can you be boring on purpose?"

"I've been doing it my whole life by accident."

"Then we're ahead."

She drew the second mark lower down, where the company's own wharf jutted into the current like an accusation.

"The Company Wharf inspection. This is the one that should frighten you, so let it." Her voice dropped, not for drama but because the walls of her laundry had heard enough confessions to fill a churchyard, and she'd grown careful about which ones she added. "Everything leaving company ground gets walked past a tallyman and a watchman, and the watchman reports directly to Vane. Not to the harbourmaster. To Vane. If Vane has put word out about a missing bundle—and he will have, by now, if he's half as careful as I think he is—then the watchman isn't looking for cargo. He's looking for a face. Yours."

Ansel's hand went, very slightly, toward their coat. Toward the page.

"Not here," Odile said quietly. "Nothing written, nothing shown, until I say. In this room we speak of it as laundry. You're moving a bundle of linens downriver. Say it back to me."

"A bundle of linens."

"Good. The watchman's name is Pell. Pell takes his supper in the guard hut at half six and he takes a bribe the way other men take communion—regularly, and without thinking about it too hard. But bribes buy passage, not silence. If Pell waves you through and someone asks him later, he'll remember your face to save his own skin. So whatever face you wear at the Tollgate, wear a different one at the wharf, and neither of them can be yours."

"That's two faces."

"It's three doors," Odile said. "Do the arithmetic."

* * *

She got up, went to the corner where her brother Callum's old trunk sat under a drop cloth, and did not open it. She only rested her hand on the lid, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping dog you weren't sure of yet.

"The third door is the Icehouse Bridge." She drew it at the river's last bend before the marshes, where the water narrowed and the ice always went rotten first. "There's one road out of Sablemouth that doesn't ford the river, and it crosses there. Since the autumn thefts, there's been a sentry posted nights and days. And below the bridge—" she tapped the paper twice— "is the low archive."

Ansel leaned forward. "The flood archive."

"The old customhouse records. Everything the town ever wrote down and then wanted to forget, shelved in a cellar that sits four feet below spring water level. Birth registers. Death registers. Shipping manifests. Coroner's inquests." She met Ansel's eyes. "Whatever your page says, there'll be something down there that says it too. Two documents make a fact. One document makes a story, and stories get burned."

"And when the ice breaks—"

"The whole bend floods. Every year, same as church. The water comes up over the banks, fills the low archive to the ceiling, and every scrap of paper down there turns to porridge. Usually it breaks mid-March. Some years late February." Odile straightened. "This year the ice has been groaning since the new moon. I've lived on this river forty years and I know what it sounds like when it's getting ready to end a thing. We don't have weeks, whatever I said before. We have weeks if we're lucky, and luck is not a plan."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a board settled. Ansel sat very still, and Odile watched them do the arithmetic she'd given them, watched it land.

"So the order is this," Ansel said slowly. "Get the corroborating record out of the low archive before the flood. Then move everything—page and record together—downriver past all three doors, to a magistrate."

"No." Odile shook her head. "You move the ledger page first. Tonight, tomorrow, soon. Because right now that page exists in exactly one place in the world, inside one coat, on one person, and Vane's men will find that person eventually—they always do. Get it across the Tollgate, get it somewhere safe outside company reach, and then come back for the archive. Split the risk. Never carry the whole truth in one basket."

"And the archive dive?"

"One problem at a time." But she was already looking at the trunk again, and Ansel saw her looking, and something passed between them that neither of them named.

* * *

Odile blew the charcoal dust off the map and studied her own handiwork the way she'd study a stain: what does it want, and what is it hiding?

Three doors. Three different keys. The Tollgate wanted a boring face. The wharf wanted a bribe and a stranger's face. The bridge wanted papers—a genuine warrant, a genuine authority, the kind of thing no laundress and no indentured diver could conjure out of thin air.

But she mended shirts for the constabulary. She knew which sergeant drank, which magistrate kept letters in his desk that his wife didn't know about, which clerk signed things without reading them. Thirty years of collars and cuffs, and every one of them a small debt, and debts were the only currency in Sablemouth that never devalued.

And in the trunk under her hand was a uniform that still fit the shape of the law, even if the law had spat its owner out.

"There's a fourth thing," she said, and hated herself a little for saying it, because saying it made it real. "My brother."

Ansel went carefully blank. "The constable?"

"The disgraced constable. Dismissed seven years ago for reasons he's never told me and I've never pried out of him, and he's been drinking at the Anchor ever since, hating this town and everyone in it, including me." She took her hand off the trunk. "He knows procedure the way I know stains. He knows what Meech looks for, what Pell takes, what the bridge sentry's shift changes at. If we're going to run three checkpoints in a few weeks' time, I'd rather have him inside this than outside it finding out."

"He could sell you."

"He could sell us both," Odile agreed. "That's what family means." She folded the brown paper map into quarters, into eighths, until it was small enough to disappear into a sleeve. "But I've watched that man drink himself honest for seven years, and drunk honesty is still honesty. He hates Vane more than he hates me. I think that's enough. I think it has to be enough."

Outside, the light had gone the colour of dishwater, and beneath the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Odile had been hearing in her sleep for three nights: the river, clearing its throat.

Ansel heard it too. Their head turned toward the wall, toward the water beyond it, with an expression Odile recognized from her mother, from every woman on this river—the look of someone listening to a large animal decide.

"How long," Ansel said.

"When it starts talking like that?" Odile tucked the map away. "Ten days. Maybe twelve. After that, the low archive belongs to the fish."

* * *

She walked Ansel to the back door, through the rows of hanging sheets that turned the passing figures into ghosts. At the threshold she caught their sleeve.

"One more thing, and then you go. The child—who's keeping it while you're running errands for the revolution?"

"Mrs. Ferro, on Rope Walk. She raised six of her own and buried four. Nothing frightens her."

"Good. Keep it that way. And Ansel—" Odile held their eyes, and let the laundress fall away for one moment so that only the woman was left. "Vane's men started asking questions today. A boy I do sheets for told me two strangers were at the Anchor asking after 'a bundle gone missing from the company door.' Asking after anyone who'd been seen near it."

Something moved behind Ansel's face—not fear exactly; more like a door being braced shut.

"Then I shouldn't walk home the usual way," they said.

"No. You shouldn't. Go by the tannery ditch and mind the ice on the boards." She released the sleeve. "And come back Thursday. Bring the child's washing—I'll do it proper. If we're going to hang together, we may as well smell nice."

Ansel almost smiled. Almost. Then they slipped out into the blue dark, and Odile stood among her ghosts and listened until the footsteps faded, and then listened to the river instead, counting the groans the way other women counted sins.

Ten days. Three doors. One brother she hadn't spoken a kind word to in two years, who would have to be found, sobered, and persuaded—all before the ice gave way.

Odile banked the stove, took up her mending, and began, stitch by stitch, to plan.
