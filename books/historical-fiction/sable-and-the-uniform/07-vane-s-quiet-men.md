## 7. Vane's Quiet Men

They came on a Tuesday, which was the worst kind of luck, because Tuesdays were the day the rope shipments arrived and Ansel was always at the coiling shed from dawn until the wharf bell rang for supper.

The two men stood out the way a pair of crows stands out against a gray sky—not because they were large, though one of them was, but because they didn't belong to any of the work rhythms around them. The dock had its own choreography: haulers, tallymen, the customs runner with his clipboard and his chilblains. These two moved through it like men reading a room they intended to buy.

The big one wore a drab coat that had been good once, before sweat and rain got into it. He had a face like a closed ledger—nothing written on it that you could use. The other was smaller, quick-eyed, with the soft hands of a man who'd never hauled anything heavier than another man's secrets. He carried a leather folio under one arm and smiled too easily.

Ansel watched them from behind a stack of hemp coils and did what they had trained themselves to do since childhood: became furniture.

It was a skill, being unremarkable. Not passivity—passivity got you trampled. It was active, this vanishing. You softened your outline. You let your face go slack and your eyes go somewhere useful but nowhere interesting. You matched your walk to the rhythm of whoever was nearest so no single gaze snagged on you. Ansel had learned it at six years old, when the Harrow & Vane indenture clerk took their name along with their mother's pearl knife and wrote *foundling, labor class* in the intake book. A child without a name learns quickly that names are what get noticed. Ansel had spent twenty years making sure there was nothing left to notice.

But now there was a ledger page sewn into the lining of a spare shirt, wrapped in oilskin, hidden in the bottom of their sea chest in the bunkhouse—and an infant three doors down at Odile Brannock's, sleeping under a name Ansel had invented for her. Sable. Named for the river that gave her up and might yet take everything back.

And here were two quiet men asking questions.

* * *

Ansel couldn't hear what they said at first. They started at the tally office, and the tallyman—Prewitt, a nervous man with ink-stained cuffs—came out looking like he'd been asked to count water. Then the big one walked the length of the wharf itself, slow, unhurried, while the quick one peeled off toward the gatehouse where the night log lived.

By midmorning the whole dock knew something was wrong, though nobody could say what. That was the genius of it, Ansel thought. Vane hadn't posted a notice or called a muster. He'd sent silence instead, and silence traveled faster than any announcement. By noon, three separate people had told Ansel, in three separate tones of voice, that strangers were asking about "a missing bundle."

*A missing bundle.* As if the child were cargo. Which, in the company's arithmetic, she was—a liability with lungs, entered nowhere, belonging to no one, except that she belonged to a fortune and a birthmark and a dead woman whose name had been scrubbed from the company's own books. Ansel had seen the second ledger entry with their own eyes by lantern light three nights ago: *disposition authorized,* initialled in Vane's small dry hand. Disposition. A word for surplus grain. A word for a body.

The big crow reached the coiling shed at half past one.

"Kavanagh," he said.

Ansel's hands kept moving. Rope doesn't coil itself, and stopping would have been a confession of something—the trouble was, Ansel wasn't sure anymore what counted as innocent. Every motion felt loaded now. That was the price of carrying a secret: you lost access to your own natural gestures.

"That's me," Ansel said, not looking up.

"Mr. Quill." The quick one had appeared at the shed's open end, folio tucked under his arm like a hymnbook. "Company security. And this is Mr. Harrow—" a beat, deliberate, letting the surname land, "—no relation. We're conducting an inventory."

"Inventory of what?"

"Of irregularities." Quill smiled again. Up close his teeth were very even, like piano keys. "There was an incident during the February storm. A delivery to the company door. It seems the item in question has gone unaccounted for."

"The storm brought half the river to our door," Ansel said. "Crates, barrels, a dead gull. What item?"

Quill's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a small recalibration, like a man testing a floorboard. "That's what we're establishing."

"I haul rope," Ansel said. "I sleep. I eat at the second table because I'm always late to the first. If something came to that door, ask the door watch. Ask Prewitt. Ask Director Vane—he was standing right there when it happened."

Something flickered across Harrow-no-relation's flat face at the name. Not surprise. Confirmation. *She knows who stood where.* Ansel cursed inwardly. Too much detail. Vanishing meant knowing less than you knew, not more.

"We'll ask everyone we need to ask," Quill said pleasantly. "Where do you sleep?"

"Bunkhouse C. Third cot from the wall."

"You won't mind if we have a look at the quarters. Standard practice after an inventory flag."

"Flag," Ansel repeated. "I've been here nineteen years and never been flagged."

"There's a first time for everything," Harrow said. It was the first thing he'd spoken, and his voice was worse than his silence—mild, almost kind, the voice of a man describing weather to someone he intended to drown in it.

They went together, all three, because refusing would have been the loudest possible answer. Ansel walked between them and made their feet heavy and stupid, made their shoulders round, and hated every second of the performance because it used to be effortless and now it was armor, and armor weighs.

* * *

Bunkhouse C smelled of wet wool and pipe smoke and forty men's unwashed ambition to someday wash. The cots stood in ranks like bunks on a prison ship, which was essentially what they were. Ansel's sea chest sat at the foot of the third cot from the wall, iron-bound, older than Ansel's indenture, holding everything they owned in the world: two shirts, a wool coat, a tin of mother-of-pearl buttons salvaged from a diver's coat that no longer existed, a lock of hair wrapped in waxed paper, and—beneath a false floor of rotted canvas—an oilskin packet containing the only true sentence ever written about Sable's parentage.

Quill opened the chest without asking permission. Of course he did. Permission was for people who existed on paper.

He went through it the way a customs man goes through luggage—fast, thorough, contemptuous of privacy as a concept. Shirt. Shirt. Coat, pockets turned out. The tin of buttons; he held it up to the light, tipped the buttons into his palm, and Ansel's heart performed a slow cold somersault, because those buttons were iridescent, were pearl, were the one object in the room that connected Ansel to the pearl beds and therefore to everything, and if this man knew anything about the heiress—

"Pretty," Quill said, and poured them back, and moved on.

Harrow stood at the doorway and watched the room instead of the search. Watching who watched him. Cataloguing faces. Ansel understood then that the search of the chest was theater; the real search was of the bunkhouse, of the way the other men looked or didn't look at Ansel, of whether anyone's eyes flickered toward a hiding place. Harrow was reading the audience. Ansel kept their own eyes on the middle distance and thought about rope.

Quill lifted the false canvas, frowned at the rot, probed it with two fingers, found the hollow underneath.

Empty.

Because it wasn't empty—because Ansel's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world—but empty to his fingers, because the oilskin packet was not in the chest.

It was in the rope.

Ansel had moved it four days ago, on instinct, after Odile had said over her mending, *"A secret keeps best where hands already go and don't look."* She'd meant her laundry baskets. Ansel had meant the coiling shed. There was a particular shipment of tarred hemp line, destined downriver, still awaiting transport—coils stacked in the shed's corner, each coil wound tight enough to swallow a folded page in its heart without bulging. Page thirty-one of the Harrow & Vane inheritance register lay at this moment inside a coil of number-four tarred line, wrapped in oilskin, buried in fiber, indistinguishable from cargo. Waiting to be shipped away, actually—Ansel had told themself it was temporary, that they'd retrieve it before the shipment left. They'd been lying. Part of them had wanted it gone from the bunkhouse, had wanted it anywhere but near their own sleeping body.

Now that cowardice stood between Ansel and discovery, and Ansel loved it the way you love a stranger who saves your life.

"Hollow's rotten through," Quill said, disgusted. "Nothing."

"Nothing," Ansel agreed.

Quill straightened, dusted his hands, and delivered his real message standing over the emptied chest. "Mr. Vane values discretion, Kavanagh. He also values thoroughness. If a certain item surfaces—if anyone remembers anything about a certain item—there's a reward for a clean memory and consequences otherwise." He tapped the chest lid once, twice, like a doctor percussing a lung. "You seem like a man who understands inventory."

"A woman," Ansel said quietly.

Both men stopped.

"The bundle," Ansel said. "If we're speaking plainly. It was a baby. Say it, if you're going to hunt it."

The silence stretched. Harrow's flat face achieved some new depth of flatness. Quill's smile finally died, and what replaced it was more honest and much worse: professional interest, sharpening.

"Noted," Quill said. And then, softly, almost kindly, which was how Ansel knew the trap was closing: "Funny thing about babies, Kavanagh. Everybody who sees one remembers it. Faces are the one cargo that ships itself into your head." He settled the folio under his arm. "Sleep well."

* * *

They didn't sleep well. They didn't sleep at all.

Ansel lay on the third cot from the wall listening to the bunkhouse breathe—forty men snoring, turning, muttering at dreams—and replayed the day like a tallyman checking figures. *Say it, if you're going to hunt it.* Stupid. Reckless. The old self, the vanished self, would never have said it. But something in Ansel had begun to chafe against the vanishing, had begun to want—God help them—to be *seen saying* the true thing, and that wanting was a crack in the hull, and Quill had heard it.

They rose before the bell, in the blue-black hour when even the river seemed to be holding its breath, and crossed the frozen yard to the coiling shed with their collar up and their heart hammering in their ears louder than their boots on the ice-slick boards.

The coils were where they'd left them. Nobody had touched the corner—no fresh boot prints in the frost, no disturbed dust on the topmost coil. Ansel knelt, worked the oilskin packet out from the heart of the hemp with numb fingers, and held it against the lantern flame just long enough to confirm: page thirty-one, whole, dry, the ink legible, the truth intact. *Disposition authorized.* Somewhere across town a baby slept under a borrowed name, and here was the document that could give her back her own.

Ansel couldn't put it back in the coil. The shipment was scheduled—two days, maybe three, and Quill's people would surely be watching the manifests now, watching everything that moved downriver. Couldn't keep it in the bunkhouse; the chest had been opened once, it would be opened again, and next time there would be no lucky rot. Couldn't carry it on their body—Harrow had catalogued their build, their walk, their outline; a man who reads rooms intends to search them.

What remained was Odile. Odile with her laundry baskets and her mending and her brother's disgraced uniform hanging in the attic like a ghost waiting to be worn. Odile, who had said *a secret keeps best where hands already go and don't look,* and whose hands went everywhere in this town without anyone ever looking twice at a laundress.

Ansel rewrapped the page, buttoned their coat over it, and stepped out into the dark.

The river groaned beneath its ice—a long tectonic complaint, the sound of spring gathering its arguments. Weeks, Odile had said. Perhaps less. When the ice broke, the low archive would flood, and every corroborating record would be pulp, and page thirty-one would be a dead leaf with writing on it.

Halfway across the yard, Ansel saw the lantern.

It hung at the gatehouse, and beneath it stood a third man Ansel had never seen before, talking to the night watch, and even at this distance—even in the blue-black hour—Ansel recognized the posture. Folio under the arm. Soft hands. The patient stillness of someone counting departures.

They weren't two quiet men.

They were as many as Vane needed.

Ansel turned, unhurried, a worker heading to an early shift, and walked the long way around the salt sheds toward Odile's street, feeling the page press against their ribs like a second heartbeat, and understanding at last that the routine was over—that from tonight forward, every ordinary act would be a performance, every silence a negotiation, and the hunted feeling in their chest wasn't fear of being caught.

It was the certainty that they already had been.
