## 8. The Constable Who Wasn't

The uniform smelled of camphor and another man's life.

Ansel had taken it from Odile's attic two days after the plan was drawn, folding it into a grain sack with the care of someone handling a live eel. Callum Brannock's constable's coat, dark blue wool gone soft at the elbows, brass buttons dulled to the color of old honey. The trousers fit well enough. The coat hung a little loose across the shoulders, because Ansel had spent ten years learning to take up as little of the world as possible, and bodies remembered that even when minds tried to forget.

Odile had pressed it flat with an iron heated over her laundry stove, and she'd stood there while Ansel dressed in the back room behind her washtubs, arms crossed, jaw set like a woman watching someone walk into deep water.

"It hangs wrong," she said.

"It hangs fine."

"It hangs like a coat on a hook. A constable fills it. He stands like the ground owes him something." She came forward and took Ansel by both shoulders, turned them square, pushed them back half an inch. "Chin up. Not proud—entitled. There's a difference. Proud men get challenged. Entitled men get saluted."

"I can't do entitled."

"You can do invisible. I've watched you do invisible for six years." Her hands were rough from lye and hot water, and they were gentle anyway. "Entitled is just invisible turned inside out. Same trick. You stop looking at people before they look at you."

Ansel thought about that all night, lying in their bunk with the ledger page sewn into a spare shirt and Sable sleeping in the laundry basket beside Odile's stove downstairs, and by morning they understood what Odile meant. Invisibility wasn't emptiness. It was discipline. Ten thousand small decisions—eyes down, shoulders in, mouth shut, step aside—stacked until a person became furniture. And every one of those decisions could be reversed.

They practiced in the attic mirror, which was really just a cracked pane of glass propped against the wall, showing a smeared half-face in bad light. Ansel put on the coat. Put the chin up. Let the shoulders fall open instead of curled in. The figure in the glass changed so completely that Ansel flinched.

It wasn't that they looked bigger. They looked *owed*.

"That's it," Odile said from the stair. "God help us."

* * *

The Tollgate district began where the cobbled streets ended and the graded road took over, a wide throat of commerce through which everything leaving Sablemouth by land had to pass: grain wagons, cattle drives, mail coaches, the twice-weekly diligence to Marrowfield. The tollhouse itself was stone, squat, built like a strongbox, with a covered archway wide enough for two wagons abreast and a constabulary post attached to its eastern wall—a lean office of smoked glass windows where two officers sat out the cold hours between inspections.

Ansel walked toward it wearing Callum Brannock's name on the badge inside the coat pocket, because wearing the number visibly felt one step too far even now, and discovered within the first hundred yards that the world rearranged itself around blue wool.

It started small. A carter who would ordinarily have driven straight through the walking traffic reined in and nodded, actually nodded, touching his cap brim. Two boys scuffling over a stolen apple saw the coat and separated like oil and water, guilt evaporating off them in visible steam. A woman with a market basket stepped around a puddle rather than through it, though the puddle lay in Ansel's path and not hers, as if the ground itself might be embarrassed to splash a constable.

Nobody looked at Ansel. That was the intoxicating part. For ten years Ansel had engineered being unseen, and it had cost constant labor, constant subtraction. Now invisibility came free—not because Ansel was nothing, but because the coat was everything. People didn't avoid Ansel Kavanagh. They avoided the institution, and Ansel was merely standing inside it, warm, sheltered from every eye that had ever stripped them down to *indentured girl* or *diver's brat* or *you there, hold this*.

A man selling roasted chestnuts gave Ansel one, gratis, wrapped in paper, pressing it into their hand before Ansel could refuse. "For the cold, officer. You lot stand about in it all day."

Ansel held the chestnut like contraband.

At the tollhouse archway, a wagon piled with barley sacks was held up while a clerk checked manifests, the driver arguing about a surcharge, and Ansel passed through the whole knot of friction without slowing. The clerk glanced up, saw the coat, glanced down. The driver stopped mid-sentence to let Ansel by. Nobody asked anything. Nobody wanted anything. The world parted, and Ansel walked through the seam.

It should have felt like victory. It felt like drowning in reverse—being pulled upward into air that belonged to someone else.

Because here was the shame under the shine: none of this deference was for Ansel. It was for the uniform, and the uniform was borrowed, and beneath it Ansel remained exactly what they had always been—property, technically, still entered in Harrow & Vane's books at some valuation per pound of labor. If the coat came off in front of these people, the chestnut-seller would want his penny back and the carter would want an apology and the whole warm current of regard would invert into contempt, faster than a river turns to ice. Authority, Ansel saw now, was a costume everyone agreed to believe in, and the agreement could be revoked at any moment, and the revocation would be *personal*.

A constable near the post door spat and said, "Miserable shift," in the tone of one colleague commiserating with another, and Ansel heard themselves answer, "Mm," in a low register they didn't know they owned, and the constable grunted and went inside, satisfied. Ansel stood in the cold with a stolen voice in their mouth and a stranger's chestnut going cool in their hand.

*This is how Vane sees the world,* Ansel thought. *Doors that open. Names that open doors.* No wonder he guarded them like a dragon.

But there was no time to be sickened by it properly. The registry office opened at nine.

* * *

The Registrar of Deaths for the Tollgate District kept his records in a narrow brick building wedged between a chandler's and a pawnshop, and his name, according to Odile, was Mr. Pell, and according to Odile's brother, Pell owed Callum Brannock a debt from an incident involving a falsified weight certificate and a magistrate's leniency that had never been explained to Odile's satisfaction.

"Be kind to him," Odile had advised. "Not friendly. Kind. Kindness frightens clerks more than threats—they don't know how to log it."

The interior smelled of ink and coal dust. Rows of bound registers lined the walls, spines labeled by year, and behind a waist-high counter sat Mr. Pell himself: sixty-odd, spectacles thick as bottle ends, a man assembled entirely of careful motions. He looked up, saw the blue coat, and his pen stopped.

"Constable." His voice had the dryness of paper stored too long. "Unexpected."

"Just routine inquiry." Ansel laid Callum's warrant card on the counter—not the real one; Odile had copied the particulars onto a facsimile in her best copperplate—and watched Pell read it without picking it up. "I'm following up on a death registered last spring. Female, mid-twenties, name entered as—" Ansel gave the alias from the ledger page, the false name under which the heiress had died in company custody. "I need the entry verified against the original registration. Date, attending physician, place of death."

Pell's fingers had gone very still on his desk.

"Verification requests go through the district superintendent," he said. "Written requisition, countersigned."

"The requisition is in process." Ansel let a silence sit, three seconds, four—the kind of silence Odile had coached, the kind that made the other person fill it. "I'm instructed to confirm the record exists intact in the meantime."

Something moved behind Pell's bottle-thick lenses. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who had been expecting this visit the way you expect weather you've seen coming for days.

"Intact," Pell repeated.

"So far as known."

He rose without hurry, went to the shelf, and ran one finger along the spines—last year, the year before—and pulled down the spring register of the previous twelve months. He did not open it on the counter. He carried it to the end of the room, to the window where the light was worst, and stood with his back to Ansel for a long moment, and Ansel understood that whatever happened next was happening in the space between Pell's conscience and Pell's fear, and that the coat was only the key turning in a lock that had already been built.

When Pell returned, he brought the register, open, plus a sheet of foolscap and the blotting pad, and he said, quietly, "Copyists make errors. Best to check the original against any transcription you've been given." He turned the register around. "You'll want to note the attending physician. Dr. Hessel. And the place of death—" his finger tapped an entry, "—the Harrow & Vane infirmary, wharfside annex."

There it was. The false name, the date eleven days after the child would have been born, the cause of death written in Pell's own careful hand: *puerperal fever*. Attended by a doctor who had signed and departed. Registered by the company clerk, one T. Mudge, as next of kin: *none*.

*None.* Ansel looked at that word and felt something go very cold and very steady in their chest. The richest orphan in Sablemouth, filed under *none*, and eleven days later a bundle appeared at the company door with a note saying forget this face.

"Your transcription," Pell prompted softly.

Ansel wrote, copying each line onto the foolscap in a hand that shook only slightly. Pell blotted nothing, offered nothing, and when Ansel finished, he drew the register back, closed it, and said:

"The original is sound. Whatever copy you were given that disagrees with it—" he resumed his seat, picked up his pen, "—is the document worth investigating. Constable."

It was as close to an alliance as a frightened clerk could offer. Ansel folded the foolscap away inside the coat, next to the badge, and paused.

"Why help?" The question escaped before discipline could catch it.

Pell adjusted his spectacles. Behind the thick glass his eyes were tired and clear.

"Thirty-one years I've recorded who died, where, and who claimed them," he said. "Most entries are grief. Some are arithmetic." He wet his pen. "I've never liked the arithmetic ones. Good day, Constable."

* * *

Outside, the cold hit like clean water. Ansel walked fast, not toward the post, not toward the tollhouse—away, cutting through the chandler's alley where the coat meant nothing to the rats and the gulls, and leaned against a salt-crusted wall, and breathed.

The foolscap crackled against their ribs. The death certificate copy. Corroboration, in a clerk's honest hand, for the ledger page sewn into the shirt lining. Two documents now. One checkpoint passed in advance, in a sense—the paper trail already moving where Ansel's body couldn't yet.

And underneath the triumph, the shame, still fizzing. Because Ansel had done it wearing a dead man's disgrace, had spent another man's ruined name like coin, and it had *worked*, and working felt good, and feeling good about it felt like becoming Vane. Borrowed authority, Odile had called it, but borrowing implied giving back, and Ansel was beginning to understand that some things, once worn, changed the shape of the body beneath.

The chestnut was still in their pocket. Ansel ate it standing in the alley, cold, tasting of charcoal and salt, and it was the best thing they'd eaten in years, which was its own kind of indictment.

Then footsteps at the alley mouth—two men, heavyset, in coats cut better than dockwork required, and one of them said, without raising his voice, in the mild tone of a foreman noting a detail:

"There. Blue coat. Thought the Brannock lad's kit went missing."

Ansel did not run. Running confirmed everything. They straightened, chin up, entitled, and walked past the two men at a constable's unhurried pace, close enough to see the Harrow & Vane pin glinting on one lapel, close enough to hear the second man murmur, "Let him walk. Watch where he goes."

Vane's quiet men had found the scent.

Ansel rounded the corner, kept walking, mind moving faster than feet—*they'll search the attic, they'll watch Odile's door, they know the uniform exists*—and the plan that had seemed solid at dawn began, in the space of twenty steps, to come apart like wet rope.

Three checkpoints. Two documents. And now, hunters on the route.

The ice on the river groaned once, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep.
