## 9. Old Debts

The trouble with mending constables' coats, Odile Brannock had learned over eleven years of laundry steam and lye-burned knuckles, was that every stitch you put into a uniform taught you the shape of the man who wore it. She could read a sergeant's temper in the wear at his elbows. She could tell which inspectors took bribes by how carefully they kept their cuffs clean. And she had known, for three days now, that her brother Callum would come home, because she had taken his old uniform out of the trunk in the attic and the house had felt wrong ever since—like a tooth pulled from a jaw, like a silence with a shape.

She'd told herself he'd never know. He hadn't touched that trunk in six years. He kept his shame the way other men kept whiskey: sealed up, out of sight, taken only in private doses.

But shame, Odile knew, was the one debt that collected itself.

He came on a Thursday, in the blue hour between lamplighting and full dark, when the fog off the Sable came up the hill thick enough to drink. She heard him before she saw him—the particular drag of his left boot, the heel worn crooked from six years of walking nowhere in particular—and she set down the shirt she was pressing and stood very still in the kitchen, listening to him fumble at the door he still had a key to, because she had never had the heart to change the lock.

"Odile." His voice came through the wood, sanded smooth by drink but underneath it still carrying that official timbre, the voice he'd used on drunks and dock thieves for nine years. "I know you're in there. The light's moving."

She opened the door.

Callum Brannock filled the frame the way ruin fills a room. He'd been a big man once, broad through the chest from drill-yard work, and the bigness was still there but it had gone soft and slumped, settled low around him like ballast. His mustache, once waxed to regulation points, hung gray and untrimmed. His eyes were the worst part. They were clear. Whatever he'd drunk tonight, he hadn't drunk enough to blur what he'd come to say.

"You've been in my trunk," he said.

"Come in out of the fog."

"I said—" He stopped himself. That was new, too; the old Callum would have rolled straight over her. He stepped inside, dripping, and stood in the kitchen doorway looking at the ironing board, the mending basket, the neat stacks of constabulary linen waiting for Monday delivery, as if seeing all of it for the first time as what it was: an intelligence post disguised as a washhouse. "How long have you been reading their papers, Odie?"

"I mend collars. I don't read them."

"You always were a liar," he said, without heat, and sat down heavily in the chair by the stove, the chair their father had died in, and put his face in his hands. "Three days ago I went up for my good boots. Winter's coming on and my others are done. And the trunk was moved. You can't fool me about that trunk, Odile. I carried it from the depot myself. I know its weight to the ounce, and I know where it sits against the floorboards, because those boards have a dip in them and the trunk sits in the dip like a boat in a berth." He looked up at her. "The uniform's gone, isn't it. My uniform."

Odile poured tea she knew he wouldn't want and set it in front of him anyway, because their mother had raised them that way: grief, rage, accusation—all of it got handled better with something warm in reach.

"It needed airing," she said.

"Don't." The word cracked like ice going out. "Don't do the laundress voice at me. Don't fold me away in a drawer. Who has it? Is someone wearing it? Because if some scut is walking the Tollgate district in Brannock brass, I'll hear about it inside a week, and when I hear about it, so will everyone else, and then we'll both be standing in front of a magistrate while some inspector explains to me what impersonating an officer carries these days." He leaned forward. "It's a hanging matter, Odie. Or near enough. They don't hang you for the coat. They hang you for whatever you did while wearing it."

"And who would know better than you," she said quietly, "what a man does in a coat?"

* * *

He flinched. She watched it land, watched the flinch travel through him like a current through wet rope, and she hated herself a little for aiming it there, and hated him more for giving her the target.

Because here was the thing Odile had never said aloud, not in six years, not even to herself in the dark hours when the laundry press hissed like the river: she had never believed her brother's disgrace was the whole story. The official version was simple enough. Constable Callum Brannock, nine years' service, commendations for flood rescue and wharf riot duty, dismissed in the winter of '89 for falsifying a report—a drowning report, no less, the lowest crime a river-port officer could commit short of murder. The commissioners had made an example of him. His name had come off the duty roster and gone into the gossip of every washhouse in Sablemouth within the month.

But Odile had mended his coat the night he came home after that last shift. She had held it under the lamp looking for tears and stains, the way she always did, and she had found neither. What she had found was her brother sitting at this same table, sober for once, staring at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger, saying over and over, *I wouldn't sign it, Odie. I wouldn't put my name on it.* And then, by morning, he had signed something—or been said to have signed something—and the name Brannock was mud from the Tollgate to the Icehouse Bridge.

She had asked him once, only once, what report it was. He had looked at her with an expression she'd spent six years pretending not to understand, and he'd said, "You don't want to carry it too," and had drunk himself steadily toward oblivion ever since.

Now she set down the teapot and folded her arms, and gave him back his own words.

"The coat is being used," she said. "By someone doing what you couldn't. Someone carrying paper past checkpoints that would stop anyone else. Paper that belongs to a child."

His eyes narrowed. Drink made him slow, but it had never made him stupid; that was the tragedy of Callum Brannock, that he drowned his wits nightly and they floated back up every morning intact. "What child?"

"A foundling. Left at Harrow & Vane's door in February with a note telling whoever found her to forget her face. And Vane intends to do exactly that, along with a ledger page proving whose she is, and along with anyone who remembers it." She let that sit. "You remember the Marchand girl. The heiress. Fell off the Icehouse Bridge two winters back. Very sad. Very thoroughly investigated."

Something moved behind Callum's eyes—something old and drowned coming up for air.

"What about her?" he said. His voice had changed. The official timbre was gone entirely now; what remained was raw.

"The baby wears a birthmark behind her ear. Same as the Marchand girl had. Same as the Marchand girl's mother had, if the portraits in the society pages are to be believed. And there's a page out of a company ledger that says the Marchand estate passes to that child, and a second entry right beneath it authorizing the child's 'disposition.'" Odile heard her own voice go flat and hard, the voice she used on delivery boys who shorted her soap. "Disposition, Callum. Of a baby. Written in a counting house in ink worth more than our whole street."

Callum sat very still. Steam ticked in the pipes. Somewhere down the hill a ship's bell counted eight, and neither of them moved until it finished.

"That's not possible," he said finally. "Vane wouldn't—Harrow & Vane is a chartered company. There are magistrates on their subscription list. Half the bench drinks their sherry."

"Now you're learning," Odile said.

* * *

He stood up then, abruptly, and went to the window, and stood looking down at the street where the fog was turning the gaslamps into smudges of themselves. She watched his back, the slope of it, the way his shoulders had forgotten their parade-ground set, and she thought: *he's deciding*. She knew that posture. It was the posture of a man weighing whether to walk back out into the fog and the bottle, or whether to turn around and pick up a weight he'd set down six years ago.

"You should burn that coat," he said to the window. "That's the smart move. Burn the coat, give the baby to a church, keep your head down. That's what I'd tell any citizen who came to me with a scheme like this. That's what I'd write in the report." A pause. "If anyone still let me write reports."

"We're not burning anything."

"No." He turned around, and his face had gone through the drink and out the other side into something harder. "No, of course not. You never could leave a thing alone. You were four years old digging in the tide flats for things that bit you." He came back to the table but didn't sit. "Here's what happens next, Odie, and you'll listen, because I'm the only person in this city who knows exactly how badly this goes wrong. First: whoever's wearing my coat gets made inside a fortnight. The Tollgate sergeants know every crease of every uniform on their beat—I should know, I signed off their kit inspections. Second: Vane keeps men on retainer for exactly this kind of errand. Quiet men. I knew two of them when I wore the badge; they were quiet then, too. Third—" and here his voice dropped, and Odile felt the hair rise on her forearms, because she recognized the tone now, the tone of a man reciting procedure, the last liturgy he still believed in—"third, if that ledger page is real, it isn't evidence yet. It's paper. Paper becomes evidence in front of a magistrate, and the only way paper gets in front of a magistrate in this town without Vane hearing about it first is if it arrives attached to a sworn statement from a credible witness." He stopped. Swallowed. "Or a disgraced one."

Odile looked at her brother for a long moment.

"You're offering to testify."

"I'm offering nothing." But his hands, resting on the back of their father's chair, had tightened on the rail until the knuckles whitened, and she saw that they were shaking, and not from drink. "I'm telling you what the law requires, because knowing the law is the only thing I've got left, and I'll be damned if I watch you spend it badly. If you want that child's claim to hold water, you need a witness who can speak to the circumstances of the Marchand woman's death. An official witness. And there is precisely one man in Sablemouth who knows what was written and unwritten in that drowning report." His jaw worked. "And he's standing in your kitchen smelling like the Anchor Tap."

"Then tell me," Odile said. "Tell me what was in the report."

For a moment—one long, dangerous moment—she thought he would. His mouth opened. Something rose in his face like a body rising in flooded water, slow and unstoppable, and Odile braced herself for it, for the truth that would explain six years of bottles and locked trunks and a brother who flinched at his own name.

Then it sank again.

"Not tonight," he said hoarsely. "Not drunk. If I say it drunk, it's a drunk's story, and Vane's people will peel it apart by breakfast. If I say it—" he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes—"if I say it, I say it sober, in daylight, in front of a magistrate, and I lose whatever's left of me forever. Do you understand? The day I testify, the last door closes. No reinstatement. No pension. Nothing. Just the truth and a coat I can't legally wear." He lowered his hands. "So I need time. One day. Give me one day to decide whether I'm a witness or just another drunk shouting in a washhouse."

Odile studied him. Eleven years of reading men through their linen had taught her that fabric didn't lie but men always did, mostly to themselves, and Callum was lying to himself right now about needing a day—he'd already decided; she could see it in the set of his mouth—but she also knew better than to rush a man across a bridge he was still building.

"One day," she said. "And Callum. The coat stays where it is. Whoever wears it answers to me."

A ghost of the old grin flickered across his face, the grin that had made half the constabulary call him *Lucky Brannock* back when luck was still a thing he had. "Wouldn't dream of touching it. Though I'll tell you this for free, Odie, since you're mending for the force and ought to know: check the lining. Regulation greatcoats and tunics get relined at the depot every few years, and mine was due the winter I was dismissed. Never went in. Which means—" he tapped the table twice, a habit from his report-writing days, tap-tap, item noted—"which means whatever's sewn into that lining now wasn't put there by the quartermaster. Whoever altered my coat knew what they were doing, or they didn't, and either way a sharp-eyed sergeant at the Tollgate is going to run his thumb along that seam and ask why a ten-year-old tunic stitches like it came off the needle yesterday."

Odile went cold. She thought of the ledger page, fine company paper folded small, hidden where she'd have hidden it—in plain cloth, in the one place a search would pat but not unpick.

"I'll see to the stitching," she said.

"See that you do." He took his hat off the peg, hesitated at the door with the fog pressing gray against the glass behind him, and for a second he looked, briefly, unbearably, like the constable he'd been—young, straight-backed, sure of where his feet belonged. "Odie. The Marchand girl's death. When I tell it, you're going to wish you'd burned the coat."

Then the door shut behind him, and Odile stood alone in the steam-smelling kitchen with the tea going cold and her heart going fast, and she understood that the plot had just doubled in weight: they had gained a witness, or they had gained a leak, and there was no way to know which until the moment it mattered.

Down the hill, faint through the fog, she heard his crooked boot-heel fade toward the Anchor Tap—and then, after a dozen steps, stop. Turn. Head instead toward the dark bulk of the Company Wharf, where the night watchmen knew him by sight and the gate ledgers recorded every visitor by name.

Her brother was going to Harrow & Vane's door, drunk, at nine o'clock at night, and Odile was already reaching for her shawl.
