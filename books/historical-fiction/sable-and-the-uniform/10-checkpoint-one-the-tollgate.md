## 10. Checkpoint One: The Tollgate

The ledger page rode against Ansel's ribs like a second heartbeat.

Odile had sewn it into the lining of the constable's tunic three nights before, working by candlelight with a sailmaker's needle and thread the color of the wool, her fingers steady in a way her voice was not. "If they search you," she'd said, biting off the thread, "they'll pat the shoulders, the breast, the belt. Nobody pats a hem. Nobody in the history of uniforms has ever patted a hem." She'd turned the coat inside out, examined her work, and pronounced it done in the tone of a woman who has just buried something alive.

Now Ansel stood in the pre-dawn gray of Harrow & Vane's grain yard, wearing that coat, and understood what she had meant.

The plan had come together the way ice forms—slowly, invisibly, and then all at once. The company moved grain downriver twice a week, wagon convoys from the bonded store to the Tollgate, where the municipal inspector checked manifests against cargo before the loads passed into the city's hands and, eventually, onto the steamers at Company Wharf. A convoy needed an escort. An escort needed papers. And since the resignation of Constable Brannock—Callum's disgrace now two years old, his name still struck through in the district rolls but not yet forgotten—the Tollgate sergeants had grown accustomed to seeing company men in borrowed police blue when the constabulary itself was short-handed. Which was always.

"You'll be escort number three," Odile had said. "Nobody looks hard at number three. Number one talks to the sergeant. Number two checks the wheels. Number three stands by the tail of the last wagon looking bored."

"I can do bored," Ansel had said.

"You've been practicing your whole life."

* * *

The fog came off the river thick as wet wool, and the grain wagons moved through it like slow ships, four horses apiece, the drivers hunched under oilskins. Ansel walked at the tail of the last one, hands clasped behind their back in imitation of every constable they had ever observed standing still, and discovered that the uniform changed the weight of walking itself. In the plain coat they had worn for eleven years, people's eyes slid off them like rain off slate. In the blue, eyes landed. Stayed. Weighed. A woman sweeping her step straightened as the convoy passed. Two boys stopped their game and stood at something like attention. A dog that would have snarled at any other stranger only watched.

It should have felt like power. It felt instead like being made of glass—a body suddenly visible, suddenly breakable, every seam and stitch a question someone might ask.

*Don't think about the page.* They thought about nothing else. The page: thirty lines in Director Vane's own crabbed hand, recording the foundling's parentage and the sum of nine thousand pounds held in trust, and beneath it, dated four days later, a second entry authorizing *disposition of encumbrance per directors' resolution*. Encumbrance. The word for a child. The word for Ansel, too, once—the ledgers at the indenture office had used it, *one encumbrance transferred, age six, name entered as none given*. The page against their ribs was warm from their own body heat, which was absurd, paper didn't hold warmth, but they could feel it anyway, could feel it the way Sable must have felt them carrying her these past weeks, a weight that was also a life.

They had left her sleeping in Odile's back room with Mrs. Quill, the widow who took in washing and asked no questions, and the leaving had cost more than any dive into cold water ever had.

"Oi." The driver of the last wagon, a heavy man named Prewitt who smelled of oats and pipe smoke, tipped his chin forward. "Gate."

The Tollgate rose out of the fog all at once, a brick arch spanning the river road, lanterns burning yellow on either side of the customs door. Two constables of the genuine article flanked the arch, truncheons tucked, breath steaming. Beyond them, in the lit doorway of the tollhouse, a figure in a sergeant's greatcoat stood with a manifest board under one arm.

Ansel counted heartbeats. Kept walking. Bored. Tail of the last wagon. Number three.

* * *

The first escort—a real constable, moonlighting, named Hask—went up to the sergeant first, as planned. Papers exchanged. Voices low and professional. The second escort circled the wagons kicking tires and lifting canvas corners, as planned. Ansel stood at the tailboard and studied the middle distance, as planned, and it was all going exactly as planned right up until the sergeant finished with Hask and looked directly at them.

"Sergeant Molyneux," Odile had said, spreading her intelligence across the kitchen table like laundry to be sorted. "Twenty-two years at the Tollgate. Eyes like a fishmonger counting coins. He'll know Hask and he'll know the wheel-man, because they've run this route before. You he won't know, and that's your danger and your armor both. He can't challenge you without knowing who you are—but if you give him nothing to know, he'll make something up."

"Molyneux," the sergeant said now, coming down the tollhouse steps with his boots ringing on the cobbles. He was not a large man. He did not need to be. "New face."

Ansel touched their cap brim. "Escort, sir. Third wagon."

"I can see where you're standing. I asked what you are."

"Harrow & Vane detail, sir. Temporary assignment." Keep it short. Short answers were true answers; long ones were confessions.

"Temporary." Molyneux turned the word over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. His eyes went over the uniform with the unhurried thoroughness of a man undressing a lie. Cap—correct issue, though the sweatband was stiff in a way that suggested it hadn't been worn daily. Tunic—correct, buttons correct, belt correct. Boots—

The boots. Ansel had thought of the boots. Had thought of everything except—

"These are garrison pattern," Molyneux said. "Constabulary wears the half-laced. Where'd you draw those?"

The fog pressed in. The horses stamped. Somewhere ahead, Hask was saying something to the wheel-man, their voices a comfortable murmur that had no idea it was about to become irrelevant.

"Quartermaster's error, sir," Ansel said. "Reported it. Told to wear them till replacement."

"Mm." Molyneux stepped closer. Close enough that Ansel could smell the tea on his breath and see the small scar through his left eyebrow. "Name?"

Here it was. The moment the whole scheme balanced on, the needle-point Odile had warned about: *he will ask your name, because asking names is his trade, and you cannot answer, because you haven't got one worth the asking.*

"Brannock, sir."

They heard themselves say it and felt the world tilt. It had been nearest to hand—Odile's brother, the disgraced constable, the name already half-known along the route, plausible, available. It was also, they realized a half-second too late, possibly the worst choice available, because Molyneux's face changed. Not dramatically. A settling, like silt in still water.

"*Brannock*," he said. "There was a Brannock here. Callum Brannock. Struck off the rolls winter before last." His eyes came up from the buttons and pinned Ansel where they stood. "You'd be what relation?"

And Ansel—who had rehearsed manifests and procedures and the exact angle of a bored stance, who had sewn secrets into linings and memorized grain weights—had nothing. The silence stretched. One heartbeat. Two. Behind Molyneux, Hask had stopped talking and turned to look.

"Brother," Ansel said finally, because there was no other direction to fall.

"Brother." Molyneux smiled without warmth. "Funny thing about brothers. They tend to know things. Like—" he raised his voice slightly, conversationally, without turning his head, "—like whether the force issued its men garrison boots in '88, or whether that's a story a person tells when they've never worn issue anything in their life."

His hand came up—not fast, not threatening, just administrative—and took Ansel by the collar of the tunic. Turned the fabric toward the lantern light. Read the maker's stamp inside the collar the way a man reads a label on a bottle of wine he suspects is vinegar.

"This is Brannock's coat," Molyneux said quietly. "I inspected him in it a hundred times. There's a burn mark on the inner cuff from a candle at the Anchor Tap, New Year's Eve, '87. I wrote the report on it myself."

The fog closed in. The river waited somewhere below, black and patient. Ansel's mind went white and empty, and into that emptiness, unbidden, came the image of Sable asleep, fists curled, the birthmark on her wrist like a small brown comma—and the understanding, cold and total, that everything ended here, at a tollgate, over a boot.

Then a voice from the tollhouse doorway said: "Sergeant. If you're quite finished molesting my escort, I've a manifest to sign and a tide to catch."

Callum Brannock came down the steps out of the fog like something conjured—greatcoat buttoned, warrant card already in his hand, his gait carrying just enough stagger to be weather rather than drink. He had been at the Anchor Tap until midnight, Odile had said, and Ansel had believed her, and had therefore put him nowhere in the plan, because a man that far gone could not be relied on for anything. Apparently he had gone home at some point. Apparently he had slept. Apparently, sober, Callum Brannock was a different animal entirely.

Molyneux's grip on the collar loosened by degrees. "Inspector. This man claims to be your—"

"My escort. Yes." Callum reached them and stood at Ansel's shoulder, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched theirs. Up close he smelled of soap, not whisky; he had dressed carefully, and the care showed in details Ansel hadn't known to look for—the warrant card angled correctly, the cap set at regulation tilt, everything precise as a man performing himself. "There was a question about the boots, I understand."

"There was a question about the whole uniform," Molyneux said. "It's mine. I know it. That's Callum Brannock's tunic, burn mark and all, and this is either his ghost or somebody wearing a dead man's—"

"A suspended man's," Callum said mildly. "Not dead. Though I appreciate the sentiment." He produced a folded paper from inside his coat. "Magistrate Corven's letter, authorizing auxiliary escorts for company convoys while the district runs short. Dated the ninth. Your office countersigned it—here." His finger tapped the bottom corner. "Auxiliaries draw mixed kit, Sergeant. Half the auxiliaries on this river are wearing their dead fathers' coats. It's a scandal. It's also policy."

Molyneux took the letter. Read it. Read it again. Ansel watched him read it and understood that the letter might be entirely genuine or entirely invented and that they would never be able to tell, and that this—this smooth, seamless, procedural fluency—was what Odile had meant when she said her brother knew procedure better than the men who enforced it.

"The magistrate's letter covers the escort," Molyneux said slowly. "It doesn't cover the name. This one gave me *Brannock*."

"And I'm sure that seemed wise at the time," Callum said, with a glance at Ansel that was, beneath its irritation, almost admiring. "Auxiliary uses the sponsoring officer's name on the gate book—it's in the same circular, paragraph four, so the watch commander knows whose authority he falls under. Look it up if you like. Circular 12-C, Michaelmas term." He leaned in, and his voice dropped into a register of pure institutional weariness. "Aldous. Twenty-two years, and you're going to write a report explaining why you delayed a lawful grain shipment at slack water to interrogate a magistrate-authorized escort about his boots? Corven signs the Tollgate budget. Think about how that conversation goes."

The silence lasted five seconds. Ten. A horse blew out its breath in a long white plume.

"Sign your book," Molyneux said at last, thrusting the gate ledger at Callum rather than Ansel. "Both of you. Wagon's cleared. Canvas stays down till the wharf inspection—you know that."

"We know that," Callum said pleasantly, and signed. He wrote Ansel's false name beneath his own in a quick confident hand, and watching him do it, Ansel felt something loosen and something else tighten—the loosening was relief, the tightening was the knowledge that they had just been saved by a man they did not trust, by a name that was not theirs, in a book that would now forever record that a *Brannock* had passed the Tollgate at dawn on the day a certain ledger page went missing from a certain counting house. Trails. Everywhere trails. Being visible meant leaving tracks, and tracks could be followed home.

"Walk with me," Callum murmured, taking Ansel's elbow as the convoy groaned forward through the arch. Under his breath, pitched for them alone: "Boots were a gift from God and you spent it on a name. Never spend a gift on a name. Names get checked. Burn marks get checked. Boredom doesn't." He glanced sideways, and for a moment the performance dropped and Ansel saw the man underneath—hungover, frightened, and fiercely awake. "My sister is going to kill me for being here. So before she does: the page is in the coat, yes?"

Ansel said nothing.

"That's yes," Callum said. "Good. Because Molyneux will write tonight's incident in his pocket book before his supper—he writes everything, it's his one virtue—and the entry will say *auxiliary escort, name given as Brannock, referred to Inspector Brannock*. Which means that by tomorrow morning, anyone who reads that book knows a stranger in my old coat passed this gate with company grain." He exhaled through his teeth. "Vane keeps friends at the Tollgate. It's only a matter of time before a friend mentions it."

The arch fell behind them. Ahead, through the thinning fog, the masts of the Company Wharf rose like a forest of gallows, and Ansel walked on in a dead man's coat with a stolen fortune against their ribs, trying to decide which was heavier: the page, or the price of having been seen carrying it.

Behind them, at the tollhouse, Sergeant Molyneux was already reaching for his pocket book.
