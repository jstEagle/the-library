## 11. The Price of a Name

The summons came at dawn, and it came politely, which was how I knew I was already dead.

"Director Vane will see you in his office," said Mr. Quill, the under-clerk, standing at the end of my bunk row with a lantern held too high, its light flat on my face like a hand. "Immediately."

Not *you're wanted*. Not *report to the counting house*. *Will see you.* The grammar of a man who had already decided what I was worth.

I dressed in the dark while the other bunks breathed around me. The ledger page was not on my person—it never was, anymore; it lived sewn into the lining of a coat I did not wear to work—but my hands still went through the old ritual anyway, touching the places where things could be hidden, confirming they were empty. Rope coil in the loft. Nothing here. Nothing here but a man with no name worth taking.

That was the whole of my armor, and I put it on button by button.

* * *

Vane's office sat above the counting house like a head above a body that did all the eating. I had been inside once before, six years old and newly indentured, made to stand on a chalk mark on the floor while a clerk read out my description—*female, approx. six years, brown hair, brown eyes, no distinguishing marks, name: none given*—and Director Harrow himself had looked through me the way you look through a window at weather. That had been Harrow. Harrow was dead. Vane was worse, because Vane saw people.

He saw me now. He was standing at the window with his back to the door when Quill shut me in, watching the river, and he let the silence run long enough that I understood it was a tool he kept sharpened on every visitor.

"Kavanagh," he said at last, without turning. He knew my surname. My stomach dropped a full fathom. "Or is it Kavanagh? The ledgers are inconsistent. Some say *no family name recorded*. Some say the mother gave one at intake and it was struck for tidiness." Now he turned, and he smiled the way winter smiles. "You've been with us fourteen years and I couldn't have told you yesterday whether your eyes were brown or black. Remarkable, isn't it. A person can work a wharf for fourteen years and leave no more impression than water leaves on a piling."

"I'm sorry, sir," I said, because that was the liturgy.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." He crossed to his desk but didn't sit, which meant this wasn't business; sitting was for business. Standing was for favors. "There was an incident in February. A bundle left at the company door during the storm. It was dealt with. Sent along to the workhouse by morning, as such things are." His eyes stayed on mine with the pleasant pressure of a thumb on a bruise. "But there seems to be some confusion among the staff. Talk of effects. A blanket. A note. Items that ought to have gone with the child and appear to have gone—elsewhere."

"There was no note that I saw, sir."

"No?" He opened a drawer without looking at it, the way a man opens a drawer he has rehearsed opening. "I have your bunk record here. Your laundry record. Your ration record. Fourteen years, and do you know what I notice? Not one entry in your own hand. Not one signature. Do you know why?"

Because I can't write, I thought. Because the company taught me rope knots instead of letters. Because a diver's child who could write might write her way off the wharf.

"No, sir," I said.

"Because you don't exist, Kavanagh. Not legally. Not as far as any registry in this province cares. You are a line item. You are cargo that walks." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow the cruelest thing available to him. "And I want you to understand that I am not saying it as a threat. I'm saying it because I'm about to make you an offer, and I want you to weigh it honestly."

He set two documents on the desk between us.

I knew papers. I had spent a life learning which papers mattered by watching who carried them and who saluted them. These were heavy ones. Cream stock, wax seals, the Harrow & Vane crest pressed deep enough to cast a shadow.

"Emancipation," Vane said. "Full discharge of your indenture, signed by me, witnessed by the harbor registrar. And this—" he tapped the second sheet, "—a certificate of free status. Legal personhood. A name entered in the provincial rolls. *Ansel Kavanagh*, resident of Sablemouth, free to sign contracts, hold wages, marry, rent rooms above ground, walk through the Tollgate without showing anyone your wrist-tag. Everything you have never had, in two sheets of paper."

The room tilted very slightly, the way the wharf tilts when a heavy load shifts.

I want to tell you I stood there unmoved. I want to tell you I thought of Sable sleeping in the flour bin behind Odile's stove, of the ledger page against my ribs, of everything I'd already risked. I did think of those things. But underneath them, rising fast and silent like a body coming up from deep water, was something older and hungrier.

A name. In a book. In ink that officials respected.

I had spent fourteen years being *the girl on the rope line*. Before that I had been *the pearl-bed woman's brat*, and before even that—six years old, kneeling on the intake floor—I had learned that a name is not a possession. It's permission. My mother had called me something across the water every evening until the river took her, some soft syllable or other, and the company clerk had heard it wrong or hadn't bothered hearing it at all, and so it had gone into the book wrong or not at all, and that error had eaten my whole life. Every door I'd ever been turned away from had been closed by a missing line of ink.

And here was a man offering to write the line.

"You would be free," Vane said gently. "Truly free. No more dawn calls. No more wrist-tag. You could take wages from another firm. Leave Sablemouth entirely, if you liked. Go downriver, upriver, over the sea. Most people live their whole lives without ever once being asked to show their papers. Imagine joining them." He paused, letting the picture finish developing. "All I ask is a small housekeeping matter. The foundling's effects. The blanket, the wrapping, anything left with the child that night—and especially any paper that may have come with it. A note, we believe. Possibly more. Hand those to Mr. Quill, and these—" he slid the documents an inch toward me, "—are yours by Friday."

"The child's things," I said. My voice sounded far away, like someone calling from mid-river.

"A dead woman's linen and a scrap of writing. Sentimental objects, nothing more. Whereas you—" and here he did sit, folding his hands, becoming reasonable, becoming almost kind, "—you are a living person with one life to spend, currently spending it invisible. I am offering to make you visible, Kavanagh. Permanently. Legally. All you have to give up is a rag."

*A rag.*

I thought of the birthmark. I thought of the heiress I'd glimpsed once from the rigging of the *Merrow's Debt*, seven years ago, laughing at some gentleman on the pier—a lady so far above me she might as well have been painted on the sky, and the only reason I remembered her at all was the small wine-colored mark below her ear, shaped like a comma, like a sentence that wasn't finished. Then a baby in my arms in a storm, screaming, and the same comma below her ear, unfinished sentence, unfinished sentence.

They hadn't sent that child to any workhouse. I'd known it since February the way you know ice is thin—by the sound under your feet. Whatever paper came with her, whatever the note said, Vane wanted it burned, and the baby erased, and everyone who remembered either of them erased after. I was not being offered freedom. I was being offered the price list for my soul, itemized in cream stock and wax.

And God help me, I stood there wanting it anyway.

You cannot understand unless you have been nobody. Papers were not abstractions to me. They were the difference between Quill walking past a constable with a nod and me pressing my wrist to a lantern. They were the difference between renting a room and sleeping in a sail loft. When I imagined signing my name—not a mark, a *name*, in front of witnesses who wrote it down correctly—I felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since I was six years old. Vane had found the one lock I didn't know I carried and turned it gently, and I hated him for knowing, and I hated myself for the warmth spreading through me anyway.

"I need time to consider," I said. Which was the first honest thing I'd said since entering, and we both knew it.

"Of course." Vane nodded, gracious as spring. "Until Friday. Quill will draw up nothing until then. But Kavanagh—" and the kindness drained out of his face so completely and so fast that I finally saw the thing underneath, the cold engine of him, "—understand what happens if those effects surface somewhere they shouldn't. There is a magistrate who owes this company certain considerations. There are registries that can be amended as easily as they were written. If a certain page appears in a certain courtroom attached to a certain foundling, then a certain clerk will discover that a certain indentured worker named Kavanagh has been miscounting company property for years. Theft. It carries seven years' hard labor. For a person with no legal existence, there is no labor camp that must feed you. Do you follow the arithmetic?"

"I follow it, sir."

"Good." He smiled again, and it reached all the way to his eyes, which was the worst thing yet. "You always struck me as intelligent. Invisible, but intelligent. Those are often the same quality, viewed from different sides of a desk."

Quill opened the door for me. On my way out, Vane added, almost as an afterthought:

"Oh—and do give my regards to Mrs. Brannock. Fine laundress. We value discretion in our contractors. I trust she values it too."

* * *

I walked back along the wharf with the morning coming up gray off the water and my heart going like a drum in a parade I hadn't agreed to march in.

He knew about Odile. Of course he knew about Odile. A director doesn't survive in Sablemouth by not knowing things; he survives by knowing everything cheap and acting on the expensive parts only. The mention of her was a message: *I see your allies. I count them. Friday.*

I stopped at the rope loft under pretense of checking inventory and put my hand into the coil where the ledger page slept in its oilskin. Still there. Still real. Paper has a way of feeling less real every day you carry it, and Vane's cream stock had felt very real indeed.

Here is the arithmetic he'd left me, laid out honest:

Take the offer, and I get everything I have ever wanted—except the child gets nothing. She goes into the rolls as a foundling of no parentage, raised at parish expense until some factory or fishing fleet eats her at twelve, exactly as they ate me, and the comma-shaped mark below her ear becomes just a mark on just another girl, and the heiress stays dead twice over. I get a name; she loses hers forever. The trade is that clean.

Refuse, and I stay cargo that walks. No name, no papers, no protection—only a stolen page, a borrowed uniform hanging in a laundress's attic, and a plan with three checkpoints and a river that wants its evidence back.

And yet.

I stood in the loft with the rope smell thick around me and thought about my mother, and for once I didn't think about her drowning. I thought about her on the good days, on the shingle with her breath-control rhythm tapped out against my back—*in slow, hold slow, out slower*—teaching me the one inheritance that couldn't be struck from a ledger by any clerk alive. She died holding her breath for pearls other women wore. Nobody ever wrote her name right either. But she never once, not on the worst day, tried to buy her way out of herself by selling someone smaller.

The bell rang for the morning shift. Down on the wharf, the line was forming, and I took my place in it, wrist out, tag up, invisible as ever.

Except something had changed, and it took me half the shift hauling grain sacks to name it: Vane's offer hadn't weakened me. It had shown me exactly what I was worth to him, which meant I now knew precisely how frightened he was. Men don't buy rags with emancipation papers. Men don't learn laundresses' names over blankets. Somewhere in the low archive beneath the Icehouse Bridge sits a record he cannot amend, and the ice above it is groaning, and he knows it as well as I do—which means the clock he's really running isn't mine.

It's the river's.

At noon, crossing the yard, I passed two men I didn't know standing by the coal shed, not working, not talking, watching the office door. Vane's quiet men, or quieter ones. One of them tipped his hat to me—an odd courtesy—and I walked on with my face arranged into nothing while my mind ran ahead downriver, checkpoint to checkpoint, to a bridge where the guards had recently doubled.

Friday, he'd said.

I intended to be under the Icehouse Bridge long before Friday. And I intended, when the time came, to stand in front of witnesses and speak a name aloud—the child's true name, and mine—with no paper's permission at all.

But first there was a uniform in an attic, and a route through three gates, and a drunk disgraced constable whose brother's coat fit me better than my own life ever had.

The ice on the river boomed once, far off, like a door closing—or opening. I pulled my cap down and went back to work, carrying my answer where Vane would never think to search for it.

Under my ribs. Where the truth had always lived.
