## 12. Icehouse Bridge

Odile Brannock had spent thirty years reading men by the state of their collars, and she read the two men on Icehouse Bridge in the time it took her to pass beneath it: one bored, one not. The bored one leaned on the rail, watching the river carry its freight of broken ice floes toward the sea. The other stood with his weight forward, hands clasped behind his back, scanning the near bank the way a man scans a room where something of his has gone missing.

She kept walking. A laundress with a basket of collars and cuffs had no business lingering on the ice road, and every second of lingering was a second the sharp one might spend remembering her face.

The basket was real. That was the trick of it—she had learned early, from her mother, from the river itself, that the best disguise is not a costume but a true thing carried for a false reason. The collars were genuine. The invoice in her pocket was genuine, made out to the Icehouse Company's foreman, who had indeed ordered two dozen starched collars for his men and would indeed receive them, eventually, if Odile ever found her way back to the shop. She had simply added a destination to her errand.

The ice road ran along the east bank, packed hard by sledges, white and rutted, running from the Tollgate district all the way down past the icehouses to the mouth of the river. In ordinary winters it was a highway—farmers hauling ice cut from the millpond, carters taking the shortcut rather than pay the bridge toll. This winter it was nearly empty. Word had gone round, the way word goes round in Sablemouth, that the Icehouse Company had posted guards after a theft, and honest carters had decided their loads could wait for spring.

Odile did not blame them. She counted the guards as she walked: two on the bridge itself, one at the icehouse door, and—when she angled her head, pretending to fuss with her scarf—a fourth smoking a pipe at the top of the stairs that led down to the river gate. Four men to guard a building full of ice. Four men, in her long experience, was not a number a company paid for out of caution. Four men was a number a company paid for out of fear.

She reached the end of the ice road where it met the shore path and turned, as any laundress would, toward the workers' row behind the icehouses. The foreman's wife took in washing. That much was true as well. Odile had washed for her twice before, in the autumn, and the woman had praised her starch work to anyone who would listen.

"Miz Brannock." The foreman's wife—Tilda, broad and red-handed—met her at the door with a look Odile had learned to dread, because it was the look of a woman about to pay less than she'd promised. "You heard, then."

"Heard what?"

"About the theft." Tilda glanced over her shoulder into the house, then stepped out onto the step and lowered her voice, which in Tilda meant the whole street would know by supper. "They took a man's coat off the drying line, if you can believe it. A coat. And inside the coat—" she held up one finger— "the foreman's own key ring. Company keys. They fished them out of the coat and left the coat in the mud like it was nothing. Like a man's coat is nothing."

"Terrible," Odile said, and began unpacking the basket, because a laundress's hands must always be moving.

"Tilda." Her husband's voice came from inside, sharp. A moment later the foreman filled the doorway—a big man gone soft in the middle, with the pale, boiled look of a man who spent his days in an icehouse. He looked at Odile the way men look at furniture. "You'll take the washing and go. And you'll not come round asking after the theft, because there's nothing to tell and men asking after it."

"I never ask after anything," Odile said truthfully.

He grunted and withdrew. Tilda gathered the bundle—a pillowcase of collars, a sack of shirts—and pressed a coin into Odile's palm, and it was even, nearly, the agreed amount.

But Odile lingered on the step a moment longer, adjusting her scarf, and Tilda, who had a laundress's own appetite for talk and a foreman's wife's loneliness, could not help herself. "They've got men on the bridge day and night," she whispered. "And they've had the river gate opened twice this week. Opened, in February. My Tom says the water under the icehouse is black as ink and cold enough to kill in a minute, and they've had men down there in diving dress, if you please. Diving dress! For an icehouse."

"For an icehouse," Odile agreed.

"And the third thing," Tilda said, and now her voice dropped so low Odile had to lean in, "is the archive. You know the old flood archive, under the Custom House quay? The one they said would be drained come spring? Well. My cousin's boy works the pumps there, and he says they've started already. Started the pumps this week, weeks early, and the engineers are in a state about it, because the ice hasn't broken and the river's still high, and pumping now is like bailing a boat with a hole in it. But the Company's paying for it, so the pumps run day and night." She straightened, satisfied, and took her coin's worth of gossip to its grave. "You'll have the rest of the washing next week, if the world hasn't ended."

"If the world hasn't ended," Odile said, and went.

* * *

She did not go straight home. She walked the shore path north, past the icehouses, past the shipyard, until she could see the Custom House quay from the shelter of a stack of pit props, and there she stood in the wind and did arithmetic.

The flood archive sat below the quay, a stone vault built in her grandfather's time to hold the port's duplicate records—bills of lading, registries, certificates—everything Sablemouth had ever written down twice, in case of fire. When the spring floods came, the river backed into the vault through the old culverts, and the clerks had long ago stopped pretending they could keep it dry; they simply moved the current year's papers out in March and let the vault drown, and pumped it dry again in May, and the old records sat in their oilskin wrappers under the water like bodies in a tomb, safe because no one wanted them and no one could reach them.

The ledger page in Ansel's possession named the child. The registry in the flood archive would prove the name—birth entered, mother entered, the heiress's own hand witnessed on the line. One document was a claim. Two documents was a case. And the case, Odile knew, was only as good as its weakest paper, and the weakest paper was sitting under four feet of black river water in a vault that the Company was, at this moment, draining ahead of schedule.

Why drain it early? She turned the question over the way she turned a collar, looking for the stain. If Vane wanted the records destroyed, the flood would do it for free in a few weeks. Draining the vault early meant he wanted access to it—wanted to go through the papers himself, wanted to pull the registry out before the water could swallow the evidence of what he'd done. Or wanted to make sure it was pulled and burned, quietly, with witnesses, so that no one could ever say it had existed.

Either way, the arithmetic was the same. The plan had been built on weeks. The plan now had days.

She thought of Ansel's face when she'd told them, three nights ago, that the ice would break within the month and the route was good. She thought of the way Ansel had nodded—Ansel always nodded, Ansel had been nodding and keeping their head down for twenty years—and how that nod had been the whole of their strategy for staying alive. She thought of the baby, Sable, who had taken to gripping Odile's little finger with a grip like a winch, and who would grow up, if they failed, to be a workhouse number with a fortune's name ground off her like a maker's mark filed from silver.

And she thought of Callum.

Her brother had been sober for six days now, which by his standards was a geological era. He sat in her kitchen in the evenings drinking tea and talking procedure—toll schedules, inspection rotations, the exact wording of a warrant—and the terrible thing, the thing she had not yet said aloud to Ansel, was that he was good at it. Thirty years of constabulary habit did not drain out of a man with the drink. He knew the Tollgate sergeant's name and his habits. He knew which clerks took bribes and which took flattery. He knew, better than anyone in Sablemouth, how the machinery of papers and stamps worked, because he had been one of its cogs until the day he'd refused to grind a lie into it.

He was also, she reminded herself, a man who had sold his own boots for drink, and who wanted his uniform back, and who had looked at her—only once, only for a moment, on the fourth evening—with an expression she had not been able to read, and had not liked.

She pushed off from the pit props and walked home by the back lanes, and by the time she reached her door the arithmetic had become a plan, and the plan had become a problem, and the problem had a name.

* * *

Ansel was there when she came in, sitting on the floor by the stove with the baby against their shoulder, feeding her mashed pear off the tip of a spoon with the concentration of a man defusing a mine. The uniform hung in the corner under a sheet, where it had hung since Ansel's first walk in it—a presence in the room, like a third person who never spoke.

"You're back early," Ansel said.

"I'm back with bad news, which travels faster." She set down the basket and unwound her scarf, and while her fingers worked she told it: the guards, four of them, the coat and the keys, the diving dress under the icehouse. And then the archive, and the pumps running day and night, weeks early.

Ansel had gone very still. The spoon hung in the air. Sable, deprived of pear, made a small imperious sound, and Ansel fed her the spoonful without taking their eyes off Odile.

"They're not waiting for the flood," Ansel said.

"No. The flood was going to be our ally—drown the archive, destroy what we couldn't reach, and no one the wiser. Vane's taken that off the table. He wants the vault dry and empty before the ice breaks. Whatever's in there that can hurt him, he means to have it in his own hands within days."

"Then we go in first."

"With what? The vault has one door and a stair, and both are watched once the pumps are running. The culverts are under the water line until the river drops. Ansel—" she heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used on men who wanted their collars cheap, "—the only way into that vault before the Company empties it is through the river. Through the ice. In February."

Silence. The stove ticked. Sable, sensing the change in the air the way babies sense weather, began to fuss, and Ansel stood and walked her, and Odile watched them do it—watched the small economy of the motion, the child's head tucked under their chin, the rocking that was really a kind of thinking—and she saw the moment the fear in their face turned into something else. She had seen it before, in her brother, in the years before his disgrace. It was the look of a person deciding what they were willing to lose.

"My mother dove the pearl beds," Ansel said. "Cold water. Deeper than this. She taught me the breathing before I could write my own name." A pause. "I never wrote my own name. That's the joke of it."

"It's not a joke."

"No." They looked at the sheeted uniform, then at the window, where the light was going the color of old pewter. "How many days do the pumps need?"

"Tilda's cousin's boy says the engineers want a week to get the vault dry enough for the clerks to go in. Callum will know better—he'll know the pumping schedule, the watch rotations, all of it."

"Then ask him."

"I will. And Ansel—" she caught their arm. "There's a thing I have to say, and you'll not like it. Callum knows too much now. The route, the checkpoints, the child. If Vane gets his hooks into him—drink, debt, the promise of his badge back—"

"You think he'd sell us."

"I think he's sold things before. Boots. A watch. His pride, which was the dearest of the three." She held Ansel's eyes. "I think we should plan the last run so that he never knows the date of it. Tell him a false day. Move the true one."

Ansel was quiet a long moment. The baby had gone still against their shoulder, her breath slow and even, her small fist curled in the collar of Ansel's shirt.

"He taught me the toll procedure," Ansel said at last. "He stood in your kitchen and gave us the Tollgate, and it saved us, and I watched his face while he did it and I believed him."

"I believed him too. That's what frightens me."

"Then we do what you say. A false day." Ansel looked down at the child. "But Odile—if it comes to the vault, and it's me under the ice, and something goes wrong—"

"Nothing will go wrong."

"Something always goes wrong. That's the one law the river keeps." They shifted the baby gently to the crook of their arm. "If something goes wrong, she goes to you. Not to a workhouse. Not to the parish. To you. Say it."

"I say it."

"Say it like you mean it."

Odile Brannock had buried a mother, outlived a husband, and raised a brother who broke her heart on a seasonal schedule, and she had never once in her life said a thing she did not mean. "The child comes to me," she said. "On my name, which is the only paper I own outright."

Ansel nodded. And that nod, Odile thought, watching them settle the baby into the blanket-lined drawer that served as her cradle, was the same nod they gave to everything—a nod that agreed and concealed, that had kept them alive through twenty years of being no one—but this time there was something underneath it, some new weight, and it frightened her more than the four guards on the bridge.

It was only later, when Ansel had gone to the wharf for the night shift and the house was dark, that she understood what she'd seen. It was the look of a person who had already decided to go into the water, and was simply waiting for the arithmetic to catch up with their courage.

She sat down at the kitchen table and did the arithmetic herself, in her head, the way she did everything: pumps running day and night, a week to dry the vault, the ice breaking in three weeks or less, Callum's watch rotations to be learned and his date to be lied to, and somewhere in the middle of it all a dive through river ice into a drowned room full of paper, by a person who could not swim in the eyes of the law because the law did not know they existed.

Days, then. Not weeks.

She put her head in her hands, and from the drawer by the stove, Sable slept on, breathing the small even breaths of a person with no idea what her name was worth, or what it was about to cost.
