## 13. Callum's Confession

The Anchor & Chain opened its doors at six in the morning, and Callum Brannock was usually the first one through them, or near enough that it made no difference to anyone counting.

He counted himself among those who made no difference. It was a comfort, some mornings, to be a man the town had already finished with. No expectations left to fail. The constabulary had taken his warrant card eleven years ago and given him nothing in exchange but a reputation as the officer who couldn't be trusted with paper—which was, he'd learned over long practice, a better thing to be than the officer who couldn't be trusted with people. Nobody asked a man like that for favors. Nobody asked him anything at all.

He was on his third pint of small beer and first regret of the day when Odile walked in.

His sister did not come to the Anchor & Chain. Odile had opinions about public houses the way other women had opinions about scripture, and she held them without drinking in any of them. So when she came through the door with the fog still on her shawl and her eyes going around the room like she was taking inventory of every soul in it, Callum knew two things at once: something had gone wrong, and he was somehow part of it.

She found him at the corner table by habit—he always took the corner, back to the wall, an old professional instinct that had outlived the profession—and sat down across from him without asking.

"You're up early," he said.

"I'm always up early. You're just never sober enough before noon to notice." She signaled the barman with two fingers. "Tea."

"God help us all."

"Don't start, Cal."

They let the tea arrive and settle between them. He watched her hands. Odile's hands were never idle—she was worrying the seam of her glove now, which meant there was something under the worry, something with weight to it.

"You've been in my trunk," he said.

Her fingers went still. That was how he knew he'd guessed right; Odile lied beautifully with her mouth and never at all with her body. Eleven years of reading witnesses had taught him that much, and eleven years of being read by nobody had taught her to forget that he could still do it.

"The grey uniform," he said. "Second hook, behind the winter coat. I noticed last month. I told myself I'd imagined it, because the alternative was that my sister had taken my disgrace out of storage, and what would she want with that?"

"Callum—"

"No." He put his hand flat on the table. It wasn't steady, and he hated that it wasn't steady, and he kept it flat anyway. "You don't get to sit there with your tea and your arithmetic face and pretend you borrowed a shirt. That uniform is not a shirt. A person wearing that uniform can walk through the Tollgate. Can walk into the counting house of Harrow & Vane and have the door *held open* for them. Do you understand what you've put out into the world? Because I do. I wore it for nine years, and I know exactly what it opens, and I know exactly who notices when it opens where it shouldn't."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she took off her gloves, folded them, and set them beside the cup with the precision of a woman laying out instruments.

"There's a child," she said.

He waited.

"A baby, left at the company door in February. Vane wanted it sent to the workhouse by morning. There's a young person named Ansel Kavanagh who took the child instead, and stole a page from Vane's own ledger proving whose child she is—the heiress's. The one who drowned." She said it evenly, the way she reported weather. "Vane's men are hunting the page and everyone attached to it. And the only corroborating records are sitting in the low archive under Icehouse Bridge, which floods when the ice goes, which will be soon. Days."

Callum drank. Small beer, but he drank it slowly, because his mouth needed something to do while the rest of him rearranged itself.

"The Merrow girl," he said. "Lavinia Merrow."

"You remember her."

"Everybody remembers her. Pretty heiress, dead at twenty-six, drowned in her own boathouse slip with a full flask of laudanum in her blood and no reason in God's green world to take it. Verdict: misadventure. Signed off by—" and here his own voice turned strange in his ears, "—by Constable C. Brannock, attending officer."

The room noise seemed to come back all at once, the clink and murmur of the morning trade, men who had known him for years and thought they knew his whole story. *Couldn't be trusted with paper.* If they only knew.

"That's not why they dismissed me," he said quietly. "Whatever version you've carried all these years, Odie. It wasn't drink. Drink was the excuse they used because it was tidy and it stuck. I was dismissed for refusing to countersign a second report."

Odile didn't move. "A second report?"

"After the first one was filed. Two days later, a senior man—Rourke, you'd remember him if you hadn't been nineteen and busy—came to my lodgings with a revised copy. Same incident, different wording. In the revision, Miss Merrow had been seen earlier that evening in the company of a man described in detail. A man with a grievance against her family. The revision wanted me to confirm I'd taken statements supporting it." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table, another quarter. "There were no such statements. I never took any. The description in the revision was of a dockhand who'd quarreled with old Merrow over wages—a real man, a real quarrel, and a man with an alibi I happened to know because I'd been drinking three feet from him at the time. But the report didn't want an alibi. It wanted a murderer-shaped hole, and it wanted my signature at the bottom of it."

"And you refused."

"I refused. Rourke smiled at me like I was a slow child and said, 'Brannock, reports don't write themselves, but they can certainly write themselves without you.' Three weeks later I was up before the review board for irregularities in my patrol ledger—entries I swear to this day I never made—and that was that. Dismissed for falsification." He laughed, and it came out with edges. "For falsification. The one charge in the whole rotten business that happened to be true, only the falsifying was done *to* me."

"Who ordered the revision?" Odile asked. "Rourke doesn't move without someone behind him."

Callum looked at his sister. Eleven years he'd kept this locked, partly out of shame, mostly out of a very clear-eyed understanding of what happened to men who talked. Rourke had retired comfortable. The dockhand with the alibi had drowned himself in the cut within the year—another verdict of misadventure, though nobody had bothered to make Callum sign that one.

"Harrow," he said. "Not Vane. Old Harrow himself, or so Rourke let slip once when he was deep in the cups and feeling grand. Vane was just the pen then. The company stood to inherit the Merrow shipping interests if Lavinia died unmarried and intestate, and there was a clause—there's always a clause—about next of kin. Except." He stopped.

"Except what?"

"Except I heard, years after, that there *was* kin. A child. Born early, hidden away, hushed up—the Merrows were proud people, and a bastard granddaughter would have complicated the inheritance worse than a murder would." He rubbed his eyes. "I never confirmed it. It was tavern talk. But I remembered it, all these years, the way you remember a door you should have opened and didn't."

"That's the child," Odile said simply. "That's Sable. Ansel has the page. Vane's own ledger records the parentage—and a second entry authorizing, and I quote, *disposition*."

Callum set down the cup very carefully, because his hand wanted to do something else with it.

"*Disposition.*"

"Yes."

"They're going to kill a baby over a shipping clause."

"They're going to try. Ansel is going to try harder. But testimony—" Odile leaned forward, and now he saw it, the thing under the worry, the actual shape of why she was here. "Cal, a stolen ledger page is evidence. A magistrate can call it forged. But a sworn statement from a former constable, on record, saying he was ordered to falsify the drowning report of the child's grandmother—that's corroboration. That turns a piece of paper into a case. You don't even need to testify publicly. A deposition, witnessed, sealed. Enough to hold Vane until the registry comes up out of the archive."

"And when Vane learns a disgraced constable has put his name to it?" Callum spread his hands. "I have no warrant, no standing, no protection. I'm a drunk in a corner of the Anchor & Chain. He wouldn't even need to dirty his hands. One word to the right people and I'm charged with the falsifications I was sacked for—or worse, I'm found floating somewhere with a flask of laudanum in my blood, and some poor attending officer signs *misadventure*, and we've all come round the circle again."

"Yes," Odile said. "That's all true."

He stared at her. "That's your argument? You come all this way to ask me to hang myself and your argument is *yes, that's all true*?"

"My argument is that you've spent eleven years drinking yourself toward a confession you've never made to anyone. Not to me. Not to God, if the state of your church attendance means anything. You keep the uniform, Cal. A man who wanted to be finished keeps the coat? You polish the buttons—I've seen the buttons, don't look surprised, I mend constabulary wool, I know brass when I see it maintained." Her voice dropped. "You've been waiting eleven years for someone to come and tell you the door was still open. Well. It's open. It leads somewhere terrible. Come through it anyway."

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the wharf road, because he couldn't bear to sit still and the Anchor & Chain had begun to feel like a coffin fitted to his exact measurements.

The river was doing its late-winter work. The ice had gone grey and rotten at the edges, and great plates of it ground against the pilings with a sound like slow applause. Men on the Company Wharf were double-checking lines, the way they did every year before the break, when the current woke up and started collecting whatever wasn't secured. Callum had pulled two bodies out of that water in his working years. Both times he'd thought: *the river gives back what it takes, eventually, but never in the condition you hoped.*

He let himself into his rooms above the chandler's shop. They were exactly as depressing as he'd left them, which was the point of them—one room, one bed, one trunk, one chair with a broken slat he'd never fixed because fixing things implied a future worth fixing them for.

The trunk sat under the window.

He knelt and opened it. His kit was inside, folded the way Odile must have refolded it after borrowing—neater than he ever kept it, her laundry-woman's hands all over it, and he felt something twist in his chest that wasn't quite anger. The tunic. The belt with the brass buckle gone soft with polish. The warrant card slot, empty these eleven years, empty tonight.

And pinned inside the collar, where he'd sewn it himself and forgotten he'd sewn it, a small square of cloth with a number stamped in fading ink. His number. 4-1-9. He'd been proud of it once. He'd been twenty-two and bulletproof and certain that the badge meant what the oath said it meant.

He sat on the floor with the tunic across his knees for a long time.

Here was the arithmetic, and Odile would appreciate the arithmetic: refuse, and the child died, and the ledger burned, and Rourke died comfortable in his bed the way comfortable men die, and Callum went on drinking in his corner until the corner closed over him. Agree, and maybe the child lived—but Vane's quiet men were already asking questions in the riverside districts, Odile had said, already searching bunks, and a name put to paper was a name Vane could find.

There was a third option, the one he'd been circling since the Anchor & Chain. Not refuse, not agree—confess. All of it. Not a deposition extracted for utility, not a single corroborating statement deployed like a tool. The whole account. The revision, the order, the alibi, the dockhand who'd drowned himself eight months later with Callum's silence in his pockets. Put it all on the record and let the record do what records do.

It would cost him everything that remained, which was approximately nothing, which was oddly freeing. It might also cost Odile, and Ansel, and a baby who had committed no crime except existing expensively.

Or it might save all three.

He was still holding the tunic when he heard boots on the stair—two sets, unhurried, the tread of men who had no need to hurry—and the knock that followed was the knock of the constabulary, the formal triple rap, except Callum hadn't been constabulary for eleven years and the men outside knew it perfectly well.

"Mr. Brannock," said a voice through the door, pleasant as a clerk's. "Company matter. Might we have a word about your sister?"

Callum looked down at the tunic in his lap, at the number 419, at the collar where a stolen page could be sewn as easily as a stamped square of cloth.

Then he laid it aside, folded, buttoned, ready—and went to answer the door.
