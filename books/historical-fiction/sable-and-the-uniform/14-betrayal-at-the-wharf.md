## 14. Betrayal at the Wharf

The morning of the second run, Odile braided the ledger page into my coat seam with a sailmaker's needle and hands that did not shake.

"Oilskin first," she said. "Then the lining. Then the thread pulled so tight even God would have to look twice." She bit the thread off and pressed her thumb flat against my shoulder blade, as if sealing something in. "You'll feel it there all day. Good. Let it remind you what you're carrying."

I could feel it. I had felt it since the Tollgate—since Sergeant Merrick's eyes had crawled over me like flies over meat and Callum had stepped in with his bored recitation of procedure, *grain manifest, bonded carrier, wave him through, man, some of us have suppers waiting.* The page against my back had felt less like paper than like a coal.

Now we were three days from Checkpoint Two: the Company Wharf inspection. Our own ground. Harrow & Vane's own stones, their own clerks, their own tally-men who knew every face that belonged on that wharf and every face that didn't.

That was the problem I should have seen. It was our ground—and Callum Brannock knew exactly how we meant to cross it.

* * *

He'd been sober for nine days, which should have told me something. A man like Callum is never more dangerous than when he's trying to be worth something again.

He came to Odile's kitchen the night before the run, smelling of soap instead of gin, hair combed, wearing the expression of a man rehearsing good news. He set a wrapped parcel on the table—fresh rolls, still warm, an offering.

"I've had word," he said. "From the magistrate's office. There's to be a review of my dismissal. A hearing. They want the original file."

Odile didn't look up from the mending basket. "That so."

"That so." He sat down heavily, uninvited, the way family does. "Nine years, Odile. Nine years I've been *that one*, the constable who wouldn't sign. Do you know what it would mean? Pension arrears. My name cleared. Standing in front of men who—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "It would mean I was right. That it cost me something and it was *worth* it."

"And the warrant?" Odile asked, needle paused mid-stitch. "For the archive?"

"After the hearing. It follows naturally. One thing earns the next." He looked at her, then at me, and there was something wet and shining in his face that I recognized too late as not hope but need—the kind that makes men pliable. "I'm asking you both to trust me a few more weeks. Just a few. Don't run anything through the wharf yet. If Vane hears the route before my name's clean, the whole thing sours. Wait for me."

The kitchen was quiet. The fire ticked. Sable, asleep in the laundry basket lined with flannel, made her small sound like a kitten swallowing.

It was Odile who said no, and she said it gently, which was worse. "Callum. We can't wait. The archive drains in four days. The ice goes when it goes. We move tomorrow or we don't move at all."

"Then take me with you," he said too fast. "Let me stand at the inspection myself. A constable's uniform, a real face under the hat—"

"You gave up the uniform," Odile said. "We're just borrowing its shape."

Something closed behind his eyes then. A shutter going down over a lit window. I saw it and misread it as wounded pride. I was a fool. I knew hunger when I saw it—I'd been raised on it—and I looked at this man's hungry face and called it something kinder.

He left without touching the rolls.

"He'll come round," Odile said, threading her needle again. But she said it to convince herself, and her stitches that night were the tightest I'd ever seen her pull.

* * *

Checkpoint Two worked like this: grain barges came down from the upper mills to the company elevator, and every bonded carrier crossing the wharf gate passed the tally desk where a clerk checked manifests against faces. Papers opened doors at the Tollgate. At the Company Wharf, papers were nothing—it was *familiarity* that got you through, a face the gate men had seen ten thousand times hauling, sweating, swearing. So we went as cargo ourselves. I'd ride the barge in as a hired deckhand, one of forty anonymous backs, walk off through the gate in the press of the shift change, and be gone into Sablemouth before the count came up short.

Simple. Simple things are what betray you, because simple things depend on people.

The morning was grey and hard, the river running high and green-black under rotting ice. I stood at the rail of the *Marguerite B* among real deckhands, my hands rope-burned and honest, the ledger page sewn between my shoulders like a second spine. At the wharf gate I could see the usual tally clerk—and behind him, two men I didn't know, standing with the loose-limbed patience of dogs told to wait.

Vane's men. I knew the breed. I'd grown up alongside it.

My mouth went dry. Options ran through me like rats. Turn back—no, deckhands didn't turn back, turning back was a signal. Go forward and trust the anonymity of forty men. Trust that whatever those two were watching for, they weren't watching for me.

Then the tally clerk lifted his head, found me in the crowd of disembarking men—and nodded. Not the bored nod of routine. A nod with direction in it. A nod toward the side door of the weighhouse, where a third man stood holding it open.

They had a description. Not a face—they couldn't have my face, nobody had my face, that had been my whole life's craft—but a shape. A height. A way of walking. Someone had described me to them.

Someone who'd seen me walk out of Odile's kitchen.

The side door stood open like a held breath. Beyond it, shadow. Beyond the shadow, questions I would not survive. And behind me, forty deckhands pressing up the gangway, the gate funneling us all forward, no room to hesitate because hesitation was its own confession.

I did the arithmetic of the body, which is faster than the arithmetic of the mind. Gate ahead: men waiting. Side door: men waiting. River: cold enough to stop a heart in ten minutes.

The river.

There is a moment before every terrible decision when the world becomes very clear and very quiet. The gulls went silent. The ice groaned somewhere out mid-channel, a sound like a giant shifting in sleep. I thought—absurdly, in the last calm half-second—of my mother's hands, blue-veined and strong, teaching me to breathe on the pearl beds. *In through the nose, little fish. Slow. The water isn't your enemy. Panic is.*

I went over the rail on the river side, away from the wharf, in the space between one deckhand's shoulders and the next, and dropped thirty feet into February water.

* * *

Cold is not a sensation. It is a fact that replaces every other fact.

The river took me like a hand closing. My chest seized shut—a fist inside my ribs squeezing everything out—and for three full seconds I could not have drawn breath if air had been offered. Below the surface the world turned the color of bruise, green-black light falling in slanted columns, ice fragments overhead like broken crockery.

*In through the nose. Slow.*

I couldn't. My body wanted to gasp, to thrash, to sprint for air it couldn't reach. Panic rose in me like floodwater. And underneath the panic, older than it, my mother's rhythm waited—the pearl-diver's count, the one she'd drummed into me before I could talk, the inheritance no ledger ever recorded. *Four counts down. Hold. Four counts up.* I forced my lungs into the pattern. In through the nose, slow, against the scream of instinct. Hold. The fist around my chest loosened by degrees.

I swam with the current, not against it, letting the river carry me downstream beneath the wharf pilings, under the hulls of moored barges whose keels passed over me like the bellies of whales. The oilskin bundle rode sewn against my spine, and I thought of it with a strange tenderness—a dead woman's secret riding a living diver's back, both of us holding our breath together.

Above me, through the murk, I heard the wharf erupting. Shouting. Boots on planking. A lantern beam swinging down through the water in long smearing fingers.

"He went off the boat!" someone bawled. "Off the *rail*, I seen him—"

"Get lines! Get the grapnels!" That voice I knew—Rennick, Vane's senior man, the one with the patient hands. "He's carrying something. Whatever he was carrying, it's in the water or it's on him. Drag the channel!"

*On him,* I thought. They'd assume the current took any loose goods south in seconds. But they'd drag for a body, because bodies float eventually, and Rennick was thorough.

So I did not float.

I let myself sink. Down past the piling line, into the deeper channel where the cold went from pain to something beyond pain, a white silence. My mother had worked water colder than this off the northern beds, coming up with pearls and frost in her lashes, laughing. I had her blood's stubbornness if not her trade. I found a piling furred with weed, hooked my arm through a mooring ring bolted low on the timber, and hung there in the dark like something planted.

Minutes passed. I counted them in breaths. Four counts down, hold, four counts up—except there was no up. I learned to raise my face into the trapped air pockets along the piling where ice shelves met wood, sip a lungful, sink again. Once a grapnel dragged past close enough to comb the weeds beside my boot. Once a boat hull slid directly overhead and I watched the tarred planks pass a hand's breadth above my face, close enough to touch, and touched nothing.

The cold stopped hurting and started thinking for me. Thoughts arrived slowly, one at a time, like workmen crossing ice. *Ansel Kavanagh.* The name surfaced and I held it the way I held the ring—because letting go meant going wherever the river decided. *You have a name now. You chose it. You signed it in your head before any court asked. Don't lose it here.*

Somewhere above, the shouting thinned. The dragging boats moved downchannel, chasing the logic of floating things. Rennick's voice, fading: "He's gone to the bottom, then. Nothing stays in this river. Nothing comes back."

Nothing comes back. I hung in the dark and almost laughed, except laughing underwater is how divers die.

When the light above went from iron-grey to the first hint of afternoon, I hauled myself along the piling hand over hand, arm by screaming arm, to the old ferry slip a quarter mile down—an abandoned rotted thing the company had written off years ago, because the company wrote off everything it couldn't profit from. I dragged myself up onto ice-slick boards, and the air hit my lungs like a knife being swallowed, and I lay there shaking so hard the slip timbers chattered with me.

Alive. The word had no meaning until then. Alive, and robbed of everything else. The run was blown. The route was blown. Callum—

Callum.

The realization arrived without surprise, which is the cruelest way betrayal lands. Those two men at the gate hadn't been guessing. They'd known the barge, the day, the disguise-as-deckhand. Only three people knew. Odile would sooner sew her own lips shut. Which left a man with a parcel of warm rolls and a hunger for a hearing, a man who'd asked us to *wait*, a man who'd needed us to delay just long enough for him to make his arrangements—and when we wouldn't, he'd sold the route to buy his reinstatement with the only coin he had left.

I lay on the ruined slip with the river draining out of my clothes, and I grieved him in advance, because I understood him completely. That was the worst of it. Nine years erased will make a man sell anything. I'd spent my whole life being erased for free.

And then my hand went to my back—to check, the way you check a wound—and found the oilskin bundle still sewn tight between my shoulder blades, swollen with river water but sealed, intact, the ledger page dry inside its folds.

They thought it was at the bottom of the Sable. They thought Ansel Kavanagh was at the bottom of the Sable. Rennick himself had pronounced it: nothing comes back.

But the ice was breaking early this year. The archive was already draining. And somewhere across the city a laundress was about to learn that her brother had put a price on us—and a drowned man was about to walk out of this river and tell her.

I got my feet under me, and started up the bank.
