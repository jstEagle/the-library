## 16. The Workhouse Ledger

Odile Brannock had spent thirty years reading men by their laundry, and Callum Brannock came back to her kitchen looking like a shirt boiled too long—shrunk in all the places that mattered, the starch gone out of him.

He sat at her table with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, and he wouldn't look at her. That was answer enough.

"You told them," she said.

"I told them about the Tollgate run." His voice was flat as river ice in January. "Not the route. Not the girl. Just enough to make them think I was useful. I needed them to believe I could still deliver something."

"And did they pay you?"

"They promised me a hearing with the magistrate. Reinstatement review." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound, like a hinge rusting shut. "A hearing. Eleven years I've been dead on paper, Odile, and they offered me a *hearing*."

She took the cup from his hands before he cracked it and set it down hard enough to slop. Outside the window, Sablemouth smoked under a sky the color of wet wool. Three days since Ansel had come up out of the river half-drowned, blue-lipped, clutching an oilskin packet to her chest like a second heart. Three days since the wharf trap. Three days of Vane's quiet men walking the streets with their collars up, asking after a laundress and a girl who dressed as a constable.

The clock was no longer ticking. It was falling.

"Sit there," Odile said. "Don't touch anything. Don't speak to anyone. If Vane's men knock, you're my brother and you're drunk and you know nothing, which will require no acting whatsoever."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

Ansel was upstairs in the attic room, sitting on the floor with Sable asleep across her knees, and she looked up when Odile came in with the look of someone who has already counted the exits.

"The page is ruined," Ansel said. "Riverwater got into one corner. The ink ran where it says 'disposition.' You can still read the parentage line, but—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "They'll say I forged it. They'll say the water damage is convenient. A clerk at the magistrate's office can destroy this in ten minutes if he wants to."

"He'll want to. Vane pays the magistrate's club dues."

"Then it's over."

"It isn't over." Odile lowered herself onto the crate that served as a chair, knees cracking like kindling. She had rehearsed this speech on the walk home and abandoned every version of it, because the truth didn't need dressing. "It's only over if we play their game. Papers, checkpoints, signatures—all of it belongs to them. Every road we try to walk downriver is a road they built and toll. So we stop walking their roads."

Ansel shifted the baby higher on her chest. "What road is left?"

"The one they flooded." Odile let that sit a moment. "The low archive under the old workhouse. Before Harrow & Vane bought the block and raised their new counting house, the parish kept its registry down there—births, indentures, deaths, the whole wet memory of this town. When they raised the levee and let the river cellars fill, they didn't burn the records. Burning takes witnesses. They just drowned them. Left them sealed behind two feet of ice and water and told everyone the archive was lost."

"You've said so before. It doesn't matter. Even if the heiress's registry entry is down there, nobody can get to it until spring thaw—and by then the water's been through everything twice. Paper doesn't survive that."

"No," Odile said. "But it survives three more days. And so do we, if we're quick and we're mad."

Ansel went very still. Sable stirred and settled. Somewhere below them, Callum's chair scraped against the floorboards, once, and went quiet—he was listening, of course he was listening.

"The ice breaks when it breaks," Odile said. "Every year it goes early or late, but this year the thaw came up the valley fast. The millers are already pulling their wheel gates. We have days. Maybe less. The archive sits under four feet of standing water behind a door that hasn't opened in nine years. But the water is still, Ansel. Still and cold and dark. And I know someone who can hold her breath longer than any diver should."

"I nearly died yesterday."

"Yes."

"In water warmer than that cellar will be."

"Yes."

Ansel looked down at the child on her lap, and Odile watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing for weeks now—the same sum, always the same sum: her life against this small weight, her name against this small face. Odile had watched people make that calculation her whole working life and she knew most of them chose themselves, and forgave themselves for it, and slept fine. She was waiting to see whether this one would be different.

"We'd need light," Ansel said finally. "And air. And a way through the door."

Odile smiled without any warmth in it. "We need a warrant."

* * *

Magistrate Aldous Pettibrew was a widower of sixty-one who took his breakfast at the Commercial Hotel every morning at eight, his boots cleaned overnight by the hotel's laundry, and who believed—correctly—that no one knew what he did on Thursday evenings.

Odile knew. Odile mended for the hotel, and the hotel's linen came through her hands, and a woman who has washed other people's collars for thirty years develops an eye for what collars conceal. Pettibrew's Thursday shirts came back to her with a particular perfume that had nothing to do with the widow Hessel, whose boarding rooms stood above the chandler's, and everything to do with a woman named Mrs. Quill who kept accounts for three businesses on Rope Walk, only one of which sold rope.

She had known for two years. She had done nothing with it, because knowledge was capital and capital was not to be spent lightly.

Thursday evening, she spent it.

Pettibrew received her in his chambers off the courthouse square, a room of green leather and ledgers, and he poured sherry with the steadiness of a man who did not yet understand why his stomach had dropped when the housekeeper announced her. He sat. He waited. He was, she granted him, not a stupid man.

"Mistress Brannock," he said. "This is irregular."

"So are Thursday evenings, sir."

The silence stretched. He reached for the decanter again and she watched him decide against it, and that was the moment she stopped being polite.

"I don't want money," she said. "I want paper. A search-and-recovery warrant, properly drawn, authorizing entry to the flooded parish archive beneath the old workhouse block, for purposes of recovering records threatened by the spring flood. Signed by you, sealed by your office, dated tomorrow."

His face went through several colors in quick succession, ending somewhere around old candle wax. "That archive is closed. Condemned. There's nothing down there but ruin."

"Then a warrant to recover records from ruin threatens nothing, does it? Costs you nothing. Except your signature, which you'll find costs rather a lot tonight."

"Brannock." He set down the glass with exaggerated care. "Whatever you imagine you know—"

"Mrs. Quill," Odile said. "Rope Walk. Thursdays. Two years, near as I can count, and the last Thursday of every month she books a room at the Fathom Inn under her sister's name, and your carriage waits in the chandler's yard with the lamps doused because you think doused lamps make a carriage invisible instead of merely suspicious." She folded her hands. "I wash the Fathom Inn's sheets too, Magistrate. I know what Thursday smells like."

The clock on his mantel ticked. Down in the square, a cart rattled past, and neither of them turned toward the sound.

"If this comes out," Pettibrew said quietly, "I am finished. Ruined. My office, my pension, my daughter's marriage prospects—"

"Aye, and if it doesn't come out, you keep all of it, and all it costs you is one warrant that will be forgotten in a week." She leaned forward. "I'm not blackmailing you toward ruin, sir. I'm blackmailing you toward staying exactly where you are. That's the cheapest bargain anyone will ever offer you. Take it."

He was silent a long time. Then: "Recovery of records requires a licensed agent. A sworn officer to execute the warrant. I can't sign it open-ended."

"You'll sign it for Constable Callum Brannock."

That startled him genuinely. "Your brother? He was dismissed—"

"For refusing to falsify a drowning report. Which you know, because the falsified version crossed your desk, and you countersigned it, which means you also know whose order it was, and if you ever wondered why a man of your caution let that pass—it's because the man who ordered it buys your club dues." She watched him flinch and pressed it home, gently, almost kindly. "Reinstating Callum Brannock to execute one warrant doesn't embarrass you, Magistrate. It makes you look scrupulous. A wronged officer restored to service to save the town's records from the flood—the *Courier* would print it on the front page if they weren't so busy printing Vane's shipping notices."

"You'd make me a hero."

"I'd make you invisible. Same thing, in this town."

He poured himself the sherry after all. Drank it in one motion. Then he pulled a fresh warrant form across the blotter, and his pen scratched in the quiet like a mouse in a wall.

* * *

She laid it on the kitchen table between the bread board and the lamp, and Callum read it three times, his lips moving on the third pass.

"Search-and-recovery," he read aloud. "One authorized execution. Constable C. Brannock, reinstated pro tem." He looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and sober for the first time in months. "You did this. You walked into Pettibrew's office and—"

"Don't ask how. Asking how means knowing, and knowing means you're liable too."

"Odile." He put the warrant down. "Why? After what I did. After I sold the Tollgate route. Why would you—"

"Because you're the only sworn officer in Sablemouth who isn't Vane's, and because you're the only man alive who knows procedure well enough to walk us through that door legally, and because—" She stopped. Took a breath. "Because I'm your sister, and being your sister has cost me eleven years of sleeping with the window shut so I don't hear you singing in the street, and I am tired of paying for your disgrace with my nights. This settles us both. Do you understand? You carry this warrant, you stand at that door in a real uniform with real authority, and for one hour you are the constable you were meant to be. After that, what you do with yourself is your own affair."

Callum looked at the warrant a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough.

"Pettibrew signed this knowing what it's for?"

"Pettibrew signed this knowing what happens if he didn't."

"And Vane?"

"Vane thinks the evidence drowned at the wharf. He thinks Ansel crawled out empty-handed, because that's what his men reported—they saw her go under, they saw her come up, they didn't see the oilskin because they were too busy congratulating themselves. For three days he's believed it's over." She tapped the warrant. "In three days the ice breaks and the archive floods for good and even if he suspects, it won't matter. Unless we're slow. Unless we quarrel. Unless one of us gets ambitious or frightened or drunk."

"I haven't touched a drop since I came back."

"I know. I checked your breath when you weren't paying attention."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile used to live, before. "You always did."

"Somebody had to."

* * *

They planned it that night at the kitchen table with the shuttered lamp turned low, and Ansel came down from the attic with Sable wrapped against her shoulder and drew the archive's layout in spilled flour: the sunken stairwell off the old workhouse yard, the iron door at the bottom, the registry vault beyond it where the parish books had been shelved before the company sealed the block.

"The door will be padlocked," Callum said. "Nine years of rust on the lock. Warrant or no warrant, we'll need to cut it, and cutting takes time, and time down there—"

"Time is the whole problem," Ansel said. "Once the door opens, the air in the corridor changes. Warm air moving over that water starts the thaw faster on anything it touches. And the ice on the river above is thinner than it looks—we heard it groaning yesterday. If it shifts while I'm under..."

"Then we don't cut the lock." Callum was staring at the flour map, and Odile saw the change come over him, the thing rising in him that had made him a good constable before it made him a broken one. "Petty's Manual, section twelve—emergency salvage entries. A sworn officer executing a warrant may force entry and *must* file a condition report within the hour. The lock doesn't need to survive. Only the paperwork does. I bring bolt cutters, I note the time of forced entry, and while I'm writing it, Ansel is already in the water."

"You'd falsify the timing?" Odile asked.

"I'd *round* the timing," Callum said, with the first real steel she'd heard in his voice in eleven years. "There's a difference. I learned it the expensive way."

Ansel traced the vault in the flour with one finger. "The registry books were shelved on stone plinths—raised off the floor. If any of them survived, it's the ones on the top shelf. Births and baptisms, 1870s. The heiress was born in '78. Her entry will list both parents. Full names. Witnessed."

"And Vane's ledger gives the child's parentage," Odile said. "The parish book confirms it independently. Two documents, two sources, one story. No clerk can call that forgery."

"No clerk can," Ansel said quietly. "But Vane can call it theft, and he has men, and we have a hearse wagon and a drunk—"

"*Sober*—"

"—and a warrant that expires the moment someone senior to Callum says otherwise." Ansel shifted Sable to her other shoulder. The child slept on, utterly trusting, and Odile thought: there it is, that's the whole war in one image. "When do we go?"

Odile had been down to the river that morning. She had walked out on the Icehouse Bridge in her plainest coat and listened, the way her mother had taught her to listen to water off the Kerry coast, and what the river had said underneath her feet was not *soon*. It was *now*.

"Tomorrow night," she said. "High water slack, just past two. The cold is worst then, but the ice is stillest. Callum wears the uniform—his own this time, with his own warrant. Ansel goes in the water. I drive the wagon and watch the yard." She looked around the table at the two of them: her brother with his ruined face and his new spine, and the diver's child with the diver's lungs holding a sleeping infant that had no idea what it was costing anyone. "If any part of this goes wrong, we scatter and we never meet in daylight again. All of you swear it now."

They swore it. Callum solemnly. Ansel with a glance at the baby and then a nod that was more binding than any oath.

"One more thing," Odile said, and blew out the lamp, so that only the banked fire lit them. "Tomorrow, when you're under that water, Ansel—remember what your mother taught you. Not the breathing. The rest of it. The part where the water isn't your enemy unless you make it one."

In the dark, Ansel's voice came back steady.

"She taught me the river keeps what's honest. Tomorrow we'll see if she was right."

Above the town, far up the valley, the ice on the river began, very softly, to sing.
