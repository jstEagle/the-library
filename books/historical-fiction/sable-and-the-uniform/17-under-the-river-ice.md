## 17. Under the River Ice

The ice on Sablemouth's lower basin was the color of old teeth, and it sang.

I had heard it all my life from the wharf — that low groaning, the high whistling crack that ran out across the flats like something walking. Dockmen said the river talked in February. My mother said it was only the water stretching, getting ready to move. She taught me to listen to it the way other children learned to listen for their names.

Now I stood at the edge of a hole Callum's axe had cut, and the river was talking directly to me.

"You don't have to do this," Odile said. She held the lantern low so its light wouldn't carry past the broken warehouse wall. "We can find another way."

"There is no other way." I said it without heat. It was simply true, the way tide tables are true. The archive beneath the old customs annex had been flooded when they dynamited the sluice gates three winters back — a cheap way to demolish what the company no longer wanted inspected. The registry of indentures, births, and deaths for the whole lower ward sat under eleven feet of black water, and by tomorrow night the spring breakup would take the roof down with it. Vane had moved the drainage crew upriver. He wanted the records drowned and forgotten, the same as everything else.

The same as me.

"Ansel." Odile caught my sleeve. Her hands were strong from thirty years of laundry boilers; they could wring the fear out of most men. They didn't manage it tonight. "If you go under and come up wrong, I can't follow you. Neither can he." She jerked her chin at Callum, who stood apart with his warrant folded in his pocket like a thing he wasn't sure was real yet.

"Then hold the rope," I said, "and count."

* * *

They stripped me to my shirt and trousers because wool drags a body down like grief. The oilskin packet went against my chest, strapped flat, empty now except for air — room for the registry pages I meant to bring home. Odile had waxed the seams twice. Water could have the rest of me. It couldn't have the paper.

Callum crouched at the hole's edge and tested the ice around it with his boot heel. It held. He looked older than his years in the lantern light, the disgrace worn off him these last days like tarnish rubbed away, leaving something plainer underneath.

"The annex door faces east," he said. "Iron frame, half-rotted at the sill. You'll fit through if you go shoulders-first and don't fight it. Inside, the registry shelves run along the north wall — third case from the door, marked in brass. Lower ward, births and deaths." He paused. "I filed papers in that room for six years, Kavanagh. I know where everything is. That knowledge cost me my coat. Use it."

"Why did they flood it?" I asked him, though I knew.

"Because a flooded room needs no explanation," he said. "A burned one gets investigated. Water is patient. Water doesn't leave ash." His jaw worked. "It was the same week they asked me to sign the drowning report. They were clearing house. Everything that remembered her, going into the dark."

The river breathed under the ice. A long, cold exhalation through the hole, smelling of silt and iron and things given up long ago.

My mother used to say the river keeps what it takes, but not kindly. It keeps things the way a miser keeps coins — buried, uncounted, resentful. She said a diver's job was not to fight the keeping but to borrow from it politely. Go down humble. Come up grateful. Never grab.

I knelt at the edge of the hole and put my hands in the water.

Cold went through them like a sermon. Not pain exactly — pain comes and goes — this was more like being told a fact. *You are small,* the river said. *You are temporary.* Every winter of my childhood lived in that sentence. Every year I'd been made to feel unremarkable, unlisted, unnamed. The water had been saying it to me since before Vane ever bought my labor at six years old.

But kneeling there, I found the sentence had changed.

*You are small,* the river said. And for the first time in my life I answered: *Yes. Small enough to fit through the door.*

* * *

I went in the way she taught me, feet first at first, letting the cold close over me by degrees — ankles, knees, hips — because a body shocked all at once spends its breath screaming inside itself. Then, when the water reached my ribs, I tipped forward and slid under like a coin off a table's edge.

Blackness. Total, absolute, and then not total: the lantern light above the hole made a dim gold ceiling far overhead, wavering, already far away. The rope ran taut along my side, Odile's hands somewhere at the end of it, counting. I counted too. Mother's rhythm. Four counts down, four counts held, eight let out slow through the nose. The breath of the pearl beds. The breath she died doing.

The cold clamped my chest and I let it. Fighting it burns air. This was the first lesson of diving and the last lesson of my whole life in Sablemouth: don't fight the thing holding you. Move inside it.

My boots found the annex roof. Tiles, slick with a decade of silt, sloping toward a gap where the ridge had fallen in. I moved along it hand over hand in the blind dark, reading the building through my palms the way I'd once read riverbed gravel for the hard knock of shell. Somewhere below my feet lay the registry. Somewhere below that, the mud. Somewhere behind me, upriver, a director sat warm by a fire believing the past was safely underwater.

He was almost right. Almost was the whole width of the world.

The eastern wall came against my reaching hand sooner than expected — Callum's geography was true — and I traced down it until my fingers crossed the doorframe. Iron, furred with rust. The sill had rotted exactly as he'd promised; there was a gap at the bottom wide enough for a body willing to be humble. I turned sideways, hooked my elbows over the threshold, and poured myself through shoulders-first, the way a diver enters a crevice after a pearl that has no business being reachable.

For one bad moment I stuck. Hips jammed, arms pinned, head hanging into the flooded room with nothing beneath me but black water and the rope dragging wrong against my hip. Panic rose in me like a bubble, huge and bright and stupid.

Four counts. Hold. Eight counts, slow.

*I am small. Small enough.*

I exhaled deliberately — spent precious air doing it, spent it like money — and my ribs folded, and I dropped through the door into the dark of the drowned archive, and the river closed over me like a ledger closing over a finished line.

* * *

Inside, the water was different. Stiller. Colder in a way that felt deliberate, preserved, like the company had gotten its wish and pickled the past whole.

I hung there a moment, weightless in the absolute dark, and let the strangeness settle. Below me, faintly, my boots touched shelving. I'd come down between the cases. My fingers found wood — shelf edges, swollen soft with rot — and paper. Paper everywhere. It moved in the water like weed, sheets lifting and settling with currents I couldn't feel, ten years of drowned record drifting in slow procession around me.

I thought of my own name, struck from a shipping manifest when Harrow & Vane took me. *Indenture No. 4117, Kavanagh, Ansel, age 6, mother deceased, father unrecorded.* Somewhere in this black water was the page that proved I had been a person before the company made me cargo. I hadn't come for mine. I'd told myself I hadn't come for mine.

Third case from the door. North wall. Brass marker.

I swam blind, trailing the wall with one hand, counting cases by the knock of my knuckles on their frames. One. Two. The third case's brass plate met my fingertips raised in relief, letters I couldn't read but recognized by size and spacing — Callum had described them, and memory made the description true under my hand. *LOWER WARD. REGISTRY OF BIRTHS AND DEATHS.*

I pulled the oilskin packet around front and got the flap open with numb fingers. Then I began taking pages.

Not grabbing. Taking. Politely, as she'd have said. Each sheet I freed from its rotted binding I smoothed against the shelf, folded once, and slid into the oilskin, feeling by touch for the heavy rag paper of official entries, the ribbon-impressed seals, the dense columns of a registrar's hand. I couldn't read a word down here. There was no light and no time. I was stealing the past by weight and texture, trusting Callum's geography and my own dumb luck, and every second the cold took another degree of feeling from my hands.

Births. Deaths. The heiress's death certificate would sit in this very case — the false one, the drowning report Callum refused to sign, and beside or behind it perhaps the true entry, the birth record of a daughter everyone was ordered to forget. *Forget this face.* The note at the company door had been written by someone who knew this room existed and what it held, and had chosen the river over the shredder because the river asks fewer questions.

The building groaned.

It was a deep sound, structural, felt in the chest more than heard — the annex roof shifting under the ice above, or the ice shifting over the roof, or the river deciding it had been patient long enough. A thread of silt bloomed suddenly through the water around me, fine as smoke, and the drifting sheets all lifted at once and hung trembling.

Fear went through me, clean and total, and I noticed something I had never known about myself before:

I wasn't afraid of dying.

I was afraid of failing. Of coming up with empty hands. Of the child upstairs in Odile's back room waking tomorrow to a workhouse cart because Ansel Kavanagh had grabbed instead of borrowed, rushed instead of waited, spent breath on panic. Fear had been the shape of my whole life — fear of notice, fear of the water, fear of the men in good coats — and kneeling in that drowned room, lungs burning, hands dead, building groaning, I understood that the fear had never once protected me. It had only kept me small in the way the company liked its property small.

So I set it down.

Not bravely. Not cleanly. I set it down the way you set down anything too heavy to carry further — awkwardly, gratefully, with no dignity at all — and with both hands free I went down the case to the bottom shelf, where the oldest bindings were, where a birth record from the right year would live, and I took what was there.

The roof groaned again, longer, ending in a sound like a great door unlatching.

Time to be grateful and go.

* * *

Getting out was harder than getting in, because the way out was up and my body had begun giving opinions. My limbs were slow, wooden, badly steered. The doorway was a gray suggestion above me — the lantern light had strengthened, or the roof had opened, I didn't stop to learn which. I kicked for it and my boot caught the doorframe and I hung there a horrible half-second, snagged again, the river pressing me back into the room like a creditor.

Four counts. Hold. Eight.

*I am small.*

I turned sideways, exhaled, folded, slipped through — and the annex chose that exact moment to die. The groan became a roar transmitted through water rather than air, and the whole structure shuddered, and tiles rained past me in the dark, and the current that hit me then was not preservation anymore. It was the river collecting.

I went with it. Up, at an angle, hands over head, following the gold smudge of light the way a diver follows the bright patch that means open sky. The rope burned through my grip when I caught it — Odile pulling, Odile hauling, Odile counting aloud somewhere in a world that had air in it — and the ice ceiling rushed down at me and broke around me and then there was wind, actual wind, actual stars, and I came up through the hole coughing the river out of myself while two people dragged me across the ice like landed fish.

I lay on my back on the frozen basin, steaming, gasping, alive, and laughed — a cracked, ugly sound I didn't recognize as mine.

"Let me see it." Odile's hands were already at the straps, at the oilskin. "Tell me you've got it."

The packet came free of my chest. She got the flap open with her laundress's fingers, quick and sure, and held the lantern close, and we both bent over the folded pages dripping on the ice.

Rag paper. Registrar's hand. Ribbon seal, water-marked but legible. And at the top of the topmost page, in faded iron-gall ink, a heading Callum read aloud over our shoulders, his voice going strange halfway through:

"*Registry of Births — Lower Ward.*" A pause. "Kavanagh. There are names here. Dozens of them. Indentured children — recorded, dated, parented." His finger stopped. "There's yours. Page forty-one. *Ansel Kavanagh, born the ninth of March, daughter of Maren Kavanagh, pearl-diver, of the Sablemouth beds—*'"

"Later." My teeth were chattering too hard for ceremony, but my hands had gone still. "The heiress first. Find the birth. Find *her* daughter's birth."

Odile turned the pages with the care of a woman handling altar linen, and the lantern flame leaned in the wind, and the river beneath us boomed once, long and low, like a witness clearing its throat.

Then Odile went rigid.

"Ansel," she said quietly. "This entry — it's witnessed. Signed by the attending midwife and countersigned—" She looked up at me, and the light caught her eyes, and what I saw in them frightened me more than the dive had. "Countersigned by *Elias Vane*. Dated nine days before the drowning report. He signed her existence into the record himself, and then he spent ten years pretending she was never born."

Far upriver, faint but unmistakable, came the crack of ice giving way — early, days ahead of any forecast, the breakup beginning its long march toward us with the evidence finally safe and the man who wanted it destroyed about to learn how badly he had failed.

"We have until dawn," Callum said, looking north. "Maybe less. The bridge checkpoint will mobilize when the patrols see the ice moving." He turned to me, and for the first time since I'd known him, the disgraced constable looked like a constable. "Get dry, Kavanagh. Get the uniform. We're making the crossing tonight."

I looked down at the page in Odile's hands — at Vane's own signature, his own hand betraying him across ten drowned years — and felt the last of my fear go out of me like breath through the nose, slow and even and done.

The river keeps what it takes.

Tonight, I had taken some of it back.
