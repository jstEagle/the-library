## 1. The Weight of Glass

August Crane had a rule about the lens: you never touched it with your bare hands.

The oil of a fingerprint, he told Tilly every autumn when she forgot and pressed her nose to the glass, was enough to burn a weak spot into the crystal when the lamp got hot. A weak spot meant a dark seam on the horizon, and a dark seam meant a ship that couldn't see the light until the light was too late to matter. So they polished with lambskin cloths, both hands, small circles, and they did it every day whether the glass needed it or not, because the discipline mattered more than the shine.

He was doing his circles now, at the top of the tower, while below him the household made its noise. He could hear everything through ninety feet of spiral stair if he listened: Bram's boots on the kitchen flags, heavy as moorings; Nell's pen scratching in the ledger she kept for them — not the town ledger, their own, a cheap thing from the stationer's with a marbled cover gone soft at the corners; Tilly singing something tuneless over the wash basin. The sounds came up the stairwell like weather. August had lived alone in this tower for two years after Marianne died, and he had learned then what silence actually weighed. It was heavier than glass. It was heavier than anything he'd carried since.

The lamp itself was cold — daylight hours, no need for fire — but he went through the ritual anyway. Wick trimmed to a quarter inch. Oil reservoir full, the brass cap seated clean. The clockwork that turned the lens wound to its full eight hours. He checked the rotation by hand, one slow revolution, watching the great bull's-eye panels gather the gray window light and throw it around the room like a coin passed from palm to palm. First-order Fresnel, shipped from France before the war scares, a hundred and six pieces of crystal in a brass cage, each one worth more than the whole town below. The government owned it. The government owned the tower, the oil, the stairs, the very ground under his boots. August owned only the work, and the work was enough. It had been enough for eleven years.

He counted stores next, because counting was half of keeping. Down to the storeroom off the gallery, where the smell of sperm oil and salt-soaked paint lived together like an old married couple. Fourteen tins of oil. That was the number that mattered. Fourteen tins against a winter he could feel coming in his knuckles the way sailors felt it in their teeth. A normal season burned through twenty-two, maybe twenty-four if the storms came thick. He'd ordered thirty in September. The requisition boat out of Plymouth had brought twelve and a chit saying the balance would follow *when shipping permitted*.

*When shipping permitted.* August turned the phrase over the way he'd turn a stone on the beach, looking for what lived under it. Shipping permitted. As though the sea were a clerk who could be asked politely to hold the gales until London found room on a manifest. He wrote the shortfall in the tower log in his small even hand — *oil rec'd 12/30, balance pending* — and did not write what he thought, because the log was read by men in offices who had never stood a watch.

Flour, he had plenty. Salt pork, adequate. Potatoes in the clamp behind the coal shed, a good hill of them this year. Tea — he checked the caddy and winced. Enough for three weeks if he stretched it, and Tilly stretched it thin already without being asked, which was her way. She economized the way other children breathed.

That was the arithmetic of the lighthouse, always: what can be carried, what can be kept, what will run out first. August had been making those sums so long they ran beneath his thinking like groundwater. Every object in the tower had a weight in his head and a date attached. The spare lantern glass, wrapped in flannel in its crate: carried once from the depot, kept against breakage, good for years yet. Marianne's chair by the kitchen stove: carried nowhere now, kept past all sense, would run out never. He knew it was sentiment. He let himself have exactly one item of sentiment per inventory, and that was it.

* * *

Supper was barley soup with the last of the smoked herring, bread Nell had baked because baking was the only domestic labor she'd admit to enjoying, and a dish of stewed apples sweetened with honey Bram traded off a fisherman named Cade who owed the old man more favors than either of them could count.

They ate at the big table in the kitchen, all four of them, which had stopped feeling strange sometime last spring and now felt like the only correct arrangement of the world. Tilly sat across from August with her elbows on the wood — he'd given up correcting that particular sin months ago — and narrated her day between mouthfuls. She was nine going on forty-nine, small and quick, with a cowlick at the crown that refused all treaties and a way of looking directly at you when she talked that some adults found unsettling. August found it honest.

"—and Mrs. Penhale said I could carry her mending basket down to the quay tomorrow for a penny, but I said two pennies because it's uphill coming back, and she said *cheek*, and I said *arithmetic*, and she gave me two pennies." Tilly held these up as evidence, two copper coins flat on her palm like communion wafers.

"Uphill coming back," Nell repeated. She had a dry voice, precise as her handwriting. She sat straight-backed even at supper, sleeves buttoned to the wrist, brown hair pinned with mathematical severity. Eleven months under this roof and she still ate like a guest at someone else's table, careful, watchful, taking less than offered until August pushed the bowl at her a second time. "You understand you've just invented commerce."

"I didn't invent it. I *practiced* it."

"She practiced it," Bram said gravely, into his soup. "God help us all."

Bram was sixty if he was a day, built like a bollard, with white stubble on a jaw like a cliff face and hands that had done things to rope and timber and, once or twice, to men, that he didn't talk about. He'd washed ashore into August's life four years back with nothing but a seabag and a story he told nobody. He took the night watches August couldn't cover alone, split wood, gutted fish, and taught Tilly knots on the theory that a girl who could tie a bowline would never entirely drown. He called August *Keeper* even off duty, and it wasn't mockery. It was the highest title the old man had left to give.

"And how is the account book?" August asked Nell.

"Balanced." She said it with quiet satisfaction, the way another woman might say *in love*. "We're ahead of where we were last quarter. Barely. If the fish hold through November and nobody needs a doctor."

"Nobody needs a doctor," Tilly said firmly, as though decreeing it could make it law.

"Mrs. Penhale's cough says otherwise," Bram said, and got a glare for it.

August listened to them the way he listened to the barometer — reading pressure in small changes, learning the weather of his own house. Nell had come to them a year ago October, off the mail coach with one valise and a name that August had known within a fortnight wasn't hers. He'd seen enough forged papers in eleven years of harbor traffic to know the breed, and whatever Nell's real history was — clerk somewhere south, trouble with sums that weren't sums, trouble with a man or a firm or both — she'd fled it hard enough to leave scorch marks. He'd never asked. She'd never offered. She balanced their accounts, tutored Tilly in figures and penmanship, and slept with a locked box under her bed that everyone pretended not to see. In a house of strays, the rule was simple: you didn't ask where the animal had been before the storm. You fed it.

The wind came up while they ate. Not the storm itself — too early in the year, and the glass still holding steady-ish, 29.4 and sulking rather than falling — but the vanguard of it, the long southwest swell that arrived a day ahead of any front and set the harbor water slapping at the pilings in a new, impatient rhythm. August felt it change the sound of the house the way you feel a floor shift under load.

"Blowing harder," Bram said. Not a question. Old sailors stated the obvious as a form of prayer.

"Aye."

"Topsail schooner'll have trouble rounding the point tonight if she's out there."

"There's nothing due." August had memorized the shipping lists the way other men memorized psalms. "Cade's boats are all beached. Nothing from Plymouth till Friday, if it comes at all."

"If it comes at all," Nell murmured, and there was something under the words, some current, and August looked at her and she looked at her bowl and the moment passed the way such moments passed in their house: noted by everyone, claimed by no one.

Tilly, mercifully, was immune to undercurrents. "Can I sleep up in the watch room tonight? If it blows?"

"You may not."

"You let me in June."

"In June the wind was a kitten. Tonight it'll be a dog with teeth. You'd hear it all night and dream about drowning."

"I don't dream about drowning," Tilly said with dignity. "I dream about flying."

"Same thing," Bram said, "if you do it wrong."

She laughed, and August let himself laugh too, and for a few minutes the kitchen was warm in the ordinary way, candle-flame and soup-steam and the dog asleep under the table with his chin on Bram's boot. This was the contentment, August thought. Not some grand arrival but this exact texture: barley and lamplight and a child's laugh, the four of them held inside walls he maintained with his own hands. He had not expected a second household after Marianne. He had certainly not expected it to assemble itself out of wreckage, piece by piece, an orphan here, a deserter-shaped silence there, a runaway clerk who baked bread on Sundays — but it had, and he had learned not to examine the miracle too closely, the way you don't stare at a candle flame or it goes out.

After supper Tilly dried dishes and stacked them wrong, as always, and Bram went out to double-check the boat tied at the slip below, and Nell climbed the stair to her room with the candle held high, and August stood a moment alone in the kitchen listening to the wind test the shutters.

Then he climbed the tower for the lighting.

* * *

The evening ritual was older than any of them. Lamp lit at dusk, wick coaxed, the clockwork released so the lens began its slow patient turning — one revolution every forty seconds, a flash every ten, the character of the light printed in the Admiralty list so that any master in any sea could read it and know where he was. *Crane Light, occulting white, ten seconds.* A sentence that had saved lives August would never meet.

He stood in the lantern room after the mechanism caught, hands loose at his sides, and watched the beams go out over the water like long arms reaching. Below, the town was lighting too — a scatter of yellow windows along the harbor street, smoke standing up crooked from the chimneys in the freshening wind. Two hundred souls, give or take, in houses older than the nation. Fishermen and their wives, the chandler, the innkeeper, Mrs. Penhale with her cough and her mending, Cade with his boats, children running the shingle. None of them rich. Most of them owed somebody money, because coastal towns always did — debts to merchants in Plymouth, mortgages held by gentlemen who had never seen the place, favors banked against winters like the one gathering itself beyond the horizon.

And somewhere out there, past the point, past the darkening water, the swell was telling him what was coming. Big sea. Long interval. The ocean clearing its throat before it spoke.

August checked the barometer again before he came down. 29.2 now, and falling — slowly, sulkily, but falling. He wrote it in the log. *Wind SW freshening, glass falling, swell increasing.* Then, because the log was also where he talked to himself, he added: *Early for a blow of this size.*

He sat with that line a while. There was a habit the sea had, in bad years, of sending its warnings early and often. And there had been letters, that summer — official crests, polite language — about *surveying the coast*, about *assessing harbor capacity*, about questions he'd answered and filed and tried to forget. Government interest in a poor harbor usually meant one of two things, and neither of them was good for the people in the windows below.

But that was tomorrow's weather. Tonight the light was burning, the household was under his roof, the soup pot was scraped clean, and Tilly's two pennies were on the mantel where she'd left them, gleaming like treasure.

August banked the stove, took up the candle, and started down the stairs — ninety feet of iron treads he knew better than his own heartbeat — while above him the great lens turned and turned, throwing its steady white sentence out onto the black water.

Out past the point, far off, almost too far to swear to, he thought he saw sails.

Gray ones, square-rigged, riding the swell with the unhurried confidence of something that had come a long way on purpose.

He stood a long moment at the little gallery door, wind pulling at his coat, telling himself it was a fishing boat, a merchantman beating for Plymouth, anything at all.

But he counted the masts anyway.

Three.

And no fishing boat he'd ever known came in three-masted, at dusk, ahead of a falling glass, toward a harbor with nothing in it worth taking.

Except, perhaps, everything.
