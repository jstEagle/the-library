## 2. Gray Sails at Dawn

August Crane woke before the storm glass told him to.

He had kept the habit thirty years — rising in the dark, hand finding the cold brass of the lamp housing by memory alone, the way a blind man finds his own door. But this morning something else woke him: a silence in the harbor that was not quite silence. The gulls had moved. He lay a moment in his narrow bed above the oil room and listened to them wheeling and crying out beyond the north point instead of over the water where they belonged, and he thought: *something is on the water.*

He dressed in the dark. Wool trousers, flannel shirt, the jersey Nell had knitted him two winters back with one shoulder a finger too long because she'd been guessing at his measurements from a borrowed coat. The stairs wound up through the tower's spine — one hundred and forty-one iron steps, each with its own small voice underfoot — and he climbed them into the gray hour before dawn, when the world is all rumor and no testimony.

The light was burning steady behind him. He had trimmed the wick at midnight, fed it at four, and now it threw his shadow long down the gallery as he stepped out into the wind.

The wind came off the sea the way it always did in October, smelling of salt and iron and the cold coming south. But beneath it, faint as a stain, came coal smoke. Coal smoke, on a coast where every boat burned sail or oar or nothing at all.

He raised the telescope Bram had given him for Christmas eleven years ago — a good glass, London-made, salvaged off a wrecked yacht with the maker's name still legible — and put it to his eye.

She sat three miles out in the roads, exactly where the deep-water channel began: a steam cutter, black-hulled, twin funnels venting thin gray ribbons that the wind tore apart before they could fall. Naval pennant at the main. Guns — he counted twice to be sure — two of them, small but real, swiveling on their mounts like heads turning to look at you. She rode at anchor with the insolent stillness of a thing that intended to stay.

August lowered the glass. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; it was one of the things the Board paid him for, along with his eyesight and his sobriety and his willingness to live alone with a machine. But somewhere beneath his ribs, in the place where a man keeps the things he doesn't say, a door swung open onto a cold room.

*Coal smoke,* he thought. *In October. Before the storms.*

Nobody sent a gunboat ahead of the weather unless they meant to take something worth more than the weather would cost them.

* * *

By full tide the cutter's boats were coming ashore, six of them in line abreast, pulling hard against the chop with the crisp unsociable rhythm of men who rowed by order rather than need. August watched them from the gallery while Tilly laid the breakfast things below and Nell stood at the kitchen window with her arms folded tight, watching the same water through the salt-fogged pane.

"Steamer," she said, when he came down. Not a question. Nell had spent five years forging customs papers in a Port Office and knew what government tonnage looked like even at three miles.

"Army," August said. "Two guns."

Bram set down his mug without drinking. The old sailor's face did something complicated and then settled into nothing at all, which was how August knew it was bad. Bram had a face for weather and a face for war, and this was the second one.

"Troop strength?" Bram asked.

"Forty in the boats. More below decks, I'd judge. A hundred, perhaps."

"A hundred men and two guns to collect taxes from fishermen." Bram turned his mug slowly on the table, around and around, like a man grinding something between his palms. "That's not collection. That's occupation."

Tilly looked up from the bread she was slicing, knife paused mid-stroke. At twelve she had the family talent for reading a room faster than any of them would admit. "Occupation of what? There's nothing here."

"There's a harbor," Nell said quietly. "Deep enough for transports. And there's a war coming nobody's named yet, and harbors are worth naming wars over." She caught August looking at her and lifted her chin, half defiance, half apology — the look of a woman who had learned that knowing things could be dangerous and knowing them aloud more so. "I've seen the freight schedules, August. I'm not guessing."

"You shouldn't know freight schedules," Tilly said.

"I know lots of things I shouldn't." Nell smiled at the girl, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Eat your breakfast."

But none of them ate much, and when the first boots hit the shingle below the town, all four of them were at the windows.

* * *

They came up from the beach in column, forty men in dark blue with white belts and white cross-straps, rifles shouldered, boots striking the wet cobbles in a rhythm that the town had never heard before and understood immediately. Behind them rode an officer on a gray horse, and behind the officer a wagon with a folding table strapped across it, and behind the wagon a second column of men carrying crowbars and canvas satchels and coils of rope.

August stood in the lighthouse yard with Bram at his shoulder and Tilly pressed against his side where he could feel her breathing, fast and shallow as a bird's. Below them the town was emptying into the street the way water empties into a drain — drawn, helpless, unable to look away. Fishermen in oilskins. Women with aprons still on, flour to the wrists. Old Marta Kessel from the net house, who had buried two husbands and feared nothing on this earth except God and debt collectors, standing in her doorway with her arms crossed like a gate.

The column halted in the square by the Customs House. The officer dismounted. He was tall, broad through the shoulders, with a cavalryman's seat even after dismounting and a face that might have been handsome if it ever relaxed — which, from this distance, it did not. Gold braid at the collar. A major's insignia, fresh and bright, the kind that hadn't been rained on yet.

"He'll read it from the steps," Bram said. "They always read it from the steps."

The officer took a paper from an aide, unfolded it, and read it from the steps.

His voice carried on the wind in pieces — *by order of* — *requisitioned for the use of* — *effective immediately* — *all vessels moored within the harbor limits* — *any resistance will be met with* — and the wind ate the rest. It didn't matter. The town heard what it needed. August felt it move through the crowd like a current through kelp, everyone bending the same direction at once.

Then the soldiers split into squads and went to work.

They worked efficiently, August had to give them that. No shouting, no broken windows, none of the theater young troops liked. They walked down the quay boat by boat, painting a number on each hull in white lead — *R-1, R-2, R-3* — slipping a paper seal under the tiller ropes, posting a sentry beside each one with his rifle grounded. Tom Yeo's lugger. The Pedersen boys' smack, painted green, that their father had built the year they were born. Widow Anselm's little punt that wasn't worth requisitioning and got numbered anyway, because the army did not distinguish between what it wanted and what it could count.

Old Sam Prowse, who had fished those waters fifty years and once swam a mile in winter to save a stranger's dog, stepped forward when they reached his boat and put his hand flat on the gunwale. A sergeant spoke to him. Sam shook his head. The sergeant nodded to two privates, who took Sam gently by the elbows and moved him back — gently, August noted, professionally, the way you'd move furniture you'd been instructed not to scratch — and painted the number on the hull while Sam stood there with his hand still open in the air, holding the shape of a gunwale that was no longer his.

Tilly made a sound against August's side. Small, bitten off. He put his arm around her and felt how rigid she was, how she held herself the way he held himself when he didn't want to shake.

"They can't do that," she whispered. "It's his boat. He *built* half of it."

"Yes," August said.

"But they can't just—"

"No," he said. Which was not an answer, and she knew it, and pressed her face into his jersey so he wouldn't see what her face did next.

* * *

Through the morning the work spread. By noon the soldiers had reached the Customs House itself and come out with armfuls of books and boxes, loading them on the wagon under a tarpaulin while the clerk — poor hollow-eyed Mr. Quill, whose entire existence was those books — stood wringing his hat in his hands and saying something over and over that the wind wouldn't carry. A lieutenant patted him on the shoulder in a way that was probably meant kindly. Quill sat down on the step afterward and didn't get up for a long time.

Nell watched the wagon load from the kitchen window, and August watched Nell watch it, and saw her go the color of candle wax.

"The records," she said. Her voice was level the way a wire is level before it snaps. "They're taking the records."

"Customs ledgers," August said. "Cargo manifests. Nothing of yours."

"Everything is of mine." She said it lightly, and it landed heavy. She turned away from the window and busied her hands with bread that didn't need doing. "A person's whole life is in a ledger somewhere, August. That's what a ledger is. It's a list of everyone the law has decided to remember."

Bram grunted from his chair by the stove. "Then God help whoever's in the book they want most."

* * *

In the afternoon the officer came to the lighthouse.

August saw him coming up the cliff path an hour before he arrived — the gray horse picking its way over the turf, two troopers trailing at a respectful distance — and had time to stand at the gallery rail and decide what he would be when the man arrived. Angry was available. So was deference, cheaply worn. He chose neither. He chose the thing he wore best, the thing the tower had taught him: stillness. He met the officer at the foot of the stair with his hands at his sides and the wind pulling at his collar.

"Lieutenant Colonel Ellery Vane." The man removed his glove to offer his hand, and August, shaking it, felt how warm the glove-hand was, how the flesh inside the leather had been riding all day in comfort while other men rowed. "Requisition authority for this district. You're the keeper."

"I'm the keeper."

"This your household?" Vane's eyes went past August's shoulder, up the path, to where the lighthouse stood white against the scudding gray. He did not wait for an answer to look at the answer. "Board report says one man. The census says otherwise."

"The census is two years old."

"So am I informed of many things two years old." Something crossed the colonel's face then — quickly, badly concealed, like a fish under ice — and August felt the shape of it before he could name it, the familiarity of the jaw beneath the braid, the set of the eyes. He had last seen those eyes at a funeral in a rain-sooked churchyard seven years ago, red-rimmed and refusing tears, belonging to a younger man in civilian black who had shaken August's hand once, briefly, and never written since.

The wind moved between them. Somewhere below, a soldier's laugh carried up the cliff and died.

"You'll want to inspect," August said. "Come up, if you like. Mind the hundred and forty-one steps. They're honest about their age."

Vane looked at him for a long moment — measuring, August thought, or remembering, or both — and then inclined his head. "I'll inspect another day. Today I only bring notice." He drew a paper from inside his coat, and though the wind tried to take it he held it flat against his thigh and read without hurrying, and his voice, close up, had a timbre August recognized from somewhere deeper than the funeral — from a supper table twenty years gone, a girl laughing, a boy sulking over his plate, a house that no longer existed anywhere but in the two men standing on this cliff pretending not to recognize each other.

"—the lighthouse, its apparatus, stores, and dwelling being designated a station of military significance, the keeper is directed to maintain normal service until further instruction, subject to requisition of such stores as the authority may determine—"

"You're taking my oil."

"I'm taking what the schedule requires. Your allowance will be honored." Vane folded the paper. For a moment neither man moved. The troopers waited by the horses with the patience of men who had watched this scene in four towns already. Then Vane said, not quite looking at August, his gaze fixed on some middle distance where the sea met the sky: "You're well, then. Keeping well."

"My wife is dead seven years, Colonel. Other than that."

Something hardened in Vane's face, and August understood — with the terrible clarity the sea grants a man at certain hours — that the hardness was not anger. It was the armor anger wears when it's losing.

"There will be further notices," Vane said. He pulled his glove back on, finger by finger. "Posted at the Custom House. Read them."

He mounted, gathered the reins, and paused — one breath, no more — with the gray horse sidling beneath him and the wind flattening the grass along the cliff top.

"She'd have hated this town," he said. "Too much weather."

Then he rode down the path, and the hoofbeats faded, and August stood in the yard until the gray shapes dissolved into the gray afternoon, feeling the door in his ribs swing wider onto the cold room.

* * *

That evening the household gathered in the kitchen because the kitchen was the warmest room and the warmest room was where you went when the world turned strange. Bram had lit the stove high. Nell had made tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. Tilly sat on the floor with her back against Bram's knees, which was her place, established years ago by silent treaty, and for a while nobody talked. The lamp hissed. The wind worried the shutters. Down in the harbor, through the window, they could see lanterns moving among the impounded boats — sentries walking their beats, patient as tide.

"Five days' coal on that steamer, maybe six," Bram said at last, to the ceiling. "They'll ride out the first blow at anchor, then land the rest. Engineers, likely. Sappers. They mean to build something here."

"What?" Tilly asked.

"Doesn't matter what. Matters that they mean to be here when the great storms come, and they don't mean to leave." Bram's hand rested on the girl's hair, absently, the way it had rested on tillers and capstans for fifty years. "And they've got every boat in the harbor under seal. Every one."

"Except the dinghy under the point," Tilly said. "The leaky one. Nobody sealed the leaky one."

Everyone looked at her. She shrugged, twelve years old and unrepentant. "I notice things."

"That you do," Bram said gravely, and August saw the ghost of a smile pass between the old man and the child, brief as a fish flashing under gray water — the household's oldest trick, finding one bright thing in the dark and holding it up.

But the brightness sank, and the quiet came back, and it was Nell who broke it, setting down her cup with a click.

"They posted the first notice tonight. I walked past on my way back from the chandlery." She smoothed her skirt over her knees, twice, three times, a gesture August had learned meant her hands needed employment or they'd start shaking. "It lists what's required of the town. Boats. Stores. Records." A pause, precisely measured, like a wick trimmed to length. "And at the bottom, in smaller print — evacuation berths. For essential persons only, before the storms make landfall. To be allocated by the requisition authority."

"How many berths?" August asked.

Nell looked at him across the lamplit table, and her eyes were very steady and very frightened, and she said:

"Twelve."

Twelve berths. August let the number sit in the room. He thought of the faces he'd counted that morning on the quay — Sam Prowse and his open hand, Marta Kessel in her doorway, the Pedersen brothers, the widow Anselm, Quill wringing his hat, the children, the old ones, the nets and the crofts and the crooked lanes full of people who had washed up here from wrecks of their own making and made a harbor of each other. He thought of his own kitchen, and counted — without wanting to, the way you count waves — the household sitting around his table.

Four of them here. And a town of sixty-three souls below.

Twelve berths, and October nearly spent, and the barometer already beginning its slow, dumb descent toward the truth.

Outside, the first spits of the coming weather ticked against the glass like fingers testing a lock, and down in the dark harbor the lanterns walked their rounds among the stolen boats, and the gray ship rode at anchor with her funnels smoking, patient as arithmetic.

August rose to trim the lamp, because the lamp always needed trimming, because trimming the lamp was the one thing in the world he still controlled. On the stairs he passed Tilly, sent up to bed, lingering at the arrow-slit window with her chin on her fists.

"Uncle August," she said. "How do they choose who gets a berth?"

He stopped on the step. Below him the kitchen murmured; above him the lamp burned steady against the dark; and the question hung between floors like the smell of coal smoke — arriving, unmistakable, impossible to un-breathe.

"I don't know," he said.

And Tilly, who noticed things, looked at him with her mother's grave eyes and said, "I think we should find out before somebody chooses us."
