## 3. Names in a Ledger

Nell Ashcombe had three names in her head and one of them was true.

She kept them sorted the way she kept her sums — clean columns, no smudging. *Eleanor Voss*, clerk of the Harbor Trust, who had signed for ledgers and invoices for four years under a hand so steady no one ever looked twice at it. *Nell Ash*, the name the fishwives used when they waved her into their kitchens for bread and gossip, the name Tilly called up the stairs at supper. And underneath both, folded small as a pressed flower, the name she'd been born with, which she took out only at night and never said aloud because saying it felt like striking a match near gunpowder.

Four years of quiet. Four years of columns that balanced, of forged papers filed so neatly inside the Trust's own records that the records themselves became her alibi. That was the trick nobody taught you: don't hide the forgery somewhere dark. Hide it in the light, between honest pages, where no clerk thinks to look because clerks trust other clerks.

Then the soldiers came ashore at dawn, and by noon the drums were beating in the square, and by two o'clock Nell was standing in the requisition house — the old customs hall, its brass fittings polished by forty years of honest trade now hung with bunting for dishonest business — watching Captain Ellery Vane put his gloved finger on the shipping ledger.

"The ledger," he said again, for the benefit of the room.

The room was half the town's worth of men and women who still had trades to defend: chandlers, pilots, the harbor trust's remaining clerks, Bram Wicker standing at the back with his arms crossed like a man attending his own trial. Soldiers lined the walls with the particular boredom of men guarding furniture.

"The ledger is the property of the Crown's harbor administration," Vane continued, "and it will be transferred to army custody this afternoon. Every vessel registered. Every cargo recorded. Every debt, lien, license, and obligation entered against every family in this town." He let that sit. He had a way of letting things sit that made you understand he'd rehearsed the silence. "You will be assessed from these pages. Loyalties as well as debts. I would remind you all that a record is not merely a history. It is a verdict."

Nell's hands were cold. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather through glass. Her eyes went to the ledger on the trestle table — fat, leather-cornered, its spine cracked white from daily use — and her mind began, helplessly, to count.

Page forty-one. The Voss registration, renewed annually, countersigned by herself in her own careful hand, each renewal a little more convincing than the last because each year her skill improved and her fear sharpened. Page fifty-eight, the surety bond against the *Kestrel*'s debts, guaranteed by E. Voss, clerk — a guarantee built on nothing but ink and nerve. Pages sixty-two through sixty-four, the correspondence copies, including two letters she had drafted herself authorizing transfers that had never been authorized, bearing a signature she had practiced until her wrist ached — the signature of a dead man whose death she had learned about eleven days after she'd spent his money.

All of it filed under *Voss*. All of it filed in the light.

And the army was going to take the book and read it line by line, looking — Vane had said it himself — for exactly what she was.

* * *

She made it out of the customs hall without running, which cost her more than running would have. Down the steps, past the impounded fishing boats riding sullen at their new moorings, past a corporal checking a list against the world as though the world might have changed since morning. Her legs carried her up Harbor Lane on pure clerical habit, and it wasn't until she reached the turn to the lighthouse path that she realized she'd been gripping her own wrists behind her back hard enough to bruise.

August met her at the door before she knocked. He did that sometimes; she'd stopped wondering how he knew.

"Out with it," he said.

"They're taking the ledger."

He went very still. August Crane could go very still the way deep water goes still, and Nell had learned to read what moved underneath. "When?"

"This afternoon. Army custody. He stood there and told us it's a verdict." She heard her own voice go thin and hated it. "August, my papers are in that book. Not separate — *in* it. My registration, my bonds, the transfer letters. Four years of me, bound in calfskin, going straight into the hands of men whose whole trade is reading names."

"Come inside."

"I can't—" But she could, and did, because her knees had opinions of their own.

The kitchen smelled of yesterday's bread and lamp oil. Tilly was at the table doing sums she'd invented for herself — Nell caught a glimpse of the page: *berths 5, household 6* written in a child's blunt pencil — and the girl looked up, saw Nell's face, and closed the paper over with a quickness that told Nell the arithmetic was being shared with no one yet.

"Soldiers are taking the shipping ledger," August said to the room, which was his way of telling Tilly things without deciding whether to tell her things.

Tilly frowned. "The big book? Mr. Quill lets me look at the ship drawings sometimes."

"Not anymore, I expect," Nell said, and heard how flat her voice came out.

Bram was in the corner mending a net that hadn't fished since the boats were seized, working the knots with fingers like knotted rope. He didn't look up. "Requisition house'll be guarded now."

"It was already," Nell said. "Two at the door. One walking the perimeter." She stopped. She had counted them automatically, the way she counted everything, and counting them out loud made them real. "Three."

"You counted," Bram said, and something in his voice wasn't surprise. It was recognition — one professional acknowledging another's reflexes.

"I'm a clerk. Counting is—" She sat down hard on the bench. "Counting is the least of it. You don't understand what's in those pages. What *I* put in those pages."

August pulled out a chair and sat opposite her, forearms on the table, waiting. He had a talent for waiting that most people mistook for patience. It wasn't patience. It was a kind of pressure, gentle as tide.

"Tell him," Tilly said unexpectedly. "He fixes things."

"He doesn't fix this."

"Tell him anyway."

So Nell told him — not everything, not the name beneath the names, but the shape of it. A clerk named Eleanor Voss who had appeared in the harbor records four years ago with excellent references and no verifiable history. A surety bond backed by air. Two authorization letters in the hand of a man who had been dead when she wrote them. Money moved to pay debts that weren't hers to pay, debts of people who'd have gone to the debtors' prison otherwise — she watched Tilly's face at that part, and Tilly didn't flinch, because Tilly knew which debts, or guessed — and all of it documented, countersigned, cross-filed, *legible*.

"I built myself out of paperwork," Nell finished. "That was the whole design. A person made of records can't be doubted, because you can point at the records. And now the army is going to point at mine."

"And if they find the false entries?" August asked quietly.

"If they find them, I'm not a runaway clerk who bent some rules. I'm a forger. Fraud against the Crown's administration, in wartime requisition conditions." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "They hang people for less when they're feeling generous. When they're feeling thorough, they ask questions first. About where else I've been. About who helped me. About—"

She stopped. *About the household.* About an orphan with no papers at all and an old deserter keeping his head down and a lighthouse keeper married to the wrong sister of whatever Vane turned out to be. Her terror had been all for herself for one full hour, and only now did it turn outward and become properly enormous.

"They won't stop at me," she said. "That's how inquiries work. I know how inquiries work, I've *filed* the outcomes of inquiries. They pull one thread and then they pull the threads attached to the thread. They'll want to know who harbored me. Who vouched. Who—" 

"Nell." August's voice was low and even. "Breathe."

She breathed. Outside, the gulls were making a sound like argument, and far off in the harbor a soldier's whistle cut across the water, sharp and administrative.

* * *

By late afternoon the ledger had been carried out of the customs hall under escort, and Nell knew because she made herself watch it happen from the rise above Harbor Lane, because not watching seemed worse — a clerk should know where the documents are, always, even when the documents are the thing that will kill her.

Six soldiers. A sergeant carrying it wrapped in oilcloth like contraband, which, she thought, it now was — just in the wrong direction. They marched it to the requisition house and she watched the door close on four years of her life's architecture.

She stayed on the rise until the lamps were lit along the lane, doing what she always did when panic got too large: she made lists.

*What they will check first:* registrations against parish records. Her registration cited a parish in Kent. She had chosen Kent because it was far and because the vicar whose signature she'd copied had died the winter before, leaving his register conveniently chaotic. That would hold for weeks, maybe months.

*What they will check second:* the surety bond. The dead man's estate. Estates get settled slowly, but war accelerates paperwork; an army assessor hunting revenue would write to London within the fortnight. London's reply would come back wrong. Wrong in a way that unraveled everything stitched to it.

*What they will check third:* the letters. The handwriting itself. She had imitated a living hand once, badly, and been caught within the hour by a supervisor who only laughed — *you press too hard, girl, guilt makes heavy strokes* — and had never pressed too hard again. But army examiners were not laughing supervisors. They had magnifiers and time and no affection for her.

Weeks. Maybe a month if the storms slowed the post. The storm glass at the lighthouse window had been falling for two days; August had mentioned it at supper without emphasis, the way he mentioned tides. If the great storms came early, the roads washed, the packet boats suspended — mail delayed, assessors stranded, inquiry stalled.

Weather, she thought, staring down at the lit windows of the requisition house. Her life might depend on weather. On barometers. On the same sea that was going to try to drown the whole town.

There was another option and she made herself look at it squarely, because clerks look at things squarely. She could run tonight, before the examination began. Alone. Take nothing that connected her here, leave no thread for anyone to pull, walk inland in the dark and become a fourth name in a different town and start the whole architecture again, older and wiser. The household would be safe. No thread leading back to a lighthouse keeper and a child and an old man who'd already served his sentence once in some other form.

It was a good plan. It was the correct plan. She ran the numbers on it three times and they balanced every time, and balancing numbers had never once made her feel sick before.

She was still standing there, cold to the bone, when boots crunched on the path behind her and she nearly went over the rise with fright.

It was Tilly, breathless, coat buttoned wrong. "August says come home. Supper." The girl peered at the requisition house below, then up at Nell, with that unnerving directness children have and adults spend years training out of themselves. "You were going to do something stupid."

"I was going to make a list."

"That's what stupid looks like before it happens. I do it too." Tilly shoved her cold hand into Nell's cold one, matter-of-fact as arithmetic. "August says nobody decides anything alone in this house. House rule. He made it up after Bram tried to give away his coat."

"I'm not giving anything away."

"You're giving yourself away." Tilly tugged her down the path. "Come eat. Then decide stupid with everybody."

Nell let herself be tugged. Behind them the requisition house burned yellow in every window, and somewhere inside it, under oilcloth or open on a trestle, the ledger lay with her whole constructed self inside its pages — patient, accurate, and waiting, like everything else in this town, for the storm.

At the foot of the path they met August coming up, lantern raised, and his face in the lamplight stopped them both. He'd been to the harbor. Whatever he'd seen or heard there had rearranged him.

"They've posted the berth list," he said. "Five berths for this household." His eyes found Nell's. "And Vane has ordered the ledger opened tomorrow morning. First entry examined at eight o'clock."

Nell's blood went from cold to ice. Eight o'clock. Twelve hours.

Twelve hours before someone in a red coat turned to page forty-one.
