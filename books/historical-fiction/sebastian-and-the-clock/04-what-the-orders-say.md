## 4. What the Orders Say

The knock came at mid-morning, three measured raps, and August knew before he opened the door that it would be a soldier's knock — even, unhurried, the sound of a man who has never once been made to wait.

He opened it anyway.

Captain Ellery Vane stood on the gallery steps with the sea wind pulling at his coat, and for a long moment neither of them moved. Ten years had passed since they'd last stood face to face, at the graveside in Portsmouth, when the ground was still soft over Margaret. Vane had worn his uniform then too. He'd put his hand on August's shoulder, said *take care of yourself*, and walked away before the first shovel of earth fell. That was the whole of the eulogy between them.

"Mr. Crane." Vane's voice was exactly as August remembered — clipped, precise, the voice of a man who read orders aloud for a living. "I'm conducting inspections of the coastal installations. I'll need to see your lamp room."

August leaned against the doorframe and let the silence stretch until it became its own kind of answer. "Inspections," he said. "That what they're calling it now."

"It's what the paperwork calls it." Vane held up a leather folio, stamped with the War Office crest. "May I come in, or would you prefer I write up the lighthouse as uncooperative?"

*Uncooperative.* August turned the word over. It was the kind of word that went into ledgers. The kind that followed a man through every door afterward.

He stepped back. "Mind the wet on the stairs. The spray's been coming over the wall since Tuesday."

Inside, the tower smelled of brass polish and lamp oil and the bread Tilly had left cooling on the sill — the ordinary smells of an ordinary morning, and August felt a sudden fierce protectiveness toward all of them, even the smell of the bread, because this man had no right to any of it.

Vane climbed the spiral stairs without comment, his boots ringing on the iron treads, and August followed, counting the turns the way he counted everything — one hundred and twelve to the watch room, one hundred and thirteen if you were tall as Vane and took longer strides.

At the top, the captain stopped at the glass and looked out over the harbor. The army cutter rode at anchor where the fishing boats used to be, gray hull against gray water. On the quay below, soldiers were stacking impounded skiffs like firewood.

"You've taken their boats," August said.

"I've requisitioned their boats. There's a difference."

"There is," August agreed. "One of them floats."

Vane didn't smile. He crossed to the lamp itself, the great Fresnel lens with its rings of polished glass, and studied it the way a jeweler studies a stone — professionally, without wonder. "When was the mechanism last serviced?"

"Serviced? She's serviced every day. Every hour, some days, when the weather turns."

"That's not what I asked."

"And that's not an answer." August came around the lamp so they faced each other across the lens, and through the curved glass Vane's face bent slightly, widened, became something almost boyish. August remembered that boy. He'd liked him once, at the wedding, when Ellery Vane was twenty-three and laughed easily and told Margaret she was marrying beneath her but marrying well anyway, and everyone had pretended it was a joke.

"When did you last have the clockwork inspected by a qualified engineer?" Vane said.

"The clockwork keeps perfect time. You can set your requisitions by her."

"Mr. Crane—"

"Keeper Crane," August said. "I've held the post eleven years. I earned the word."

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes — not anger, August thought. Something wearier than anger. He took out a notebook, wrote briefly, snapped it shut.

"Your light is adequate. Your records will be reviewed by the quartermaster. Now—" he paused, and August saw him choose the next words with visible care, "—there is another matter. Have you heard the forecasts from the north stations?"

"I hear the wind. That's my forecast."

"The barometers are falling along the whole coast. Three ships have reported pressure drops unprecedented for the season. The Admiralty believes a great storm is forming — perhaps the worst in thirty years. It will make landfall here within the fortnight." Vane said it flatly, a supply report, tonnage and dates. "The army is organizing an evacuation."

"Evacuation." August tested the word and found it rotten. "Of the town."

"Of persons deemed essential. There are berths available on the transport *Meridian* — five berths for this station and its associated household."

The number landed in the watch room like a dropped lantern. Five. August thought instantly of Tilly, of Bram, of Nell, of old Widow Marsh two doors down who couldn't climb her own stairs anymore, of the fisher families with four children apiece. Five berths against a town of better than two hundred souls.

"Five," he repeated.

"For essential persons." Vane's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "The determination of essentiality is made according to criteria established by the Board."

"Criteria established by men who've never seen this coast."

"The criteria were established by men who drowned thirty people on this coast twenty years ago because they evacuated too slowly." Vane said it sharply, and then, as if hearing himself, went quiet. The wind filled the pause, moaning faintly around the lantern gallery above.

August stared at him. "You know about that."

"I know about every failed evacuation on record. It's why I'm here."

*It's why I'm here.* The words hung there, and August found himself unable to read them — whether they meant the posting, the storm, or something older and heavier that had nothing to do with either. Ten years of silence sat between them, ten years since Margaret died with August holding her hand in a rented room while her brother sent letters that arrived too late, always too late, and then stopped arriving altogether.

"She wrote to you," August said quietly. "Near the end. Did you ever get those letters?"

Vane's hand closed around the folio until the leather creaked. For a moment August thought he wouldn't answer at all.

"I got them," Vane said finally. "All three. I was at sea. By the time they reached me, she'd been dead five weeks."

"You could have written after."

"Yes." The word came out stripped bare, no armor on it anywhere. Then the armor came back up, plate by plate, and Vane was the captain again, consulting his notebook as though the weather outside were a line item. "The berths will be allocated by Thursday. You should submit your household list to the requisition office. Names, ages, occupations. Anyone not on the list will be classified as—"

"Don't say it."

"—residual," Vane finished anyway. "Residual population remains at their own risk. I'd advise you to choose carefully who goes on that list, Mr. Crane. Or Keeper Crane." He moved toward the stair, then stopped, one hand on the rail, not looking back. "Margaret used to say you could measure a man by what he refuses to leave behind. I never understood what she meant until recently."

"Then you're slower than she was," August said. "She understood everything by the time she was nine."

For just an instant — gone so fast August might have invented it — something that was not a captain looked out of Vane's face. Grief, maybe. Or the memory of grief, worn smooth as sea glass from years of handling.

"Thursday," Vane said, and went down the stairs.

August listened to the boots fade, the door open and close, the footsteps cross the gallery rocks and diminish toward the causeway. Then he stood alone in the watch room with the great lens turning its slow circles, throwing its long blade of light across a sea that hadn't yet decided what it meant to do.

Five berths.

He got paper and sat down at the little desk beneath the clockwork, and began, in his careful keeper's hand, to make a list. He got as far as writing the word *household* at the top before he stopped, pen hovering, because the household had edges that wouldn't hold still. Tilly, certainly. Bram — Bram had kept this light alive through two gales single-handed when August lay fevered. Nell, who had appeared eight months ago with forged papers and quick hands and nowhere else in the world.

And beyond the household: the widow Marsh. The Halloran children, whose father was lost last winter. Half the harbor, if he was honest, half the town, all of them people who had drifted here because the world elsewhere had no use for them, gathered under his light like moths who'd learned to stay.

The pen hovered. Outside, the barometer needle slid another notch down, so gently you'd miss it unless you were watching, and August was always watching.

He wrote the first name. Then the second. Then he sat back and looked at the page, at the small dark shapes of the letters, each one a person, each one a weight, and understood that whatever he wrote next — and whatever he refused to write — would follow him longer than any storm.

Down in the kitchen, he could hear Tilly singing to herself as she swept, off-key and unbothered, and the sound of it went through him like a splinter.

Five berths. Six in his own house already, before he counted anyone else at all.

He dipped the pen again, and began to do arithmetic he already knew he couldn't finish.
