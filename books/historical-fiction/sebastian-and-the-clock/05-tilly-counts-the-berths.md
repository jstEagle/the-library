## 5. Tilly Counts the Berths

Tilly learned to count on the lighthouse stairs. August had taught her the summer she arrived, all hundred and fourteen of them, one for each foot above the rock, and she'd climbed them every day since until the numbers lived in her bones the way salt lived in her skin. Counting was the one thing she was better at than anybody in the house. Nell could read faster. Bram could tie knots nobody had names for. But numbers — numbers were hers.

So when Mrs. Petchy came up from the harbor with the news folded inside her shawl like a dead bird, Tilly did what she always did. She counted.

"Five berths," Mrs. Petchy said, standing in the kitchen doorway with rain dripping off her bonnet. "That's what the corporal told the harbor master. Five berths on the first evacuation boat for your household."

Tilly counted on her fingers without meaning to. August. Nell. Bram. Herself. That was four. She looked around the kitchen as if the fifth person might be hiding behind the flour crock.

"We're only four," she said.

Mrs. Petchy's face did something complicated, the way grown-up faces did when they were trying not to say a thing by saying six other things instead. "The army counts the household as six," she said. "They count the ones who've signed the keeper's register. You, the keeper, the old man, the clerk girl. And they count—" she stopped.

"My mother's name is still on it," Tilly said. "Isn't it."

August hadn't struck the name out. Tilly knew that because she'd seen the register once, laid open on his desk, and there her mother was in faded brown ink: *Martha Crane*, written in a hand that wasn't August's, because he couldn't bear to write her name himself and couldn't bear to cross it out either. The army didn't know she was dead. The army was counting a ghost.

"So it's five berths and we're four living people," Tilly said slowly, working it through the way August worked through the lamp wicks, careful, in order. "Which means one berth extra. Which means somebody else could—"

"Tilly." Mrs. Petchy crouched down to her level, which grown-ups did when they thought they were about to be kind, and which usually meant they weren't. "Child, listen to me. There are forty-one souls in this town who'll fit no berth at all. Whatever you do with that spare place, don't go telling folk about it. There are mothers in the lower street who'd walk their babies to your door if they thought there was a seat going empty."

Tilly looked past her, out the window, down at the town. Gray roofs under gray sky. Smoke rising thin from chimneys where people were cooking suppers they'd maybe not finish. She counted the chimneys. Eleven she could see, and she knew three more hid behind the fish sheds, and the Widow Sorrel's cottage sat apart by the dunes with its own sad little thread of smoke.

Fourteen chimneys. Forty-one souls. Five berths.

She was nine years old and she understood, suddenly and completely, what the army had done. They hadn't come to save anyone. They'd come to take the ledger and the boats and the harbor, and they'd thrown five seats off the back of the wagon like scraps, so everyone would fight over them instead of fighting the soldiers.

* * *

She didn't tell August right away. That was the part she'd think about later, turning it over in bed like a stone with a sharp edge. She could have gone straight up the stairs and said, *August, there's a spare berth, let's give it to the Widow Sorrel,* and he'd have put down whatever he was holding and listened, because he always listened to her even when she was wrong, especially when she was wrong.

Instead she kept it. She carried it around for two days like an egg in her pocket, warm and breakable, and she began to bargain.

It started small. Mrs. Petchy had said don't tell folk, and Tilly obeyed — mostly — but there were ways of talking that weren't telling. On the second morning she walked down to the harbor with a basket of dried cod Nell needed delivered to the Petchys', and while Mrs. Petchy counted the fish twice because counting was contagious apparently, Tilly said, casual as anything:

"If it came to it, and there was a seat, whose baby would you take?"

Mrs. Petchy's hands went still over the fish. "Whose baby," she repeated.

"If there was one. A spare. Whose would you carry aboard?"

"Tilly Crane." Mrs. Petchy set the basket down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "You tell me plain now. Is there a berth?"

"No," Tilly said, too fast.

"You've just asked me whose baby I'd save. That's not a question a girl asks if the answer's nothing."

Tilly looked at the floorboards. "I'm not saying there's one. I'm asking what you'd do if there was. It's a different thing."

"It is not a different thing at all." But Mrs. Petchy answered anyway, because of course she did, because the question had already gotten inside her the way it had gotten inside Tilly. "I'd take the Fenwick baby. Little Ada. She's not walking yet and her lungs are bad, and her mother's dead these two months. If it came to choosing — and God forgive us all that it's come to choosing — you take the one who can't run."

Tilly nodded like this was ordinary gossip and walked home with the empty basket banging against her knee, and inside her head the arithmetic was already rearranging itself. One berth. The Fenwick baby. But then what about Ada's father? He couldn't hand his daughter to strangers and stay. Or could he? Would he? And if Ada took the berth, then not the Widow Sorrel, and the Widow Sorrel was seventy-one and had buried everyone she'd ever loved and would die in this town alone whether or not a storm took her, except dying alone in a storm was different from dying alone in spring—

Stop, she told herself. Stop counting. But counting was the only tool she had, and the problem was made entirely of numbers, so she counted harder.

* * *

By the third day she'd spoken to six households without ever once saying the word *berth*. She'd invented a game, almost, a terrible game she played with herself in doorways and on the harbor steps: *if there were one, who?* She learned things she hadn't wanted to know.

She learned that Mr. Quill the cooper had a lame leg and would say *take my boys* before anyone finished asking, that he'd already rehearsed it, you could hear the rehearsal in how fast it came out. She learned that the Sorrel woman wouldn't go even if they carried her, that when Tilly brought her a pot of broth (an excuse, everything was an excuse now) the old lady looked at her with eyes like wet slate and said, "You're weighing me, child. I can feel it. Put me on the side of the scale that stays." She learned that the Danner family of five had heard rumors of the berths and had begun sleeping in their clothes, and that young Sam Danner, who was eleven and enormous, had started following the soldiers around doing odd jobs, trying to make himself essential. Trying to earn arithmetic.

And she learned — this on the fourth evening, from Nell, who said it quietly over mending without looking up — that the list wasn't even fixed. "Berths get traded," Nell said. "I worked in a shipping office, Tilly. Nothing with a number attached stays numbered. The corporal's cousin will want passage. An officer's mistress will appear from nowhere. Five berths tonight might be three by Friday."

Three. The word dropped through Tilly like a stone through water. Three berths, four living Cranes-and-company, and forty-one townsfolk, and a ghost named Martha taking up room on a government list because a grieving man couldn't bring himself to strike a line through his wife's name.

That night she climbed the stairs. All hundred and fourteen. She stood in the watch room where August was trimming the wick, the great lens turning above them slow as a planet, throwing its blade of light across the water and the rain.

"August," she said. "Your counting's wrong."

He turned. He had lamp oil on his fingers and tiredness all the way down to his boots. "Is it?"

"The register. It says six for our house. But Mama's not coming back." She made herself say it plainly. Grown-ups flinched from plainness; she'd learned that too. "You have to strike her out. Because the army gave us five berths for six names, and one of those names is a dead woman, and meanwhile the Widow Sorrel and the Fenwick baby and Mr. Quill's boys are all going to stand on that dock and watch a boat leave with an empty seat on it, an empty seat that belongs to nobody, that belongs to a ghost."

The lamp turned. Light swept across his face and away, across and away, like the world blinking.

"I know," he said finally.

"You know?"

"I read the requisition notice pinned at the harbor office. I've been standing in front of it an hour a day hoping the numbers change." He wiped his hands slowly on a rag. "I haven't struck her out because striking her out felt like killing her twice. That's the truth of it, Tilly. It's selfishness, not grief. Or both wearing each other's coats."

Tilly had expected him to argue. She'd prepared arguments. Their absence left her strangely light and strangely lost, like a stair missing in the dark. "Then strike her out tomorrow," she said. "Because Nell says the number's only going down, and every day we keep a ghost's seat is a day a living person loses their place in line."

August looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight his face was all angles and shadow, and she saw something move through it that she'd learn later to call shame.

"When did you become the accountant of this family?" he asked, and tried to smile.

"Somebody has to count," Tilly said. "Nobody else is doing it. Everybody else is just hoping."

"That's the most frightening sentence you've ever said to me." He crouched to her height, oil rag still in hand. "Listen. Tomorrow I go to the harbor office and correct the register. Tonight—" he hesitated, and she watched him decide to tell her the truth, watched him choose it, which was the thing she loved about him best even when she hated him, "—tonight, Captain Vane comes to inspect the light. Officially."

"Vane." The name again. It had been around the house lately like weather. "Mama's brother. The one who hates you."

"He doesn't hate me. That would be simpler." August stood, turned back to the lens, to the work. "Go down and help Nell with supper. And Tilly — whatever you've been doing in the town this week, all your quiet questions — stop. People remember kindness longer than they remember orders, but they remember desperation longest of all. If the wrong person learns there was a spare berth and hears your name near it..."

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Tilly went down the hundred and fourteen stairs carrying the whole cold weight of it: five berths, forty-two souls if you struck the ghost, and somewhere below in the gathering dark a captain climbing toward their door with a list in his pocket that decided who breathed next spring and who fed the fishes.

At the bottom, Nell was setting bowls, and Bram sat by the fire mending a net with hands that had tied ten thousand knots and were, Tilly noticed for the first time, trembling slightly.

She counted the bowls. Four. She counted the chairs. Four. She counted the people who would sleep under this roof tonight, and then she counted the berths, and then she counted the days until the boat, and the numbers would not sit still, none of them, they kept shifting under her fingers like fish.

Outside, the wind changed direction and began to come off the sea with a sound she hadn't heard before, low and patient, like something enormous clearing its throat.
