## 6. Bram's Confession

The storm glass had begun to fall.

Bram Hollis felt it in his hands before he read it in the instrument — a dull ache in the knuckles that had nothing to do with age and everything to do with the sea changing its mind forty miles out. He'd carried that ache through forty years of weather, and it had never once lied to him.

He said nothing about it at supper. August had enough weight on him already; you could see it in the set of his shoulders when he passed the bread, the way his eyes went to the window every time the wind gusted. Tilly chattered about the berths — five for six, she kept saying it like a sum she couldn't make come right — and Nell smiled at all the wrong moments, her spoon moving in circles in a bowl she wasn't eating from.

Bram ate his stew and listened to the wind and did his arithmetic too, and his arithmetic was older and uglier than anyone else's at that table.

Because he knew what an evacuation looked like. He'd seen one done badly once. Done by the book, even — which was worse.

* * *

He left the cottage after the dishes were cleared, the way he always did, taking his pipe down to the shingle below the seawall where the town wouldn't see him and he wouldn't see the town. But tonight he didn't stop at the shingle. Tonight he walked on, past the boat shed with its impounded fleet sitting dry and useless on their cradles, past the requisition house with its new lantern burning in the upstairs window, until he stood at the foot of the lighthouse stair with his heart going like a drum.

August opened the door before he knocked. He always did. Forty years a sailor teaches you to hear footsteps on stone.

"Couldn't sleep," Bram said, which was true, though not the whole truth, and they both knew it.

They climbed together to the watch room, where the lamp turned above them with its slow patient sweep, and August poured two fingers of the rough whisky he kept against cold without being asked, and Bram sat down on the old chart chest that smelled of tar and thirty years of August's tobacco, and for a long while neither man said anything at all. That was the thing about August Crane. He could wait out a silence like waiting out weather. He never tried to fill it. He just let it blow itself empty.

Bram drank. The lamp swept. Somewhere below, the first real gusts of the vanguard storm came off the water and leaned against the tower like a drunk leaning on a post.

"I served under your brother-in-law," Bram said finally. "Ellery Vane."

August's face didn't change. But his hand stopped halfway to his glass, and Bram watched him set it down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Go on."

"Gibraltar station. Eighteen years back. I was master's mate on a transport — the *Providence*. Vane was a lieutenant then, junior, but ambitious the way a fire is hot." Bram turned the glass in his fingers. "There was a fever ship came into the roads. Plague aboard, or near enough that London panicked. Orders came down to evacuate the civilian quarter across the harbor before the quarantine line closed. Twelve hundred souls. Fishing families, mostly. Moors, Spaniards, a colony of English factors who'd married local women and weren't welcome either side of the water anymore."

The lamp swept. The shadow of the mullions crawled across the floorboards and away again.

"We had four boats," Bram said. "Four. For twelve hundred people. And the order said the evacuation would be completed by dawn because the quarantine squadron was sailing at slack water and the transport was wanted elsewhere." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all, just an old rotten taste coming up. "Wanted elsewhere. You know what that means. Somebody's career was waiting somewhere more convenient."

"And Vane?"

"Vane read the orders aloud on the quay. Every word. I'll grant him that — he didn't soften them, didn't pretend they were anything but what they were. Then he organized us to carry them out. Loading priority lists. Women and children first, then the infirm, then men of working age. He stood on the quay with a ledger—" Bram's hand shook, just once, and he steadied it against his knee "—a great black ledger, and he ticked names off as they boarded, and when the tide turned and the last boat pulled away, he closed the book and reported the operation complete. Evacuation effected per instructions. Ninety percent compliance."

"Ninety percent," August said quietly.

"A hundred and eighty souls left standing on that quay when we pulled out. A hundred and eighty who didn't fit the arithmetic. And the fever took the quarter two days later, all of them, to a soul — except that's not even the worst of it." Bram drained the glass and stared into it. "The worst is that some of them didn't wait to die of fever. When the boats were fifty yards out, they started going into the water. Swimming after us. Grabbing the oars. We had to — " His voice cracked like green wood. "I put my boot on a woman's fingers. To make her let go. She'd have swamped the boat otherwise. Thirty souls drowned that night, August, and half of them drowned reaching for the gunwales of the boats that were supposed to save them."

* * *

The lamp turned. The wind rose a note.

"Why are you telling me this now?" August asked. Not unkindly. Just wanting the shape of it.

"Because I deserted three days after." Bram set the empty glass down on the chart chest with exaggerated care, as if his hands might betray him if he didn't give them a task. "Slipped over the side at Algiers with my kit and my pay and never signed another article under the Crown. Forty years I've been running small boats and keeping my mouth shut and telling myself the desertion was the shameful part. The coward's part. Running from the uniform instead of doing something inside it." He shook his head. "It took me till this week to understand the desertion wasn't the shame. The shame was the night I stayed. The night I rowed away on orders and called it duty."

"You were following orders."

"Aye." Bram looked up, and met August's eyes straight, because he owed him that much and more. "So is Ellery Vane. That's what I've been chewing on since the cutter dropped anchor. Everyone in this town looks at those red coats and sees tyrants. I look at them and I see men reading from a book somebody else wrote. Vane isn't evil, August. He's worse in a way — he's obedient. He'll tick the names, close the ledger, report ninety percent compliance, and sleep sound as a child, because the orders were followed and the record is clean. And when this is done, when the storms have taken whatever the army leaves behind — he'll write his report, and it will be accurate, and it will be damning, and it will change nothing, because nobody reads the dead pages of an orderly report and weeps."

August was quiet a long time. Out past the gallery, the sea had gone the color of pewter, with white horses beginning to run.

"You think this town ends on a quay," he said.

"I think I've seen this play before, and I know how the second act goes. First they take the boats." Bram nodded toward the harbor, where the impounded fleet sat dark on their cradles. "Then the ledger — every name, every debt, every loyalty, so they know exactly who matters and who doesn't. Then the berths, counted fine as flour, and a loading list with priorities. And then one morning the tide turns and the last boat pulls away and there are people still standing on the shingle, and the officer closes his book and reports the operation complete." He tapped the chart chest with one knuckle. "Vane will do it exactly the same way, because it worked last time. Because it's in the manual now. Because a hundred and eighty souls is an acceptable remainder when the alternative is inconveniencing a squadron."

"Tilly says five berths for six."

"Tilly counts better than most admirals." Bram almost smiled, and it hurt his face. "That girl does sums in her head the way I used to do soundings. She'll have the whole town's odds calculated by Friday, God help her, and she'll hate herself for knowing."

* * *

They sat with it. The whisky bottle stood between them, untouched now, and above their heads the great lens turned and turned, sweeping its beam out over the water where the storm was building in the dark.

"I'm telling you this," Bram said at last, "because you deserve to know what kind of man you've got living under your roof. A deserter. A man with a woman's blood on his boot leather, forty years old and fresh as yesterday. If the army ever digs into my papers — and they will dig, they're digging into everyone's — they'll find it. And they'll use it. They'll use it against you, against Nell and her papers, against the girl. I'm a liability in this house, August. Always have been, maybe. Never more than now."

"No," August said.

Bram blinked. "No?"

"You're not a liability." August leaned forward, elbows on knees, and in the rotating light his face went from bright to dark and back again, like a man standing in a doorway between rooms. "You're the only man on this coast who knows what's coming from the inside. Everyone else sees soldiers. You see the machinery. Do you know what that's worth?"

"It's worth nothing if I just sit on it."

"So don't sit on it." August held his gaze. "Tell me everything. Every detail of that night. How the loading was run, how the priorities were set, where the failures came — because if Vane runs this the same way, and you say he will, then every mistake he made at Gibraltar is a crack we can use. Where did the arithmetic break? What did he count wrong? What would he count wrong again?"

Bram stared at him. Forty years of carrying a thing alone, and here was this quiet man asking him to open it up and lay it on the table — not to be forgiven, but to be *used*. It was such a different ask than absolution that it took him a moment to understand it had been made at all.

"The tide," Bram said slowly, and the words came easier than any words had come in years. "The tide broke the schedule. That was the crack. The whole operation was built to a timetable that ignored the water, because the timetable came from London and London doesn't own the tide. They loaded to the list instead of loading to the weather, and when the tide turned early there were boats on the quay with room in them and no time to fill them. If they'd loaded to the sea instead of the paper—"

"They'd have saved the hundred and eighty," August finished.

"Some of them. Enough to matter." Bram felt something shift in his chest, some load settling into a new balance, and realized his hands had stopped aching. "And Vane never learned it. That's the beauty and the curse of a man like that — he believes the paper. He'll build his timetable for this evacuation off the same ledgers, the same priorities, the same faith in lists over weather, and the sea will break his schedule again, because the sea always does. That's our crack, August. That's where we get in."

"Get in and do what?"

Bram looked at the lamp turning above them, at the steady gold blade sweeping the dark, and thought of a black ledger on a trestle table with every name in the town written fair and square, debts and loyalties and secrets laid out for a man who believed in lists.

"I don't know yet," he admitted. "But I know we can't wait for his schedule to run. Whatever this household decides to do — about the berths, about the ledger, about Nell's name in it — it has to happen before the loading starts. Once the boats are in the water and the list is ticking, it's Gibraltar all over again, and none of us will be able to stop it. Before, August. Everything happens *before*."

August nodded slowly, and Bram saw the same reckoning move behind his eyes that moved behind the lens overhead — patient, turning, sweeping the dark for whatever was out there.

Down in the harbor, a lantern flickered in the requisition house window, and a door opened, and lamplight spilled onto the street where a figure in a red coat stood a long moment looking out at the tower on the point — looking, Bram would have sworn, straight up at the light, and at the two men inside it.

"He knows the house has ears," Bram murmured. "Question is whether he knows whose."

August rose and trimmed the wick, unhurried, and said only: "Then we'd best decide what we're going to be caught doing — before someone decides for us."
