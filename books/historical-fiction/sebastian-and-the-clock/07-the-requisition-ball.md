## 7. The Requisition Ball

The invitation arrived on the morning tide, delivered by a private so young his chin had not yet decided on a beard. He stood at the foot of the lighthouse stairs with a card in a gloved hand and would not come up, though rain spat sideways off the harbor.

"Compliments of Captain Vane," the boy said. "Officers' reception. Tomorrow evening. Requisition House."

August took the card without touching the boy's glove. Heavy paper, cream-colored, printed in London with a flourish of engraved scrolls — *A Reception Given by the Officers of His Majesty's Forces, to Which the Principal Inhabitants of Harrow Point Are Respectfully Invited.* The word *respectfully* sat there like a coin placed on a dead man's eye.

"The principal inhabitants," August said.

"Yes, sir." The boy shifted. "It's the ball, sir."

"There hasn't been a ball at Harrow Point since before the war with France."

"No, sir." A pause. "There's to be music. From the cutter's band."

August looked past him at the gray water, where three transports now lay at anchor in a line like a fence being built across the harbor mouth. "Tell the captain I'll consider it."

"He said you'd say that, sir. He said to tell you the principal inhabitants are expected." The boy flushed. "Expected, sir."

So it was an order dressed as an invitation. August turned the card over. On the back, in ink still sharp enough to bite the page, someone had written in a small precise hand: *Come, August. We should speak as family, if we can manage it. E.V.*

He read it four times. Then he put the card in his coat pocket, where it lay against his chest all day like a splinter working toward the heart.

* * *

Nell found him at dusk on the gallery, watching the light swing its long arm out over the water and pull it back again.

"You're going," she said. It was not a question.

"I'm going."

"They'll have the ledger there. It was carried up from the customs house this afternoon under guard — Bram saw it. Two soldiers, locked case, straight into Vane's own quarters." She pulled her shawl tighter against the wind. "That's why they're holding a ball. Not for dancing. For display. They want the town to see the book sitting on its table like a judge."

"Then they'll get what they want," August said. "A room full of frightened people looking at a book."

"And what do you want?"

He thought about lying to her and did not bother; she'd have smelled it through the wind. "I want to look Ellery Vane in the face in front of forty witnesses and ask him how many berths he means to leave empty. I want him to say the number out loud. Numbers are different spoken aloud. Written down, five is ink. Spoken, it's five families standing in the rain."

Nell was quiet a while. Below them Tilly's voice rose from the kitchen, teaching the gull — she had named it Admiral — to say *supper*, which the bird refused on principle but rewarded with screams anyway.

"She can't come with you," Nell said finally. "Neither can Bram. You understand that. If you walk into that room with your household behind you, they'll count us. They're already counting us."

"I know."

"And if Vane offers you something in front of everyone —"

"He won't."

"He might. Men like that offer things in public precisely because refusing in public costs more than accepting." She touched his sleeve, brief and hard, like tying off a knot. "Whatever he puts in front of you, August — don't take it while he's watching. Take nothing while he's watching. Promise me."

He promised. He was already wondering, even then, what he would do when the moment came.

* * *

Requisition House had been the customs master's home once, back when Harrow Point had a customs master and enough shipping to custom. Now the army had hung lanterns along its front until the old stone house glowed like something stranded, a whale lit up on a beach. Inside, the ballroom — which had been, in living memory, a wool store and before that a chapel for whichever congregation had most recently lost its argument with the sea — blazed with candles and rang with the cutter's band playing a quadrille no one danced.

They came because they were told to come. August watched them from the doorway: the fisherfolk scrubbed raw, their good clothes smelling of camphor chests; the shopkeepers standing in knots near the wine table, drinking little, talking less; Mrs. Quill the schoolmistress in a dress twenty years out of fashion, holding her spine straight as a mast. Forty souls. Perhaps fifty. Every one of them carrying the same arithmetic behind their eyes, and every one of them pretending otherwise.

Soldiers lined the walls in dress uniform. The effect was of a room politely held prisoner.

"Mr. Crane."

Vane materialized at his shoulder the way officers did, as if rooms arranged themselves around them. He wore full dress, sword and sash, and looked, August thought, like a portrait of someone — correct, composed, and entirely elsewhere.

"Captain." August inclined his head exactly far enough. "You've gone to great expense."

"The Crown goes to great expense wherever it pleases." Vane offered a thin smile. "You came. I wasn't certain."

"You wrote *as family*."

"And here we are, two men who share a grave." Something moved behind Vane's eyes and was gone. "Dance with no one, drink with no one, and stay away from the east table. Those are my only instructions. Enjoy yourself."

He moved off into the crowd, and August saw the crowd part around him the way water parts around a keel — not from love, but from the certainty that arguing with his wake was pointless.

August stayed away from the east table for one hour by the mantel clock.

In that hour he learned more than any speech would have taught him. He learned that the wine was French and plentiful while the town's boats rotted impounded in the harbor. He learned that a lieutenant named Corbeck had been telling anyone who'd listen that the evacuation list was *final, absolutely final, drawn up in London itself* — which meant it was not final, because lists drawn up in London were never final until the last boat left. He learned that Mrs. Sorrell, the widow with the dying husband upstairs in her house on Rope Walk, had asked three different officers about berths and been answered three times with the same gentle sentence: *All in good time, madam.* He learned that good time was a currency the army minted freely and never honored.

And he learned, moving through the crowd with his hat in his hand like any other principal inhabitant, that people had begun to look at him. Not the way they'd always looked — the keeper, half a step outside the town, married to a woman from away. This was newer and heavier. *Crane will fix it. Crane knows him. Crane is family.*

He wanted to tell them: I haven't spoken to that man above a dozen words in nine years, and the last ones were shouted. He wanted to tell them: grief doesn't purchase berths. But Mrs. Quill caught his eye across the room and lifted her chin slightly, the way she did when a pupil gave a right answer, and he understood that hope, once assigned, could not be handed back.

* * *

At half past ten, Captain Ellery Vane mounted the small dais at the head of the room and asked the band to stop.

"Ladies and gentlemen of Harrow Point." His voice carried effortlessly, trained to cross gun decks. "I will be brief, because I know you did not come for speeches."

A ripple that was almost laughter went through the officers. None of it reached the townspeople.

"In three days' time — perhaps sooner, if the weather masters us as it intends to — His Majesty's ship *Providence* will stand into this harbor to evacuate designated persons ahead of the great storm. Five berths have been allotted to the household of the lighthouse keeper. Other allotments have been communicated privately. I regret —" and here he paused, and August watched him pause, watched the machinery of it, the officer choosing the exact length of silence — "I regret deeply that the Crown's capacity does not match this town's worth. More than that I am not authorized to say."

Mrs. Quill's voice cut the room like a gull's cry. "How many, Captain? How many berths in all?"

Vane looked at her with something that might, in a better man, have been pain. "Madam."

"Numbers, Captain. You're fond of ledgers. Give us the number."

Silence. The candles hissed. Somewhere in the walls the old house settled on its stones.

"One hundred and twelve," Vane said.

August heard it land. Not all at once — in pieces, in the small sounds a crowd makes when arithmetic finishes its work. Someone near the windows began quietly to weep. Old Tam Weller, seventy if he was a day, said in a clear voice, "There's four hundred souls in Harrow Point," and no officer corrected him, and that was the worst of it: no correction, no denial, just the number hanging there, 112 against 400, and the band's first violinist looking anywhere else.

Then August stepped forward.

Later he could not fully account for it. He had promised Nell he would ask nothing publicly. But the number was spoken now, spoken aloud, and it seemed to him that a man who shared a grave with these people could not stand silent beside it.

"Captain Vane." His voice sounded strange to him, rusted from disuse in rooms like this. "I'm given to understand the list of designated persons is not yet closed. That amendments may still be made."

Every eye in the room swung between them like a lamp beam.

"That is so," Vane said carefully.

"Then I ask you, before this town — amend it. Open the transport holds. Rig extra decks. There are ships enough riding at anchor out there to carry every soul in Harrow Point to safety, and you know it as well as I do. The Crown found money for the wine tonight. Let it find money for the boats."

"Mr. Crane." Vane descended one step from the dais, and his voice dropped into a register meant for him alone, though the whole room strained after it. "You presume on an old connection."

"I presume on nothing. I plead. There's a difference, and the difference is that pleading costs the pleader everything and presumes nothing in return." August felt the heat rise in his own face and did not fight it. "My wife is buried on your headland, Captain. Half this town dug the grave. And now you stand in a lit room with wine and music and tell four hundred people that one hundred and twelve of them will live. I am asking you — in front of witnesses, so that neither of us can later pretend otherwise — to make the number larger."

For a moment — one moment, no longer — something broke the surface in Vane's face, some drowned thing rising toward the candlelight. August thought: *there. There he is. Marguerite's brother. The boy who cried at her wedding.*

Then it sank.

"The allotments," Vane said, loud enough for the room, "are fixed. By order of the Admiralty and the War Office jointly. I am their servant, Mr. Crane, nothing more." He turned to the assembled town, and his voice became pure ceremony. "You have my word that every designation will be made with care and justice. Good night to you all."

The band struck up again, horribly cheerful. The crowd broke apart like a wave against a wall.

* * *

August did not remember leaving the dais side of the room. He remembered the wine table, and a glass in his hand he had not poured, and Mrs. Quill beside him saying, "You tried, Mr. Crane," and meaning it kindly and making it worse.

He remembered the doors opening at midnight to let in the latecomers — a last few stragglers summoned by second messenger — and the wind that came in with them, wet and heavy, tasting of the storm still two days out at sea. He remembered the candles guttering in the draft and an adjutant ordering the doors closed, closed, *closed*.

And then he remembered turning, aimlessly, having nowhere in the world to go except home — and seeing the east table.

During the speeches it had stood guarded. Now the guards had been drawn off to manage the doors, the crowd had thinned, and the trestle table in the alcove beyond the potted palms stood bare except for a single object: a leather-bound book, large as a Bible, its brass clasps open, its pages fanned slightly as if breathing.

The shipping ledger of Harrow Point.

No one stood within six feet of it. The nearest officer had his back turned, laughing at something. The candles nearest the alcove had burned down to stubs, and the shadow of the palms fell across the table in stripes, so that the book lay half in light and half in dark, like a thing deciding what it was.

August crossed the floor. He did not decide to; his legs decided, and his conscience ran behind shouting promises he'd made to Nell about taking nothing while Vane was watching. Vane was not watching. Vane stood at the head of the room receiving the farewell courtesies of Mrs. Quill with a bowed head, a man performing humility.

Ten feet. Six. Three.

Close enough to see that the ledger was not one book but many sewn together — decades of them, the customs house memory of the whole coast, every cargo, every debt, every name ever entered at Harrow Point. Close enough to see that pages had been marked with ribbon in a dozen places, and that one of the ribbons, green, sat in a section whose header read *Persons Resident — Irregular Entries.* Nell's country. Her forged papers lived somewhere behind that green ribbon, and a clerk with orders and an hour alone could hang her for it.

Two feet.

The book smelled of dust and salt and other people's lives. It weighed, he thought absurdly, like a child. It would weigh like that all the way home.

"Mr. Crane?"

He stepped back from the table as if it had burned him. Lieutenant Corbeck stood at his elbow, polite, watchful, a glass of wine in each hand.

" admiring the archive," Corbeck said. "Remarkable record-keeping, our predecessors. Every soul in this coast accounted for." He smiled. "Every soul accounted for. That must be a comfort, in a place like this."

"It's a book," August said. His voice was steady. He was almost proud of that.

"It's the town, Mr. Crane. In ink." Corbeck raised his glass a fraction. "Good night to you. Mind the wind coming home — it has teeth tonight."

August walked out of Requisition House into the dark, and the wind did have teeth, and he barely felt them. Behind him the candles blazed, the band played, the guards returned to their posts around a table that was, he now knew, unguarded more often than any soldier would admit — a table watched by men who believed no fisherman would dare.

His hands would not stop shaking, and it was not the cold. It was the weight his arms remembered from two feet away, the ghost-weight of a thing not yet stolen, already heavy as a child, already heavy as a town.

He had gone to the ball to beg. He walked home knowing begging was finished.

There was another way to move a ledger.
