## 8. Nell's Name

The requisition house had been the harbormaster's office once, back when the harbor had a master and not a garrison. Now its windows were papered over with army canvas and a sentry stood at the door with a rifle he kept meaning to lean against the wall and never quite dared to. Nell watched it from the chandlery across the lane, where she'd gone ostensibly for lamp oil and actually because from behind the counter of Wick & Sons you could see every soul who came or went through that door.

She'd been watching for three days.

Her name was in there. Not *Nell* — never Nell — but the name underneath it, the one she'd buried in London six years ago along with her old handwriting, her old accent, and a man named Farrow who had taught her both forgery and regret. The ledger held the shipping records of every vessel that had called at this coast for forty years, and somewhere in its pages, entered in a clerk's careful hand on a wet October morning in 1853, was the record of a young woman who signed herself *Eleanor Voss* and booked passage north with two trunks that did not belong to her.

Eleanor Voss did not exist anymore. Nell Quill, runaway clerk, existed instead — existed in August Crane's kitchen, at Tilly's elbow, mending Bram's coat. But paper did not care what a person became. Paper remembered everything. That was the whole reason she loved it and the whole reason it terrified her now.

"You're going to wear a groove in my floor," said Wick, who was eighty and mostly deaf and pretending to be both so he could eavesdrop without admitting it.

"I'm shopping."

"You've picked up the same tin of oil four times." He didn't look up from his ledgers — honest ones, the poor man. "Whatever's across the lane, it'll still be there tomorrow. That's the trouble with soldiers. They breed."

She put down the oil. She bought it, because leaving without buying would look like something, and paid too much, and went out into the gray morning with her heart going like a drum in a parade.

* * *

It happened on the fourth day.

She was at the lighthouse, doing sums for August — the household accounts, what they could lay in before the storms sealed the roads — when Bram came up the tower stairs two at a time, which for Bram meant one at a time with great determination.

"They're reading names," he said.

August looked up from the wick-trimmer. "What?"

"Down at the requisition house. A lieutenant and a corporal and that clerk fellow with the bad teeth. They've got the ledger open on the table and they're calling people in one at a time. Cross-referencing." Bram's face was the color of old rope. "Verifying residency, the notice says. Every name in the book gets read out and confirmed."

Nell's hands went very still on the page in front of her. She had been adding a column of shillings; the sum sat there unfinished, a small wrongness, like a clock stopped mid-tick.

"All of them?" she said. Her voice came out level. She'd learned to do that years ago, and she hated how easily it still worked.

"Every name." Bram wouldn't look at her directly, which was its own kind of message. Old sailor, old deserter, old enough to know what fear looked like when it arrived quietly in someone else. "There's a queue down the street. Fishwives. The ferryman. They're marking each one off as they're confirmed."

Confirmed against what, she wanted to ask, and already knew: against the descriptions in the army's own circulars, against the wanted lists that traveled with every requisition force, against whatever intelligence London had forwarded about persons of interest on this coast. And against each other — because all it would take was one neighbor, one frightened shopkeeper, one grudge with a memory, saying *that woman came here five years back and nobody knows from where.*

August set down the wick-trimmer with exaggerated care, the way he did when his hands wanted to shake and he refused to permit it. "Then we don't panic."

"I'm not panicking," Nell said.

"You've got that face you make when you're doing three things at once and none of them are adding shillings."

She looked down. The column waited. She finished it — eleven pounds, four and six — and found her voice had stayed level after all. "It's nothing. My papers are good. Better than good. I made them myself."

That was the truth and it was also the problem, and she watched August hear both halves of it and decide, kindly, to say only the first half aloud. "Good," he said. "Then there's nothing to do but carry on. Tilly will want supper early. She's been down at the point counting boats again."

Bram left. August went back to the lamp. And Nell sat in the watch room with her finished sum and thought about how many ways a single line of ink could kill a person.

* * *

She went down to the town that evening and saw for herself.

They'd set up a table under the awning where the fish auction used to run, and the clerk with the bad teeth sat behind it with the ledger open like a bible, and the lieutenant — young, bored, conscientious in the way that was most dangerous — called the names aloud one by one. Each person stepped forward. Each answered questions. Where born. When arrived. Occupation. Kin. And beside the clerk, the corporal turned pages in a leather folder of printed sheets, comparing, comparing, comparing.

Nell stood in the shadow of the net-drying shed and counted.

Forty-one names read. Thirty-eight passed through quickly, waved on with a chalk mark. Two were questioned longer — old Sorrel the smuggler, whose answers made even the corporal smile; and a widow named Petrie who wept and was eventually let go. One name, the thirty-ninth, produced a different result entirely.

"Josiah Crane," the lieutenant read. "Fisherman. Resident eleven years."

"No record of arrival," the corporal said. He turned a page. Turned another. "No kin listed prior to 1848. Sir, there's a flag here—"

The lieutenant sighed like a man tired of flags. "Bring him round the back."

They took Josiah Crane around the back of the shed, and the queue pretended not to watch, which is what queues do, and Nell slipped away through the alley behind Wick's with her pulse loud in her ears. Josiah Crane was a harmless man. Josiah Crane was exactly as guilty as she was — which is to say guilty of arriving somewhere and becoming someone new — and if they could pull *him* out of a line on a flag, then the moment they reached the E's, the V's, the entries from October 1853—

*Eleanor Voss. Passage north. Two trunks, contents undeclared.*

She hadn't stolen the trunks. That was the irony she could never explain to anyone who hadn't lived it. She'd carried them for Farrow, and Farrow had vanished at the dock, and the trunks had contained — well. It hardly mattered now. What mattered was that a clerk somewhere in London had written her description into a report, and reports bred copies, and copies ended up in leather folders carried by corporals with nothing to do but compare.

Back at the lighthouse, supper was on the table and Tilly was talking about the boats again — how many were impounded, how many remained, whether the *Meridian* would come — and Bram was telling her to eat her greens, and August was listening to all of it with that still attention that made you feel heard even when he said nothing. Nell sat among them and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was sitting inside the only home she had ever built on purpose, and that she was the reason it might burn.

She'd done the arithmetic before bed, in the little room under the eaves where she kept her real papers sewn into the ticking of the mattress. Five berths, six souls — no, that was the household's arithmetic, and tonight hers was simpler. One fugitive. One ledger full of evidence. Forty more days until the great storm, less if the barometer kept falling, and every day a table under an awning where names were read aloud and compared against folders.

One of those names would be hers.

Unless there was no one left in the town for it to match.

* * *

She told them at breakfast, because lying to them had become harder than lying to anyone alive, and because a plan made alone was just a trap with better manners.

"I'm leaving," she said. "Tonight. Before they get to the V entries."

The kitchen went quiet the way a room goes quiet when something breaks. Tilly's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Bram studied his tea. August set down his cup.

"That's not happening," he said.

"It's the only sensible—"

"Nell." He said her name the way he trimmed wicks, carefully, refusing to let his hands hurry. "Sit down."

"I am sitting down."

"Then stay sitting and listen." He folded his hands. "You leave tonight, alone, inland, in weather that's about to eat the roads, with every soldier in the district looking for exactly one runaway clerk. You think that's safety? I've seen what happens to people alone on this coast in November. I light a lamp against it every night of my life."

"And I've seen what happens to households that shelter paper," she shot back. "You haven't. You don't know what it is, being wanted. You've never had to become a different person before breakfast." She heard her own voice climb and forced it down. "I'm sorry. But I've done this before. Disappearing is the one thing I'm good at."

"You're good at sums," Tilly said suddenly. Everyone looked at her. The girl's chin was up and her eyes were wet and furious. "You're good at sums and teaching me figures and fixing Bram's coat because he won't fix it himself. Disappearing isn't a thing you're *good* at, it's a thing that keeps *happening* to you, and it's not going to happen anymore, because—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway, twelve years old and brave as a mast in a gale— "because we're not letting it."

Nell looked around the table. At the child who had claimed her. At the old man who nodded slowly, once, like a verdict. At August, who said nothing, because he had already said it, and because he knew — she could see he knew — that words weren't what would hold her here or send her away. Circumstances would. Ink would.

"The berths," she said quietly. "If I go, there's no question about the last one. It's solved. Four of you, five berths, and I stop being a risk to everyone in this town the moment I'm over the ridge."

"And if they catch you on the road?" Bram said. "If they find your papers are forged — and they will, love, because you made them yourself and you'll tell me that's a virtue right up until a corporal holds them next to a candle — what then? Prison hulk. Transportation. Or worse, because a spy-shaped hole in their paperwork makes officers nervous, and nervous officers do arithmetic with hanging." He shook his head. "I deserted once to save my own skin and thirty men drowned learning what my cowardice cost. Don't ask me to bless yours."

"That's not fair," she whispered.

"No," he agreed. "It's not. None of it is."

August rose and began gathering the dishes, which was what he did when a conversation needed a body in motion. "No one decides anything today," he said. "The cross-checking will take days yet. They're at the C's." A pause, and then, gently, with the precision of a man placing a lamp exactly where it's needed: "Which means we have time. And time is the one thing worth more than any berth. We spend it together, not scattering it."

But that night Nell lay awake under the eaves with her fingers resting on the seam of the mattress tick, on the folded papers that carried her second self, and she did not believe in time. Time was what other people had. She had a head start and a window, and every hour she spent loving these people was an hour the ledger gained on her.

She began, silently, to pack.

* * *

It nearly didn't matter, in the end, because it almost happened first.

Two mornings later she went down for oil again — the same tin, the same counter, the same old man's knowing silence — and while she stood there, the queue outside the awning moved, and she heard the lieutenant's voice carry across the lane the way voices carry in cold air.

"Eleanor—" a pause, a squint at the faded entry, "—Voss. Passenger, October 1853. Passage north, two trunks. Present whereabouts unknown." He looked up at the corporal. "Is there a flag on this one?"

The world narrowed to a keyhole. Through it: the corporal turning pages. The rain starting, fine as sifted flour. Wick behind her, suddenly, deliberately loud, dropping a tin on the floor and cursing like a man half his age.

"Flag says *description enclosed*," the corporal said. He unfolded a printed sheet. Read. Looked up, scanning the queue, scanning the street, scanning —

Nell turned her face to the shelf of lamp oil and became a woman buying oil. Gray bonnet. Plain coat. Hands busy with a tin. Twenty feet of cold air between her and a man holding a piece of paper with her old life printed on it.

"Description's ten years stale," the corporal said at last. "Half the coast matches it. Mark it *unresolved* and move on."

Chalk squeaked. The next name was called. The queue shuffled.

Nell paid for her oil with steady hands — she was proud of her hands, later, the way you're proud of a rope that held — and walked home at an ordinary pace, and did not let herself shake until she was behind the closed door of the lighthouse with the bolt drawn, and then she shook so hard the tin rattled against the floor.

August found her there. He didn't ask what happened. He sat down on the step beside her, in the cold stairwell, and put his arm around her shoulders, and let her be afraid for as long as it took, which was a long time, long past the point where the lamp above them needed trimming and he ignored it, which he had never once done in his life.

When she could speak, she said: "Tomorrow. They'll be through the S's tomorrow, and then it's V, and next time there won't be an old man dropping tins. I have to go, August. Tonight. Please don't make it harder."

He was quiet a moment. Then he said, "Where would you even go?"

"Away. Inland. Anywhere the sea isn't."

"And when the storm comes down and the roads drown and there's no lamp anywhere to walk toward?" His voice wasn't angry. That was the worst part. It was tired, and fond, and absolutely immovable. "You'd trade a maybe-hanging for a certain freezing, and call it saving us. Nell. Listen to me. Nobody in this house survives alone. That's the whole lesson of this coast. That's why the light burns."

"Then the light burns for people who deserve it," she said, and pulled out from under his arm, and went up the stairs to pack in earnest, and this time he did not follow, and his silence followed her instead, all the way up, like a hand laid gently on the door.

She had the bundle tied by midnight. Papers, needle, thread, the spare coin sewn into her petticoat hem, one change of clothes rolled tight as a fuse. She sat on the bed with it in her lap and listened to the wind coming up out of the southwest, earlier than it should, stronger than it should, worrying at the tower like a dog at a bone.

Below her, faintly, she heard August come in from the gallery, and Bram's low voice, and then — unexpected — raised voices, urgent, and boots on stairs, and Bram appearing at her door with the wind still swirling in behind him and his face lit strange in the storm-lantern's glow.

"Nell," he said. "You'll want to hear this. There's a wagon at the gate — army supply, bound inland before the weather closes the pass — and there's a girl missing from her bed, and a berth unclaimed, and August wants every soul in this house awake *now*."

The bundle slid from her lap to the floor, forgotten.

Tilly was gone.
