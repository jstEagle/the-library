## 9. Storm Glass Falling

The barometer began to fall on a Tuesday, and it fell the way a man falls from a mast — not all at once, but with that terrible gathering certainty once the hands have lost their grip.

August Crane had kept instruments long enough to read them like faces. The mercurial glass in the watch room had been steady at thirty inches for most of the month, sulking a little at dawn, recovering by noon. But Tuesday morning it stood at twenty-nine and six-tenths, and by the time he climbed down to fetch bread from Nell it read twenty-nine and two-tenths, and by supper — when Tilly was still pretending she hadn't heard about the berths, and Bram was carving a gull out of scrap pine with a knife too dull for the work and too stubborn to be told so — the needle had slid below twenty-nine and was still traveling.

He tapped the case twice, out of habit, as if the instrument might be lying.

It wasn't.

"Wind's backing," Bram said without looking up. He'd been saying it for three days, each time with less of the old sailor's relish and more of the old sailor's gravity, which was worse. "Southeast now. Come round to northeast by morning, you'll know."

"Know what?"

Bram set his knife down. "You'll know."

Outside, the light turned its slow white wheel over a sea that had gone the color of wet slate. There were no stars. There had been no stars for two nights, and the horizon had stopped being a line and become a suggestion, a rumor the water told itself. August stood in the gallery with his collar up and listened to the swell arrive — longer between waves than yesterday, deeper in its voice. A groundswell. It came from far off, from some cathedral of weather out past the shipping lanes, rolling its message ahead of itself the way armies sent drummers.

He thought: three days.

He thought: maybe two.

* * *

The vanguard arrived Wednesday night, and it did not come politely.

The first gust hit the tower at half past nine like a fist against a door — one blow, hard enough to make the lamp flicker in its glass — and then the wind found its rhythm and settled into the long work of unmaking things. August heard the shingles go off the harbormaster's roof in sheets, a sound like cards being torn. He heard the church bell ring once, unassisted, and never again; something had knocked it crooked or carried the clapper off into the dark. Down in the town, dogs began to bark and could not stop, and the sea came up the low streets at the top of the tide until the lanes nearest the quay ran brown and foaming, and men waded to their neighbors' doors in water to their knees to carry out what could be carried.

What could be carried. August's whole life had been an education in that arithmetic, and now the storm was teaching it to everyone at once.

Tilly came up the tower stairs just after midnight, soaked to the shoulders, her hair plastered flat, her eyes enormous. She'd been down helping the Pells move their grandmother's chest to higher ground, and she stood in the doorway dripping and would not come fully in, as if she'd forgotten she lived here.

"You should have stayed," August said.

"I'm strong." Her chin went up. "I carried the chest end."

"I know you're strong." He took the blanket off the watch room chair and put it around her, kneeling to do it, so that they were eye to eye. "That's why I want you where I can see you. Strong is wasted where I can't see it."

She looked at him for a moment, and he saw her decide not to argue — saw, too, the other thing underneath, the thing she'd been carrying since the berths were posted, the arithmetic she did behind her eyes whenever she thought no one watched. Five berths. Six of them. A child doing sums no child should have to do, and arriving every time at her own name as the remainder.

"Tilly," he said.

"It's coming harder, isn't it." Not a question. She'd learned to hear weather the way he'd taught her, out on the gallery rail with her hand cupped behind her ear. "The real one."

"Yes."

"How long?"

He looked at the glass. Twenty-eight and seven. "Less than we thought."

* * *

Thursday morning the army cutter fired a gun.

Not a warning shot — a signal gun, three reports in quick succession, and then a launch pulled for the quay through water the color of dishwater, and by ten o'clock there were soldiers in the market square nailing a fresh notice to the requisition board while the wind tried to tear it out of their hands. August went down because everyone went down, the whole town funneling into the square like water finding a drain, hoods up, coats streaming, and there he read the order over the shoulders of the fishmonger's sons.

EVACUATION ADVANCED. THE TRANSPORT MERIDIAN WILL RECEIVE PERSONS ENTITLED TO BERTHS ON SATURDAY'S MORNING TIDE, WEATHER PERMITTING. ALL OTHER VESSELS AND PERSONS REMAIN SUBJECT TO EXISTING ORDERS. BY DIRECTION OF CAPTAIN E. VANE, H.M. FORCES.

Saturday. Three days gone. What had been a week was now a night and a day, and the crowd understood it all at once, the way crowds do — a single groan moving through them like the groundswell through the moorings, and then the noise breaking apart into voices, questions, a woman beginning to cry near the fish stall and not troubling to hide it.

"They can't," said old Pell, who had been saying they can't since the army landed, and whose saying-so had never yet changed anything. "The sea won't permit it. No ship loads in this."

"The Meridian will try," said Nell beside him. She had her hood down despite the rain, her clerk's face pale and set, reading the notice a second time as though the words might rearrange themselves if given another chance. "They'll load her at anchor with whatever boats are left, and God help anyone who misses their tide." Her voice dropped. "August. Saturday. That's—"

"I know what that is."

Nell's forged papers sat in the ledger in the requisition house, in the pages under E, under a name that was not hers but fit her like a glove fitted by a good craftsman. Every day the ledger remained in army hands was a day the cross-referencing crept closer to her. And now the days themselves were being taken away, counted down not in weeks but in tides.

"We need to talk," she said quietly. "All of us. Tonight."

"Not tonight," August said. "Tonight I stand my watch. Tomorrow. Tomorrow we'll talk with clear heads."

She gave him a look — the look of a woman who had spent years keeping books for men who always believed tomorrow would hold still for them — but she only said, "Tomorrow, then," and pulled her hood up, and the crowd broke apart around them like spray.

* * *

The storm's vanguard did not leave. It settled in.

Friday was a day of gray violence, the wind working around to the northeast as Bram had promised, and the rain coming horizontal now, stinging, driving so hard that standing on the gallery felt like standing in a river running sideways. The seas built through the afternoon until the breakers on the shoal were throwing spray a hundred feet up, white geysers visible even through the murk, and the harbor became a thing August barely recognized — the moored boats plunging at their impounded anchors, the quay awash, the low streets gone entirely, the town reduced to its high ground and its stone buildings and the people crowded inside them listening to the world try to tear its seams.

At four o'clock the supply wagon came up the cliff road to the lighthouse, horses fighting the traces, and a lieutenant shouted up through the rain that Captain Vane required the keeper's attendance at the requisition house at eight that evening regarding final arrangements, and that the captain said — the lieutenant hesitated, clearly repeating language he didn't understand — "the captain said to tell you the tide waits for no grief."

August stood in the doorway with the wind trying to take the door off its hinges and considered the sentence. The tide waits for no grief. Ellery's phrasing, or Ellery quoting someone else's orders in his own bitter grammar. Either way it meant: hurry. Either way it meant the machinery above his brother-in-law was grinding faster than any man inside it wanted.

"Tell him I'll come," August called back, and the wagon ground away downhill, and he closed the door on the roar.

Inside, the household was waiting in the kitchen, and he saw at once that they had already talked without him. Nell sat with her arms folded, composed the way ice is composed. Bram had stopped carving entirely, which frightened August more than the barometer. And Tilly —

Tilly was watching him with an expression he couldn't read, and that frightened him most of all.

"Well?" Nell said.

"Saturday morning tide. Loading at anchor. Berth-holders only." He hung his coat, and water ran off it onto the stones, and for a moment none of them spoke over the sound of the storm leaning on the house.

"Then it's tomorrow night or never," Nell said. "For the ledger. While every soldier they have is busy keeping the quay from washing away, there'll be a skeleton guard in that house and no lamps lit because the shutters are blown in — I walked past at noon, August, the whole seaward face is open to the weather, they've moved everything inland to the dry rooms, and the records table—" She caught herself, glanced at Tilly, and lowered her voice anyway. "The records will be stacked, not filed. Waiting for transport. Waiting to go aboard."

"And if you're caught," August said, "in wartime requisition, tampering with official documents—"

"If I'm caught, I'm caught alone, and I'll say I acted alone, and every word of that will be true if we plan it right." Her chin was up. It was the same chin as Tilly's, he realized, though they shared no blood at all. The household chin. "I've been a coward with my own name for six years, August. I won't be a coward with everyone else's."

"She'll not go alone," Bram rumbled. "I know that house. I helped build the powder magazine at Portsmouth that's built the same way, and I'll tell you where the guards stand when the wind's in the northeast, because I've stood there myself." His big hands lay flat on the table, and August saw they were trembling, and saw also that it wasn't age. It was purpose. The old man had been waiting thirty years for a tide he could turn against the tide that had drowned his thirty souls, and here it was, rising up his own street.

"No one decides anything tonight," August said. "I have to meet Ellery at eight. Whatever he says there changes what we do tomorrow, and I'll not have us committed blind." He looked around the table — the clerk, the deserter, the orphan — his household, his true ledger, the entries he'd have chosen over any fortune. "Keep the fire. Keep each other. I'll be back before midnight."

Tilly followed him to the door. She put her hand on his sleeve, and her grip surprised him — a small hand with a strong man's hold.

"August." Her voice was very quiet under the storm. "If he offers you a berth tomorrow — your name's on the essential list, isn't it? The keeper's always on the list. Everyone knows the light has to have a keeper till the last minute, and then they take him off." Her eyes searched his face. "Promise me you won't give it away. Promise me you won't be clever."

He knelt, as he had the other night, and took her shoulders.

"I promise you nothing gets decided without me looking at you first," he said. "That's the promise I can keep. The other one — Tilly, listen to me — the other one depends on how many names fit in how many places, and that arithmetic belongs to all of us, not just me."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

She held his gaze a long moment, a child refusing to be comforted by anything less than the truth, and then she let him go — let him go with a look that said she would find the truth herself if he wouldn't give it, that she was already counting, already bargaining in the dark of her own head, and the door closed between them with the wind's help and he stood a moment in the draft feeling the shape of something going wrong that he could not yet name.

* * *

The requisition house at eight o'clock was lit by storm lanterns, its seaward windows boarded where the wind had taken them, sandbagged at the sills like a place preparing for siege rather than departure. Soldiers moved through the corridors hauling crates, and the great trestle tables of the main room stood stripped and empty, everything packed into oilcloth-bound bundles stacked against the inner wall — and among those bundles, wrapped in canvas but not locked, not chained, not guarded by anyone within thirty feet, the shape of ledgers. Many ledgers. One of them fat with every name on this coast.

Vane stood at the map table with two lieutenants, and when he saw August he dismissed them with a jerk of his head, and for a moment the two men faced each other across the chart of the coast they'd both grown up beside, the candle flame bending in the draft from the broken window.

"You got my message."

"The tide waits for no grief."

"It doesn't." Vane's face was gray with fatigue, his collar open, his uniform jacket thrown over a chair like a man who had stopped performing for anyone. On the table between them lay the evacuation manifest, and August's eye went to it the way a starving man's eye goes through a window to a table. Names in columns. Berths numbered. He could see the ink from here but not the words. "Saturday's loading is a farce and everyone knows it. The Meridian's master wants to weigh anchor by noon whether the boats are done or not. My orders from the admiral's office assume the storm arrives Sunday. The storm will not wait for the admiral's office." He pressed both hands flat on the chart. "Nothing in this business moves at the speed it should, August. Everything moves at the speed it must."

"Then say what you brought me here to say."

Vane looked at him for a long moment — and August saw it again, what he'd been seeing for weeks now behind the uniform and the grievances: a man in a machine, gears above him and below him, turning because stopping meant being crushed. When he spoke, his voice had dropped its officer's edge entirely.

"There's a berth with your name on it. Keeper, essential personnel, last off. You know that. You've known it." He turned the manifest around and pushed it across the table. "And there's a column next to it. Persons designated for removal by local authority's discretion. Discretionary berths, they call them in London. Two of them for this district." His finger touched two blank lines at the bottom of the page. "Two names, August. Chosen by me. Or chosen by you, if you bring me something worth the trade."

The candle bent. Somewhere overhead a shutter tore loose and went clattering down the street like a fleeing animal.

"What kind of something?"

"A list." Vane's voice was level and terrible. "The people hiding in this town with reasons to hide. The ones not on any register. You've kept this light twenty years; you know every soul on this coast and every soul the registers don't account for. Give me the names of the undesirables, and I'll fill those two lines with whoever you like besides — your fisherman, your widow, anyone legitimate. Two lawful berths, yours guaranteed. In exchange for the names of everyone living outside the law under my nose."

The rain came against the boarded window like thrown gravel. August looked down at the manifest, at the two blank lines, at the space where a name could be written that would mean a boat, a life, a morning beyond the storm — and beneath that thought, cold and rising as the groundswell, came the understanding of what the trade actually was. Not two lives bought. Others sold. Nell with her forged papers. Whoever else crouched in lofts and cellars along this coast trusting the dark to keep them.

And Ellery watching him across the chart, offering damnation in the grammar of paperwork.

"You want me to write names," August said slowly, "and you'll write mine."

"I want this district evacuated without a scandal, and I want—" Vane stopped. Closed his eyes briefly. "I want it done before the storm makes liars of every order I've been given. Yes, August. Your berth is certain either way; the keeper goes last regardless. This is only about the discretionary lines. Think of who they save."

"I am thinking of who they cost."

"Then think quickly." For the first time, something cracked through the fatigue in his brother-in-law's voice — urgency, almost pleading, a man shoving a decision at someone else because he could not bear to be the one who made it. "The manifest closes tomorrow at noon. After that the ink dries and nothing moves, and the Meridian sails with whatever names are written, and everyone else—" He gestured at the boarded window, at the black roaring beyond it, and did not finish.

August stood in the lantern light with the storm shaking the house and the manifest between them, and he thought of Tilly counting, of Nell's name sleeping in a canvas bundle thirty feet away, of Bram's thirty drowned souls, of the two blank lines like empty berths swinging at anchor.

Tomorrow at noon. He had one night.

"I'll have your answer by morning," he said, and turned for the door, and behind him Vane called out — not the captain's voice, not the brother's voice, some third voice worn thin as wire:

"Whoever's names you don't write, August — the sea writes them instead."

The wind took the door out of his hand as he left, and the night received him, and the barometer at home, when he climbed the tower and lit the lamp and checked it with numb fingers, read twenty-eight and one.

Falling still.
