## 10. The Last Berth

The summons came at low tide, carried by a boy in an army greatcoat two sizes too big for him, who stood at the foot of the tower as though the sea might rise up and take him if he lingered.

"Captain Vane requests the keeper's attendance at the requisition house," the boy recited, eyes fixed somewhere over August's shoulder. "Four o'clock. He says—" the boy faltered, checked a folded paper, "—he says it concerns berths."

August took the paper without reading it. The boy was gone before he'd finished folding it away, running back down the harbor road with his coat flapping like something trying to escape him.

Inside, Nell looked up from the table where she'd been mending Bram's oilskins, needle suspended mid-stitch. "Berths?"

"So he says."

"That's not how he says things." She set down the needle. "Vane doesn't request. He summons and dresses it up polite."

"He's your captain's brother-in-law now, is he?" Bram said from the corner, where he sat sharpening a knife that had not needed sharpening since the army came. He'd been sharpening it every evening for a week. August had stopped commenting on it.

"He's nothing of mine," Nell said, too quickly, and went back to her stitching with a violence that made the oilskin crackle.

Tilly was not in the house. She'd been out since morning, and August knew where — or near enough. He'd watched her go from the gallery, small and determined, cutting across the dunes toward the Widow Marsh's cottage with a slate under her arm. She'd been keeping accounts all week. He'd found her lists: names in her careful schoolhand, columns ruled straight, numbers beside them. He hadn't asked what the numbers meant. He already knew. Five berths. Six of them. And beyond their own household, a town full of people doing the same arithmetic in kitchens and fish cellars all along the shore.

He dressed in his good coat — the one he'd worn to his wedding, twenty years gone, smelling faintly of camphor and another life — and walked down into the town.

* * *

The requisition house had been the Harrow Inn before the army came. Now its sign hung face-down against the wall and soldiers' kit filled the stable yard, and the taproom where fishermen had once argued about herring prices held trestle tables stacked with papers, crates stenciled WAR DEPT., and a map of the coast pinned to the wall with the harbor marked in red ink like a wound.

Vane received him in the parlor, which still had the inn's good chairs and a fire that burned too warm for the season. Two orderlies stood by the door. A clerk sat at a side table with his pen moving steadily, recording everything, and August understood that this was the point: whatever was said here would exist afterward, in ink, in London.

"Crane." Vane did not rise. He looked tired in a way August had never seen in him — not soft-tired but ground-down tired, the fatigue of a man carrying weight he couldn't set down. "Sit."

"I'll stand."

"You always were contrary." Vane gestured anyway, and when August didn't move, continued as though he had. "You've seen the weather glass?"

"Falling since Tuesday."

"It will fall further. My superiors have moved the evacuation forward — the transport *Meridian* arrives within the week, and after her, nothing until spring. Possibly nothing at all, if the forecasts from the Admiralty hold." He said it flatly, a man reading coordinates. "There are berths for one hundred and forty souls. This parish holds better than four hundred."

The number landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. August heard it fall and heard no bottom.

"And you've come to tell me my share of the arithmetic."

"I've come to offer you a berth." Vane leaned forward. Up close, August could see the gray coming into his temples, the lines around his mouth that hadn't been there at Marianne's funeral. Grief had worked on him too, in its own joyless way. "One berth. Guaranteed passage on the *Meridian*, priority loading, quarters assigned. For you."

"For me."

"The keeper of the light is listed as an essential person. Your knowledge of the harbor approaches is documented. There is— interest, in London, in preserving men of technical competence." A pause, precise as a blade laid flat. "Conditional on certain assurances."

"There it is."

"You haven't heard it."

"I don't need to. I've watched you circle it for three days like a gull over a dead fish." August kept his voice level. The clerk's pen scratched. "Say it plainly, Ellery. You want names."

Vane's jaw tightened. For a moment he looked less like a captain and more like the young man who had come to the lighthouse after the funeral, awkward with grief, holding his sister's ring because August couldn't bear to touch it. Then the officer reassembled himself, piece by piece, and the moment was gone.

"The requisition requires a complete accounting of persons resident in the parish. Some residents are not— properly accounted for elsewhere. Deserters. Debtors. Persons using names that are not their own." His eyes flicked, just barely, toward the window, toward the town. "You keep the light above this harbor, Crane. You see every boat that comes in. Every face that walks the strand. If you provided a list of such persons—"

"You'd strike them from the rolls. No berth. Left for the storm."

"They'd be *left to make their own arrangements*. Which is more than they deserve, most of them."

"And in exchange, my name goes on the ship's list."

"In exchange, you're treated as what you are. An essential man. Not—" Vane spread his hands, and the gesture was almost gentle, which was somehow worse than cruelty, "—not whatever else you've chosen to be out here."

August looked at him for a long moment. The fire popped. The clerk wrote.

"You mean not one of them," August said. "Not a drifter, not a debtor, not a man who married beneath his brother-in-law's opinion and buried his wife in a churchyard your family wouldn't visit."

"That is not—"

"Marianne is in that churchyard. Under a stone I cut myself. And every person in this town who isn't properly accounted for — every runaway and ruined man and woman hiding behind a borrowed name — they're hers too, in every way that matters. They eat at my table. They mend each other's coats. You want me to sell them for a deck berth and call it competence." He shook his head slowly. "No."

"Crane—"

"No." Louder now, and the clerk's pen stopped. "I'll give you one list, Captain, free of charge. It has one name on it. Mine. Write it wherever you like. Strike it, if that pleases you. But I'll not hand you a single soul in this town, and if you come to my light again with offers dressed as kindness, I'll show you the way I showed the last smuggler who tried to buy the lamp — over the rail and into the tide."

The silence stretched. One of the orderlies shifted his weight. Vane sat very still, and August saw something move behind his eyes that wasn't anger — something more like exhaustion, or envy, or the look of a man watching someone refuse a bargain he'd already been forced to accept himself.

"You're a fool," Vane said quietly.

"Almost certainly."

"You'll drown with them."

"Then I'll drown with them." August turned for the door, then stopped, because the thought had finally finished forming — the thought that had been building since the boy brought the summons, since Tilly's lists, since the word *berths* first crossed the harbor water. He turned back.

"How many are left? After the essential persons, the officers' allocations, the families with papers in order. How many berths remain unassigned?"

Vane hesitated. It was a small hesitation, but August had spent thirty years reading weather, and he read it.

"One," Vane said.

* * *

The walk home took longer than usual because everyone wanted to speak to him.

Mrs. Petchy at the bakehouse, flour to her elbows, asking with elaborate casualness whether he'd heard anything about the ship's list. Old Cobb mending a net outside his door, who didn't ask anything at all, just looked at him with eyes like wet stones and then back down at his work. The Marsh children playing a game that was mostly standing very still and staring at the horizon. At the head of the harbor road, Sam Oakes caught his sleeve — Sam, whose lungs were going, who hadn't managed the stairs to the light in a year, whose wife Ada had died in the winter and whose daughter lived three counties inland and hadn't written since summer.

"They say there's a berth left," Sam said. His breath whistled on the wind. "They say the keeper gets to say."

"They say a lot of things."

"August." Sam's grip was stronger than his chest should have allowed. "My girl's in Ashcombe. If I don't go now, I die here, and she won't even know to come bury me."

"I know, Sam."

"Do you? Do you know what it is, to lie awake counting the waves and thinking, that one's for me, maybe that one?" He let go, suddenly ashamed of his own strength, hands twisting together. "I'm not begging. I'm just — saying it out loud, so somebody knows it's true."

"I know it's true," August said. "That's worth something. Saying it out loud."

He walked on. Behind him he heard Sam coughing, long and deep, the sound carrying over the water like a signal from a wreck.

By the time he reached the tower the light was failing and the wind had backed around to the northeast, the bad quarter, the one that drove the sea straight into the harbor mouth. He climbed the stairs two at a time out of habit and stood in the lantern room while the glass panes shuddered around him, looking down at the town below.

Lamps were being lit. He counted them without meaning to — a habit older than grief. Forty-one lamps, forty-one households, and behind each one the same arithmetic being done in whispers. Five berths for six. One hundred and forty for four hundred. And one — one unclaimed, unassigned, hanging in the air of a warm parlor like a sword by a thread.

Everyone looked to him. That was the truth of it, the truth he'd been walking through all the way home. Not because he'd asked for it. Because he kept the light. Because when the fog came down, they steered by him; when the wrecks came ashore, they pulled survivors to his door; when the army came with its ledger and its lists, it was his table they gathered at, his judgment they trusted without ever saying so. Keeper of the light. Keeper of the town, though no commission had ever said those words. And now keeper of the last berth — the one name that would live, chosen from among all the names that would not.

He thought of Sam's whistle-breath. Of Mrs. Petchy's grandchildren asleep above the bakehouse. Of old Cobb, who had fished these waters fifty years and would sooner sink than leave them. Of the Marsh children, still and staring at the horizon. Of Nell, whose real name lived in a stolen book in a soldier's strongbox. Of Bram, who had already given his life away once and wore the debt like ballast. Of Tilly, nine years old, with her slate and her careful columns, bargaining in the dunes because no adult would say aloud what the numbers meant.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a keel, the thought he could not surface: *my own name.* The one he'd offered Vane for free. The one that would fit the empty berth as neatly as a hand fits a glove — essential person, technical competence, documented knowledge of the harbor approaches. All he had to do was stop fighting. Sign the assurance. Become, on paper, what he had never been in fact: a man alone, accountable to no one, belonging to no household, owing nothing to anyone.

It would be so easy. That was the horror of it. The berth was shaped exactly like his loneliness.

Below, a lamp moved along the harbor road — small, quick, familiar. Tilly, coming home through the dark with her slate hugged to her chest, running the last stretch against the rising wind. He watched her until she reached the tower door, and heard her boots on the stairs, and braced himself for whatever the day's arithmetic had taught her.

She burst into the watch room flushed and salt-lipped, hair whipping loose from its braid, and thrust the slate at him before she'd caught her breath.

"I've got it," she said. "I've got it figured. Listen —"

But whatever she'd figured, the words died in her throat, because the lamp behind him guttered in a gust through the ventilator, and outside, far out past the shoals, the sky had gone the color of a bruise, and the first long swell of the great storm came rolling in under the dark like something waking up.

"August," Tilly whispered. "Where's mine?"
