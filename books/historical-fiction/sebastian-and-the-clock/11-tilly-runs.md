## 11. Tilly Runs

Tilly had learned arithmetic the hard way, which was to say she'd learned it from Nell, at a kitchen table, with a stub of pencil and a slate that smelled of salt and old rain. But no lesson had prepared her for the sum that lived in her chest now, ticking over and over like a clock with a loose gear.

Six people. Five berths.

She did it again on her fingers under the blanket, though she knew the answer by heart. Six into five left one out, and everyone in the house pretended not to know who the one would be. They were kind about it, the way grown-ups were kind about things they thought children couldn't see. August had started saying *we'll sort it* in a voice too bright. Bram had taken to humming when the subject came near, loud enough to drown it. Even Nell, who never lied about numbers, had gone quiet as a closed book.

But Tilly could see. She'd been the one nobody wanted first. She knew how the sum went.

* * *

She'd come to the lighthouse three winters ago, carried up the cliff path by a fisherman who found her half-starved in the lee of his boat, and for three years she had told herself the story of that night until it wore smooth as sea glass: *August Crane looked down at me and said, well then, you'd better come in out of the wind.* It was a good story. She kept it like a coin in her pocket and touched it when she needed to be brave.

But coins got spent. And lately she'd caught herself doing sums she didn't want to do.

The army men had posted the berth list on the requisition house door, and Tilly had gone to look at it the way you look at a wound — because you can't help it. Five names for their household, if the household even counted as one. August's name was there, first, because the light had to be tended until the last possible hour. Nell's name was there, written careful, because someone had argued for it. Bram's name was there. Hers was there. That made four, and there was Mrs. Pellow next door with her bad chest and no family at all, and the Fenwick boy whose mother had drowned two springs back, and old Sam Ottery who'd fished these waters fifty years and couldn't read the list he was standing in front of.

Tilly had counted the whole town against the whole list, right there on the cobbles, and the number that came out was so large and so cold that she'd had to put her hand against the wall to keep steady.

Forty-one souls in the harbor town. Nine berths remaining after the essential persons were named.

Thirty-two left behind.

She hadn't told anyone. She'd walked home along the cliff path with the wind pushing at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades, and she'd thought: *I am small. I eat less than Bram. I take up less room than anyone. If one must stay, it should be the one who costs the least.*

And underneath that thought, quieter, older, the one she couldn't drown out no matter how loud the sea got: *and they will let me, because I was never really theirs.*

That was the lie. She knew it was a lie even as she believed it. But lies born of fear don't care about being known.

* * *

The supply wagons came twice a week from the garrison road, hauling flour and lamp oil and now, increasingly, hauling things *out* — crates of records, the good silver from the harbor inn, anything the army decided was worth saving before the storms took it. The drivers were sullen men who smoked clay pipes and spoke to no one, and the horses were big slow bays that knew the road better than the men held their reins.

Tilly watched them from behind the net-drying frames for two days before she worked out the pattern. The wagon left at mid-morning, took the coast road north past the churchyard, and turned inland where the land folded up toward the moor. It came back empty by dusk. Nobody rode in the back. Nobody checked the back. The army had not yet learned that this town was full of people who had spent their lives hiding things in plain sight — contraband brandy, untaxed tea, children who weren't on any register anywhere.

On the third morning the barometer in the lighthouse passage had fallen so far that August tapped it twice and frowned at it like a stubborn horse. The sky had gone the color of wet pewter. The gulls had quit the harbor entirely, which Bram called *the sea giving notice*, and the first shingles had begun to lift off the Cooper house down the lane, flapping loose like birds trying to escape.

"The great storm's vanguard," August said at breakfast, and his voice was flat in a way Tilly had learned meant frightened. "It'll be here inside two days. Maybe less."

"Then the ship comes sooner," Nell said.

"Sooner means the berths close sooner." August looked at his hands. "I'm going in to Vane today. There has to be room for—"

"There isn't," Tilly said.

They all stopped. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But it was out now, sitting on the table among the bread and the dripping like something dragged in from the tide line.

"You've seen the list," August said carefully.

"I've counted the town." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Forty-one. Nine berths. You can't argue your way around arithmetic, August. Nell taught me that."

"Tilly—" Nell began.

"I'm not stupid," Tilly said, and stood up, and pushed her chair in very neatly because if she didn't do something with her hands she would cry, and crying would make them comfort her, and being comforted would make her stay, and staying would make everything worse. "I'm going to check the hens."

There were no hens. There hadn't been hens since the soldiers impounded the coop. But nobody stopped her, because they were all looking at each other over the wreckage of the conversation, and that was the moment Tilly understood that she would have to save herself from being saved.

* * *

She packed the way she'd learned to pack the winter she ran from the workhouse master: nothing that rattled, nothing that mattered more than bread. A shawl. Half a loaf wrapped in cloth. The coin-story of the night August found her, which weighed nothing at all and was the hardest thing to leave.

She wrote a note. She sat with the pencil over the paper for a long time, because everything she tried to write sounded either like goodbye or like a lie, and she wanted it to sound like neither. In the end she wrote:

*I counted wrong on purpose. There are six of us and five berths and I am the smallest so I fit through the smallest gap. Don't come after me. The wagon goes inland and inland is away from the water and I'm not afraid of roads, only of being the reason someone else drowns. Tell August the light needs him more than I do. That's not true either but it's near enough. — T.*

She left it under the slate on the kitchen table, weighted with the salt cellar, and she looked at the room once — the mended chairs, Bram's pipe on the sill, Nell's ledger-keeping hand visible everywhere in neat stacks of paper — and she did the thing she'd done leaving the workhouse, which was to not look back after the door.

The wagon stood at the cooper's yard taking on barrels. The driver was at the inn door arguing about his chit. The rain had started, fine and needling, the vanguard's scouts sent ahead of the main body. Tilly crossed the yard with her heart going like a gull in a gale, climbed the tailboard, and burrowed into the gap between a flour sack and the wagon's side, pulling a tarpaulin corner over herself.

The canvas smelled of tar and horse. She lay still and listened to her own breathing and told herself the story of the coin: *well then, you'd better come in out of the wind.* Except this time she was going the other way, out into the wind, and the story cracked a little in her hands and she held the pieces anyway.

The wagon lurched. Wheels ground on wet cobbles. Through a gap in the boards she saw the lighthouse rise above the town, white and patient, its gallery a thin dark line against the pewter sky, and she pressed her face to the gap and watched it shrink.

*Goodbye,* she didn't say. *I'm making the sum come out right.*

* * *

The coast road gave way to the moor road and the moor road gave way to weather. By the second hour the fine rain had thickened into sheets that came sideways, and the wagon slowed, and the driver swore steadily in a voice pitched to carry over the wind. Tilly lay in her hollow between flour and plank and felt the cold come up through the boards and settle into her bones like a tenant who intended to stay.

She had imagined inland as a place. Now she understood it was only a direction, and directions didn't feed you or warm you or tell you stories when the dark came down. The moor rose gray on either side, all rock and heather and standing water, and there was not a roof in sight, and the wind had a voice now, a long rising note like something enormous drawing breath.

She thought about the note under the slate. *Don't come after me.* She had written it believing it. Now, cold and jolted and smaller than she'd ever felt, she heard how it would sound to August — heard him reading it with his big careful hands going still, heard Nell say *she counted wrong on purpose, oh, Tilly, no* — and the crack in the coin-story split clean through.

Because that was the thing about sacrifice that nobody told you: it was supposed to feel noble, and instead it felt like the workhouse, like running, like being the girl nobody claimed. She had dressed fear in sacrifice's clothes and marched it out the door, and now, alone under a tarpaulin on a moor road in the teeth of a gathering gale, the clothes blew off and there was just a ten-year-old girl in the rain who wanted her people.

*Turn back,* said some small sensible voice. *At the crossroads, slip down, run home, tell them you changed your mind, they'll forgive you, they always—*

The wagon stopped.

Boots crunched on gravel. Voices — two drivers, maybe three, and another voice with an officer's clipped edges to it. Tilly went absolutely still, breath shallow, the way she'd hidden from the workhouse master's rounds.

"—road's washing out past the ford," a driver said. "We shelter at the tollhouse or we lose the load."

"Lose the load and answer for it," the officer's voice said. "Tollhouse, then. Move."

The wagon turned. Tilly felt the change in the lean of the floor, felt the wheels find ruts filled with running water, and understood with a lurch worse than any rut that she was now traveling deeper inland, away from home, into the storm's gathering heart, in a wagon full of army flour bound for a tollhouse full of soldiers.

She should jump. She knew she should jump. But the ground was moving past in a brown blur of floodwater, jumping meant the road, the road meant the water, and the water already had the color of something that swallowed things.

The rain hammered the tarpaulin like fists. Somewhere behind her, far south and far away, a lighthouse stood on a cliff with its lamp turning, and the man who kept it was walking into the requisition house to fight for berths that didn't exist, for a household that was already one child short and didn't know it yet.

Tilly pulled the tarpaulin tighter, put her arms around her knees, and did the only arithmetic left to her: one small girl, one long road, one storm coming, and no way back that her courage could find.

The wagon rolled on into the gray.

* * *

In the lighthouse kitchen, the salt cellar stood guard over a page of pencil writing, and the wind began, very softly, to sing against the glass.
