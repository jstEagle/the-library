## 12. Ledger Night

Nell had stolen before. She wanted that said plainly, at least to herself, in the dark of the lighthouse kitchen while the storm tore its first shingles off the town and flung them into the sea. She had stolen a penknife at nine years old, a loaf at eleven, an employer's seal at nineteen — wax, stamp, and all, pressed into a candle stub so her forged letters could carry the weight of a gentleman's name. Every theft had bought her a little life. This one would have to buy six.

The salt cellar sat on the table like a small white headstone. Beneath it, Tilly's page of pencil writing: *gone inland on Harrow's wagon — don't look for me — I counted it fair.* Nell had read it four times and each time the words rearranged themselves into something worse. The child had run because she'd done sums no child should do. Five berths, six souls, and Tilly had subtracted herself.

"We'll find her," Bram said from the doorway, dripping. He had been out along the coast road since dusk, walking into the wind like a man arguing with it. "Harrow turned back at the ford. Wagon's sheltering at the tollhouse. If she jumped off anywhere, it was before the marshes."

"Then we look for her tomorrow." Nell folded the page and put it inside her bodice, against her heart, where she kept everything she couldn't afford to lose. "Tonight we do the other thing."

August stood at the window with his back to them, watching the harbor go to pieces. The army's cutter dragged its anchor and re-dragged it, lanterns swinging on the quay like hanged men. Rain came sideways now, and beyond it, far out, the sky over the deep water had gone a color Nell had no honest word for — green-black, bruised, the color the fishermen called *the lid coming down*.

"The blackout starts at eight bells," August said. "Vane's orders. All lamps doused in the requisition house so the garrison can't be seen from seaward. Soldiers billeted there will sleep. The officers won't."

"The officers drink," Bram corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is us."

Nell laid out what they had gathered across two days of listening and pretending to be dull. A plan drawn in pencil on the back of a flour invoice, because even now she couldn't bring herself to write anything incriminating on good paper. The requisition house — old Mrs. Pell's boarding house, seized in the first week — stood with its back to the hill and its front door facing the square, watched by a sentry under an oilcloth awning. But the house had been built by a man afraid of fires, and men afraid of fires gave their houses back doors, and the back door of the Pell house opened onto the alley where the wood was stacked, and the lock was a cheap brass thing that had surrendered to Bram's knife once already when they were boys stealing apples from the same orchard.

"The ledger sits in the parlor," Nell said. "I saw it Thursday. Trestle table, middle of the room, under a glass dome like it was the crown jewels. Two candles burning near it and a chair pulled up close, because Captain Vane works late."

"And Vane?" August asked.

"Gone to the cutter. They're war-counciling the storm tonight. That's our window." Nell looked down at her hands. They were steady. She made a point of noticing that. "One hour, maybe less. We take the ledger, we bring it here, and then—" Her voice snagged on the next part, and she made herself say it anyway. "Then we do what has to be done to it."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The wind filled the silence for them, pressing at the shutters like a crowd.

"You understand what you're asking," August said quietly. It wasn't quite a question. He had spent three days not saying it, and now the storm had said it for him. "That book is every family in this town. Names, debts, who paid and who didn't, who signed the loyalty declaration and who refused. If the army gets it home to London, half this harbor is ruined on paper forever. Debts collected from people who've lost everything. Loyalties marked down against them for the rest of their lives. And if we're caught with it—"

"I know what happens if we're caught with it," Nell said. "I've known since I was nineteen years old what happens when the paper wins and the person loses. My whole life is a forged signature in somebody's record, Mr. Crane. My name isn't even my name. Do you know what that's like? To be *Nell* only as long as no officer opens the right book?"

August turned from the window then, and looked at her — really looked, the way he looked at weather coming in off the water, measuring it without flinching.

"That's why it has to be you in the room," he said. "You know which pages matter. I'd tear it blind."

* * *

They went down through the town at half past ten, three shadows moving between shadows, the rain doing half their work for them. The streets were flooding already — the low lanes by the fish cellars ankle-deep in black water that smelled of weed and tar. Somewhere out past the mole the sea was making a sound Nell had never heard it make, a long continuous roar like a train that never arrived, and above it the lighthouse beam swept around and around, stubborn as a heartbeat, the last lit thing on the coast because August Crane had refused the order to douse it and dared anyone ashore to come and argue.

Bram went first, up the alley with his shoulders bent against the rain. At the woodpile behind the Pell house he paused, put one hand flat against the wet boards, and for a moment — just a moment — Nell saw him as he must have been thirty years ago, a young sailor standing outside another building full of sleeping soldiers, deciding something about himself that would follow him to his grave.

"You don't have to come in," she whispered.

"I deserted once," Bram said. His voice was almost lost under the wind. "Ran from a drowning beach because a captain told me the boats were full. Thirty souls in the water and me rowing away with dry hands. I've paid for that every day since in a coin nobody counts but me." He set his knife to the lock. "Tonight I pay some of it back. Open the door, Nell. I'll watch the alley."

The lock gave with a click that seemed enormous. It wasn't. Inside, the house was quiet as a held breath.

* * *

The parlor of the requisition house still smelled faintly of Mrs. Pell's lavender, fighting a losing war against pipe smoke and boot leather. Furniture had been shoved to the walls; maps hung where portraits used to; and there, in the center of the room, exactly as Nell had memorized it, stood the trestle table with its cargo of empire.

The ledger lay open under its glass dome, lamplight pooled on it from a single shaded oil lamp left burning low. Beside it: a decanter, a half-written dispatch, Vane's gloves folded like a dead bird.

Nell lifted the dome with both hands and set it on the carpet, soft as setting down a sleeping child. Then she stood over the book and let herself see it whole — the columns of names she knew, her neighbors' names, written in an clerk's beautiful indifferent hand. *Crane, A. — keeper — loyal, of use. Bram, J. (no surname given) — laborer — character doubtful. Nell Marsh (alias) — clerk — see attached inquiry.* Her own alias, there in ink, with a red thread tied to the page's edge like a tourniquet. Someone had already begun the work of finding her.

She took out the pencil stub and the scrap of paper she'd carried for two days and began to mark. Not randomly — she'd learned forgery by learning which details power actually reads. The debts of the fishing families, whose repayment the army meant to squeeze out of a drowned town: those pages. The loyalty declarations, the refusals, the informer's reports on who had hidden a deserter or fed a smuggler: those pages. The inquiry into *Nell Marsh*: that page most of all.

"August," she breathed. "The knife."

He worked beside her in silence, big hands surprisingly careful, holding pages flat while she chose. When she nodded, he tore — a sound like a small rip in the world, repeated forty times, fifty, until the ledger's spine gaped and the floor around the table was littered with paper. Each page she folded into her bodice with the others until she could feel them all against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

What remained in the book was worse than nothing and better than ruin: the ordinary record of an ordinary town, grain shipments and harbor dues, nothing any widow could be hunted with. She stared at it, breathing hard, and felt something in her chest that was not quite guilt and not quite triumph — the strange clean coldness of justice done outside the law, where the law itself had become the criminal.

Then the front door opened.

Not the sentry changing — footsteps indoors, unhurried, boots on Mrs. Pell's good boards. Voices in the hall. An officer had come in out of the storm, and behind him another, and one of them was laughing at something, and they were coming toward the parlor because the lamp was burning and the lamp meant someone was supposed to be working.

August moved without hesitation. He blew the lamp, took the dome from the carpet, set it back over the mutilated ledger, and pulled Nell into the dark of the window embrasure as the door handle turned.

Two officers entered carrying their own candle. Nell pressed into the curtain with August's arm iron-hard across her, close enough to smell wet wool and rum. One officer crossed to the table, lifted the dome absently, frowned at the book.

"Spine's broken," he said. "Vane'll want that seen to. Whole town in one book and the book's falling apart."

"Book'll be in London by Friday regardless," the other said. "Storm's moved the timetable. Boat leaves at dawn whether the harbor's fit or not." He drained something from a glass. "God help whatever poor devils are left ashore."

They laughed again, softly, and took their candles away down the hall.

Nell counted sixty heartbeats before she moved. Then she and August went out through the parlor window they'd unlatched an hour before, into the alley, into the rain, Bram falling in beside them like a shadow remembering its body.

They ran the flooded lanes with the torn pages soaked against Nell's skin and the great storm roaring closer offshore, and when they reached the lighthouse kitchen at last, streaming, shaking, alive, Nell laid the papers out on the table one by one in the lamplight — forty-seven names saved from forty-seven fates — and heard herself say, in a voice she barely recognized:

"It's done. God forgive me. It's done."

It was August, wringing the sea from his coat, who answered — and what he said stopped her cold:

"Nell. Where's Tilly's page? The one from under the salt cellar. It's gone."
