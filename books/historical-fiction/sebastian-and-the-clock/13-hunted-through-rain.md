## 13. Hunted Through Rain

Bram had been sixty years old the last time a soldier pointed a rifle at him with intent behind it. He was seventy-one now, standing in the doorway of Nell's rented room with rain hammering the roof slates above him, and he found the old arithmetic of it came back easy: count the men, mark the exits, decide which one you'd hit first.

There were four soldiers in the street below, and a sergeant with a list.

"House to house," the sergeant was saying, his voice carrying flat under the drumming rain. "Captain Vane wants every room turned before noon. Anyone asks, we're looking for contraband."

Contraband. That was the polite word for it. The ledger was gone from its trestle table in the requisition house, and Captain Ellery Vane had woken to find his orders — his whole authority over this town — reduced to paper that no longer existed where it ought to be. Somewhere between midnight and dawn, three people had made thieves of themselves, and now the army was going door to door like a man patting his coat pockets for a watch he'd sworn was there at supper.

Bram shut the door softly and turned back into the room. Nell sat on the edge of her cot, still dressed from the night before, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't touched. The ledger itself lay open on the floor between them, its pages darkened by damp where the rain had gotten at them during the carry home through the gullies. August had taken half of it away already — torn free, folded small, hidden in places only he knew. What remained here was the spine and the back half, and it was Nell's job this morning to do the tearing.

"You heard them," Bram said. "They'll be at the Widow Marsh's inside ten minutes, then they'll work up the lane toward us."

"I know." Nell's voice was steady but thin, like wire pulled near to snapping. "I'm not slow, Bram."

"No," he said. "You're not." He crouched down beside her and took the ledger's ruined spine out of her hands. "But your hands are shaking, and mine are old enough to have an excuse. Let me."

She let him. That was trust, and he felt the weight of it settle into his chest like ballast. Sixty years ago he'd been a young able seaman under a captain named Harrow, and Harrow had trusted him too — trusted him to hold the evacuation boats steady while the tide took thirty souls out of the shallows at some nameless colonial cove, all because the order said go and Bram's hands had obeyed before his conscience could catch up. He'd carried that night inside him ever since, the way a hull carries water it can't pump out. Every year heavier. Every year slower.

He wasn't going to obey anything today without thinking first.

* * *

The tearing of paper is a loud thing when you're listening for footsteps. Bram worked fast anyway. Names, debts, loyalties — page after page of a town's whole soul written in a clerk's careful copperplate, and each sheet he freed he handed to Nell, who fed them one by one into the little iron stove until the room smelled of ink burning off hot metal. The ash rose gray against the black of the stove door. Outside, the rain kept on like it meant to drown the world a second time before the great storm even arrived.

"The Marsh woman's page," Nell said suddenly. "Stop. She owes nine pounds to a shipping concern that doesn't exist anymore. If that burns, nobody can ever collect it again."

"That's the point of it."

"I know it's the point. I want to *watch* it burn properly." She leaned close as the page curled, and for a moment — just a moment — something almost like peace crossed her face. Then boots in the lane, and the peace was gone. "That's them at the Marshes' door."

"Then we're done here." Bram stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow, and looked around the room with a professional eye. The stove was warm but not smoking. The ledger's remains went under the floorboard beneath Nell's cot, weighted with a flatiron. "When they come, you've got a fever and you've had it two days. Sweat through it. Don't wash your face."

"And you?"

"I'm going to be in the yard," Bram said, "splitting wood in the rain like a mad old man. Because a mad old man is exactly what they expect to find."

* * *

They were quicker than he'd guessed. By the time he'd split a dozen logs and soaked his shirt through twice, the soldiers had finished at the Marshes', and two of them came up the path with their collars turned up and their displeasure showing.

"Name?" said the younger one, a boy who couldn't have seen twenty winters.

"Bramwell Oakes."

"Live here?"

"I keep the yard. I keep most yards along this lane. Town's too poor to afford more than one pair of shoulders my age." He tipped the axe toward the house. "Girl inside has fever. You wanting to carry that back to your barracks?"

The boy hesitated, and the older soldier — a corporal with a face like a closed ledger himself — pushed past Bram and up onto the step. "We're turning rooms. Stand aside."

Bram stood aside. There was no sense dying on a doorstep over a hiding place that might or might not hold; better to make them work for nothing and learn nothing. He watched through the window as the corporal moved through Nell's room with rough efficiency — bed stripped, chest upended, floorboards thumped with a boot heel. At the board beneath the cot his heel struck the flatiron and paused.

Bram stepped back into the yard and began splitting wood again, loudly, badly, letting the axe ring off the block so the sound carried. It was an old trick and a simple one: give a searching man a reason to hurry. No soldier wants to stand in a wet house listening to a lunatic chop timber while a fevered girl coughs in the corner. The corporal came out two minutes later, empty-handed, and the boy gave Bram a look that was half apology, half contempt, and they moved on up the lane.

Nell appeared at the window, pale as milk, and mouthed something he couldn't hear. He nodded once and kept chopping. His heart was going like a drum in a parade, but his arms stayed steady, and he thought: *this is what endurance is, boy. Not courage. Just staying put when staying put hurts.*

He'd learned that at the cove, too late to save anyone.

* * *

By afternoon the search had teeth in it.

Word came up the lane with the fisherboy Jem, running through the rain with his oilskin flapping: they'd found nothing in any house, and Vane had ordered the harbor road sealed, and there were patrols on the dune track now with lanterns despite the daylight being worth nothing in this weather. And — Jem caught his breath — they'd taken Old Marta's son for questioning, and the cooper too, and they were asking everyone the same question: *who moved through the streets after the lamps went out?*

"After the blackout," Nell said quietly, when Bram repeated it. "They know the timing. They know someone used it."

"They know more than timing." Bram set down his mug. "They'll know the size of it. Three sets of prints in the requisition house mud, maybe. A window forced or not forced. Vane's no fool — he commanded landings before he ever saw this coast. He'll read a room the way I read weather."

"Then he'll read August next."

"Aye." Bram looked out at the rain, gray on gray, the sea invisible beyond the seawall except where the waves broke white enough to show. "Which is why one of us needs to be somewhere interesting tonight."

"Bram—"

"Listen to me, girl." He kept his voice low and level, the voice he'd used on frightened hands hauling lines in weather that killed better men than either of them. "August they need at the light — if he goes missing, that tower goes dark, and even Vane won't risk that with the great storm coming. You they'll hang if they catch, forged papers or no forged papers. But me?" He smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile; it was the smile of a man who'd been waiting sixty years for a debt to come due. "Me, they'll question. And I've had practice not talking. Ask anyone who ever served with me. Ask Captain Harrow, if you can find where they buried him."

"You deserted," Nell said. Not cruelly. Just stating the shape of it.

"I ran from one night," Bram said. "I'm not running from another."

* * *

He didn't tell August. That was the hardest part — harder than the cold, harder than the waiting. He passed the lighthouse keeper on the seawall path at dusk, both of them hunched into the wind, and August asked how Nell fared and whether the soldiers had troubled the lane, and Bram answered true to both and lied by omission about the rest. There was a look in August's eyes these days, a set to his mouth, that told Bram the man was carrying decisions too heavy to share out. Adding another weight would sink him.

Besides. Some cargoes a man ships alone.

The requisition house sat at the head of the harbor road, a merchant's mansion requisitioned along with everything else, its windows now lit against the failing light. Bram approached it not as an intruder but as a nuisance — that was the plan, the whole of the plan, and plans this simple were the only kind that survived contact with soldiers. He walked up the drive at half past eight, in full view of the sentry, carrying a covered basket.

"Begging for the fevered girl," he announced to the sentry, holding up the basket. "Broth and bread. Your own surgeon said hot broth keeps fever down — ask him."

The sentry, a different boy than the morning's, wavered. Bram had counted on that: hunger makes beggars of sentries everywhere, and army bread was army bread. While the boy deliberated, Bram let his gaze travel past him, casual as a gull's, through the open door into the hall — and there he saw what he'd come to see.

Vane stood in the lamplight with a map spread on a table, two officers bent over it, and on the side table beside him lay a document case, open, and in the case — Bram's eyes were old but they'd spent a lifetime reading shapes at distance — papers. Fresh copies. Someone in London's chain of command kept duplicates, or a clerk had begun reconstructing what was lost, and either way the hunt wasn't for a book anymore. It was for *people*. Names remembered, names guessed, names to be matched against whoever broke first under questioning.

"Move along, old man," the sentry said finally, taking the basket after all, because of course he did. "Before I write you up."

Bram moved along. Ten paces down the drive, he stopped, turned, and did the stupidest brave thing of his long stupid brave life.

"You'll not find your ledger in any house," he called back, clear across the rain. "Because it isn't lost. It's *free*. Same as this town means to be."

The shout brought boots at a run. He could have run too — his legs still had a season or two left in them — but running was the same as leaving, and leaving was the thing he'd sworn off forever on a beach thirty years dead. So he stood in the rain with his empty hands open at his sides, and let them take him.

* * *

The room they put him in had once been a parlor. Now it held a table, a lamp, and a chair, and Bram sat in the chair with his wrists sore from the irons and his back aching from the wall he'd spent part of the night against, and he gave them the same answer he'd given all night.

"I stole nothing. I know nothing. I'm an old man who shouted at a sentry."

The interrogator was a lean lieutenant with patient eyes, worse than a brutal one. He didn't strike. He circled, and asked, and waited, and asked again.

"Three sets of footprints, Mr. Oakes. Small, large, and large."

"Tide comes in twice a day. Lots of feet on a harbor road."

"The window latch was lifted with a knife blade. Do you own a knife?"

"I own an axe. Ask the sentry whose wood I was chopping when your corporal searched our lane."

"Where is the girl Nell from? She appears in no record before eighteen months ago."

"She appeared in my life eighteen months ago, hungry, and I fed her. That's the whole of her record as far as I'm concerned."

The lieutenant studied him for a long moment. Rain lashed the shuttered window. Somewhere below, the sea was getting ready for something.

"You served," the lieutenant said at last. It wasn't a question. "Your hands. Your posture. The way you counted our rifles this morning — yes, we watched you do it. Where?"

Bram looked at him, and something old and tired surfaced in his chest, and he made the decision he'd been making in pieces since the cove.

"Navy," he said. "Able Seaman Bramwell Oakes, discharged thirty years past. And before you dig further, Lieutenant — whatever you find in the fleet's books about me, it'll be the truth, and the truth is I obeyed an order once that drowned thirty people, and I have spent every day since learning the difference between loyalty and obedience. So ask me about the ledger all night if it pleases you. You'll get nothing. Not because I'm loyal to thieves. Because I'm loyal to a town that fed me when the Navy forgot I existed."

The lieutenant wrote something down. The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind found a new note, lower and longer, the note the sea sang before it stopped singing altogether.

"Captain Vane will see you himself at first light," the lieutenant said, rising. "I'd think carefully about what you want him to hear."

Bram settled back against the chair and let the irons clink, comfortable as an old man by his own fire.

"Tell him I'll say the same thing I said to the last officer who thought a drowning town was a supply problem," he said. "And tell him to bring his patience. I've got thirty years of it saved up, and nowhere else to spend it."

The door closed. The lock turned. Bram closed his eyes and listened to the storm gathering beyond the walls, closer now, vast and certain, and he thought of Tilly's laugh and Nell's steady hands and August's stubborn lamp, and he thought: *hold, old man. Hold the line this time.*

Above the wind, faint through stone and shutter, he heard the first shingles begin to tear loose from the roofs of the town — and then, nearer, boots in the corridor, many of them, moving with purpose toward the interrogation room, and Vane's voice cutting through the noise like a blade:

"Open the door. Wake him. The storm's come early — and I need to know where that ledger is *tonight*."
