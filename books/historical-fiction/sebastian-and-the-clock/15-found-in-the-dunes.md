## 15. Found in the Dunes

The wagon stopped twice before it reached the crossroads, and both times Tilly held her breath so hard her ribs ached.

The first time was at the toll gate, where a soldier with rain running off the brim of his cap leaned in through the canvas flap and counted heads with his forefinger — *one, two, three* — drivers, all of them, men hauling salt pork and powder up the coast road before the storms closed it. He never looked behind the barrels where Tilly lay curled among sacking, one arm around a jar of pickled herring to keep it from rolling, her heart going like a gull's wings against a window.

The second time was worse, because the wagon didn't stop at all. It slowed, and voices came through the wood slats, and she heard the word *berths*, and then the word *Crane*, spoken flat and official by someone who had no right to say it that way, like it was just a word and not the roof over her head.

Then the wheels turned again and the town fell away behind her and there was nothing under her but road.

She had planned it badly. She knew that now. She had planned it the way you plan anything when you are eleven and crying into your sleeve so nobody hears: fast, and in the dark, with only half the facts. The supply wagons went inland on Tuesdays — Nell had said so once, complaining about the price of lamp oil — and Tuesdays came every seven days whether or not you were ready for them, and this Tuesday had come while she still had two shillings sewn into her petticoat and a heel of bread in her pocket and a speech prepared in her head about how it was simple arithmetic, *five berths, six of us, and I'm the smallest and the newest and the least needed*—

Except the wagon hadn't gone inland after all. It had turned north along the coast, toward the army depot at Harrow Point, and somewhere past the old fish-weirs it had stopped to change horses, and Tilly had climbed down between the wheels and dropped into the dune grass like a stolen thing, and stood there listening until the creak of the wagon faded, and realized she did not know where she was.

That had been hours ago. Or maybe only one hour. It was hard to know, because the wind had come up out of the southeast the way Bram always said it would, *the big blow, girlie, when it comes it comes all night and all day and asks no pardon*, and now the whole world was gray moving on gray, grass flattened into silver rivers, sand lifting in long ghost-ribbons and stinging her cheeks where they were already stung raw.

She had found the hut because she'd stopped looking for anywhere else.

It sat in a fold of the dunes like something the sea had coughed up years ago and never bothered to reclaim — a fisherman's hut, tar-black boards, roof half stripped, door hanging from one leather hinge. Inside there was an overturned boat, keel up, propped against one wall as if waiting for its owner to come back and turn it over. A net rotted in the corner. A crate. Nothing else. No stove, no lamp, no person.

Tilly got inside and pulled the door as shut as it would go, which left a gap wide enough for the wind to pour through like cold water, and sat down in the lee of the overturned boat, knees to chest, arms around them, and began to do sums.

This was what she did instead of praying. August had taught her numbers on slow winter evenings with chalk on the watch-room floor, saying *numbers don't lie to you, Tilly; people will, weather will, but four is four*. So she added. Two shillings. Half a loaf. One petticoat. No coat, because coats were heavy and she'd been afraid of being seen carrying one. Wind rising. Light failing. Distance home — she tried to work it out and couldn't, because she had never once in her life paid attention to the road north, why would she have, why would anyone —

And then the sum that mattered, the sum underneath all the others, the one she kept doing anyway even though she already knew the answer, the way you keep touching a sore tooth:

Five berths. Six Cranes.

August. Nell. Bram. Herself. And — here was where the arithmetic got cruel, because the household wasn't really five, was it, it was six, because Mrs. Pellow next door ate supper with them four nights out of seven since her husband took to his bed, and Mrs. Pellow had nowhere else, and Mrs. Pellow's husband was dying, and when he died she would be exactly what Tilly was: nobody's, with no papers and no claim and no name written anywhere except in a ledger full of other people's debts.

Five berths. Six Cranes. Seven, counting Mrs. Pellow. Eight, counting Mr. Pellow, except he wouldn't live to sail anywhere.

Tilly had lain awake three nights doing this sum, and every night it came out the same way: somebody stayed. Somebody always stayed. In the stories Nell told, in the ballads Bram hummed, in every shipwreck and famine and war there was ever a song about, the arithmetic was merciless and somebody was left standing on the dock watching the water take the rest away.

So she had made herself be the somebody. That was the whole plan. It wasn't brave, she knew that too — she'd heard Nell say once that the bravest thing was staying to fight and running was just fear wearing boots, but Nell was wrong about this one thing, Tilly was sure of it, because staying meant watching August choose, and watching August choose meant watching him carry it forever, the way he carried the grave-marker slate in his coat pocket, the way he carried everything, quietly, until it bent him.

Better she should go. Better she should be a missing person than a chosen-over person. Better the sum should solve itself.

That was the plan, and plans made by eleven-year-olds crying into sleeves are worth exactly the bread in their pockets, and the bread was gone now, eaten in the dark of the hut because her stomach had overruled her, and the two shillings might as well have been two stones, and the light through the gap in the door was going from gray to iron, and she could hear the sea coming.

Not the ordinary sea. She'd grown up on the ordinary sea; it was the sound of the town breathing. This was different. This was the sea getting up off its knees. Every few minutes now there came a boom through the ground itself, deep, patient, enormous, like something turning over in sleep, and between the booms the wind screamed down the dune hollows in voices that almost made words.

*Tilly.*

No. Not words. Just wind.

But she answered anyway, because she was cold enough now that her teeth chattered the answer loose: "Here." A small voice in a big empty hut. "I'm here."

Nobody came. Of course nobody came. Nobody knew where here was.

She thought about August finding the empty bed. She had left the blanket folded — that had seemed important at the time, some kind of message, *I went neatly, don't blame yourself* — and she pictured him standing over it with the lantern, and Nell coming to the doorway, and Bram saying nothing at all, which was how Bram said the worst things, and she started to cry and found that crying hurt her face because her face was half frozen, and that was such a strange and terrible fact that she cried harder, and the harder she cried the colder she got, and somewhere in the middle of it the shaking started and wouldn't stop, and she understood, in a distant, bookish way, like reading about someone else, that the shaking was the cold winning.

She pulled her knees tighter. She thought about the watch room. About the smell of brass polish and lamp oil and August's coat. About the sound of the lens turning overhead at night, that soft mechanical heartbeat she had fallen asleep to every night for two years, *tick, swish, tick, swish*, the light going round and round keeping its promise to ships it would never meet.

*I want to go home,* she told the overturned boat, because there was no one else to tell. "I changed my mind. I want—"

The door moved.

Not opened — moved. Swung inward a hand's width against its broken hinge, and the wind came through it sideways with a fistful of sand, and in the gray light of it stood a figure with a hood blown back and hair plastered black across her face, holding a storm lantern that guttered and nearly died and then caught.

"Tilly," Nell said. Not a shout. Something worse than a shout — quiet, ragged, furious with relief. "Oh, thank God. Thank God, you stupid, brave, *awful* child."

* * *

Nell crossed the hut in three strides and knelt and took Tilly's face in both hands, and her hands were freezing too but they felt like summer, and she looked at her the way you look at a ledger line you thought was lost forever.

"Your lips are blue," Nell said. "Do you understand me? Blue. We're leaving now, this minute, and you can hate me the whole way home, but we're leaving."

"I'm not going," Tilly said. Her voice came out in pieces. "There's only five berths. I did the sums."

"I know you did the sums." Nell shrugged off her own coat — she'd worn two, Tilly saw, she'd dressed for this weather because Nell always dressed for the worst case, it was her one law — and wrapped it around Tilly's shoulders and worked the buttons with fingers that shook. "I do sums for a living, or I did, before I became a criminal. Do you know what I did with my sums? I ran away alone. Three days ago I had my bag packed and my route mapped and I was going to walk out of here and let the soldiers find whatever they found, because I'd decided my life cost less than yours. Does that sound familiar?"

Tilly said nothing. The wind filled the silence.

"It's not noble," Nell said. "I want you to hear this clearly, because nobody said it to me in time. It feels noble. It feels like the bravest thing you've ever done. And it's actually just fear with good handwriting. August figured out I was leaving before I'd laced my boots, and do you know what he said?" She paused, and her voice cracked on it. "He said, *you don't get to decide what you're worth to us. That was decided the day you walked in with wet shoes and a lie on your lips, and the decision was final.*"

"He said that?"

"He said it grumpily. There was polishing involved." Nell laughed, or sobbed, it was hard to tell which, out here in the screaming gray they sounded the same. "Now. Can you stand? Because August is out in this looking for you, and Bram is with him, and the sea is coming up over the flats, and I swear to you, Tilly Crane, if we don't find them before the tide turns—"

The name went through Tilly like warm water. "He's looking?"

"He hasn't stopped since noon. He's torn the town apart. He's—" Nell stopped, pressed her lips together, mastered it. "He's carrying you, love. Whether you meant him to or not. That's what family does. You can't spare them by disappearing. You just make them look for you in the dark."

Tilly stood up. Her legs barely belonged to her. The world tilted and Nell's arm came around her waist like an iron bar, small woman, astonishing strength.

"Home," Tilly said. Just that.

"Home," Nell agreed, and pushed the door open into the storm.

* * *

Outside was a different country. The dunes had become ocean themselves, grass streaming, sand flying so thick the lantern made only a brown halo a foot across. They went hand in hand, Nell hauling her, leaning into wind that shoved back like a living thing, and Tilly learned quickly that you didn't walk in this weather, you *fought* it, step by grudging step, and that the hollows between dunes were the only mercy, and that when the wind eased for a moment you could hear beneath it a new sound: the sea, close, much closer than it had any right to be, roaring over the flats where at low tide Tilly had gathered cockles a hundred times.

"The causeway'll be under," Nell shouted against the wind. "We go the high line, past the weirs — there!"

A shape loomed out of the flying sand. Two shapes. Big ones, moving fast, and then the lantern light caught a beard crusted white with spray and a voice like a landslide: "HERE! Here, we've got her — August! *AUGUST!*"

Bram. And behind him, stumbling up the dune with no lantern at all because he'd given his light to the search line, coatless, hatless, soaked through and not caring, came August Crane.

What happened next was not dignified. Tilly would remember that all her life, and treasure it. August did not scold. He did not stand back with folded arms and lecture. He crossed the last ten feet at a run and dropped to his knees in the sand and gathered her up against his soaked coat, and this tall careful man who measured everything — stores, oil, distance, grief — held her hard enough to hurt and shook, and said her name twice into her hair like a man checking a count, *Tilly, Tilly*, and then, muffled and rough: "Never. Never again. Whatever the sums say. Never."

"I left the blanket folded," Tilly said into his collar. It seemed suddenly like the most important fact in the world. "So you wouldn't blame yourself."

August pulled back and looked at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and wild and absolutely certain. "You folded the blanket," he repeated, "so I wouldn't blame myself," and then he laughed — one broken bark of it, half drowned in the wind — and hugged her again, and over her head she heard him say to Nell, hoarse: "Where?"

"Harrow Road hut. Old Pell's. She's half-frozen, she needs fire and brandy and dry clothes within the hour."

"Then we're past talking." August lifted Tilly clean off her feet and settled her against his side like cargo, like the most precious cargo ever carried up that coast, and looked at the others, and the light of Bram's lantern showed his face set into the expression Tilly knew from the worst nights, the nights the tower shook — calm built on top of terror, load-bearing calm. "The causeway's drowning. High line, single file, Bram leads. If anyone goes down, we go down together. Nobody gets left. Is that understood?"

"Understood," said Nell.

"Aye," said Bram, and spat into the wind for luck, which the wind threw straight back at him.

They went. The storm walked beside them the whole way, shoulder to shoulder, shrieking its arguments, and the sea came up over the flats behind them with a sound like the end of the world arriving early, and once a wave broke across the low dune ahead and Bram's arm shot out and caught Nell by the collar and hauled her back from a wall of white water that came and went in a breath, and nobody spoke of it afterward because there was no time and no breath to spare.

And through all of it Tilly rode against August's side, wrapped in two coats and one impossible certainty, watching his jaw set against the wind, and she finally understood the thing she'd gotten wrong in her sums. She had subtracted herself to save the total. But you couldn't do arithmetic with people. August had told her that himself, once, with chalk dust on his hands: *four is four*. And six was six, and six was what they were, and the missing berth wasn't a hole in the household — it was a debt, and August Crane did not leave debts unpaid.

The lighthouse rose out of the murk at last, pale and immense, its gallery lamp burning steady above the chaos, and Tilly felt the tears start again, warm this time, thawing her face all the way down.

Inside the tower door, out of the wind for the first time in hours, August set her down and held her at arm's length, and behind him, framed in the doorway, a third figure stepped in from the rain — an officer's greatcoat streaming, an officer's face gray with exhaustion and something older than exhaustion — Captain Vane, of all people in the world, come through the storm to a keeper's door.

August went very still.

"You'd best come in, Ellery," he said quietly. "Before the sea decides for both of us."
