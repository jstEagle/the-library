## 16. The Choice Made

The list lay on the watch room table like a thing that had washed ashore — sodden with meaning, impossible to put down.

August Crane sat before it with the lamp turned low and a pencil worn to a stub in his fist. Outside, the wind had found its voice again after two days of muttering, and it came off the sea in long moaning gusts that set the tower humming like a struck bell. The barometer stood at 28.6 inches and falling still. Every hour it dropped, the evacuation timetable shrank by another fraction, until now there was no timetable at all — only dawn tomorrow, one ship, and five berths for a household of six.

Except that wasn't true anymore either. It was worse than that, and better, and stranger.

Word of the berths had traveled through the town the way water travels through a wall — finding every crack. And all day they had come to his door. Not to beg, most of them. That was the thing he hadn't expected, the thing that sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone: how few of them begged. They came to *ask*, quietly, the way you'd ask a neighbor about the weather, whether there might be room, whether their names might be spoken, and then they'd thank him and go back down the cliff path into the rising wind, and August would stand in his doorway watching them go and feel something break a little more each time.

Tilly had started it, of course. His girl with her fierce arithmetic, counting families on her fingers, writing names on scraps of paper she kept folded in her pocket like contraband. She'd made him see it plainly: the berths were not five seats at a supper table. They were the difference between a name that got carried to safety and a name that stayed behind to be erased by saltwater.

And now the list. He looked down at it again.

*Crane, August.* His own hand had written it first, days ago, when the household had seemed a simple sum — six souls, five berths, and the keeper goes last because the light needs keeping. That part was simple. That part had never troubled him. A lighthouse keeper stays with his light; it was in the word itself, *keeper*, the way *mother* is in the word *mothering*. He had made his peace with the storm months before the storm existed.

But below his name the list had grown. Bram, who would not leave even if August ordered him to, and who could not be ordered anyway. Nell, whose forged papers were safe now inside the stolen ledger's torn pages, but whose face still flinched at every knock. Tilly — Tilly above all, Tilly first, Tilly written twice and underlined once in a moment of weakness he hadn't crossed out. And then the others, the ones the town had sent him, because the town had decided — without asking him, or perhaps by asking him with their eyes at every doorstep — that if anyone must choose, it would be the man who already spent his nights deciding which ships lived.

*Mrs. Halloway.* The widow's name sat near the bottom in Nell's careful clerk's hand, added yesterday evening.

August picked up the page and read the entry again, though he knew it by heart. Agnes Halloway, sixty-one years, widow of the pilot Josiah Halloway drowned nine years back on the very shoals the rescue ship would have to thread. Lungs failing since autumn — Doc Petersen said spring would not find her, and Doc Petersen was an optimist by trade. She lived alone in the blue-shuttered house on Harbor Lane with her husband's charts and a cat named Admiral and no living kin within three hundred miles.

No one else had claimed her berth. No one else had claimed *her*. August understood why — the arithmetic of abandonment was cold, and a dying woman was a heavy passenger — but understanding did not make the cold any warmer.

He heard footsteps on the iron stair, the particular uneven tread he knew better than his own heartbeat, and a moment later Bram filled the doorway, ducking under the lintel out of habit though he'd cleared it ten thousand times. The old sailor's knuckles were split from hauling storm shutters all afternoon, and rain had darkened his shirt across the shoulders.

"She's asking for you," Bram said. "Mrs. Halloway. Sent the Admiral's boy up with a note." He held out a scrap of paper gone soft at the creases.

*Mr. Crane — I know what is being counted. I know what I cost. Come sit with me a while, if you can spare it from the living. — A.H.*

August read it twice. "The living," he repeated. "She writes better sentences than half the officers in this town."

"Aye." Bram lowered himself onto the supply bench, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "You've been staring at that list since the light went round four times. I've been counting."

"And?"

"And you'll wear a groove in the floor before you wear a hole in the problem." Bram leaned forward, forearms on knees, and studied August with eyes gone pale as winter water. "So let an old fool say it plain, since nobody else in this town will. You mean to give your berth away. Not Tilly's, not Nell's, not mine — yours."

August set the pencil down. "The light needs me."

"The light needed you before the army came and it'll need you after, and that's not the reason and we both know it." Bram's voice was gentle, which was worse than anger. "You're choosing to be left behind because being left behind is the only sacrifice left that hasn't been taken from you already. They took your wages, your post, your good name with Vane. They took the ledger and gave it back in pieces. So now you're hoarding the one thing they can't requisition — your own skin — just so you can hand it over yourself, on your own terms."

The words went in deep and clean, the way truth does, hurting precisely because nothing in them was false. August looked at the lamp overhead, turning its slow patient circles, and said nothing.

"I deserted once," Bram went on. "You know the story now — thirty souls on the water and me rowing the other way. I've had fifty years to learn the shape of that night, August, and here's the shape of it: it wasn't cowardice. Cowardice would've been simpler. It was that I couldn't bear to *choose* who came into the boat, so I chose myself out of it entirely, and called it shame ever after." He tapped the table beside the list. "Don't you dare call it duty. Duty keeps the light. This other thing — this is just a man trying to drown quiet."

* * *

He walked down to Harbor Lane as the light failed, through streets running with seawater where the vanguard gales had already breached the low wall. Shingles littered the lane like fallen leaves. Somewhere inland a shutter banged without pause, a metronome counting down hours nobody could number.

Agnes Halloway opened the door herself, wrapped in a shawl the color of old rope, and her breath whistled going in — a thin high sound, like wind through a keyhole, that August had heard from dying men and never from a woman standing upright in her own doorway refusing help.

"You look terrible," she said.

"So do you."

"Good. Then we're honest with each other, which is more than this town has managed all week." She led him to the chair by the fire, the good chair, and took the worn one herself, and the cat climbed onto her lap and stared at August with imperial suspicion. On the wall behind her hung Josiah's charts, ink faded to the brown of dried kelp, every shoal and channel of this coast drawn by a dead man's steady hand.

"They're saying five berths," Agnes said. "For the whole town's worth of need. Five." She laughed, which turned into coughing, which she rode out with the patience of someone long practiced. "Josiah used to say the government measured the sea in inches. I finally believe him."

"Mrs. Halloway—"

"Agnes. We've buried enough between us that you can manage my Christian name." She waved off his protest. "I didn't ask you here for sympathy. I asked because I hear things, and what I hear is that the keeper's household has a list, and my name's on it, and no one will speak for me because everyone's too decent to say aloud what the arithmetic says." Her eyes, watery and bright, fixed on him. "So I'll say it. I'm dying, August. Not next spring, not in autumn — soon, maybe weeks, and the storm may hurry it along. A berth spent on me is a berth drowned. Give it to a child. Give it to Nell's young man, whoever he is. Give it to the fisher boy with the crooked leg who'll never get work otherwise. Any of them weighs less than I do."

"Is that what you want?"

"It's what's *sensible*."

"That isn't what I asked."

The fire popped. The cat kneaded her lap. Outside, the wind rose a full tone, and somewhere down the lane a shutter gave up entirely and went clattering away into the dark like a bird released.

Agnes Halloway was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — smaller, and somehow larger. "My husband drowned nine years ago on those shoals, and do you know what I wanted? Not the body. Not even a grave. I wanted one person in this town to say his name out loud afterward, and not one did, because grief makes cowards of us all. I've been unclaimed for nine years, August Crane. Unclaimed and undrowned and sitting in this house listening to the sea take other people's men." She lifted her chin. "I won't spend my last weeks being a burden people are too kind to refuse. If I go on that ship, I go because someone *chose* me — chose the dying woman, against all sense, because she's theirs. Do you understand the difference?"

August understood. God help him, he understood completely.

"Yes," he said.

"Then think on it." She closed her eyes, breath whistling. "And take a chart on your way out. Josiah's inner channel, the one past the Meridian shoals. Your ledger knows the channels on paper. Josiah knew them in his bones, and I drew what he told me the winter before he died, and I've been waiting nine years for it to matter to somebody." She smiled without opening her eyes. "Seems the sea keeps its own ledger. It always settles accounts."

* * *

Back at the tower, the household waited in the watch room like a jury that had already deliberated. Tilly sat cross-legged on the floor with her scraps of paper spread around her, a general among campaigns. Nell stood at the window watching the harbor, where the transport *Providence* rode at anchor, lights doubled and trembling in black water. Bram occupied his bench, saying nothing, having said everything.

August laid the chart on the table. Then he laid the list beside it. Then he took the pencil stub from his pocket, and the room went very still.

"Five berths," he said. "Six of us, plus Agnes Halloway, plus whatever the town sends up that ladder before dawn. I've done the sums every way there is. There's no way that saves everyone, and every way that saves almost everyone costs exactly one name." He looked at each of them in turn. "I wrote mine first, weeks ago, because the light needs keeping and I'm the keeper. I'm not striking it because I've stopped believing that. I'm striking it because tonight I learned there's a woman on Harbor Lane who'd rather die chosen than live pitied, and because—" his voice caught, and he let it, "—because Bram's right. Some of this is pride wearing duty's coat. But not all of it. The light will need hands tomorrow night regardless of whose commission says so. And the rest of you—" he looked at Tilly, "—the rest of you are the point. You're what all of this was ever for. So."

He crossed out *Crane, August* — one line, clean, the way you strike a name from a muster book — and beneath Mrs. Halloway's entry he wrote in small deliberate letters: *Berth granted. Chosen.*

"There," he said quietly. "Done."

Tilly burst into tears and fury in the same instant, which was her way. "That's not fair, that's not *fair*, you can't just decide—" and Nell gathered her in, and Bram put his huge cracked hand on August's shoulder and squeezed once, hard, saying with pressure what he couldn't say with words, and August stood in the middle of it feeling lighter than he had since the gray sails appeared — hollowed out and filled up in the same motion, surrender and strength turning out to be the same act viewed from different sides.

It was Nell who broke the circle, going rigid at the window.

"Light on the cliff road," she said. "Riding fast. Army horse."

They watched the lantern bob and swing down toward the tower, and August felt the old dread rise and then, strangely, settle — for what more could they take from him? He had already given away the last thing he owned.

The knock came. He opened the door on Captain Ellery Vane, soaked to the ribs, mud to the knees, holding a sealed order in one fist and wearing an expression August had never seen on that proud face before — the look of a man carrying news he hated.

"Crane," Vane said, rain streaming off his hat brim. "The *Meridian* sailed early to beat the storm. She's overloaded with our own wounded, and she's standing straight onto your shoals — and there is no pilot left alive on this coast who knows the channel." He thrust the order forward. "Except, perhaps, a dead man's widow. Get your coat. And pray to God her husband drew good maps."
