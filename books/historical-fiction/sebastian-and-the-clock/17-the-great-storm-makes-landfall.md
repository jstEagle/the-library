## 17. The Great Storm Makes Landfall

The sea came for the town before the sun did.

August stood in the gallery at first light with the barometer's needle pinned against its stop and watched the harbor turn from gray to white. The swell had built all night — long, deep-keeled rollers out of the southeast that lifted the moored fishing boats until their masts swung like pendulums and dropped them again into troughs deep enough to hide them whole. The *Meridian* lay at anchor off the mole, a three-masted transport riding high and empty, her boats already pulling for the stone quay through water the color of wet slate.

Dawn was not so much a light as an argument about one. A bruised band of it sat on the eastern horizon behind the storm cloud, and against it August could see the waves breaking over the outer shoal — not in lines but in walls, standing up twenty feet before they folded.

He had kept lights through forty years of weather. He had a keeper's memory of storms the way other men had scars: the gale of '31 that carried away the north parapet; the October blow that drove a collier onto the Middle Ground with ninety souls aboard and left him hoarse from shouting into a speaking trumpet until sunrise. He knew what this was. This was the thing the others had been rehearsals for.

Behind him, down the spiral stair, his household was doing arithmetic with their lives.

* * *

They had packed in the dark, by lamplight, saying almost nothing. There is a particular silence that falls over people preparing to be separated, and August had heard it once before — at his wife's deathbed, when everyone in the room understood the hour had come and none of them would say so.

Tilly had packed twice. First she had filled a canvas sack with everything she owned in the world, which took four minutes and filled half the sack, and then she had unpacked it and repacked it with things that were not hers at all: August's spare spectacles, Bram's good coat, the tin whistle Nell kept hidden in her trunk because playing it reminded her of a brother transported to Australia. August had watched her do this from the doorway without letting her see him watching. She was eleven years old and she had learned somewhere — he did not want to know where — that love was something you carried for other people.

Nell had burned nothing this time. Her forged papers were safe in the lining of her boot, and the torn pages of the ledger were sewn into the waistband of Bram's oilskins, wrapped in waxed cloth against the salt. She moved through the cottage touching things — the table, the door frame, the shelf where August kept the lamp manuals going back sixty years — as if memorizing them by hand.

Bram had said only one sentence all night. Standing in the yard with the wind tearing at him, looking up at the tower, he had said: "She'll hold." Meaning the light. Meaning August. Meaning, perhaps, both.

Now, in the gray beginning of the last day, they stood together in the watch room while below them the town emptied itself onto the quay.

"Berth list," Nell said, and read it aloud one final time, though every name on it had been argued over, traded, wept over, and finally fixed three days ago. "Mrs. Callow. Tilly Crane. Nell Fairweather. Bram Odell. The Petch children, two berths between them." She looked up. "And you're not on it."

"I'm on it," August said. "Last line."

"You crossed yourself off."

"I gave it away. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Tilly said quietly, "and we all know there isn't."

The wind hit the lantern room then with a fist of rain, and the great lens above them groaned in its carriage like a living thing. August put his hand flat against the glass. Beyond it, the *Meridian*'s recall gun fired — a dull thump swallowed by weather — and the first boatloads began moving toward the ship.

"It's time," he said.

* * *

The quay was chaos wearing the mask of order. Soldiers in soaked greatcoats formed a corridor from the harbor stairs to the ship's boats, and through that corridor the town funneled itself: families with their arms full, old men carrying nothing because there was nothing left to carry, Mrs. Callow upright and furious in her widow's black because dying slowly had not made her patient and neither had drowning quickly threatened to.

August walked his household to the head of the stairs and stopped.

There are farewells that are speeches and farewells that are silences, and he had decided sometime in the black hours before dawn which kind these would be. If he spoke too much he would hear his own voice break, and if they heard it break they would stay, or try to, and staying meant drowning in a town the army had written off.

So he embraced each of them once, briefly, like a man closing a ledger.

To Bram: "Watch the horizon for me."

To Nell: "The pages burn well. Make sure they get the chance."

To Tilly — and here his resolve nearly failed, because she looked up at him with her chin set exactly the way her mother's used to set, defying him to make this mean less than it meant — he knelt down on the wet stone and held her face in both hands and said, "You are the best of everything I ever did right. Do you hear me? Everything."

Her mouth trembled. Her eyes did not. "Keep the light lit," she said. "I'll know if you don't. I'll know even from out there."

"I've never once let it go dark."

"Not once," she agreed. And then, because she was eleven and made of flint and fire: "Not ever."

He handed them down the stairs to the soldiers, and stood at the top, and watched the boat take them out. Bram rowed. Of course Bram rowed — the old man had seized the oars as if he'd been waiting forty years for one more pull against a sea that owed him. Tilly sat in the stern and did not look back until the very end, and then she looked back once, hard, the way you drive a nail, and turned forward forever after.

The boat rose on a swell, hung there against the sky, and slid down the far side of it, and was gone into the spray.

August stood on the emptying quay in the rain and felt the shape of his life change around him, the way a house changes when a wall comes out. For six years the lighthouse had been his work and these three had been his home, and now the work remained and the home was a boat disappearing east, and he found — this surprised him — that he did not regret it. Not one ounce of it. Every name on that berth list had been bought fairly, paid for with his own, and a trade freely made sits easy on a man even in a hurricane.

He turned back toward the tower.

That was when he saw Vane.

* * *

Captain Ellery Vane stood apart from his own evacuation, at the landward end of the quay, in a rain-soaked uniform with no baggage at his feet. His orders — August knew them by now, had heard them recited like scripture — placed him aboard the *Meridian* with the last detachment. The ship's captain would not wait past slack water, and slack water was a fiction today; the tide no longer mattered, only the intervals between the worst of the seas.

"You're late for your boat, Captain," August said, coming to stand beside him.

Vane did not look at him. He looked at the harbor, at the shoal where the seas were standing up and folding, at the tower behind them both.

"So are you," he said.

"I live here."

"Yes." A pause. The wind shoved them both, and Vane took it without shifting his feet. "My orders place me on that ship, Crane. I have read them four times. I signed the acknowledgment myself." He turned then, and August saw something in his face he had never seen in it before — not in the drawing rooms of his courtship, not across the grave they had shared, not in any of the bitter inspections of these last weeks. It looked like relief. Terrible, exhausted relief, the kind a man feels stepping off a ledge. "I have spent nineteen years obeying paper. Paper drowned thirty people at San Cristobal while I stood on a beach and did what the regulations required, and I have done what the regulations required every day since, and yesterday I understood that I will die of it eventually, and that the only question is whether I die of it having once, one time, done the thing instead of the order."

"The light needs two pairs of hands tonight," August said carefully.

"The light needs two pairs of hands tonight," Vane agreed. "And my ship sails without me, and by morning I will be either a hero or a court-martial, and I find I no longer care which." He picked up the one thing he had brought — a small sea chest, naval issue, which he set down at the tower door with the finality of a man surrendering a weapon. "Your wife was my sister, Crane. I requested this posting to find her husband. I told myself it was duty. It was not duty. I am done mistaking the two."

For a long moment the only sound was the sea working at the harbor mouth like a jaw.

"She'd have laughed at you," August said at last. "Right to your face. Then she'd have put you to work."

"I know," said Vane. "It's why I came."

They climbed the tower together, two men who had spent weeks circling each other like dogs across a fence, and closed the watch-room door against the world.

* * *

The storm made landfall at noon, and it did not arrive so much as take possession.

The vanguard squalls of the previous days had been scouts. This was the army entire. The wind came out of the southeast at a pitch August had met perhaps five times in his life, a sound less like weather than like machinery — a vast grinding roar with the whole ocean inside it — and under it the rain went horizontal and stayed there. By one o'clock the low streets were rivers. By two, the seas were coming clean over the mole, whole green hills of water collapsing on the stone and running white through the lanes, and the *Meridian*, weighed down now with the town's last souls, warped off the quay and clawed her way seaward under reefed topsails, rolling her rails under, vanishing and reappearing between the crests like a thing surfacing to breathe.

August stood in the gallery and watched her go until the rain erased her.

Then he went inside and began the work.

The lamp was lit — it had been lit for three days straight and would not go out again while he drew breath — but lit and *kept* were different animals. The great lens needed constant attention in wind like this: the clockwork drive strained against vibration, the mercury bath that floated the assembly slopped in its trough, and every gust that struck the tower sent a shudder up through eighty tons of granite and iron that threatened, each time, to shake the lamp's flame into the chimney and kill it. One man could fight this. Two men could win it.

Vane proved better than his word. The man had served twenty years in His Majesty's navy before the army took him, and his hands remembered machinery the way Bram's remembered rope. Within an hour he had learned the winding crank, the damper, the trick of bracing the lens carriage when the gusts came in trains. He worked in silence, in soaked shirtsleeves, an officer of the Crown hauling weights in a lighthouse he had been sent to requisition, and if he thought about the absurdity of it he never showed it.

At four o'clock, with the light swinging its beam through murk thick as wool, Vane shouted over the wind: "How long has your family kept this?"

"Since 1798! My grandfather laid the foundation stone!"

"And you're the last of them?"

"The last that matters!"

Vane grunted — almost, nearly, a laugh — and threw his weight on the crank.

* * *

Darkness came early and completely, a darkness with texture, a darkness you could lean against. The beam swept out and came back and swept out, and in its revolutions August counted the hours by habit: eight bells, nine bells, the small hours stacking up.

Somewhere past midnight, at the bottom of the light's arc, both men saw it at once.

A light out on the water. Not a masthead light — wrong motion, wrong color. A flare, burning red, down low near the surface, out toward the Middle Ground where no honest vessel had any business being tonight.

Then a second flare, closer in, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat through the storm, the sound of guns — distress signals, fired in desperation by a ship fighting for her life on the shoals.

Vane was at the glass in an instant. August heard him breathe one word, and the word was "*Meridian*?" — but no. The transport had been making east and south, away. This was something else. Something smaller, struggling in from seaward, drawn toward the harbor by the one light burning on this whole drowned coast.

August's hands were already moving — trimming the wick, checking the lens, buying the flame every fraction of steadiness it could hold — and his mind was moving faster, down into the cellar, to the strongbox where a stolen ledger lay wrapped in oilcloth, its pages dense with soundings, channels, the secret knowledge of every passage on this coast.

Whatever was flaring out there in the dark was coming in blind, toward rocks that had killed a hundred ships.

But August Crane knew those rocks by heart.

"Hold the crank!" he roared, and reached for the ledger.
