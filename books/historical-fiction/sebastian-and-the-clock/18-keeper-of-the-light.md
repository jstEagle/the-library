## 18. Keeper of the Light

The sea came up the tower like a fist.

August felt it before he heard it — a shudder through the iron stanchions, through the stone, through the soles of his boots — and then the gallery window above them burst inward and the night poured in, black water and black wind together, a hundredweight of ocean flung forty feet up the rock to find them.

"Down!" he shouted, though Vane was already down, already flat against the spiral stair with his arms over his head, and August dropped beside him as the wave broke across the watch room floor and went roaring back out the way it had come, dragging a chair, a coil of rope, half the chart table's legs with it.

Then silence — that brief, obscene silence between waves — and in it the sound of the lamp.

Still burning.

August climbed. His hands knew the rungs without light. At the top of the stair the lantern room swayed in its gale like a held breath, and there was the great lens turning, turning, throwing its blade of white out over a world gone entirely to water. The wick was holding. The oil pressure was holding. What wasn't holding was everything else.

The seawater stood an inch deep on the watch room floor and rising through seams that hadn't leaked in thirty years. The gallery rail was gone on the north side — torn away whole, he could see the stumps of the bolts shining wet in the lamplight. And through the shattered window the wind came in sideways with rain in it, rain and spray and something harder, gravel or shell, sanding the paint off the walls.

He got the canvas patch over the window frame and held it there while Vane hammered the batten home, the two of them working without speaking, because speech was impossible and because neither of them wanted to hear what the other might say.

They had been at it for four hours since the ship sailed.

Four hours since the *Providence* had stood out of the harbor with every soul who could be crammed aboard, and August had watched her go from this same gallery while Tilly screamed his name from the rail until the wind took even that away. Four hours since Ellery Vane had walked off the evacuation boat, up the rock, and through his door uninvited, dripping, hatless, saying only: "You'll need another pair of hands."

* * *

VANE

Vane had commanded men through the Kaffir campaigns. He had stood on a quarterdeck in the Bay of Biscay when the mizzen went by the board. He had believed, until tonight, that he understood what weather was.

This was not weather. This was the end of the world arriving on schedule, as advertised by the barometer, and the barometer had been right and he had spent three weeks wishing it wrong.

He swung the mallet again. The batten sank into the frame and the canvas bellied and held, for now. Above him the lamp turned with its patient mechanical heart, and below him the whole tower shook like something alive being struck.

Crane worked like no soldier Vane had ever seen. No wasted motion. No complaint. When the wave hit, Crane had been moving before it landed, closing the fuel-room hatch, lashing the spare oil cans down, doing in six seconds what Vane would have needed thirty to think about. Thirty years of nights in this tower lived in the man's hands.

*Margaret's hands,* Vane thought, and hated himself for thinking it.

His sister had had those same quick fingers. She used to mend his gloves when he was a subaltern home on leave, stitching the split seams without comment, without reproach, though she must have known where the money went that should have bought new ones. He had left for India the autumn she married Crane. He had told himself it was coincidence, the timing of orders. He had told himself a great many things.

"You'll want to check the rotation gear," Crane said. Not a request.

Vane went down the ladder into the machinery room, where the clockwork drove the lens around its axis, weights descending on their chain like the slow unwinding of a life. The mechanism was slick with spray. He found the trouble at once — salt crust forming on the governor arms — and did what Crane had trained him to do in the first hour, without ever once saying *I am training you*: scraped it clean, oiled it, tested the fall.

When he came back up, Crane was standing at the lantern rail looking out at the dark, and the dark was looking back.

"The channel buoys will be gone," Crane said. "Every one of them. If anything's still out there tonight, it'll find this shore the hard way."

"Nothing should be out there."

"No," Crane said. "Nothing should be."

And Vane heard it — the thing under the words, the thing Crane carried like ballast. Somewhere out in that blackness were boats that shouldn't be out, and if they existed, then this light was all they'd have, and Crane would stand here until the sea took the tower apart rather than let it go dark.

Vane had come to hate this man for eleven years. It occurred to him now, watching him brace against the next shock of wind, that he had never once hated him for the reason he told himself he did.

* * *

AUGUST

At two in the morning the second great wave came, and it was worse than the first.

August heard it building — a sound beneath the storm's roar like a train coming through the earth itself — and he had time to shout "Off the gallery!" before the mountain of water arrived.

It didn't break over the tower. It *became* the tower. For one full second August was underwater inside the lantern room, the lamp guttering wildly in its case, the world a churning green-black chaos lit from within by the flame's last defiance. He felt himself lifted, thrown, his shoulder striking the lens housing hard enough to ring the brass. Then the water fell away down the stairs in a cataract and the wind rushed back in to fill the vacuum and the lamp —

— the lamp was out.

No. Drowning. The wick smoked, guttered, fought.

August crawled to it through the wash. His hands shook so badly he could barely work the trimmer, but the hands had done this in dreams a thousand times: wick up, draught clear, oil flow steady. The flame caught, shrank, caught again, steadied.

Behind him, coughing seawater, Vane said, "Is it always like this?"

"No," August said. "Usually it's worse."

It was almost a joke. He heard Vane recognize it as one — heard the short exhale that was as close to laughter as grief allowed. They looked at each other in the lamplight, both soaked to the skin, both bleeding somewhere, and for a moment neither of them was an estranged brother-in-law or a requisitioning officer. They were just two men in a tower the sea wanted.

"The glass is going lower," Vane said, nodding at the barometer bolted by the door. August had stopped reading it. There was nothing below the numbers it was showing.

"Then we keep it lit," August said. "That's the whole job. Everything else is commentary."

Vane wiped blood from his brow — a cut above the eye he hadn't mentioned — and said, quietly, "She used to write me about you. In the early years. Before she stopped writing altogether."

August went very still.

"She wrote that you counted everything. Stores, oil, coal. That you measured your whole life in what could be carried." Vane's voice was flat, careful, a man walking on ice. "I decided that made you small. A man who counts tins of oil while my sister — " He stopped. Started again. "I was wrong about what it meant. But I've known I was wrong for a long time, and knowing it changed nothing, and that's the worst kind of knowledge there is."

"Ellery — "

"I requested this posting." The words came out fast now, as if the dam had cracked and whatever came next was beyond his power to stop. "London offered me three commissions. I asked for this coast. This town. This light." He laughed, a terrible dry sound. "They thought I wanted the strategic harbor. Let them. I wanted to see the man my sister chose, and I wanted to tell him — " His jaw worked. "She died alone, Crane. Alone in a house in Portsmouth while I was in Calcutta, and you were here, and neither of us — "

"I was on my way." August's voice cracked on it. Eleven years old, the wound, and still fresh. "I'd sold the commission on my stores. I had passage booked. The letter reached me nine days after. Nine days, Ellery. I have done the arithmetic of those nine days every day of my life."

The storm slammed the tower again, a glancing blow this time, and neither of them moved.

"I know," Vane said. "Her housekeeper kept her correspondence. I read it after. Every letter you sent. She read them all aloud to herself, the housekeeper said. Even the ones that arrived too late to answer."

August turned away. He put his hand flat against the warm brass of the lens housing, the way he had put his hand on Margaret's back in the last year of her life when the coughing came, feeling the engine of her run.

"I built this light the year after," he said. "Not built it — I mean I rebuilt the character. Changed the flash. So that any ship that had known the old light would know something had changed here. Something lost." He swallowed. "That's not seamanship. That's just a man talking to his dead wife through a lamp."

For the first time since he'd marched ashore with his requisition orders, Ellery Vane looked at him without armor.

"Margaret would have hated this storm," Vane said. "She hated wind."

"She loved it. She only pretended otherwise so I wouldn't feel guilty enjoying it."

They were both silent, and then — absurdly, horribly, wonderfully — they were both laughing, ragged and salt-burned and half-drowned, laughing in the lantern room of a tower the ocean was trying to eat, and August understood that this was what reconciliation was made of: not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever fully, but shared peril cracking open the places where grievance had calcified, and something younger moving in the gap. Grief, maybe. Love's stubborn residue. Whatever it was, it was warmer than what had been there before.

* * *

VANE

At three o'clock the fuel line clogged.

Crane diagnosed it in seconds — salt water in the reservoir, drawn up through a seam during the flooding — and the fix required someone in the fuel room below, hand-pumping the line clear while the other fed the lamp drop by drop from the reserve can, keeping the flame alive on a teaspoon of oil at a time.

"I'll pump," Vane said. "You hold the light."

Crane hesitated. It was the hardest work in the tower, the pumping — cold, blind, brutal, shoulders screaming — and it was also, Vane realized, the work of trust. The man above controlled whether you drowned in the dark below.

"You know the valve order?" Crane asked.

"You taught me it six hours ago."

"You weren't listening."

"I was listening to every word," Vane said. "I've been listening since the cutter anchored. You just couldn't tell."

Something passed between them that had no name. Crane handed him the pump handle.

The fuel room was a coffin with pipes. Vane pumped in the dark to the rhythm of the storm, and the work stripped everything out of him that wasn't essential — the rank, the orders from London, the requisition, the ledger, the whole rotten architecture of duty he'd hidden behind. What was left was simple. A man below, keeping a promise upward. A man above, keeping a promise outward, to ships he'd never meet.

Somewhere past four, his arms failing, the rhythm broken, he heard footsteps on the ladder, and Crane came down and took the handle from him without a word and said, "Go up. Watch the flame. Tell me if it dips below the carrier."

So they traded. All night, they traded — pump and lamp, lamp and pump — two men passing the burden between them like a bucket in a fire line, and Vane understood that this, finally, was what his sister had seen in this man. Not the counting. The carrying. The way he distributed weight so that no single point broke.

Near dawn the storm eased — not ended, never ended, but eased, the scream dropping to a growl — and Vane climbed up to the lantern and looked east, where the horizon was deciding whether to exist.

It didn't exist. There was no dawn. There was only a slightly less absolute darkness, and in it, low and far off toward the shoals, a shape.

A ship's lights. Three of them, stacked wrong, listing.

Then a rocket went up from the shape — white, desperate, torn apart by wind — and Vane felt his stomach drop through the floor of the world.

"That's a transport," he said. "That's army tonnage."

Crane was already at the telegraph shutter, already reading the shape through the long glass, and when he lowered it his face had gone gray.

"It's the *Meridian*," he said. "Relief transport, out of Portsmouth. And she's on the shoal."
