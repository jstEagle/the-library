## 1. The Weight of Small Hands

The pestle made its small music in the mortar, a sound like rain on a tin roof, and Ines let it carry her the way other people let hymns carry them. Vermilion first — always vermilion, because Master Alaric had said red was the color that woke a map, the way a struck bell wakes a sleeping house. She ground it fine as flour, then finer, until the powder lay in the mortar's bowl like the heart of some small murdered thing.

Around her the workshop breathed its morning breath: linseed oil and lampblack, the sour tang of ox gall, wet vellum steaming faintly on the drying frames by the window even though the fog outside had not yet burned off. Nine indentured workers bent at their benches. No one spoke. The Meridian Trading Company did not pay for talk; it paid for hands, and Ines Delacroix had learned long ago that her hands were worth more when no one remembered they belonged to a person.

She was seventeen and had been invisible for eleven years, which is nearly the whole of a life.

* * *

Her father had been a pearl diver before the debt took him, and her mother before that, and Ines had been born with salt still in her blood and the sea's whole gray vocabulary in her ears. When the company men came for the boat — the *Sable Petrel*, her father's pride, sold out from under them to cover a season's bad luck — they had taken Ines too, almost as an afterthought. A child of eight could grind pigment. A child of eight cost less than feed for a mule. The clerk who signed her papers hadn't bothered to learn her name; he'd written it wrong on the indenture and never corrected it, so that officially she did not quite exist. *Inez Delacroy.* A ghost with a debt attached.

She had survived by being useful and unseen. Useful: she could mend a torn chart so the mend vanished into the grain of the paper, match any color from memory alone, stretch vellum without a single wrinkle. Unseen: she had made herself small in the way divers make themselves small, folding inward, conserving breath. The foreman, Ovid Marsh — a man shaped like a closed door, with a voice like a key turning — had once gone a full year without speaking directly to her. She had counted those days the way other children counted festivals.

But there had been one man who saw her.

* * *

Master Alaric Voss had come to the workshop when Ines was thirteen, transferred from the charting office above with his ink-stained cuffs and his smell of cold air and pipe smoke. He was old the way driftwood is old, weathered to something harder than the tree began as. On his first morning he had walked the rows of benches, stopped at hers, and picked up a chart she was mending — a coastline of the Amber Reach, torn nearly in half by some careless ship's officer.

"Who did this repair?" he'd asked the room.

Silence. Ines had kept her head down, brush steady, certain she would be blamed for damaging it.

"I did," she'd said finally, because lying badly seemed worse than being punished.

Alaric had turned the chart toward the light, studying the seam where torn became whole. "Hm," he'd said. And then, to the room at large: "The rest of you sign your work like merchants signing bills of lading. This girl signs nothing and leaves no trace, and yet her work will outlast all of yours." He'd set the chart down gently, the way one sets down a sleeping animal. "Invisibility is a skill, girl. But mark me — it is also a cage."

He had never called her *girl* again after that day. He had called her nothing at all for three weeks, watching her work, and then one evening he'd set a second mortar beside hers and said, "Prussian blue wants grinding finer than vermilion or it'll granulate. Show me what you know." It was not praise. It was better than praise. It was an assumption that she had knowledge worth showing.

For six years he had taught her in that sideways fashion — never lessons, only shared labor, questions posed to the room that were really meant for her. Why does this current bend here? What would you name an unnamed shoal? Where does a coast end — at the waterline, or at the last hill a sailor can see? She had answered him inside her head mostly, and sometimes aloud when the foreman was elsewhere, and Alaric had listened to her answers the way no one had ever listened to anything about her: fully, hungrily, as though she might contain a continent he had not yet charted.

Then, two years ago, on a survey run to the Serrated Islands, Master Alaric Voss had drowned.

They said a swell took him off the rocks while he sketched. They brought back his coat, heavy with seawater, and his satchel, empty. There was no body — the sea keeps what it takes — but the company held its memorial anyway, efficient as an invoice, and the workshop observed two minutes of silence that lasted ninety seconds because Ovid Marsh checked his watch.

Ines had not cried at the memorial. She had gone home to the dormer loft over the tannery and cried all night instead, silently, the way she did everything, and in the morning she had ground her vermilion and been invisible again. That was the arithmetic of an indentured life: grief was a luxury item, and she carried no credit.

* * *

Now, three hours past dawn, the inventory ledger lay open before her and the morning had gone strange.

It happened twice a year: the stocktaking, when every drawer, every skin of vellum, every cake of pigment was counted against the ledger and the shortfall charged against the workers' debts collectively, which meant, in practice, against whoever the foreman disliked most. Ines had done eleven inventories. She knew the workshop's contents the way she knew her own teeth.

So when she came to the bank of flat drawers beneath the western window — Alaric's old bench, kept unused since his death, because Marsh said reassigning it would be *bad morale*, though everyone understood he simply liked having a dead man's station stand empty as a warning — she expected dust and dried-out gum arabic.

Instead she found the drawers locked.

All of them except the bottom one, which was locked too, but differently. The others wore the standard company padlocks, dull iron things stamped with the Meridian compass rose. The bottom drawer bore no lock at all — only a plate of brass screwed over the keyhole, and engraved upon the brass, in a hand she knew down to the pressure of its nib, the way you know a face:

**I.D.**

Not the company's initials. Not Meridian. *I.D.*

Ines Delacroix. Or — her mind supplied, unhelpfully — *indenture document*. But no. The engraving was Alaric's hand. She had watched that hand for six years, traced its letters in her head at night, the distinctive open-topped *D*, the crossbar of the *I* slanted like a spar in wind. He had cut these letters into brass, which meant he had done it deliberately, slowly, with time to think about who would read them.

Two years ago. Before the drowning. Perhaps the very night before.

"Ines!" Marsh's voice cracked across the room like a whip over water. "Ledger says four ounces of dragon's blood short. You're on pigments. Explain."

"Ground to powder, sir. Weighed loose before tempering. The loss is in the binder, not the stock." She recited it flatly, eyes down, and felt his gaze slide off her the way water slides off oiled cloth. Invisible. Useful. Safe.

But her hand, when she returned to the drawer, was not steady.

She pressed her palm flat against the brass plate. Cold, then warming under her skin. Someone had claimed this drawer — had claimed *her* — and hidden the claim behind a lock, in a dead man's bench, in a room where she had spent eleven years ensuring no one would ever claim her at all. It should have frightened her. It did frighten her. But beneath the fear ran something else, older and more shameful: the ache of it. To be named. To have someone carve your initials in metal and mean *you*.

She looked around. Marsh was at the far wall berating the ink boy. The others bent to their work, dutifully blind. Ines slid her fingernail — diver's daughter's nails, thick as little hulls — under the edge of the brass plate and pried.

The screws had been filed nearly through. The plate came away with a soft click, and beneath it, seated in the keyhole, was a key. Wrapped around the key, a scrap of oilcloth, and wrapped in the oilcloth, folded small as a moth, a note in Alaric's hand:

*If you are reading this, little navigator, then either I am dead — which I doubt — or the company has grown impatient — which I also doubt. Either way: turn the key, and remember that a chart of the truth is worth more than a life of the lie. Come find what I left you. Ask no permission. — A.V.*

*Which I doubt.* She read it three times. Her mouth had gone dry as raw pigment.

Because the note was dated. Dated eleven days ago.

Eleven days ago, Master Alaric Voss had been two years drowned.

Outside, the fog was burning off the harbor, and somewhere down among the warehouses a bell rang once, twice — the counting-bell, marking the change of shift. Ines closed her fist around the key until its teeth bit her palm, and for the first time in eleven years, she did not care who saw her hands shake.
