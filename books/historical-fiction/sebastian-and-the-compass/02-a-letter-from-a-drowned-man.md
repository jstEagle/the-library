## 2. A Letter from a Drowned Man

Dusk came to the workshop the way water came into a diver's lungs—slowly, then all at once. Ines had learned to mark the hour not by the bell but by the light: how it went thin and amber across the drafting tables, how the vellum glowed like something breathing, how Master Alaric's empty chair threw a shadow longer than any man could rightly cast.

Two years, and she still caught herself waiting for him to fill it.

She ground azurite at the window while the harbor turned to hammered tin below. The mortar's rhythm was the only prayer she trusted anymore—grind, lift, grind—and her hands did it without her, brown-stained fingers moving with the patience of someone who had long ago stopped expecting anything from the world except more work. Around her the other indentureds packed their tools for the night. Nobody spoke to her. That was the arrangement she'd spent six years perfecting: be useful, be quiet, be furniture. The company did not hang furniture.

"Ines." Foreman Grole's voice, wet and gravelly as a bilge pump. "The lapis order came short again. You'll make do."

"Yes, foreman."

"And lock up proper tonight. Inspectors have been sniffing round the counting house." He said it carelessly, the way men said things that would later matter enormously. "Company's twitchy about its papers lately."

Her stomach performed a small, practiced drop. *Papers.* In the Meridian Trading Company, paper was worth more than pearls and killed faster than drowning. She nodded and kept grinding until his boots had slapped away down the stair, then sat a moment in the settling dust with her heart tapping against her ribs like a moth against glass.

* * *

It was full dark when the knock came—not a knock, really, but a soft repeated pressing of knuckles, the sound of a man too weak to insist. Ines almost didn't answer. Answering doors was for people who existed.

But the light under the door had been broken by two feet for some time, and the feet were swaying.

She opened it on a stranger propped against the frame like driftwood against a piling. Tall, or he would have been; hunger had folded him inward, collapsed him into himself the way wet paper dries. Salt-crusted coat. Cheeks hollowed to architecture. Eyes the color of deep water over sand—tired, watchful, and fixed on her face with an attention so total it felt like being touched.

"You're the girl who grinds the pigments," he said. His voice was rust and rope. "Ines Delacroix."

Every instinct she owned stood up screaming at once. *No one knows your name. No one is supposed to know your name.* "You've mistaken me for—"

"He told me you'd say that." The man fumbled inside his coat, movements clumsy, and produced a packet wrapped in oilskin and tied with waxed cord—the kind of wrapping seamen used for things that must survive water. He held it out. "He said you'd refuse it, too. Said, *tell my Ines she'll refuse, and tell her refusing won't save her either.*"

The word *my* went through her like a fishhook.

"Who are you?" she whispered, though she already knew. She'd known since the eyes. She'd seen those eyes before, two years ago, in a younger and better-fed face standing beside Alaric's chair, learning the mending of coastlines.

"Sebastian. I was his courier." A ghost of a smile, gone at once. "And I'm the reason they think he drowned."

* * *

She let him in because the alternative was letting him collapse in the corridor where the night porter would find him, and that was a thread she couldn't afford to leave lying. She bolted the door, lit no lamp beyond the stub already burning at her bench, and watched him lower himself onto Alaric's old stool as though his legs had been waiting years for permission.

The oilskin packet sat between them on the bench like something alive.

"Open it," Sebastian said. "Please. I've carried it eight hundred miles and I'd like to stop carrying it."

Her fingers weren't steady. She hated that they weren't steady. Inside the oilskin was a letter, and the handwriting on it stopped her breath mid-inhale—the tall ascenders, the little hooks on the *g*s, the ink pressed hard enough to emboss the page. She had spent three years mixing that man's colors. She would know his hand drowned, burned, or buried.

*Ines,* it began.

*If you are reading this, then Sebastian has found you, which means I am dead in earnest this time, or nearly enough. Forgive the theatre of my funeral. A drowned cartographer asks fewer questions than a disappeared one, and the Company believed what the sea told them, as the Company always does. It was the only lie I ever told them that worked.*

*I have spent these two years finishing the atlas we spoke of once, when you thought I was talking about trade routes. I was not. Every smuggled lane, every bribed harbor official, every hull that sailed Meridian colors and came home heavier with contraband than cargo—it is all drawn, Ines, all of it, in cipher upon cipher, in my hand and no other. When finished and read aloud in the right court, it will hang a dozen directors of the Meridian Trading Company.*

*It is also unfinished. My hands have begun to fail—I will not tell you what from; you will imagine something kinder than the truth. I need an heir. Not a courier; couriers can be bought. An apprentice. Someone who knows my pigments, my shortcuts, my temper. Someone invisible.*

*I have watched you for three years making yourself nothing, child, and I want you to know it never fooled me. Nothing about you is small. You simply learned early that the world punishes size.*

*The atlas is yours if you'll take it. Refuse, and burn this letter, and live as long as furniture lives—which is until the house burns down.*

*Yours in craft and conspiracy,
Alaric*

She read it twice. Then a third time, lips moving, the way she'd taught herself letters by lantern light at seven years old, huddled behind her father's diving boat while the waves talked their endless gossip.

"No," she said finally.

Sebastian nodded slowly, unsurprised. "He said you would."

"I'm indentured. My contract runs four more years. If I'm caught with so much as a stolen quill they extend the term; if I'm caught with *treason*, they—" She didn't finish. Everyone in the workshop knew what happened to indentureds caught stealing from Meridian. There was a gibbet post on the customs quay that kept the lesson current.

"Then burn it," Sebastian said quietly. "I'll go. I'm sorry to have frightened you. But Ines—" He leaned forward, and the lamplight moved in his hollowed face like water moving over a wreck. "They're already looking for me. Which means they'll look here. Which means your careful invisibility has an expiration date now, whether you take the atlas or not. Alaric understood that. It's why he sent me to you last and not first."

"That's not a reason. That's a threat wearing a dead man's handwriting."

"It's both," he said. "Most true things are."

She wanted to throw him out. She wanted it with her whole body—wanted the door shut, the packet burning in the stove, her life restored to its small safe dimensions of pigment and silence. Instead she heard herself ask, "Where is the atlas?"

His silence was answer enough, and she cursed herself for asking, because now she was implicated even in the question.

* * *

The inspectors came at dawn. Not for her—never yet for her—but through, past, around her, three grey men in grey coats walking the aisles of the workshop with their fingers trailing along the benches like men testing fruit. They counted reams of paper. They weighed ingots of silver point. They asked Grole questions in voices too low to hear, and Grole's answers made him sweat, and watching him sweat was the most instructive thing Ines had seen in six years.

At noon the proclamation went up on the workshop door, written in the company's beautiful, murderous script:

*BY ORDER OF THE MERIDIAN TRADING COMPANY: A FULL AUDIT OF ALL CHARTS, INSTRUMENTS, AND RECORDS OF THIS WORKSHOP SHALL BE CONDUCTED WITHIN THIRTY DAYS' TIME. ALL PERSONNEL SHALL SUBMIT TO INTERVIEW. DISCREPANCIES WILL BE ADJUDICATED UNDER ARTICLE NINE.*

Article Nine. Even the youngest indentureds went quiet at Article Nine.

Ines stood before the notice a long time, the crowd of workers parting around her as it always did, around nobody, around furniture. Thirty days. And somewhere in this building or out of it was a letter with her name in a dead man's hand, hidden beneath the false bottom of the pigment chest where she'd put it that morning with hands that shook and then, strangely, stopped shaking.

Thirty days for the company to find whatever trail led from Alaric's drowned ghost to a half-starved courier to a pearl diver's daughter who had spent her whole life learning how not to be seen.

That night she unbolted the door to the alley and found Sebastian waiting in the dark exactly as if he'd known she would come—thin as winter, patient as tide.

"The audit," she said. "Thirty days."

"I heard." Something kindled in the deep-water eyes. Not triumph. Relief, maybe. Or recognition.

"If I do this," Ines said, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, larger than it had ever been allowed to sound, "we do it my way. No one sees you. No one sees me. We work after hours, and Grole suspects nothing, and when it's done—" She faltered. She hadn't thought past *done*. "When it's done, I don't know what happens. But I'm done being furniture in a house that burns people."

Sebastian smiled then, fully, and it remade his ruined face into something almost young.

"Welcome to the family," he said. "Such as it is. Two orphans and a dead man's atlas."

Inside, on her bench, under the stub of candle, the letter lay where she'd left it, and Alaric's embossed words seemed to press upward through the page like something trying to surface. *Nothing about you is small.*

Ines rolled up her sleeves and reached for the locked drawer's key, and outside, far off across the black water, a ship's bell rang once—as if the sea itself had taken note, and started counting down the thirty days.
