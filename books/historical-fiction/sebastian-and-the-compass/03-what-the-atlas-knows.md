## 3. What the Atlas Knows

*Ines*

The workshop after dark was a different country. By day it belonged to the company—twelve benches, forty hands, the smell of cut vellum and boiled hide-glue, foreman Odo Vassek's boots striking the floorboards like a metronome counting out everyone's debt. But at night, when Ines turned the key in the door behind her and lit a single tallow stub shielded inside a tin lantern, the room became hers. Became Alaric's. Became theirs.

She had learned to work by sound before she'd learned to work by sight. Her father had taught her that, down in the dark of the diving bell, where the sea pressed close enough to touch and the only light came through three inches of green water overhead. *Feel first,* he'd said. *Look second.* Now she ran her fingertips along the edge of the atlas's hidden panel—the loose board beneath the pigment shelf—and felt the spine of the unfinished thing, thick as a psalter, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp.

Sebastian arrived on the fourth night, slipping through the coal-yard gate with the ease of a man who had spent two years being hunted. He carried bread under his coat and a new bruise along his jaw, and he set both on the bench without comment.

"Vassek asked about you today," Ines said. "Asked when you'd been seen last. Asked if I knew where the stranger with the grey coat had gone."

"What did you tell him?"

"That strangers all look alike to me." She unwrapped the atlas and opened it to the page they'd reached the night before: the eastern coastline of the Serpent Isles, drawn in Alaric's hand but ciphered—every depth sounding replaced with a letter, every headland marked with a symbol from no heraldry she knew. "He looked at me too long. Like he was trying to remember whether he'd ever noticed me before."

"That's more dangerous than being noticed."

"I know." She dipped her pen. "It's why I've spent six years making sure nobody could pick my face from a crowd of ten."

Sebastian pulled up the second stool—Alaric's stool, its legs worn splayed by the old man's restless knees—and bent over the page. His hair had gone lank; there was salt still in it, she suspected, from whatever boat had carried him into the harbor. But his eyes were quick, and when he spoke the cipher's logic aloud, something in Ines unclenched for the first time since the letter.

"It's a substitution keyed to the Psalms," he said. "Alaric loved that trick. Every letter stands for the one above it in a verse—he used the *De profundis*. Out of the depths. He thought it was funny."

"He would." And she found she could say that now, could speak of Alaric in past tense without the grief closing over her like cold water. Faked deaths changed grammar. "So the sounding here—*mem*—is actually..."

"Fourteen fathoms. Which is wrong." Sebastian tapped the coast. "Deliberately wrong. The published charts show fourteen. The truth is nine. Nine fathoms and a sandbar the company's ships clear only at certain tides—which means only certain ships, carrying certain cargo, at certain hours, know to clear it at all."

Ines stared at the coastline and saw it whole for the first time. Not a map. A ledger. Every corrected sounding was a smuggling route; every ciphered tide-table was a schedule; every innocent-looking anchor mark was a warehouse, a paymaster, a name. The Meridian Trading Company had built its fortune on pearls and pepper and honest freight, and underneath that honest freight ran this: a second geography, invisible as she had made herself, mapped in secret by a dying old man who had chosen to drown rather than let it die with him.

"He wasn't just finishing an atlas," she said quietly. "He was writing an indictment."

"He was writing evidence. The indictment comes later." Sebastian's mouth twisted. "If we live long enough."

Outside, the harbor bell rang the second hour, and somewhere down the lane a dog barked itself hoarse at nothing. Ines ground a fresh pinch of verdigris, because her hands needed occupation even when her mind was elsewhere, and the smell of it—sharp, green, alive—settled her nerves the way prayer never had.

They worked until the candle drowned in its own fat.

* * *

*Sebastian*

Sebastian had kept secrets so long they had grown into his skeleton, load-bearing ones. He told lies the way other men breathed. So it unsettled him, night after night in the lamplit workshop, how badly he wanted to tell this girl the truth.

She was seventeen, maybe eighteen, with ink stains to the second knuckle and her father's patience in her hands. She worked the way water worked—around obstacles, through cracks, wearing things smooth. Alaric had written her name on a drawer and called her heir, and Sebastian had watched her refuse the word *apprentice* three times while accepting everything it meant: the nights, the risk, the slow deciphering of a dead-alive man's final obsession.

On the seventh night, with rain ticking against the shutters and the audit now eleven days away, she caught him staring at the letter and said, "You knew him longer than I did. You loved him. Why won't you tell me what you did for him?"

"I was his courier," Sebastian said. Because she had earned it, and because caution, he was learning, was a currency that devalued the moment you started spending it anyway. "For eleven years. Charts, letters, plates—anything Alaric couldn't trust to the post or to company men. I carried them between the islands. I was good at it. Nobody remembers a thin man on a boat."

"And then?"

"And then I carried one thing too many." He turned the letter over in his hands, its creases gone soft as cloth. "A single folio. Soundings off the Vane Shoals, plus names—not ports, *names*. Company factors who took their cut off the smuggled runs. Alaric had finally stopped being careful, or decided it didn't matter anymore. I delivered it to a magistrate in Corvo Bay who swore he was honest." He laughed, short and ugly. "He was honest the way a hook is honest. Within a week the company knew what Alaric had, knew I'd carried it, and knew exactly which courier to hunt. The magistrate hanged himself—or was helped—and Alaric went into the sea, or appeared to. And I went into hiding, because the company doesn't kill couriers, Ines. They arrest them. They put them on a ship to the penal colonies with their tongue cut out so they can't testify, and they keep them alive for thirty years as a warning."

The rain filled the silence. Ines didn't flinch, didn't pity him—that was the thing he liked best about her, the thing that frightened him most. She just watched him with those diver's eyes that had already looked into deep water and come back.

"So the audit," she said slowly. "It isn't routine. They're not hunting the atlas. They're hunting *you*."

"They're hunting whoever finishes what Alaric started. I'm simply the easiest trail." He spread his scarred palms flat on the bench. "Which is why I should leave tonight, take the atlas half-done, and never darken this workshop again."

"No."

"Ines—"

"You'll leave when it's finished, and not before, because you can't decode the tide-ciphers alone—I've seen you try, you read the Psalms like a customs clerk reads poetry." She said it flatly, without cruelty, and something in Sebastian's chest loosened and broke at once. "You spent eleven years carrying other people's words alone. You don't have to do it anymore. That's what an apprentice *is*, you stubborn ghost."

He wanted to argue. Instead he heard himself say, "Alaric used to call me that. Apprentice. Jokingly. Said I had a cartographer's soul trapped in a smuggler's feet."

"Then sit down," Ines said, "and use them both."

And he sat. And they worked. And outside, through the rain-slit shutter, a lantern moved along the workshop wall—paused—moved on. Neither of them saw it. But the man holding it, Foreman Odo Vassek, stood a long time in the wet dark, doing sums of his own: a stranger's face he half-remembered, an indentured girl suddenly rich in secrets, and eleven days until the inspectors came to count everything in the chart room—including, perhaps, the things that weren't on any inventory.
