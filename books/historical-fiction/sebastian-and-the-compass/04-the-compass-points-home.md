## 4. The Compass Points Home

The last coastline took three nights.

Ines worked it by candle-stub and memory, her brush whispering vermilion into the bays of a shore she had never walked but now knew the way she knew her own knuckles. Alaric's cipher had grown easier as she went — his hand in hers across two years of silence, teaching her still. Each decoded reef was a smuggler's door; each inked channel, an accusation. When she finished the final sheet she did not feel triumph. She felt the way the sea felt before weather: flat, attentive, holding everything.

Sebastian slept sitting up against the map press, his gaunt face slack, one hand curled around the letter that had started all this as if even in dreams he feared someone would take it back. In the guttering light he looked younger than he had at dusk on the first night, and more breakable. She had learned his story in pieces between coastlines — how Alaric had pulled him out of a debtor's cell in Corvane, made him courier, made him son in every way but paper; how the company had caught him once already and let him go only because they wanted the atlas, not the boy.

"They will come for us soon," she said to the sleeping room. "And I think I am glad."

She surprised herself saying it. But it was true. Fear had been her native language for eleven years, since her father's boat came up empty and the Meridian Company folded her smallness into its ledgers. Now the fear had a shape she could work against, like pigment ground fine enough to float.

Above them, through the floorboards, came the sound of Tobiah Voss's boots crossing the loft. Slow tonight. Deliberate. The foreman had been slow and deliberate for six days, ever since he'd found wax shavings where no candles were kept.

* * *

Sebastian woke to the smell of lamp oil.

Not burning — just present, thick in the workshop air, the way it hung over the harbor wharfs at dawn. For a moment he could not place why that should frighten him, and then his body remembered what his mind had not finished assembling: Voss. The loft. The way the man had smiled yesterday, showing teeth gone brown as old varnish, and asked Sebastian — politely, horribly politely — whether he'd ever been to Corvane.

He sat up too fast. The workshop swam. Ines was not there.

For one lurching heartbeat he thought: taken. Then he saw the note pinned beneath the compass needle on Alaric's old desk, her handwriting small and exact as her brushwork: *Drying loft, east wall. Come alone. He watches the stairs.*

The drying loft smelled of salt and hide glue. Ines stood among the hanging sheets with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, and beside her, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the atlas — finished, whole, heavier than any book had a right to be. Forty-one sheets. Two years of a drowned man's life. Eleven years of a pearl diver's daughter learning to be invisible so that one day she could carry something this visible.

"It's done," she said.

"I know," Sebastian said, because he could hear it in her voice. "God help us, it's done."

"Voss met with a company inspector this morning. I saw them from the drying loft window. He gave them something." Her jaw tightened. "A page. One of my practice sheets, with the cipher half-worked. He's been stealing from the waste basket for weeks."

Sebastian felt the floor tilt under his certainty. "Then they'll have the audit moved up. They could come tonight."

"They could come now." Ines lifted the lantern. In its light her eyes were steady, and older than nineteen. "Which is why we don't wait for their schedule. My father's diving bell is still in the company salvage shed — the round one, copper, with the false bottom I used to hide in when I was small. It holds forty pounds of lead ballast and it can hold forty-one sheets of vellum. The *Cormorant* sails on the morning tide for the Free Ports, and her bosun owes me for mending charts he never should have been copying."

"You've planned this."

"I've been planning it since the night you walked in with a dead man's letter." Almost, she smiled. "You brought me a future, Sebastian. I'm only arranging its shipping."

* * *

They nearly made it.

That was the sentence Sebastian would carry afterward, turning it over in exile like a stone with a flaw through its heart: *we nearly made it.* The bell was loaded. The oilcloth was sealed. Ines had bribed the shed watchman with a repaired chronometer and the *Cormorant*'s longboat waited at the third slip, and they had crossed the yard between buildings in the blue-black hour before dawn when even the gulls were silent —

— and then lanterns bloomed along the workshop gallery like a rash of small suns, and Voss's voice rolled down through the dark, fat with satisfaction.

"There they are. The ghost's little ghosts."

Sebastian grabbed Ines's wrist. The salvage shed's seaward wall had a gap at the foundation, rotted timber above the tideline, a crack barely wide enough for the bell if they tipped it and pushed. Behind them, boots on gravel, eight men at least, inspector's coats pale as fish bellies in the lantern light.

"The gap," he breathed. "Go. I'll draw them—"

"No." Ines said it quietly, which somehow made it louder than shouting. She put both hands against the bell and shoved, and the copper groaned across the stones toward the gap. "Listen to me. If we're both caught, the atlas is caught — they'll search the boat, they'll search you, they'll tear the Free Ports apart looking for Alaric's heir. But if one of us is caught *alone*—" she looked at him, and her face in the lantern spill was calm as deep water, "—then the other is free, and the atlas is free, and everything Alaric drowned himself to make survives."

"I am not leaving you."

"Yes," she said, "you are. Because I'm asking. Because I spent eleven years being invisible and I have exactly one thing worth being seen for, and this is it." She pushed the bell into the gap. It stuck, turned, scraped — and slid through, out into the shingle and the waiting dark beyond. "Take it to the *Cormorant*. Take it to the Free Ports. Publish it, sell it, nail it to every customs house from here to the ice. And when they ask you who else was in the workshop—"

She stepped backward, away from him, into the open lane where the lanterns would find her. She was smiling.

"—tell them nothing. Let me be useful one last time."

"Ines—"

Boots rounded the corner. Lantern light broke across the yard. And Ines Delacroix, who had spent her whole indenture perfecting the art of being unseen, planted her feet, lifted her chin, and made herself impossible to miss.

"She's here!" Voss shouted, triumphant, pointing. "The girl! Take her!"

Sebastian took the bell's rope in both hands and hauled it toward the water, and if anyone had seen his face in that moment they would have understood that some promises are heavier than copper. The shingle tore at his palms. The sea came up cold around his knees, then his chest, and the longboat's silhouette rose against the first gray suggestion of dawn, and behind him — growing smaller with every stroke, though he did not stop hearing it — came the sound of Ines's voice, clear and unafraid, telling eight armed men exactly where they could look for the atlas.

* * *

They put her in the inspector's own cabin, which she supposed was meant to be an insult or an honor and was, in truth, simply a room with a chair. Through the stern windows she watched the harbor wake: the *Cormorant* standing out on the morning tide, sails filling, the longboat already hoisted and empty. Somewhere beneath that ship's honest cargo rode forty-one sheets of vellum and a dead man's revenge, bound for ports where the Meridian Company's writ ran thin as harbor ice.

Inspector Hale — dry, precise, tired-eyed — set Alaric's letter on the table between them and folded his hands.

"You understand your position, Miss Delacroix. Forgery. Theft of company property. Conspiracy. Your indenture means nothing against these charges. You could hang."

"I understand my position," Ines said.

"Then tell me where the atlas is, and who helped you, and perhaps the Company shows mercy. We are not monsters. We reward honesty."

She looked at the letter — at the beloved, impossible handwriting naming her apprentice, her father-in-craft, her own — and felt the old ache rise one final time: the ache of the unclaimed child grinding pigments in the corner, waiting for someone to say *you are mine.* But it wasn't an ache anymore. It had changed states, the way ore changes in fire.

Master Alaric had claimed her. Sebastian had claimed her. The work itself had claimed her, line by line, bay by bay. Whatever happened in this cabin, she would never again be the girl nobody saw.

"My name," she said clearly, "is Ines Delacroix. Daughter of Tomas Delacroix, diver. Apprentice to Master Alaric, cartographer. And you may write that down however you like, Inspector, because those names are the only confession you'll get from me."

Hale studied her for a long moment. Then he leaned forward, lowered his voice, and said the last thing in the world she expected:

"Alaric taught my daughter to draw, before he 'drowned.' Tell me, Miss Delacroix — does the atlas include the northern routes?"
