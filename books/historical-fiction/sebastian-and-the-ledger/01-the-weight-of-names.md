## 1. The Weight of Names

The lamp in the infirmary's back room had a habit of guttering at the worst moments, and Wendell Almeida had long ago stopped mending the wick. It was better this way — a small darkness to work inside, a small light to work by. Names did not need much illumination. They needed only a steady hand and a quiet room.

Wendell sat with the ledger open on their knees, the floorboard lifted beside them like the lid of a coffin for something too precious to bury deep. The board came up without a sound now; five years of practice had taught them exactly where the grain ran, exactly how to ease it free of its nails so that no one passing in the corridor would hear the whisper of wood on wood. Beneath it lay a cavity no deeper than a hand's width, lined with an old blanket that had once belonged to a soldier whose name was in the book above it. The ledger lived there between entries. It slept there, if a book could be said to sleep, among dust and camphor and the faint sweet smell of the cedar shavings Bess used to keep the moths from the winter stores.

The ledger itself was unremarkable, which was the whole point of it. A merchant's account book, bought secondhand from a peddler who had gone through Marrowfield one autumn selling everything a dead uncle left him. The columns were ruled for sums: goods received, goods sold, balance carried forward. Wendell had repurposed the ruling without shame. *Goods received*, they thought each time they dipped the pen. *Received into my keeping.*

Tonight there was one name waiting.

Corporal Ellery Vance, Second Rifles, twenty-three years old. He had died at four minutes past two in the morning, quietly, of a wound in the belly that the surgeons had pronounced survivable right up until the hour it wasn't. He had talked near the end about his mother's garden, about the way she grew beans up strings tied to the kitchen eaves because his father had never gotten around to building her a proper frame. He had asked whether the post would reach Fenlow before winter. Wendell had said yes. It was not entirely a lie; the post might reach Fenlow before winter, carrying a letter that said nothing true.

Because here was the thing Wendell had learned in the first month of holding a pen in this place, the thing that had made the ledger necessary and would make it dangerous, though Wendell did not yet know how dangerous: the casualty reports told only part of the truth, and the part they omitted was never random.

Major Quill, who signed the weekly returns, had a system as tidy as his handwriting. Men who died of wounds received in battle went on the list. Men who died of fever contracted in the trenches went on the list. But men who died of wounds badly treated — men killed by the delay in getting them off the field, by the surgeon's exhaustion, by the gangrene that set in while a convoy waited three days for permission to move — those men became *died of disease, pre-existing condition*. Or they became *returned to duty* and then simply vanished from all subsequent paperwork, as though the army had misplaced them somewhere between the dressing station and eternity, mislaid them the way one mislays a glove. And men who died under circumstances that reflected poorly on an officer — men shot by their own sentries in a confusion no inquiry ever examined, men dead of a flogging, men dead of despair with the means of despair supplied by a comrade who could not bear his screaming anymore — those men were struck entirely, erased with the clean stroke of a staff officer's pen, and their pay stopped, and their mothers waited.

Ellery Vance would go down in the official record as *died of dysentery*. It was not even a cruel lie, as such lies went. It was merely false, and falseness accumulated, and somewhere in a ministry office the war's arithmetic balanced beautifully on the backs of boys who had died of other boys' mistakes.

Wendell wrote: *Ellery Vance, b. 9th of Thaw-month, Fenlow. Mother: Ada Vance, bean rows by the kitchen door. Wound to the abdomen, received at Harrow Ford. Four minutes past two this morning. He spoke of gardens. He asked after the post. Died as himself, not as the report will say he died.*

Then, beneath, in smaller letters, the line Wendell added to every entry, the line that had become the book's liturgy: *Kept safe.*

Wendell blew across the wet ink, held the page up to the guttering lamp until satisfied, and closed the ledger. The floorboard settled home. The blanket over it went down. On top of the blanket went the crate of bandage rolls that always sat there, unremarkable, uncounted, the most innocent object in Marrowfield garrison.

From the ward beyond the partition came the sounds Wendell knew better than any music: the creak of Tam's boots making his last round, slow because Tam never hurried past a sleeping man; the tick of the great clock in the hall, which lost a quarter hour every week and was wound anyway; someone coughing; someone murmuring in a dream, calling a name — *Marta, Marta* — that no one in this room answered to, and would not again until the war gave him back to whoever Marta was, if it ever did.

Wendell banked the lamp and went out through the curtain.

* * *

The infirmary had been a wool merchant's house once, before the war swallowed Marrowfield whole. You could still see it in the bones of the place — in the wide staircase built for merchants' wives in silk, in the tall windows meant to show off a parlor's good light rather than admit it to a ward of forty beds. Now the beds stood in ranks where the dining table had been, and the parlor's fine plaster ceiling sagged under damp, and the smell of lanolin and carbolic had driven out whatever smell of prosperity the house had once carried. Forty-one beds tonight. Thirty-eight occupied. Three empty, which was as close to peace as the infirmary ever came.

Tam found Wendell at the dispensary counter, counting out the morning's doses into paper twists.

"Round's done," he said. "Halloway's fever's broken — I'd stake my wages on it, which is to say I'd stake nothing, but still. The new boy in eleven slept straight through. And the man in nineteen—" Tam paused, and his long face did what it always did when he had to carry bad news in his mouth, folding itself around the words like paper around something sharp. "Nineteen asked for you. I told him you'd come when you could."

"I'll sit with him a while."

"He asked if we write letters." Tam turned a tin cup absently in his hands. "For families. Some of the others do it, the ones who can hold a pen. He can't. His hands—" Tam didn't finish. His hands were ruined, burned at Harrow Ford when a supply wagon caught fire, and he had been in bed nineteen for nine days watching his own fingers heal wrong.

"I know," Wendell said. "I'll write whatever needs writing."

"You always do." Tam said it plainly, without ceremony, the way he said everything, and then lingered a moment longer, because Tam always lingered, as if leaving a conversation required a formal act of departure he'd never quite mastered. "Bess says supper at half past. She says if you're late she's feeding your portion to the cat, and she says to tell you the cat has been looking thin lately, which is a lie, the cat looks like a bolster with legs."

"Tell Bess I heard her the first time."

"She knows you heard her. That's why she sends the message." Tam grinned — quick, lopsided, the grin that had survived three years of war on some stubborn principle Tam himself probably couldn't name — and went off down the ward, and the men who were awake watched him pass the way plants turn toward a window.

Wendell finished the doses, washed the mortar, and let themselves, just for a moment, stand in the doorway of the ward and look at the whole of it: the lamplight low per orders, the blankets drawn to chins, the row of faces young and younger, the ones asleep and the ones pretending. This was the kingdom Wendell ruled, such as it was. No maps in it, no banners. Just names, kept safe, one at a time.

* * *

Bess Harrow's kitchen occupied the ground floor of the old stable block, and it was the warmest room in Marrowfield and possibly in the province, though Bess would deny the second claim on the grounds that the commandant's drawing room had a proper stove and she wasn't going to slander a stove she hadn't met. The kitchen smelled tonight of onion and barley and the smoked ham bone that Bess had produced from some hidden reserve with the mysterious triumph of a woman who could feed forty people indefinitely out of supplies that should have fed ten for a week.

"You're on time," Bess said, not turning from the pot. "I'm astonished. Sit down before I change my mind about the cat."

The kitchen table was scarred pine, big enough for eight, and tonight it held four: Wendell, Tam already halfway through his bowl, Chaplain Odile Ferris with her sleeves pushed up and her hands wrapped around bread to warm them, and Bess herself, standing, because Bess claimed sitting during supper gave her indigestion and everyone knew it was really because a cook who sits down stops being a cook and becomes a guest, and Bess Harrow had not been a guest anywhere in eleven years.

"There's a rumor," Odile said, in her low voice that always seemed pitched to be heard only by the person it was meant for, even in a full church. "Down at the supply depot. Wagons coming in from the rail head, more than the depot can take."

"There's always wagons," said Tam.

"Not like this. Sergeant Pell counted twelve. And they're not supply wagons, Tam. They're too heavy for supply. Troop transport." Odile tore her bread and did not eat it. "Pell thinks they're billeting a regiment through Marrowfield."

Bess snorted. "A regiment. Through *here*. There's nothing in Marrowfield but our hospital, the chapel, and mud."

"The rail junction," Wendell said quietly.

Everyone looked at them. The rail junction lay two miles east, a knot of track where the northern line met the coastal line, unglamorous and indispensable, the reason a town of nine hundred souls had acquired a garrison hospital in the first place. Whoever held Marrowfield held the timetable of the whole front's resupply.

"If they're moving troops through," Wendell said, "they'll want the junction secured. And if they secure the junction—"

"They'll want us counted," Odile finished. "Stores, beds, personnel. Everything inventoried. Everything written down."

The word *written* sat in the air a moment. Wendell kept their face pleasant and reached for the ladle. Under the table, where no one could see, their hand had gone cold.

"Well," said Bess briskly, dealing bowls like cards, "whatever they count, they won't count my ham bone, because it doesn't officially exist, and neither does the pepper, and neither does the third of a sack of flour I've got behind the false wall where the old ice box used to be. Armies can requisition what a man owns. They've never yet figured out how to requisition what a woman hides." She set Wendell's bowl down with a click that managed to be both affectionate and final. "Eat. It's worse cold."

They ate. Outside, the October wind worried at the stable doors, and the clock in the main house lost another minute of its slow dishonest way toward midnight, and none of them — not one of the four at that scarred table, not the cleverest nor the kindest — knew yet how soon the wagons would come, or what they would carry, or how completely the life they were living tonight would be taken apart and inventoried, item by item, name by name.

But Odile said grace, as she always did, and her grace was short: *Lord, remember them. All of them. Even the ones the reports forget.* And Bess said amen louder than anyone, and Tam stole bread from Wendell's plate with the impunity of a brother, and for one more evening the family held, intact, uncounted, its own.

* * *

Later — the dishes done, Tam gone to his cot, Odile walking back through the cold to the chapel with her collar turned up against the wind — Wendell made the rounds one last time.

Bed nineteen first. The boy — he could not be called anything else, though he was technically a corporal — lay with his bandaged hands resting on the blanket like two loaves someone had forgotten to bake. His eyes followed Wendell across the dark ward.

"You came," he whispered.

"I said I would."

"My mum's name is Sera. Sera Oduya. We're from Callis Ferry, down the coast." His voice was careful, rationed, a man spending words like coins he couldn't replace. "If you're writing — I mean, when you write. Tell her the hands don't hurt so much now. Not that they'll believe it, she never believes me, but. Tell her I ate everything they gave me. She'll want to know that. She worries I don't eat."

"I'll tell her," Wendell said. "I'll write it tomorrow, first thing, before the ward round. I promise."

He nodded, and his eyes closed, and his breathing evened, and Wendell stood a moment longer in the dark listening to it — thirty-eight breaths, give or take, rising and falling in the lamplit dimness, each one a small stubborn argument against every report ever filed.

Then Wendell went back to the back room. Lifted the board. Took out the ledger one more time, though there was nothing more to write tonight, and turned to the first page, and read down the column of names by feel more than sight, because the wick was nearly dead and the hour was late and some things a person reads the way you touch a scar: not to learn anything new, but to confirm it's still there.

*Josiah Penn. Marek Solin. Thomas Averill, called Tolly. Abigail Crane, volunteer, aged nineteen...*

Five years of names. Two hundred and six of them. Each one struck from some report, or diminished, or dissolved into euphemism, and each one kept here instead, in a merchant's account book, under a floorboard, in an infirmary in a muddy town at the edge of the world.

*Kept safe,* said every entry. *Kept safe. Kept safe.*

Wendell closed the book and put it home and pressed the floorboard down with both palms, gently, the way you'd press a hand over a sleeping child's chest to feel the breath go in and out.

And it was in that exact posture — kneeling on the cold boards, hands flat, head bowed — that Wendell heard, far off to the east, past the ward, past the walls, past the sleeping town: wheels. Many wheels. Heavy ones, on the road from the rail junction, coming on through the night without lamps and without hurry, the way armies move when they have decided a thing already and are only traveling to inform you of it.
