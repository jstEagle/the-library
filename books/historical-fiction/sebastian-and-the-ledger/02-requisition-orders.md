## 2. Requisition Orders

The boots came first. Wendell heard them before the sun had cleared the eastern rooftops — not the scattered, shuffling tread of the garrison's own patrols, which Wendell knew as well as their own heartbeat, but a massed and rhythmic percussion, hundreds of soles striking cobbles in unison, rolling up the Causeway Road like a drumhead drawn tight.

They stood at the infirmary window with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands and watched the column enter Marrowfield.

It was not an army, exactly. It was too neat for that, too clean. The soldiers wore grey-blue coats brushed free of mud, packs squared, bayonets fixed though no one in Marrowfield had raised so much as a kitchen knife against them. They moved through the town's narrow streets with the unhurried confidence of people arriving somewhere they had already been given. Officers on horseback cantered ahead, marking doors with chalk. Wendell watched them chalk the granary, the tannery, the coach-house of the Drovers' Rest, and then — their stomach tightening around nothing — the tall door of Saint Aldric's Infirmary itself.

"They've come to stay," said Bess at their shoulder. She had come up without a sound, the way she always did, a woman built wide and quiet as a dresser. Her flour-dusted hands were folded into her apron. "You don't chalk doors you mean to knock on politely."

"Perhaps it's only billeting," Wendell said, and heard immediately how thin it sounded.

Bess snorted. In twenty years she had cooked for this infirmary through two sieges, a typhus winter, and the year the river flooded the cellar, and she had never once believed anything optimistic that arrived before breakfast. "Tam's already down in the ward," she said. "Odile's dressing. I'd get your face on, love. Whatever they've come for, they'll want it from someone who looks like they're in charge."

"I *am* in charge."

"I know." Bess patted their arm once, briskly, and went down toward her kitchen as though an army were merely an unusually large breakfast service. It was, Wendell thought, watching her go, its own kind of courage — the courage of people who had decided long ago that fear was a chore like any other, to be got through between tasks.

Wendell set down the cold tea. Then, before dressing, before anything, they knelt on the floor of their narrow room above the ward, pried up the third board from the window — the loose one, the one that had been loose since before the war began, since a dying sapper with clever hands and nothing left to lose had shown them how to lift it without a sound — and looked down into the dark space beneath.

The ledger lay there wrapped in oilcloth, bound twice with string.

Five years of names. Five hundred and eleven of them now, each written in Wendell's careful hand: name, rank if known, wound, date, and the single word that mattered most — *omitted*. The officers wrote their casualty reports in round numbers, in favorable sums, in the arithmetic of careers. Men who died of gangrene three days after being carried off the field became *wounded, recovering*; boys who bled out before dawn became *lightly hurt*; the drowned of the pontoon crossing became, in Colonel Hargreave's dispatches, *no material loss sustained*. Wendell had begun the ledger without any plan larger than anger. It had become something else — a parish register of the erased, a book that insisted every one of those men and women had been *here*, had breathed and suffered and been loved badly or well, whatever the dispatches said.

They did not take it out. They pressed the board back down and laid the rag rug over it, and told themselves, as they buttoned their collar, that soldiers chalking doors wanted beds and stores and horses. Not paper. Nobody marched a regiment across a province for paper.

*

By nine o'clock the infirmary courtyard was full of grey-blue coats.

They came through the gates in good order and spread out with a competence that frightened Wendell more than swagger would have. Two men took the gatehouse. Four went around the back, toward the mortuary and the linen store. A sergeant with a red-banded cap planted himself by the well and began writing in a notebook — counting beds, Wendell realized. Counting blankets, stretchers, lanterns, wheelbarrows. Counting Marrowfield the way a man counts coins he has been told are his.

The patients had been moved into the main ward, all forty-one of them, arranged in their rows of iron cots. Some sat propped up to watch through the windows; the ones who could walk stood at the glass in their nightshirts like ghosts at their own haunting. Private Ellery, both legs gone at the knee since autumn, had dragged himself to the sill with his arms and now sat there grinning at the soldiers below with the pure delight of a man who enjoyed any spectacle he wasn't paying for.

At half past nine a carriage came up the Causeway Road, black and plain, and a colonel stepped down from it into the courtyard.

He was not what Wendell expected. Wendell had imagined bulk, bluster, some scarred loud officer out of the recruiting posters. This man was slight, grey-templed, precise, with spectacles and a face like a folded letter. He paused at the infirmary steps, took off one glove finger by finger, and looked up at the building — at the stone saints over the door, worn faceless by weather — with the expression of a man auditing an account.

"Wendell Almeida?" he asked, when Wendell met him at the top of the steps. His voice was mild. That was the worst thing about him, Wendell thought later. Everything about him was mild. "Colonel Hargreave. Provost authority for the district. Your requisition notice will have gone to the garrison commander; I'm here regarding records."

"Records," Wendell said.

"Hospital records." He produced a folded paper but did not offer it. "Admissions, discharges, treatment registers, mortality returns, dispensary logs, correspondence. All of it, please, gathered in one place. My clerks will catalog and receipt everything. You'll be compensated per the schedule."

"The patients' medical notes stay here," Wendell said. "Some of them can't speak for themselves. If a surgeon comes off the line and reads my chart wrong because your clerks have carted it to the coast—"

"Medical charts remain on site," Hargreave said, with a small nod, as though granting a favor rather than conceding a point. "I'm concerned with the administrative papers. Returns. Reports." Something moved behind the spectacles, quick and narrow, there and gone. "And any private records kept alongside them."

The word *private* dropped through Wendell like a stone through still water.

"We keep what we're required to keep," Wendell said. "The rest is laundry lists and soup inventories."

"Mm," said Hargreave, and smiled — a small, correct smile that involved none of his eyes. "Then it will make very dull cataloging. One place, please, Nurse Almeida. By evening."

He turned and went back down the steps, and his clerks followed him into the ward where the medical charts lived, and Wendell stood at the top of the stairs feeling the floorboard three stories above their head, in their narrow room, pressing down on the whole building's weight like a hand on a wound.

*

They told the family at noon, in the kitchen, with the door shut and the kettle screaming so that listening ears would hear nothing but steam.

Bess said nothing at all, which from Bess was a scream. She stood kneading dough she had no intention of baking, driving her fists into it, flour rising off the board in little clouds.

Odile sat very straight on the bench, chaplain's collar white at her throat, hands folded. She had been at the armistice rumors longer than any of them; she read the officers' faces at Sunday parade the way other people read scripture. "They're not here for beds," she said quietly. "Hargreave doesn't travel for beds. There's something he thinks is written down somewhere in this town, and he means to read every page until he finds it."

"He said administrative returns," Wendell said.

"And when he doesn't find his leak in the returns?" Odile looked at them, and her eyes were kind and terrible. "Wendell. Love. What does he find when he goes looking under floors?"

Nobody answered. Tam, wedged into the corner by the woodbox, went very still. He was nineteen and had the particular transparency of young men trying not to look guilty, and Wendell saw Odile see it, and saw Odile decide — visibly, deliberately — not to say anything in front of him.

"The ledger stays where it is," Wendell said at last. "It's safe. It's been safe five years."

"Nothing is safe," Odile said, "that anyone knows exists. And everyone in this room knows it exists." She rose, smoothed her skirts, and put a hand briefly on Wendell's shoulder — the blessing she gave instead of speeches. "Move it tonight. I'll keep the chapel open late."

"No." Wendell heard their own voice come out harder than they intended. "Not the chapel. Not you. If they're reading everything in this town, the last place that book should sit is under a reliquary with your name on the door. You'd be hanged for it, Odile, and I won't—" Their voice caught. "I won't spend that coin."

"You don't get to choose which of us spends what," Odile said gently. "That stopped being true the day you started writing dead men's names in secret. We're all in the book, Wendell. Every one of us who ever fed a patient or held a lamp. We just haven't been written yet." She squeezed the shoulder once and left to ring the evening office, and her footsteps on the stair sounded, to Wendell, like a clock ticking down.

*

They meant to move it that night. They would have moved it that night, alone, quietly, and perhaps everything after would have been different.

But Tam got there first.

Wendell discovered it at dusk, coming down the attic stairs with a sack of clean rags, and found the boy's cot empty and the window standing open onto the slates. For one merciful moment they didn't understand. Then they understood completely, and the cold that went through them was worse than the colonel's chalk on the door.

By the time Wendell got down into the yard, night fully fallen, lamplit, the ledger was out from under the floorboard — the rug askew, the board lifted and set down carelessly, carelessly, the way a boy handles things he believes are only important, not deadly — and Tam was halfway across the courtyard with the oilcloth bundle stuffed inside his jacket, making it bulge like a stolen loaf, heading for the gap in the hedge behind the washhouse where he had hidden things from his brothers since he was six years old.

"TAM!" Wendell's whisper cracked across the yard like a whip.

The boy froze. And in the same instant the gatehouse lantern swung up, and a voice said, "Oi. Who's that? *Stop.*"

What happened next happened in pieces, each one distinct forever in Wendell's memory. Tam turning, too slowly, the bundle shifting inside his jacket. The sergeant with the red-banded cap stepping out of the gatehouse shadow, notebook still in his hand, saying something bored and procedural. Tam running — running, God help him, a boy's whole stupid loyal heart spending itself in six strides across the cobbles. The whistle. Boots. Two soldiers peeling off the wall by the well with the easy speed of men who had done this drill. Tam reaching the hedge, plunging in, the bundle tearing free of his jacket — Wendell saw it fall, saw the oilcloth split on a stone, saw loose pages fan white across the mud like startled birds—

—and saw the sergeant's boot come down on them.

"*Please,*" Tam was saying, face-down in the hedge, an arm wrenched up behind him, and his voice was high and cracking and nineteen years old. "Please, it's nothing, it's washing, it's washing lists, sir, *please*—"

Wendell walked into the lantern light with their nurse's face on — the calm one, the one that had leaned over four hundred dying men and never once let the fear show — and heard themselves speaking in a level voice they did not recognize as their own.

"That's hospital property," they said. "Those are my papers. The boy was carrying them where I asked. If there's an offense here, Sergeant, it's mine, and you may write it in your little book."

Inside, where no one could see, Wendell was doing arithmetic of a different kind than the colonel's: *how many pages did he see, how much of a name can a man read upside-down in lantern light, how fast can I lie.* Outwardly they stood very straight while the sergeant crouched, gathered the spilled pages with two fingers like something dead, and squinted at the topmost sheet.

Name. Wound. Date. And the word *omitted* in Wendell's small, relentless hand.

The sergeant read it twice. Wendell watched understanding move across his face like weather — not comprehension of the whole, but of enough. His eyes came up, and for a moment, just a moment, they were not the eyes of a man doing his job. They were the eyes of a man who had maybe buried a friend whose death had never appeared in any report.

Then the shutters came down, and it was procedure again. "Colonel will want this," he said. "Both of them. Inside."

*

They put Tam in the gatehouse cell and Wendell in the dispensary with a soldier at the door, which everyone present understood to be a courtesy and a threat at once. Through the wall, faintly, Wendell could hear Tam's voice, steady now, repeating the only sentence he had apparently decided the world would get from him: *"I was carrying washing, sir. I don't know anything else, sir."* Over and over, patient as a psalm, while boots paced and a chair scraped and a voice like folded paper asked questions in its mild, mild tone.

Bess came to the dispensary door at some black hour past midnight, bribed the guard with bread and a flask, and stood gripping the bars with both flour-white hands.

"He won't talk," she said. "You know that, don't you. He'd let them pull his teeth first. He learned loyalty in this house, Wendell, and now it's going to cost him, and I want you to look at me and understand — I am *not* sorry we taught it to him. Don't you dare be sorry either."

"I'm not sorry," Wendell said. Their voice came out wrecked anyway. "Bess, he had it in his jacket. He was carrying it through a yard full of soldiers because he thought — because I made him think it was mine to carry alone, and he couldn't bear that, so he tried to take the weight and he's *nineteen*—"

"Aye." Bess's jaw worked. Through the wall, Tam's psalm went on: *washing, sir, I don't know anything else.* "And tomorrow that folded-paper devil is going to come asking you softer questions, and you'll give him your washing-lists and your soup inventories and your best calm face, and underneath it you'll be doing what you do. Writing it all down. Keeping count." She reached through the bars and gripped Wendell's wrist hard enough to leave marks, which from Bess was a benediction. "Because that's the family trade, love. We keep count. Now sleep if you can. Tomorrow we close ranks."

She went. Wendell lay on the dispensary cot and listened to Tam's sentence repeat itself through the wall, and to the pacing boots, and to the wind coming off the marsh where the armistice delegations were sitting in some lit tent, moving the world's pieces while a nineteen-year-old boy recited a lie about washing to save a book full of dead men's names.

Somewhere out there, Wendell thought, in the dark beyond the sentries, beyond the chalk-marked doors, there might still be one person alive who could help — if the reports were lies about that too, if the grave they'd mourned was empty, if the promise made over bad wine three winters ago still stood on its side of the bargain.

But that was hope, and hope was tomorrow's business. Tonight there was only the wall, and the psalm behind it, and the floorboard upstairs lying flat over nothing, and the sound — faint, rhythmic, indifferent — of an army settling into Marrowfield for the winter.

In the morning, they would come for the ledger's keeper.
