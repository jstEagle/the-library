## 3. What the Colonel Wants

The requisition office had been a magistrate's parlor once. Colonel Ambrose Hargreave could still see the shape of it beneath the military drabness — the ghost-rectangle of a painting removed from the wall, the paler patch of floorboards where a rug had lain, the window seat with its cushion gone and its view of the river kept intact, as though the room itself were being held in trust for a family that would never return to claim it. He approved of the choice of headquarters. A magistrate's house sat at the center of a town's obedience, and obedience, like a well-dug well, was best drawn from where it already pooled.

He worked at the magistrate's own desk, which had been too fine to burn and too heavy to move, its leather top scarred now with the ring-marks of his coffee cup. The cup was his third since dawn. He drank it black, without sugar, because sugar was rationed and he would not ask of Marrowfield what he did not ask of himself — though privately he knew this was a vanity, that no one would have begrudged him sugar, that the austerity performed for an audience of empty chairs was its own small dishonesty. He permitted himself the thought, examined it, and set it aside as one sets aside a document not relevant to the matter at hand.

The matter at hand lay in three crates on the floor beside him, and it smelled of dust and lamp oil and other people's lives.

Seized papers. Two days of requisition had turned up every ledger, register, diary, and letter the town's clerk could be made to surrender: the garrison commissary accounts, the parish burial rolls, the school attendance book, a grocer's inventory annotated in a child's hand, forty years of letters from a woman named Prudence Voss to her sister in the low country, tied in bundles and labeled by decade. Hargreave read them all. Not skimmed — read. His adjutant, Lieutenant Corrigan, a decent young man with a decent young man's impatience, had suggested once, tentatively, that some of the material might be beneath the Colonel's attention.

"Nothing is beneath my attention," Hargreave had said, without looking up. "That is rather the point of attention."

Corrigan had flushed and withdrawn, and Hargreave had felt the small cold satisfaction that came of being misunderstood in a way that confirmed his authority. They thought he read everything out of suspicion. He did not. He read everything out of respect. A war was won by knowing the ground, and the ground here was not the river valley or the ridge road; the ground was these people, their habits, their economies of feeling. Prudence Voss wrote to her sister every Sunday without fail for eleven years until the sister stopped writing back, after which Prudence continued anyway at half-length, the letters growing shorter and more formal, like a plant growing toward light that had been taken away. That was ground. You could build on that. You could fortify it or you could flood it, but first you had to know it was there.

He turned next to the infirmary records, and here he slowed.

The hospital's official returns were models of bureaucratic virtue. Bound in gray board, ruled precisely, each entry in a fair round hand: name, rank or civilian status, wound or fever, disposition. *Recovered. Transferred. Died.* The columns added correctly; he checked them twice against the quarterly summaries, and they added correctly, and there was nothing so suspicious as correctness done too well. Sloppiness was human. Perfection was authored.

And yet.

Hargreave took off his spectacles, breathed on the lenses, polished them with his handkerchief, and put them back on. It was a ritual he allowed himself when he wished his mind to understand that it was changing gears. Then he drew the infirmary's returns alongside the burial registry from the parish church, and laid over both the garrison's own casualty abstracts, and began the patient, unglamorous work of arithmetic.

Numbers were honest in a way people were not. People lied fluently and even sincerely; numbers lied only when someone had taught them to. And someone had taught these.

Not by much. That was the artistry of it. Over five years the infirmary's deaths ran four, five, sometimes six below what the burials recorded — and always below, never above, and never enough in any single quarter to draw the eye of a man who was looking only at quarters. Sixty-one dead in a season was a tragedy. Fifty-five was regrettable. No colonel reviewing fifty-five would ask why the sexton's book said sixty-one, because the difference between those numbers was the difference between grief and paperwork, and paperwork was where men like Hargreave lived, and paperwork was exactly where they had been taught not to look.

But he had looked. He spread the pages across the desk like a man laying out cards he intended to win with, and the pattern assembled itself under his eyes with the quiet inevitability of frost forming on glass.

Someone in that infirmary had been keeping a second count.

He did not know its form. A loose sheaf, a private notebook, entries in the margin of a psalter — it hardly mattered. What mattered was its existence, and its purpose, and the purpose was not hard to infer. Officers falsified casualty reports for reasons as various as the officers themselves: to protect a reputation, to flatter a superior, to keep a battalion's strength figures favorable before a transfer review. Hargreave had signed such adjustments himself early in his career, in the border campaigns, when he was young enough to believe the truth was a thing you could pay for later. He had learned since that the truth extended credit at ruinous interest. But whatever motive moved the garrison's clerks, the second count existed to contradict them, and a document whose whole reason for being was contradiction was a weapon, and weapons found their way into hands.

Hands like Reyes's.

The name arrived in his mind unbidden, and he let it sit there while he rose and went to the window. Below, the street was waking under occupation: soldiers at the corners, a cart of turnips halted and inspected and waved on, two boys following the cart at a distance with the particular sullenness of boys who had learned in two days that the world belonged to whichever adults held rifles. Across the square, the infirmary's chimneys were already smoking. He could see the smoke rise straight in the windless morning, pale gray against pale sky, and he watched it the way another man might watch a fire he suspected of burning something he loved.

Ivo Reyes. Captain, formerly of the Fourth Garrison Company, listed killed at Harrow Ford eighteen months ago — and then not killed, then alive, then walking through the enemy delegation's doors last month in an enemy uniform with an enemy liaison's papers, smiling at the armistice table as though death were a door he'd simply walked around. The report of his survival had come down the wire with the usual bureaucratic squeamishness: *status revised; presumed collaborator pending investigation.* Pending investigation. As if a man's soul could be filed in a drawer marked TEMPORARY.

Hargreave did not share the general staff's appetite for simple explanations. The simple explanation was treason: Reyes had deserted, or been turned, and now served the other side, and should be shot when convenient. But Hargreave had read Reyes's service record the way he read everything — completely — and the record showed a decorated officer, beloved of his company, commended twice for gallantry, recommended for staff college by an examiner who had written *the best tactical mind this command has produced in a decade* in ink that had not faded. Men like that did not turn coat for coin or comfort. They turned for conviction. Which meant either that his convictions had changed, or that his convictions had never been what the uniform suggested — or, third possibility, the one Hargreave found most interesting and most dangerous: that the uniform had never meant what the convictions required, and Ivo Reyes was serving a loyalty larger than either flag, and large loyalties were the ones that left bodies behind them.

And a man with a large loyalty, sitting at an armistice table where casualty figures had become the sticking point, would want exactly one thing: true numbers.

The talks had stalled — everyone said stalled as though it were weather, a fog that would lift, when in fact it was arithmetic. Both delegations had brought their counts, and the counts disagreed by thousands, and neither side would sign a peace built on numbers it believed were lies, because a peace built on lies was merely a truce wearing a treaty's clothes. Hargreave had read the minutes. He had watched the same figure argued over for nine pages of transcript, the enemy delegate saying *your own hospitals know better,* and our delegate replying with the official abstracts, and the silence afterward described by the stenographer as *some duration.*

Your own hospitals know better.

He turned from the window and went back to the desk, and the cold in him settled into its working temperature.

Because that was the thread, wasn't it. The enemy negotiator had said it with the weariness of a man reciting something rehearsed — but rehearsed from what? From intelligence. Somewhere in this town, in some infirmary or chapel or kitchen, someone had been counting honestly for years, counting past the falsified reports, and somewhere downstream of that counting the numbers had leaked eastward, and now those numbers sat at the armistice table with the weight of evidence, undermining the official abstracts, undermining the officers who had signed them, undermining — he permitted the word to surface and did not flinch from it — undermining him. Four of the falsified quarterly summaries carried his countersignature. He had signed them because his superiors had prepared them and because refusal at that altitude was not courage but suicide, and he had told himself, in the years since, the story that ambitious men tell: that he could do more good inside the machine than as a stain upon it.

A second count, made public, would make him a liar in front of both armies and God.

He poured the last of the coffee and found it cold and drank it anyway.

"Corrigan," he said, and the lieutenant appeared in the doorway with the promptness of a man who slept standing at attention. "The infirmary. Tell me what we hold there."

Corrigan consulted his notebook, though Hargreave suspected he knew it by heart. "One senior nurse, Almeida — Wendell Almeida. A cook, Bess — surname unrecorded. An orderly, Tammas Finch. Chaplain Odile Ferrant, attached from the diocese. Thirty-one patients, six of them walking wounded, the rest bed-bound. We've billeted two squads in the outbuildings and requisitioned the east storeroom."

"Almeida." Hargreave tasted the name. "How long?"

"Five years in post, sir. Came up from the relief society during the first winter."

Five years. Five years matched the discrepancy exactly — the earliest quarter where the burial roll outnumbered the infirmary's reported deaths fell within weeks of Almeida's arrival. Coincidence was possible. Hargreave had built a career on declining to believe in it.

"And the character of the staff?"

"Orderly's local. Cook's local. The chaplain's diocesan. The nurse—" Corrigan hesitated, which was unlike him, and Hargreave waited with the patience of a man who had made waiting into a weapon. "There's talk in the town, sir. That Almeida knows things. Names, mostly. There are families here who learned of their dead from the infirmary before any official word came, and some of them say the official word came wrong — said recovered, when the man was buried. The families don't complain. They're grateful. They bring the infirmary eggs."

"Eggs," Hargreave repeated.

"Yes, sir."

He almost smiled. It would have been easy to dismiss — sentiment, village softness, the small economies of gratitude that knit a community together. But he had spent two days reading Prudence Voss's letters, and he knew what eggs were. Eggs were a currency. Eggs were a town paying a debt to whoever kept its accounts honestly, and a town that paid its debts to a hidden ledger would defend that ledger the way it defended its children: without calculation, and with teeth.

"You'll draft orders," he said. "A full audit of the infirmary's records — every register, every return, every private paper on the premises, to be collected for review. Framed as administrative standardization. I want no rhetoric about sedition; sedition frightens people into burying things. Standardization bores them into compliance." He paused. "And I want the personnel interviewed. Separately. Begin with the orderly — the local one. Locals talk more easily to strangers about strangers, and less easily about their own. Establish the pattern before you touch the nurse."

"Sir." Corrigan wrote, then lifted his head. "If the records don't reconcile—"

"They won't reconcile, Lieutenant. They have not reconciled for five years. The question is not whether there is a second count." Hargreave set down the pen and looked at the window again, at the infirmary smoke rising straight as a plumb line into the empty sky. "The question is whether we find the book before the man in the enemy uniform does. Because I promise you, Lieutenant, he is looking. A negotiation does not stall over numbers unless someone believes better numbers exist, and Ivo Reyes did not survive Harrow Ford and his own obituary by believing things idly."

Corrigan stood very straight. "Shall I have him watched?"

"He is watched. He has been watched since he crossed the river." Everyone watched everyone now; it was the era's chief industry. "What I want is subtler. I want to know who in this town looks at the infirmary the way a man looks at a church. Reverence is legible, Lieutenant. Fear is legible. When you walk those streets, watch the faces of the women at the market when the infirmary is mentioned. Gratitude has a particular stillness to it. Find me the stillness."

Corrigan saluted and withdrew, and his boots went down the corridor with the brisk confidence of a man carrying orders he understood, and Hargreave sat alone with his crates and his cold coffee and the long pale rectangles of morning light moving across the magistrate's floor.

He did not feel like a villain. This deserves saying, because history will not say it: he felt like a steward. The war had to end — he wanted it ended, genuinely, had grown sick of the telegrams in the way that only a man who signs them daily can grow sick — and it could only end on numbers both sides trusted, and the numbers both sides trusted must be the numbers of record, because a state that admitted its own books were false would unravel faster than any enemy could unravel it. Order was not cruelty. Order was the dam that kept the flood of grief from drowning everything worth saving. If one nurse in one garrison town had kept a secret count out of love — and he believed it was love, he was not so coarse a man as to imagine malice — then her love was a breach in the dam, and breaches were closed, and the men who closed them were called, by those safely upstream, the responsible ones.

He gathered the infirmary returns into a neat stack, squared their edges against the desk, and weighted them with the brass inkstand. Beneath his hands the gray boards felt like the covers of a hymnal. Somewhere in this town a counterpart existed — thinner, humbler, hidden under a bed or behind a brick — and its pages held the true names of the dead, and perhaps, he thought with a chill that he mistook for diligence, a few names that powerful men had preferred unwritten.

He rang the bell for fresh coffee, and began, methodically, to write the order that would tear the infirmary apart.

Across the square, the smoke from its chimneys rose and rose, straight and thin and patient, like a line of ink drawn upward toward a page that had not yet been written.
