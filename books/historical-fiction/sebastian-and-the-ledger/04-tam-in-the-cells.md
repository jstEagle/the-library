## 4. Tam in the Cells

They took Tam at half past nine in the morning, in front of everyone.

Wendell had been changing a dressing on Private Callow's leg — the wound had turned, the smell of it sweet and wrong beneath the carbolic — when the door of the infirmary opened and four soldiers came in with their boots ringing on the boards. Not marching, exactly. Something worse than marching: the loose, deliberate slowness of men who wanted to be watched. The colonel's adjutant, Lieutenant Sorrel, walked ahead of them with a paper in his hand and his chin lifted as though he were entering a church he disapproved of.

"Orderly," he said, and did not use Tam's name. "Tam Osgood. You're wanted for questioning."

Tam was at the far end of the ward, folding bandages into their precise squares, and Wendell saw his hands stop before the rest of him did. Saw the fold go crooked. Seventeen years old and he'd learned already how fear moved through a body before the mind could argue with it.

"I've got dressings to change," Tam said. His voice held. Wendell was proud of him and hated that pride, because pride would make him brave, and bravery in front of these men was a coin they knew how to spend.

"You'll be back by evening," Sorrel said. It wasn't a promise; it was a sentence spoken in the shape of one. "The colonel has questions about the movement of documents."

Nobody in the ward breathed. Callow, feverish on his cot, moaned once and was silent, as if even infection understood.

Wendell set down the forceps. "He's needed here."

"He's needed there." Sorrel's eyes came to Wendell then, and stayed a moment too long, the way a man looks at a locked drawer. "You can come along if you like, Nurse Almeida. Colonel Hargreave is very interested in the infirmary's... record-keeping."

The word went through Wendell like a needle finding the space between ribs. *Record-keeping.* They knew. Or they guessed, which in an occupied town amounted to the same thing, because a guess gave them license and license gave them hands.

"I'll stay with my patients," Wendell said.

"Then we'll take the boy alone." Sorrel smiled — a small, tidy smile, a man pleased with his own economy. "Don't look so grim. Questions aren't wounds. Most of them close up again."

They marched Tam out between them, and he did not look back, and Wendell understood why: looking back was for people who expected to be returned to what they looked at. Tam walked forward like a boy going to market, shoulders square, and it broke something in Wendell quietly, without bleeding, the way things break inside walls.

* * *

The hours after were the longest Wendell had ever lived through, and they lived them standing still.

Bess came over from the cookhouse three times in the first hour alone, each time with some pretext — bread for the patients, broth, a question about stores she already knew the answer to — and each time her eyes asked the same thing and Wendell's answered the same way: *nothing yet.* By noon she stopped pretending and simply stood in the doorway with flour on her forearms like a woman wearing armor she'd mixed herself.

"They'll not break him," she said. "Not our Tam. He's stubborn as a knot in wet oak. His mother was the same, God rest her."

"He shouldn't have to be stubborn at all."

"No," Bess agreed, and there was no comfort in her agreeing, only truth. "But stubbornness is the only inheritance this war leaves children, so we'd best be glad he's got some."

Odile came at midday, black-clad and calm, and stood at the window looking down at the square where soldiers now lounged against the fountain as though they'd always been there, as though Marrowfield had always belonged to them and the town had merely been waiting to be told.

"They'll ask him where he was taking the book," Odile said. She spoke softly, but the ward was quiet enough that the words carried to the far cots, and Wendell saw more than one patient turn his face carefully toward the wall, offering privacy the only way the powerless could.

"He won't tell them."

"No. He won't tell them anything, which may be worse." Odile turned from the window. Her face was composed, her voice level, and yet Wendell had known her five years, had watched her sit with dying men and speak to them of God without flinching, and never once had Wendell seen her eyes move the way they moved now — quick, calculating, afraid. "A man who says nothing tells them nothing, but he also tells them everything. That there *is* something. That it matters enough to protect. They will tear apart every floorboard in that building by nightfall, Wendell. You know it as well as I do."

Wendell knew it. Had known it since the moment Sorrel said *documents* and the word landed in Wendell's chest like a dropped scalpel.

The ledger lay beneath the third floorboard from the hearth in the linen room, wrapped in oilcloth, under a layer of dust Wendell had applied by hand with a broom swept sideways. Five years of names. Five years of the dead whom the officers' reports had erased — the boys who died of fever after being listed as *recovered*, the men who bled out on the table while the morning report counted them *wounded*, the ones whose deaths would have embarrassed a commander, contradicted a statistic, cost a promotion. Wendell had written every name in their own hand, date, regiment, manner of death, next of kin where known. A ledger of corrections. A ledger, if the colonel chose to read it that way, of sedition.

And Tam had been carrying it when they caught him moving it — no. He hadn't been caught with it. Thank God, thank whatever merciful dullness governed army searches, Tam had been caught crossing the yard empty-handed, on his way to fetch it, because Wendell had told him to bring it to Bess's kitchen for safekeeping and he'd gone obediently, at the worst hour, past the worst window.

So they didn't have the book. But they had a boy, and they had a reason, and they had a floorboard.

"I have to move it tonight," Wendell said.

"Wendell—"

"If I wait for day after tomorrow it'll be under a soldier's boot. If I wait for tomorrow it'll be under a soldier's boot. Tonight, Odile. Curfew starts at nine. The patrols change at eleven — Tam told me, months ago, he watches everything, that boy notices the rotation better than the sergeants do — there's twenty minutes between the last sweep of Church Lane and the first pass of the new pair. Twenty minutes."

"And where will you carry five years of dead men through an occupied town?" Odile asked. Not unkindly. That was the terrible thing about Odile; even her objections sounded like prayers.

"The chapel."

"My chapel."

"Your chapel. Under your sanctuary. No officer in this army wants the reputation of ransacking a consecrated reliquary — not while the armistice talks are making everyone pious for the newspapers."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Outside, boots passed in the street, unhurried, proprietary. When she spoke again her voice had something in it Wendell had never heard before, or perhaps had never recognized: the sound of a woman agreeing to something she believed might damn her.

"Under the reliquary panel," she said. "I'll show you the catch tonight. And Wendell — if they take me for it, you let them. You don't trade yourself for me. Promise me that much, at least. Everyone in this house keeps promising things lately. Let mine be kept cheaply."

* * *

They waited for dark the way the wounded wait for dawn — counting, doubting, starting at every sound.

At eight o'clock a runner came to the infirmary with a printed slip: *Osgood, T., orderly, detained for interrogation. No visitors.* At nine, Bess brought supper and set it untouched outside the cells' anteroom door, and when she came back she reported that the plate had been taken in and not brought out, which meant nothing except that someone was feeding him, which meant nothing except that she needed it to mean something.

At ten minutes past ten, Wendell put on a dark coat and told the night orderly — old Pemm, half-deaf, wholly loyal — that the linen stores needed checking before the requisition inventory, and went down the back stair into the cold.

Marrowfield at night under occupation was a town holding its breath in its sleep. Shutters closed. Lamps doused per curfew order, all but the one at the guardhouse burning yellow over the square. Frost had come early this year; Wendell's breath hung in front of them like something they were trying not to say aloud. The cobbles rang under their own boots no matter how softly they stepped, each footfall a small betrayal announced to the whole listening street.

The linen room smelled of starch and cold ash. Wendell knelt at the hearth and worked the third floorboard up with a knife blade, hands steady until they weren't, and lifted out the oilcloth bundle, and for a moment — kneeling in the dark with five years of names against their chest — Wendell could not move at all.

Because this was the weight of it. Not the paper. The paper weighed nothing. It was the names that had mass: Aldous Wren, died of the fever, March, reported recovered. Corporal Ennis Vale, hemorrhage on the table, listed lightly wounded. The twins from Harrow Fen, both of them, both omitted, because their company commander was being considered for brevet. Page after page. And Tam in the guardhouse right now, seventeen years old, sitting in a chair under a lamp while men asked him questions designed to make him give up the book that said *these people existed, these people mattered, you lied.*

Guilt is a strange load-bearing wall. Wendell felt it hold up everything and support nothing. If Wendell had never written the names, Tam would be folding bandages. If Wendell had moved the book themselves weeks ago instead of trusting it to routine, Tam would be asleep in his cot. Every name in the ledger had been purchased with someone's grief, and now the ledger was purchasing grief again, compounding interest, and Wendell carried it through the dark streets of their own town like a thief carrying the evidence of a crime that was actually a mercy.

*It was my hand that wrote it,* Wendell thought, crossing Church Lane with the bundle flat against their belly beneath the coat. *Let it be my body they take for it, not his.*

Twenty minutes. The patrol had passed at eleven exactly — Wendell heard them, four men and a lantern, laughter low and easy, boys themselves mostly, bored boys with rifles — and now the lane was empty and frost-silvered and utterly silent except for the sound of Wendell's own heart, which seemed loud enough to summon artillery.

The chapel sat at the end of the lane like a patient animal, stone-dark, its little bell tower cut black against the stars. Odile was waiting at the side door with a shuttered candle, and she opened it before Wendell knocked, because she had been watching the lane for twenty minutes too, and together they went down into the chill of the nave where the saints stood in their niches with their painted eyes closed.

"Here." Odile led Wendell behind the altar, to the reliquary — a cabinet of dark oak, older than the town's oldest gravestone, holding the bones of a saint nobody could quite name anymore, which struck Wendell suddenly as fitting. Even holiness in this country survived by keeping its true records hidden. Odile pressed a carved rose at the base and the whole lower panel swung inward on a hinge worn smooth by centuries.

"Under the reliquary shelf. There's a cavity behind the plinth. The wood is dry; wrap the cloth tighter, keep off any damp." She held the candle while Wendell slid the bundle home, and the light moved across her face, and Wendell saw that her hands were shaking — Odile's hands, which had never shaken, not over wounds, not over corpses, not over the bishop himself.

"Odile."

"Don't say anything kind to me right now," she said. "I am currently believing in God very loudly and specifically, and kindness will undo it."

Wendell closed the panel. The rose clicked home. For a moment they both knelt in the dark before a box containing a saint's doubtful bones and five certain years of the war's discarded dead, and it was, Wendell thought later, the most honest prayer either of them had ever made.

Then the candle guttered, and in the new dark, from somewhere beyond the chapel walls, came a sound that froze them both where they knelt: boots. Many boots. Moving fast, moving toward the square, toward the guardhouse, and above them, thin and clear across the frozen town, a whistle blown twice — the army's signal for *assemble*, for *something has happened*, for *someone has talked or someone has broken*.

Odile's hand found Wendell's arm in the dark and gripped it hard.

"That's the guardhouse," she whispered. "Wendell. That's Tam's block."

And Wendell was already moving toward the door, guilt and loyalty pulling in opposite directions through the same chest, knowing that whichever way they ran, they would be running toward something they could not take back.
