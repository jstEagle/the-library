## 5. A Face from Peacetime

The notice went up on the garrison gate on a Tuesday, pasted over the old recruiting poster whose glue had long since given up, so that the paper hung at its corners like a leaf deciding whether to fall. *By order of the Provisional Authority: an armistice delegation of the Northern Compact will be received at Marrowfield on the fourteenth day of the month. All civilian traffic restricted between the hours of dawn and dusk.*

Wendell Almeida read it twice, once for the words and once for what the words were doing to the street around them. A baker's boy stood with his basket forgotten against his hip. An old man who sold kindling by the chapel wall spat, carefully, into the gutter — not at the notice but near it, in the way of a man who had learned that even his contempt required a safe distance.

An armistice delegation. The war had been a weather system for five years, a pressure that moved through everything — the price of lard, the sound of wheels on cobbles, the way Bess stretched a chicken across three suppers and called it abundance. Now here was a piece of paper suggesting that weather could end.

Wendell touched their pocket, where no ledger was — it hadn't lived under the floorboards since the night they'd moved it, wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of Tam's old footlocker in the infirmary cellar — and felt the habit of it anyway. Five years of names. Five years of men the officers' reports had folded away like letters never sent. If there was to be peace, then perhaps the names would finally matter. Perhaps someone would ask, at last, how many.

They should have known better than to hope in a garrison town. Hope, in Marrowfield, was just another thing the army requisitioned.

* * *

The delegation arrived on the fourteenth under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, three black carriages and an honor guard of horsemen whose uniforms were wrong in every particular — the cut too severe, the grey too blue, the buttons brass instead of bone. Enemy colors. Wendell watched from the infirmary's upper window while below them Colonel Hargreave stood on the steps of the customs house in his dress coat, rigid as a fencepost, receiving the enemy's liaison officer with a handshake that looked, from that height, like two men testing which of them could break the other's fingers first.

The liaison officer stepped down from the second carriage, and Wendell's breath did something it had not done in five years, which was stop entirely.

He was thinner. That was the first thing, absurdly the first thing — Ivo Reyes had always eaten like a man apologizing to the food, and now his cheekbones stood out like ridgelines, and the collar of the enemy grey sat loose on a neck that used to fill it. His hair was cropped short in the military fashion, and without the length of it he looked older, harder, a stranger wearing the architecture of a familiar face. But he moved down from the carriage box with that same economy Wendell knew from a hundred small moments — carrying firewood, lifting Odile's crate of hymnals, reaching across Bess's table for the last of the bread — the economy of a man who had learned young that wasted motion was a kind of debt.

*Ivo.* Dead these three years. Reported dead, mourned dead, drunk-over dead — Bess had burned a candle for him every Sunday for a month until the wax ran out and she declared she'd paid her debt and would not be sentimental about a man who'd gone off to die somewhere inconvenient. Presumed dead, and here he was stepping onto Marrowfield cobblestones in the uniform of the enemy, saluting Hargreave with a hand that did not tremble, turning his face — his living, infuriating, impossible face — toward the customs house as though he had never been a ghost at all.

Wendell's hands were flat against the window glass before they realized they'd raised them. Below, the crowd murmured. Somewhere a woman said, *that's one of theirs, that's a Compact officer,* and someone else said, *no, look at him, he's —* and whatever she concluded was lost under the horses.

He can't be. He can't. And yet.

Wendell stood at that window until the delegation disappeared inside, until the crowd dispersed, until the cold coming off the pane had sunk so deep into their palms they had to pry their fingers loose one at a time.

* * *

"You've broken the window with your face," Bess said that evening, setting a bowl of barley soup in front of Wendell with more force than the soup deserved. "You've been staring out of it for two days like a woman waiting on a letter. Eat."

"I saw him." Wendell's spoon turned in the bowl without purpose. "Bess. I saw Ivo."

The kitchen went quiet in the particular way kitchens do when grief walks back in wearing new clothes. Tam, mending a strap by the stove, let the awl rest. Odile, at the far table with her breviary, closed it without marking her place.

"That's not possible," Bess said. Not unkindly. In the tone of a woman who has buried enough to know that the dead come back constantly, in doorways and in the faces of strangers, and that entertaining them is how madness gets its foot in the door.

"In enemy uniform. Liaison to the delegation. He got out of the carriage and shook Hargreave's hand."

Tam set the strap down. "You're sure it was him?"

"I would know him if they boiled him down to a silhouette." Wendell heard their own voice crack on it and hated the crack. Three years of composure, undone by a carriage door. "He's alive, Tam. He's alive and he's wearing *their* grey, and he stood next to Hargreave like — like a colleague."

"Like a collaborator," Tam said quietly.

"Don't." The word came out sharper than Wendell intended, sharp enough that Tam flinched, and Wendell reached across the table — an apology made of a hand on a wrist, rough and brief. "I'm sorry. I don't know what I'm saying. I only know what I saw."

Odile spoke for the first time, and her voice had the measured weight she saved for scripture and hard truths. "Then we must consider what you saw carefully. A man may wear an enemy uniform for many reasons. Escape. Coercion. Spying." She paused. "Or treason."

"He was our brother," Wendell said.

"He was," Odile agreed. "And brothers are precisely the ones who can hurt you, because you have already given them the knife."

Bess ladled more soup into Wendell's bowl without asking, which was her way of ending an argument she intended to continue later. "Eat," she repeated. "Whatever he is — dead man, traitor, ghost — he'll keep till morning. Soup won't."

But Wendell did not sleep, and neither, judging by the candle-glow under Tam's door, did anyone else in that little house of the living.

* * *

The infirmary received its first patients of the armistice week two days later: two Compact officers with dysentery, delivered under escort because the terms of the delegation's presence included medical parity — each side treating the other's sick as a gesture of good faith, or a test of it, depending on whom one asked. Wendell nursed them with the impersonal excellence they gave everyone, changing sheets, measuring fevers, noting doses in the official register with the neat hand that had fooled five years of inspectors.

It was on the third day, crossing the yard between the infirmary and the well, that Wendell heard the voice.

"I'm told this town's water is drinkable, but I've been lied to by professionals before."

The voice came from the shade of the well's arch, and Wendell stopped as though the ground itself had issued an order. Captain Ivo Reyes stood with a water bottle in one hand and his cap in the other, cropped hair silvered oddly at the temples though he couldn't have seen thirty yet, watching Wendell with an expression that was trying very hard to be casual and failing in every particular that mattered.

"You're supposed to be dead," Wendell said. It came out flat, almost conversational, which surprised them both.

"I'm told the report was enthusiastic about my death. Very thorough." He took a half-step forward and stopped himself, as if remembering that the distance between them was no longer something he had the right to cross. "Wendell."

"Don't say my name." Their own voice again, and this time they didn't recognize it at all. "Don't stand there drinking my well water and saying my name like — like nothing happened. You've been dead three years, Ivo. Bess burned candles. Tam kept your boots. Odile read the office for the departed *twice*, because the first time she said she didn't believe God had taken you and wanted to give Him a chance to reconsider. And you're —" The words failed and reformed. "You're standing in front of me in their grey."

"It's a long story."

"Then tell it slowly, because I have all evening and none of the patience I used to have."

For a moment something flickered across his face — the old grin, the one that had gotten them all thrown out of a harvest dance in the year before the war, surfacing like a fish rising and then diving again before it could be caught. "Not here," he said instead, low. "There are eyes on me, Wendell. There are eyes on everyone in this town, including yours. I need—"

"What you *need*," Wendell said, and their voice cracked clean down the middle this time, "is to explain why the fifth chair at our table belongs to a traitor."

Something closed behind his eyes. Not anger. Worse — recognition, the look of a man hearing a verdict he had expected and prepared for and still not survived. "That's fair," he said quietly. "That's more than fair." He set his cap back on his head, and the motion was the old economical one, and that was somehow the cruelest thing of all, that his body still moved like their Ivo when everything else had become a question. "There's a promise you made me. Three years ago, on my last leave. Do you remember it?"

Wendell remembered. Of course they remembered. Drunken Ivo on Bess's doorstep, gripping their sleeve with terrible seriousness, making them swear that if ever — *if ever, Wendell, however it looks* — the reports named him an enemy, they would hear him out before they believed the paper. They had sworn it laughing. They had sworn it thinking it was the kind of promise one swears to drunks.

"I remember," they said.

"Then I'm asking you to keep it. Not tonight. There's a place — the old mill road, past the tannery, Thursday after dark. Come alone. Or don't come at all, and I'll understand that too." He was already moving as he said it, retreating into the role of liaison officer the way a man steps back into armor. At the edge of the arch he paused, and for one unguarded second his face was simply a tired man's face, missing home. "The soup smelled like hers," he said. "Through your window. I could smell it from the street. Tell Bess her kitchen still works."

And then he was gone across the yard, grey uniform among grey stone, and Wendell stood alone with the water bucket in their hand and their heart performing a treachery of its own — because beneath the fury, beneath the betrayal sitting in their chest like swallowed stone, some shameful part of them had spent those two minutes at the well simply, stupidly *glad*. Glad he breathed. Glad his voice still bent the air the same way.

Joy curdling, hour by hour, into something they would have to carry to the mill road on Thursday and set down between them like an accusation.

* * *

That night the found family convened without agreeing to convene, which was its own kind of ritual. Bess locked the kitchen door out of pure theater — the house had no secrets from soldiers, only from each other — and set out the last of the honey spirits, which she had been saving for the war's end and which apparently she had decided might as well be spent on its complications.

"The mill road," Odile said, when Wendell finished telling them. She said it the way she read the names of the dead, evenly, letting each word bear its full weight. "After dark. Alone."

"He asked me to hear him out. Before believing the report."

"And the report says he serves the Compact. As their liaison. To the delegation that will decide whether this war ends or grinds on another decade." Odile folded her hands. "What does your heart say, Wendell?"

"My heart says it's him. My heart says he's alive and that's the only fact in the world I trust right now." Wendell looked at the spirit in their cup and did not drink it. "My head says a dead man doesn't come back by accident. My head says the enemy doesn't appoint liaisons without knowing exactly which faces this town will recognize."

"Your head," Bess said, "has been spending too much time with Odile."

"Bess."

"She's right, though, and you know it, and I know it, and she knows it, which is the most annoying arrangement possible." Bess refilled cups all around, a general provisioning her troops. "Here's what I know. I fed that boy for six years and I know his appetite, and today at the market I watched him buy an apple and pay for it without haggling, and Ivo Reyes haggling was practically a sacrament. Either he's changed down to his bones—" she paused, and the pause held all three years of mourning, "—or he's performing. And either way, Wendell, you don't walk onto a dark road alone with a man who might be either."

"So I shouldn't go."

"I didn't say that." Bess's jaw set, and Wendell saw, with a strange clarity, the woman who had driven a supply cart through shelled roads in the second year of the war because the wounded needed flour more than she needed safety. "I said you don't go *alone*. Tam knows the tannery paths better than the tanner. Odile can pray anywhere, including behind a wall. And I make bread at odd hours, and no one questions a cart." She looked around her kitchen, at her battered, brilliant, terrified family, and her voice gentled into something that was nearly a commandment. "We buried him once together. If we have to bury him again, we'll do it properly. And if we don't — if there's even a splinter of truth in whatever story he's carrying — then we hear it out. That was the promise. Promises made to drunks still count. That's what makes them promises."

Wendell looked down at the cup in their hands and thought about the ledger beneath the infirmary floor, five years of names that existed because someone had refused to accept what the official papers said about the dead. The principle was the same, wasn't it? The paper lies; the names don't. The report says dead; the man buys apples.

"Thursday," Wendell said at last. "After dark. We'll hear him out."

Across the table, Odile met their eyes, and her expression was not disapproval — it was fear, worn openly for the first time, the fear of a woman who had already calculated what the enemy did to towns found sheltering collaborators, and what it did to families found sheltering worse.

"Thursday," she echoed. "God go with us. Because if this goes wrong, no one else will."
