## 6. The Promise

Three years before the requisition — before the boots on Marrowfield's cobblestones and the smell of ink in Colonel Hargreave's office — Bess Harrow still believed that a full pot could fix most things.

She had built her faith on evidence. Twenty-two years feeding soldiers, nurses, chaplains, and the occasional deserter had taught her that hunger made men cruel and fullness made them honest, and that nearly every quarrel in the garrison could be traced either to an empty stomach or to someone who had forgotten they'd ever been fed. Her kitchen at the garrison infirmary was small, scarred, and smelled permanently of onions and bay leaf, and she governed it with the mild tyranny of a woman who had buried a husband and raised no children and therefore decided, without consulting anyone, that every soul who came through her door was hers now. That was how she thought of them, privately, when she was kneading bread or trimming fat: my family. She never said it aloud. Saying it aloud would have invited the war to notice them, and Bess Harrow did not believe in inviting the war anywhere.

It was the last warm week of September, three years back, and the whole infirmary had turned out for what Odile called a feast of thanksgiving for the end of the border campaign, though Bess suspected God had very little to do with it and a shipment of fresh pork quite a lot.

* * *

She remembered the evening in pieces afterward, the way one remembers any night that turns out to matter — not whole, but in fragments polished by later handling. The long trestle table dragged out into the yard behind the kitchen because the weather was kind and nobody wanted to eat under a roof when they didn't have to. The lanterns hung from the beam of the old well-house, moths stuttering against the glass. Tam, nineteen then and all elbows, balancing six bowls on his forearms and daring anyone to be impressed. Odile saying grace with her eyes open, which scandalized no one who knew her. And Wendell at the far end, quiet as always, hands folded around a cup of cider they weren't drinking, watching the others with that expression Bess had never found another word for than *tending* — the look of someone keeping count of a room the way other people counted money.

And Ivo Reyes, loud and golden and already three cups past wisdom, standing on the bench with his boots on the seat, telling the story of the flooded ford at Callow's Bridge for the fourth time that summer, each telling growing a foot of river and half a company of drowned mules.

"Fourteen mules," he insisted, hand flat like a blade chopping the air. "I counted them myself. Floating past like great sad furniture."

"You were face-down in the water at the time," said Wendell, without looking up.

"I was *reconnoitering*, Almeida."

"You were being fished out by a corporal half your size."

The table roared. Bess, coming through the kitchen door with the second roast cradled in her apron like a sleeping child, paused in the doorway to watch them — the captain flushed and grinning, the nurse dry as chalk and hiding the smile behind the cup. Those two. Everyone saw it eventually: not romance, nothing so simple, but something older and stranger, two people who had recognized each other somewhere below the level of names. Reyes was all noise and Wendell all silence, and between them they made a complete sentence.

"Sit down before you break the bench," Bess said, "or you'll be eating standing up in the scullery like a dog."

"Bess." He spread his arms at her, wounded, magnificent. "Bess. Tell them about the mules."

"I'll tell you what I told you in June: mules float better than captains, and both stink worse after." She set the roast down and the smell went through the yard like a benediction. "Sit. Eat. You're leaving on the morning train and you'll want your legs under you."

He sat. That was the thing about Reyes everyone missed when they called him reckless — he obeyed Bess instantly and without argument, every time, as if somewhere in his childhood a mother had drilled the reflex so deep that no rank or liquor or war could reach it. He dropped onto the bench beside Wendell and let Odile fill his plate and ate like a man making deposits against a famine, and the evening rolled on around him: Tam's terrible singing, Odile's stories of the seminary, lantern-light on faces that were, though none of them knew it, nearly whole for the last time.

* * *

It was past midnight when Bess came out to bank the kitchen fire and found him sitting on her step.

The yard was empty, the table abandoned to the moths, dishes stacked by some sainted soul — Tam, she'd learn later — and Reyes sat alone on the stone step outside her door with his head in his hands, and the change in him was so total that for a heartbeat she thought it was a stranger. Drunk men are usually louder versions of themselves; Reyes had gone somewhere else entirely. He looked, she thought, like a boy sent to bed early from his own birthday party.

"There's cider left," she said. "Or water, which is what you're getting."

"Water," he said, muffled, into his hands. "Please."

She brought it and sat down beside him on the step, which was not a thing she did for officers as a rule, but the step was wide and the night was soft and there was something in the set of his shoulders that made rank feel beside the point. He drank half the mug in one pull and held the rest and stared out across the dark yard at the dead firepit, the folded table, the well-house beam with its empty lantern hooks.

"They've given me the Verren postings," he said.

"The what?"

"Border liaison. Six months, maybe longer. Paper work, they say. Escorting delegations, translating, carrying messages between the lines when talks happen." He laughed, and there was no laugh in it. "Talks. As if there will ever be talks."

"There will," Bess said. "Men get tired. It's the one thing they always get tired of eventually, even fighting."

"Not this one." He turned the mug in his hands. "You should see the maps they have me reading, Bess. Every road is an argument and every bridge is somebody's dead brother. Both sides are digging in like badgers, and both sides think the other one started it, and both sides are right, probably. There's no end in that country. There's just more country."

Bess said nothing, because there was nothing useful to say, and she was a woman who had learned that silence offered to a man at the right moment was worth more than most speeches. The moths ticked against the lantern glass inside. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and gave up.

"I'm drunk," Reyes said.

"I'd noticed."

"No, I mean—" He waved the mug vaguely at his own head. "I mean I'm drunk enough to say the thing I've been chewing on for a month, and sober enough — barely — to remember I said it. So listen." He set the mug down on the stone with exaggerated care, the way drunk men do when they're about to handle something more fragile than crockery, and turned to face her, though his eyes went past her shoulder, toward the infirmary, toward the small lit window above the kitchen where a lamp still burned. Wendell's window. Wendell was always the last lamp out.

"If it goes wrong," he said. "If it all goes wrong over there. If someday there's a report with my name in it — the bad kind of report. Collaborator. Traitor. Deserter. Whatever word they use when a man ends up on the wrong side of a line that moved while he wasn't looking." His voice stayed steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "Promise me they'll hear me out first. Before they believe it."

"Hear you out," Bess repeated. "From where? If you're declared enemy, Ivo, you won't exactly be welcome at supper."

"Somehow." He rubbed his face. "A letter. A word passed. Anything. Just — don't let them read my name off a page and nod and move on. Because that's how it works, isn't it? That's how it works in the reports. A name gets written wrong once, in some clerk's hand, and then it's true forever. Nobody checks. Nobody *wants* to check, because checking means admitting the report was wrong, and the report is the army, and the army can't be wrong." He picked up the mug again and didn't drink. "Wendell knows. Wendell writes those names down, the ones the reports lose. Don't think I haven't seen. Don't think I don't know what happens at that little desk upstairs after lights-out."

Bess went very still.

She had known about the ledger for two years, almost — had known since the night she'd come up to bring tea and found Wendell copying names by lamplight, the little book open, the entries in that small fierce hand: *name, regiment, wound, date, what the report said instead.* Wendell had frozen like a poacher caught in a lamp-beam, and Bess had set down the tea, read one line upside-down, and said only, "There's jam in the crock if your pen hand needs sweetening," and never spoken of it since. Some things you kept by not mentioning them; that was the whole art of keeping anything in a garrison town.

"It's five years' worth," she said quietly.

"I know." He wasn't surprised, and that told her he'd known too, longer than she had. "That book is the truest thing in this entire garrison. Truer than the colors, truer than the chapel, truer than whatever Odile says God thinks. And if I go wrong over there, I need—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "I need one person on this side of the line who'll do for me what Wendell does for those boys. Write down what actually happened. Or at least wait, just wait, until the truth has a chance to catch up with the report."

They sat with that. Down the lane the dog tried again and thought better of it. Inside the house the banked fire settled with a sound like a sigh.

"You're asking me to promise," Bess said, "on behalf of other people."

"I'm asking you because you're the only one brave enough to say yes or no honestly." He smiled, lopsided, the gold dimmed to something smaller. "Odile would pray about it. Tam would agree to anything and mean it and panic later. But you — you'd tell me if it was a fool's promise, wouldn't you? You'd say, Captain, that's a fool's promise."

"It is a fool's promise," Bess said. "Yes."

His face fell, and she put her hand flat on his arm before the fall finished landing.

"But I'll make it anyway," she said. "Because I've fed enough soldiers to know the ones who ask questions like that — the ones who lie awake inventing their own disgrace — are never the ones who betray anybody. It's the ones who can't imagine being blamed who end up guilty." She squeezed his arm once, hard, through the uniform sleeve. "So. If the day comes, and there's a report with your name in it, and it smells wrong — we'll hear you out. All of us. Before we believe a word on paper. That's my promise, Ivo Reyes, and I keep promises the way I keep accounts: exact, and with interest if you cross me."

He exhaled, long, like a man setting down a pack carried too many miles. For a moment he leaned into her side, briefly, heavily, the way exhausted men lean into anything solid, and then he straightened and retrieved his dignity along with his mug.

"Wendell," he said. "Not you. I need it from them."

"Ivo—"

"Not you, Bess. You'll hold me to it, I know, and thank you, truly. But it has to be them." He looked up again at that small lit window, and something crossed his face that Bess had no name for then, though she would find it later, much later, in a different century of her life: the look of a man memorizing a light he expected to walk away from. "If the report comes, and it's my name in it, and Wendell believes it without asking — then there's nothing left to believe in at all. Do you understand? It's not about being cleared. It's about whether the truest person I know trusts me past the edge of a piece of paper."

Bess looked at the window too. The lamp still burned. Somewhere behind the glass, Wendell was writing somebody's name down so that the world couldn't lose it, and had no idea that the world was already sharpening its knife.

"He'll say yes," she said. "But he'll say it plain, and he'll hate saying it, and he'll pretend it costs him nothing, and it'll cost him everything. That's Wendell. Go up and collect your promise, Captain, and don't you dare wake Tam."

* * *

She followed him up, ostensibly to lock the medicine cupboard, actually because she wanted to see it — because Bess Harrow had spent twenty-two years learning that you must witness the promises people make, or the promises evaporate, and she was done letting important things evaporate.

The infirmary ward at midnight was a country of slow breathing. Lamplight low. Forty cots, mostly empty since the border drawdown, the occupied ones holding men asleep in the particular surrender of the genuinely healing. Wendell sat at the desk in the corner, jacket over the chair-back, sleeves rolled, pen moving. The ledger lay open. From the door Bess could not read a word of it and knew every word of it anyway.

Reyes crossed the ward on sock feet, pulled the second stool up to the desk without asking, and sat down in it like a man arriving at a destination he'd been walking toward all night.

Wendell looked up. Bess, in the shadow of the linen cupboard, watched them take each other's measure across the lamplight — the captain flushed and grave, the nurse wary and awake.

"You're drunk," Wendell said.

"Thoroughly."

"Then it'll keep till morning."

"It won't." He laid his hand flat on the desk, next to the ledger but not touching it, careful even drunk, careful especially of that. "I ship out at dawn. Verren posting. Liaison work. Which means, Wendell, that I am about to spend six months standing exactly on the line where reports get written." He took a breath. "So I need a promise, and I need it tonight, because tomorrow I'll be sober and sensible and I'll never ask."

Bess saw Wendell's pen stop. Saw the stillness come into those thin shoulders, the listening stillness, the same stillness they brought to a dying man's bedside.

"If it goes wrong," Reyes said, quieter now, the words chosen and placed like stones across a stream, "if there's ever a report with my name in it — traitor, collaborator, turncoat, any of it — hear me out first. Before you believe the paper. Find a way. Ask me. Let me answer. That's all. That's the whole favor." His voice cracked on the last of it, just slightly, and he covered it with a smile. "You of all people know what a name looks like after a clerk's been at it."

The lamp hissed. A patient turned over in his sleep and murmured something about a river.

Wendell set down the pen. Aligned it with the ledger's edge, precisely, the way they aligned everything when feeling got too close. When they spoke, their voice was low and even and cost them visibly, exactly as Bess had promised it would.

"You're asking me to doubt an official report," Wendell said. "In wartime. On your say-so."

"I'm asking you to doubt it the way you already doubt all of them. You do it every night at this desk. I'm only asking to be included in the mercy."

Silence. Then Wendell reached out and turned the ledger around, and Bess saw the open page — a name, a date, and in the margin, in small letters: *what the report said instead.* Wendell tapped that margin once.

"This is why I keep it," they said. "Because a report is a story the winners tell about the dead. So yes. I promise. If your name appears in one — I'll hear you out first. Before I believe it. Even if the whole army believes it. Even if believing you ruins me." A pause, and then, drier, almost gentle: "Go to bed, Ivo. Your train won't wait for your hangover."

Reyes laughed — really laughed, the sound breaking open in the dark ward — and stood, and gripped Wendell's shoulder hard enough to leave a memory, and said, "Kept safe," which meant nothing to Bess then, and walked out through the moonlit ward with his boots in his hand like a schoolboy.

Bess waited until his footsteps faded down the stair, then came out of the cupboard's shadow. Wendell hadn't moved. Was looking at the closed door, pen still aligned, face unreadable except to a woman who had fed them for five years.

"Well," Bess said.

"Well," Wendell agreed.

"He'll come back with medals."

"Everyone comes back with something," Wendell said, and turned the ledger back around, and dipped the pen, and wrote.

* * *

Three years later, on the morning the notices went up in Marrowfield square — *CAPTAIN IVO REYES, DECLARED ENEMY COLLABORATOR, TO BE TREATED ACCORDINGLY* — Bess stood in front of the board with flour still on her wrists, and the words swam, and the crowd muttered around her like water around a stone.

She heard the name read aloud three times before she could move. And underneath the fear, underneath the cold dropping through her middle, rose the memory of a stone step and a drunk man's steady voice and her own hand on his sleeve — *we'll hear you out, all of us, before we believe a word on paper.*

A promise, she thought, watching a soldier paste the notice flat with a brush. Made in peacetime. Collected in war. That was always the shape of them.

Behind her, unseen, Wendell Almeida had also stopped in the square, also reading — and Bess, turning, caught the expression on their face and understood that the debt was coming due, and that the interest would be ruinous.
