## 7. Declared Enemy

The notices went up on a Tuesday, pasted to the door of the customs house with flour-paste gone sour in the rain. Wendell saw them first on the way back from the well — Bess's hands had been bad since the requisition, and the carrying of water had fallen by quiet agreement to whoever finished bandage-rolling earliest. Wendell finished early that morning because sleep had not come, or had come thin as broth skinned over, and so it was Wendell who stood in the grey light with the bucket swinging forgotten at their side and read the word *collaborator* in a clerk's careful hand.

There were three notices. One was general: a proclamation of vigilance against persons aiding enemy forces, signed with Colonel Hargreave's name in a signature like a fence built across a field. The second listed seized correspondence, dates and file numbers, the bureaucracy of suspicion laid out in columns. The third was small, no larger than a funeral card, and it bore a face.

Wendell knew that face better than they knew their own hands. It was older here — the photograph had been taken years ago, before, when Ivo Reyes had still worn the uniform of Marrowfield's garrison and smiled with one corner of his mouth because he thought a full smile undignified for a soldier. The hair was cropped shorter than he'd ever kept it. The eyes were the same: dark, watchful, holding some private joke just behind the iris. Under the photograph, in type: **CAPTAIN IVO REYES. PRESUMED DEAD IN ACTION, THIRD WINTER. NOW CONFIRMED ALIVE AND SERVING THE ENEMY AS LIAISON TO THEIR ARMISTICE DELEGATION. ANY CITIZEN POSSESSING INFORMATION CONCERNING THIS PERSON MUST REPORT TO THE PROVOST'S OFFICE UNDER PENALTY OF LAW.**

*Presumed dead.* Wendell read those words twice. They had all presumed it. They had mourned him — Odile had said the office for the dead with her voice steady as a liturgy rope, Bess had cooked his favourite meal and set a place for him at the table nobody could explain why they'd set, Tam had carved his initials into the bench by the infirmary stove where he used to sit. Five years of found family, and they had buried him with words instead of a body because there had been nothing else to bury.

And now the army said he had never been dead at all. That he had turned his coat. That the man who used to carry the smallest patients on his shoulders when the corridor got too long, who taught Tam to play cards and let Tam win, who sat up whole nights with dying men because he believed — he had said this once, drunk on leave, laughing and not laughing — *that nobody should go into the dark without someone holding their hand* — that man was now the face on a funeral card nailed to a door, wanted under penalty of law.

The bucket handle bit into Wendell's fingers. They hadn't noticed they were gripping it.

A woman from the tannery street stopped beside them, reading, her lips moving. "Reyes," she said. "Wasn't he the captain who used to come into the infirmary? The one Odile fussed over?"

"He was," Wendell said. Their voice came out flat as slate.

"Shame." The woman clicked her tongue. "Good-looking fellow. You never can tell about people." She moved on, satisfied, having filed a man's entire life under two sentences.

Wendell looked at the notice a long moment more. Then they took the corner of it between thumb and forefinger — carefully, so as not to tear it, because tearing it would be a crime now, wouldn't it, everything was a crime now — and memorized every line, every date, the exact angle of the photographed jaw. Then they picked up the bucket and walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone, past soldiers at the corner who watched them with the incurious attention of cats watching weather, and did not let their face do anything at all until the infirmary door closed behind them.

It did quite a lot once it closed.

* * *

"You've seen it." Odile was in the chapel, which was where she went when she wanted to say difficult things, because she seemed to believe God made an audience even when He declined to intervene. She was folding vestments that were already folded. Wendell had known her five years and had never once seen her fold something twice without a reason.

"I've seen it."

"Then we will speak of it once, and then we will not speak of it again." Odile set down the stole she was holding and turned. She was a small woman, square as a doorframe, with hands that had buried most of the parish twice over and a face that gave away exactly what she chose to give away. What she chose to give away now was worry. "Ivo Reyes is declared an enemy collaborator. That is not an accusation, Wendell. It is a finding. The army does not print such notices lightly — not even this army, not even now. If you are seen studying that paper, if you ask questions, if you write to anyone, if you so much as keep his letters—"

"I burned his letters the day the requisition came." This was true. It had been one of the hardest things Wendell had ever done with their hands, and their hands had held men together while they came apart. Four letters, two years old, tied with the ribbon off a parcel of figs he'd sent from leave once. The fire had eaten them in less than a minute. Wendell had sat watching until the last scrap of his handwriting curled black, and then had gone and changed dressings for six hours without sitting down.

"Good," Odile said. "That was right." She softened, the way she only ever softened in increments, like ice going to water along its edges. "Wendell. Sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"Sit down anyway." Wendell sat on the front pew, which still bore the scorch mark from the candle Tam had knocked over during the Christmas service three years ago — the year Reyes had been home on leave, the year everything had still been whole. Odile sat beside them, which she almost never did; chaplains stand, that was her doctrine. "I loved him too," she said. "You know that. He was mine to shepherd same as you, and I failed at it, because he never once needed shepherding. He needed feeding and scolding and occasionally bailing out of the river after Tam's schemes, but never shepherding." Her mouth did something that was nearly a smile and then put itself away. "And because I loved him, I am telling you the thing love least wants to hear. Forget him."

Wendell's breath went out slowly. "Just like that."

"Just like that. No. Not just like that. Like pulling a splinter — quick is kinder than slow, and either way it hurts." She folded her hands. "Listen to me. Men turn coat for many reasons. Some for money, some for fear, some because they were always hollow and simply chose the side that fit the hollowness. And some—" her voice caught on it, and she pushed through, "—some for reasons we would forgive, if we ever learned them. I grant you that possibility. I hold it myself, in the night, when I am weak. But Wendell, it does not matter. Do you understand me? Even if his reasons were holy. Even if he is innocent of every charge but the one true charge, which is wearing the wrong uniform in the wrong year. It does not matter, because *you cannot reach him*, and reaching for him will pull down everyone you love onto your own head. We have Tam in a cell. We have Hargreave's clerks going through our stores with white gloves. We have Bess counting her flour like a miser counting gold because half of it goes to the garrison now. You want to add to that account? You want to be the reason another Almeida-family name appears on one of those lists?"

"They haven't charged Tam with anything yet."

"They will. Charges arrive like weather, child. You don't argue with weather. You bar the shutters." Odile rose, and standing, she recovered her altitude, her authority, her priestly architecture. "Grieve him properly. I'll help. We'll say the office again, quietly, and this time we'll mean the words differently — not *he is dead* but *he is lost to us*. There is a difference, and the difference is survivable. And then we close that door, and we lock it, and we put our backs against it, because this family has exactly enough strength to survive what's already coming and none to spare."

She left Wendell alone in the chapel with the dust motes turning in the window light and the scorched pew and the silence where a fifth chair should have been.

Wendell sat for a long time. Outside, a patrol went by, boots striking cobbles in unison, four-four-four-four, the heartbeat of the occupation. Somewhere Tam was in a cell being asked questions. Somewhere Bess was stretching a stew to feed twelve with the bones of six. Somewhere — and here the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, sharp as a scalpel — somewhere Ivo Reyes was alive, walking, breathing, wearing an enemy uniform, doing whatever collaborators did.

*He is lost to us.*

Odile's distinction was a good one. Wendell tested it, the way they tested any structure before trusting weight to it. *Lost* meant grief without burial, mourning without an end. *Lost* meant the door locked and the back turned and the heart told, daily, firmly, to forget.

But Wendell had spent five years keeping names that other people struck from the record. The whole of their secret life — the ledger under the floorboard, the ink-stained hours after midnight, the discipline of remembering what power preferred forgotten — had been built on a single refusal: the refusal to let a declaration stand unexamined. Officers declared men dead who died differently. Reports declared causes of death that were lies. The army's paper said one thing; Wendell's ledger said another; and Wendell had chosen, every single time, to believe their own eyes over the printed word.

Now the printed word said *collaborator*. Now Odile, whom Wendell trusted with everything, said *forget him*. And underneath both declarations, patient as infection, the question Wendell could not kill: *the report says he's a traitor, but did you see it yourself?*

They had not seen it themselves. That was the whole of it. Nobody in Marrowfield had seen anything except a photograph on a door.

Wendell stood. The chapel was empty, the door shut, the patrol's footsteps fading toward the square. And Wendell became aware, with the particular clarity that comes at the moment a decision is already made and the mind is merely catching up, of the old oak beam above the confessional — and of the loose knot in it, and of what they had hidden inside that knot three winters ago, and never removed.

* * *

It had been a joke, originally. Or half a joke, which in their family was the same as a promise wearing a disguise.

The winter Reyes was home on leave — the last time, though none of them had known it was the last — the five of them had crowded into the chapel after evening service because it was the only room with a fire Bess approved of, and Tam had been teaching everyone a game with buttons for counters, and Reyes had lost spectacularly and claimed the dice were cursed. To break the curse, Odile had said solemnly, he would have to make an offering. And Reyes, laughing, had dug in his pocket and produced a button — brass, with an eagle stamped on it, prised off some uniform or other, provenance he refused to explain — and reached up on tiptoe and wedged it into a knothole in the beam above the confessional, high enough that no one shorter than himself could reach.

"There," he'd said. "Paid in full. The curse is lifted and the house holds my pledge."

"What pledge?" Tam had asked.

"The pledge of Ivo Reyes, defender of this chapel, first to be blamed and last to be fed. It stays there till I come back to claim it." He'd tapped the beam. "And when I come back, I get my button, and Bess cooks the goose, and we do this all again. That's the terms. Witnessed?" 

"Witnessed," they'd all said, because it was a game, because he was going back to the front in nine days, because nobody in that warm room had imagined that the next time the army spoke his name it would be on a funeral card nailed to a door.

Three winters. Three years of dust settling on a brass button in a knothole.

Wendell fetched the stepstool from the sacristy, the one used for trimming candles, and carried it to the confessional, and stood listening — to the chapel, to the street beyond the windows, to the building itself, which had learned to listen back these past weeks. Nothing but wind and the far-off drill-yard calling numbers. Wendell climbed.

The knot was where memory kept it. Wendell worked the button loose with two fingers, feeling the dust of three years come away in soft grey ridges, and brought it down into the light.

Brass eagle. Green tarnish along one edge where damp had gotten at it. Warm from the beam, or perhaps from Wendell's hand; impossible to say.

Here was the problem Wendell turned over, standing on a stepstool in an occupied chapel, holding a button worth hanging for: a signal must be readable to exactly one person and invisible to everyone else. Anything written was evidence. Anything spoken to a third party was testimony waiting to be extracted. The town crawled with informants and clerks and bored soldiers who read other people's post for pay. Whatever Wendell did next had to look like nothing at all.

But Reyes would walk past the infirmary. Wendell was sure of it — liaison officers to armistice delegations crossed between the lines, attended conferences, passed through towns like Marrowfield under escort. And Reyes, whatever he had become, whatever coat he wore now, had been the fourth person to learn this chapel, this beam, this stupid sacred game. If any part of him survived in there — the man who held dying men's hands, the man who'd made them promise things on leave-drunk nights — he would look up.

Wendell climbed the stool a second time. With the tip of a knife they cleaned the knothole out properly, widened it a fraction, and pressed the button back in — but not deep, not as it had been. They seated it shallow, proud of the wood, tilted slightly forward, so that it sat visibly, unmistakably, in a way it had not sat for three years. A button offered rather than stored. A pledge re-presented for claiming.

Then, because a signal meant for one man cannot be read by him unless he looks, and because hope requires a courier, Wendell did the second thing — the thing that made their pulse tick hard and fast in their throat. In the ledger of the parish poor-box, which hung openly by the chapel door and which anyone might consult, Wendell recorded that week's alms in their neat, upright hand, and beneath the sums added a single line in the column reserved for remarks:

*One item returned to store, awaiting claimant. Value: one promise, winter three years past. Condition: good.*

No name. No date but the ordinary ones. A sentence that read, to any clerk or colonel, like the harmless bookkeeping of a small church. But Ivo Reyes had helped Wendell carry the ledgers once, had teased them for writing like a schoolmistress, had read enough entries over their shoulder to know that Wendell's remarks column never wasted words, and had been present — witness, witnessed — on the exact night the button went into the beam. *Winter three years past.* If he came, if he looked, if any thread of the man they'd loved ran unbroken through the traitor's uniform — he would understand.

Wendell stepped down. Folded the stool. Put the knife away. Stood in the middle of the chapel and felt the fear arrive all at once, delayed, the way pain sometimes waits politely until the work is done — fear of Hargreave, fear for Tam, fear of what Odile would say, fear of the answer itself, because a signal can be ignored, and an ignored signal is its own kind of wound.

Odile had said to forget him. Wendell had nodded, and meant the nod, and had gone straight from the nod to a knothole with a knife.

*I'm sorry,* Wendell thought, in the direction of the vestry, where Odile was probably folding things. *I'll lie to you badly and you'll see through it immediately, and we'll both pretend otherwise, and that will be its own kind of sin. Forgive me. Or don't. But he gets heard out. Whoever he is now, whatever he's done — somebody who loved him gets to ask him why, face to face, before the reports have the final word.*

Outside, the light was failing. On the customs-house door, in the dark, the funeral card with Reyes's face would be curling at its paste-wet edges, and tomorrow a clerk would replace it with a fresh copy, and the town would go on filing a living man under *dead* and a brother under *enemy*.

In the poor-box ledger, in a remark column no one important would ever read, a promise sat waiting to be collected.

Three days later, Wendell came down the infirmary steps at dawn and found the courtyard gate open, though it was always kept barred — and a soldier's escort waiting at the lane's end, and among them, tall and straight in a grey uniform, a man whose face was older than the photograph and whose eyes went, before they went anywhere else, upward, toward the chapel roofline.

Toward the beam. Toward the button.

He had seen it.
