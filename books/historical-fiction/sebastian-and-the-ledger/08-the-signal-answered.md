## 8. The Signal Answered

The lamp in the liaison office burned down to its last finger of oil before Reyes allowed himself to admit he had seen it.

He had been pretending otherwise for three hours — pretending to read the dispatch summaries, pretending to draft a memorandum on prisoner exchanges that no one would act upon, pretending that his eyes did not keep sliding, traitorously and with terrible precision, to the north transept window of the chapel across the square. But there was no use lying to himself in an empty room. He had always been bad at it. That was the whole trouble with Ivo Reyes: even as a boy he could lie beautifully to everyone except the one person who mattered.

The signal was a candle. One candle, placed in the chapel's easternmost window at dusk and moved — he counted, though his hands were not quite steady — four times. Left, right, left, left.

*Left-right-left-left.* The rhythm Bess used to tap on the kitchen table when supper was ready and Wendell was late from rounds. *Come home; come home; come home now.* They had invented it years ago, all five of them around that scarred oak table, laughing at how absurd it would be if soldiers ever needed such a thing — and then they had kept it, because found families keep their small liturgies the way churches keep their relics, not because they are useful but because they are true.

Someone in that chapel knew him. Someone remembered.

Reyes stood, crossed to the window, and looked out at Marrowfield under occupation. The town wore its conquerors the way a body wears a fever: streets patrolled, doorways shuttered, the garrison flag hanging limp over the old armory where the colonel's clerks worked past midnight copying other men's deaths into cleaner columns. And there, across the wet cobbles, the chapel with its single candle burning in the dark like a held breath.

Wendell. It had to be Wendell. Odile would never risk the sanctuary; Bess would never leave her kitchen unguarded while soldiers walked the town; Tam was in the cells or dead, depending on which rumor you believed, and Reyes had spent two nights choosing neither answer because both were unbearable. But Wendell — Wendell who had once climbed the water tower at midnight to hang a lantern so a lost supply cart could find the road — Wendell would light a candle and move it in a pattern only five people alive had ever learned.

Four people, now. He corrected himself and hated the correction.

He checked his watch. Eleven forty. The sentries changed at half past; the new pair would be cold, bored, and eager for the brazier by the armory wall, which left the square blind for perhaps twenty minutes if the fog held. The fog was holding. It had come off the marsh at sundown, thick and salt-smelling, and lay over Marrowfield like a sheet drawn up to the chin.

Reyes took his greatcoat from the hook. In the mirror by the door he saw what the enemy delegation saw every day: a tall man in a gray uniform with the collar tabs of a liaison captain, thirty-one years old, hair going early silver at the temples, a face that had learned to arrange itself into pleasant blankness. He looked, he thought, exactly like what he claimed to be. That was the horror of it. Somewhere in the last three years the mask had grown into the bone.

He paused with his hand on the latch.

Every rational voice he possessed spoke against going. His handler, Major Sorren, watched him with the patience of a man defusing something and hoping to watch it detonate. The enemy delegation suspected him — he had seen it in Colonel Hargreave's eyes across the negotiating table, that flat appraising stare, a man taking inventory. If Reyes was seen leaving his quarters at midnight to meet a contact in occupied territory, no explanation on earth would save him. He would be shot by whichever side caught him first, and the other side would send a courteous note of congratulations.

And yet.

He went down the back stairs, past the sleeping billets, through the kitchen where a cook snored beside a banked stove, and out into the fog.

* * *

Marrowfield at night was a town holding its breath. The fog muffled everything — his boots on the cobbles, the creak of the sign above the cooper's shop, even the river, which he could hear only as a suggestion of movement somewhere to his left. Lamplighters had long since surrendered; the few lights that burned did so behind heavy curtains, and their glow arrived in the street as a soft bruising rather than illumination.

He knew these streets. That was the unbearable part. He had carried Bess's market baskets down this alley; he had taught Tam to swim in the mill pond at the end of this lane; he had kissed — no. Not that. There had been one evening, the summer before the war closed over everything like a hand closing over a candle flame, when he and Wendell had walked back from the harvest dance along the river path and neither of them had spoken for a mile, and the silence had been so full that he had thought, *this is the shape my life will take,* and then the mobilization orders came and the shape was cut away.

Three years of silence since. Three years in which the reports said Captain Ivo Reyes had died at Vellany Crossing, and then, eighteen months later, that he hadn't died but had gone over — that he served the other side now, that his name appeared on lists posted in market squares beside the word COLLABORATOR, that nurses spat at his photograph in the mess tents. He had read those reports himself, forwarded through his own delegation's intelligence section, and understood them for what they were: his cover, built brick by brick out of his own disgrace. To be believed by the enemy, he had first to be believed a traitor by his friends.

He had told himself they would understand. He had told himself it often enough that some nights it almost sounded true.

The chapel door was unlocked. Of course it was; Odile kept it unlocked, *the house of God does not bolt its door*, she used to say, though he noticed the bolts had been added anyway sometime in the second year of the war. Inside, the darkness smelled of stone and tallow and dried flowers, and beneath it all, faintly, the antiseptic ghost of the infirmary next door — carbolic and clean linen, a smell that hit him like a fist below the ribs, because it was the smell of the years when he had been happy.

A figure rose from the shadow of the third pew.

Reyes's hand went to his belt before his mind caught up — and then stopped, because the figure was slight, moving with that particular economy he remembered from watching Wendell cross a crowded ward without disturbing a single patient. A nurse's walk. A walk that had learned to carry grief without spilling it.

"Wendell," he said. The name came out ruined.

"Don't." Wendell's voice was low, roughened, and it had changed — deeper, or harder, or simply worn into a different grain by three years of things he had not witnessed. "Don't say anything yet. Come here where I can see your face."

They moved into the faint gray wash of light from the clerestory, and there was Wendell Almeida, thinner than memory, older, a fading bruise along one jawbone that made something violent rise in Reyes's chest — and looking at him with an expression Reyes had rehearsed for three years and was now entirely unprepared to receive. Not joy. Not hatred. Something between, something that hurt worse than either: the look of a person checking a coin against a memory of the coin.

"You're taller," Wendell said finally. "Or I've shrunk."

"The war takes inches from everyone."

"Does it." Wendell's eyes went to his uniform — the gray wool, the collar tabs — and Reyes watched the warmth bank itself down to coals. "I have questions, Ivo. I'm going to ask them once each, and you're going to answer straight, because I don't have the strength tonight for anything else. Do you understand me?"

"I understand."

"Are you a collaborator? Truly? In your heart, whatever that means — do you work for them?"

"Yes," Reyes said, and watched Wendell flinch. "That's the truth, Wendell. I wear their uniform, I sit at their table, I carry their papers. I am, in every way that can be proven by a court, a collaborator."

Wendell's breath went out slowly. In the dark, Reyes heard the sound a person makes when a hope dies quietly.

"And the second question," Wendell said, and here the voice cracked, just barely, just enough, "is whether any part of the man who ate at Bess's table is still inside the man wearing that coat."

* * *

There was a bench near the baptistry where they used to hide during Odile's long sermons, shoulder to shoulder, passing a stolen apple back and forth. Reyes sat down on it now and Wendell remained standing, arms folded, and the geometry of it — seated and standing, confessor and penitent, though neither could have said which was which — felt like a scene from one of the tragedies Odile read aloud on winter evenings.

"Ask me about the ledger," Reyes said.

Wendell went very still.

"Don't look like that. Yes, I know it exists. No, I don't know where it is. Yes, I know what's in it, or enough: names. Names the officers struck from the casualty reports. Five years of them, Wendell — I know how you worked, you couldn't help yourself, you'd sooner stop breathing than let a man vanish unwritten." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I know because I helped build the machine that erases them. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I sat in rooms where the counts were adjusted. I watched clerks strike men whose deaths were inconvenient — wrong regiment, wrong week, wrong politics. And eighteen months ago I started feeding the corrections back across the lines. False figures, doctored totals, casualty numbers that quietly disagree with Hargreave's official returns. My delegation thinks I'm a genius. Their negotiators keep asking why the enemy's own arithmetic won't hold still."

"And the numbers come from—"

"A ledger I have never seen. Kept by someone whose name I never speak aloud, because speaking it would burn them." He looked up. "I built my entire position on the belief that you were alive and writing. Every false figure I've fed them for eighteen months has been an educated guess dressed in your handwriting's absence. Do you see? I didn't come to your signal tonight to betray it. I came because I need it, yes — but I needed it before I ever saw the candle."

Wendell lowered themselves onto the bench after all, at the far end, a careful distance. The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere outside a patrol passed, boots squelching, voices low, and both of them froze until it faded.

"Why should I believe you?" Wendell asked at last. It wasn't hostile. It was genuinely, terribly open — the question of a person who wanted to believe and knew wanting was worthless as evidence. "Odile says love makes terrible witnesses. She says I'll believe anything you say because I spent five years—" A pause. Recalculation. "Because we were family."

"Your chaplain is wiser than I ever was. Listen to her."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Reyes agreed. "It isn't. Here's the only one I have." He reached into his greatcoat slowly — Wendell tensed — and drew out a folded paper, worn soft at the creases, and held it out. "Vellany Crossing. The action where I supposedly died. Read the casualty return. Then read the margin."

Wendell took it, struck a match with hands steadier than Reyes expected, and read. The matchlight trembled on their face.

"This number is wrong," Wendell said. "The count here — two hundred eleven dead. The real count was higher. We received the wounded. I wrote—" Wendell stopped.

"You wrote the ones they omitted. Forty-one names, Wendell. Forty-one men the report erased, and one of them was carrying a message I'd given him. When the falsified return reached our intelligence section, I recognized the arithmetic. Too clean. Too convenient. Someone on the other side was counting the men nobody counted." His voice roughened. "I've spent eighteen months trying to find that someone. Tonight the someone lit a candle in a window and tapped out Bess's supper code, and I walked across an occupied town knowing it might be a trap, and I'd walk it again, and that is either proof of loyalty or proof of folly and I genuinely no longer know which."

Silence filled the chapel the way water fills a hull — slowly, then all at once.

"The promise," Wendell said quietly.

Reyes closed his eyes. He had known this was coming; he had walked across the square composing the answer and now that it was here, no composed words survived.

"You asked me to hear you out," Wendell continued. "Bess's kitchen. You were drunk on plum brandy and homesick, and you said, *if they ever call me enemy, hear me before you believe the paper.* So I'm hearing you. This is me hearing you, Ivo. And I want — God help me, I want to believe you so badly my teeth ache. But Odile warned me. And Tam is in the cells because of papers I hid, and soldiers searched our infirmary, and there's a colonel across the square who would burn this town for one book, and you arrive out of the fog asking for exactly that book." Wendell turned to face him fully. "So I'll ask the last question. Why do you need it? Not the pretty version. The true one."

Reyes looked at the dark window where the candle had burned, and gave the true answer, because he had promised himself he would give nothing else tonight, even if it destroyed him.

"Because the talks are dying," he said. "Both sides are stalling on the casualty figures — Hargreave's returns are lies, ours are guesses, and neither delegation will sign a peace built on numbers they can't trust. There are men in that delegation who want the war to continue; the figures serve them. Your ledger is the only honest count in existence. With it, the lies collapse. Without it—" He spread his hands. "Without it, we negotiate until spring, and spring means another offensive, and another offensive means another five hundred names only you will write down. I need the ledger to end the war, Wendell. And I am terrified, every hour, that I am becoming the kind of man who would say anything to get it. Both things are true. I can't untangle them. I don't think I'm supposed to."

Wendell was silent for a long time. When they finally spoke, the anger had drained out of their voice and left something rawer underneath.

"Do you remember," they said, "what you said to me at the harvest dance? Before any of this?"

"I remember everything about that night."

"You said you'd come back when it was over and we'd reopen the infirmary and Bess would cook too much and Tam would complain and Odile would pretend to disapprove. You said that was the plan. That was the whole plan." Wendell's voice caught. "I built three years on that plan, Ivo. And then I read your name on a list of traitors, and I built three more years on hating you, and now you're sitting in our chapel smelling of fog and other people's uniforms, telling me the plan might still exist. And I don't know whether I'm talking to my brother or to the best liar either army ever produced."

"Neither do I," Reyes said. "Some nights that's the only honest sentence I have."

Wendell laughed — a short, broken sound, quickly strangled, the laugh of a person crying in the only way they permitted themselves. Then they stood, and stood close, close enough that Reyes could see the exhaustion carved under their eyes, and did not touch him, and the not-touching was louder than speech.

"I can't give it to you tonight," Wendell said. "Don't ask again tonight. I have to think. I have to talk to—" A breath. "To my family. All of them. Even the parts of the family that wear gray."

"It's more than I hoped for."

"It shouldn't be. It's less than you deserve, whichever way the truth falls." Wendell moved toward the shadows by the sacristy door, then stopped. "Ivo. The bruise on my jaw — you looked at it like you wanted to kill someone. Don't. It was a soldier being careless during the search. He's already forgotten it. That's the difference between you and them, maybe. You'd remember it forever."

"I would."

"That's what I'm betting on," Wendell said softly. "God forgive me. Go. Before the watch changes. Back door, through the churchyard, keep to the wall where the fog is thickest. And Ivo—" The voice dropped to nearly nothing. "The candle stays lit until I send word. If it goes out, don't come back. Whatever happens — don't come back."

Reyes rose. For one moment he let himself stand near enough that the old gravity between them pulled, near enough to smell carbolic and woodsmoke in Wendell's hair, three years of longing compressed into a single unbreathing instant — and then he stepped back, because some distances must be kept or they devour everything.

"Wendell. Whatever you decide — thank you for hearing me out. You kept the promise."

"I haven't decided anything," Wendell said. "Go."

He went, through the sacristy and out into the churchyard fog, keeping to the wall as instructed, and it was only halfway across the square, with the chapel dissolving behind him into the gray, that he realized two things with the cold clarity of a man stepping off a ledge.

First: Wendell had never actually denied having the ledger.

Second: from the dark upper window of the liaison office he had left three hours ago, a lamp had been burning. He had assumed it was his own, left carelessly lit.

It had been dark when he returned.

Someone had been in his room. Someone had watched him cross the square — and was, even now, probably reading the memorandum on prisoner exchanges he had left on his desk, searching it for what it could tell them about where Captain Ivo Reyes had gone on a fog-bound midnight, and why.
