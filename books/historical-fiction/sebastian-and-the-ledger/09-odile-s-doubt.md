## 9. Odile's Doubt

The chapel smelled of beeswax and cold stone, and Odile had come to love that smell more than any incense of her novitiate, because it was the smell of a place that asked nothing of anyone. Marrowfield's garrison chapel had been built by a garrison that no longer existed, for a regiment whose banners had rotted in some quartermaster's cellar, and so it belonged now to whoever needed it — the wounded who wanted quiet, the dying who wanted company, the nurses who came at odd hours to sit where no one would ask them to be strong.

She was polishing the brass rail when Wendell came in, and she knew before the door closed behind them that something had happened. Twenty years in infirmaries taught you to read a body the way other people read weather: the set of the shoulders, the too-careful hands, the way Wendell stood just inside the doorway as if the room might object to their presence. Guilt had a posture. So did hope. Wendell was wearing both at once, and neither fit well.

"You missed supper," Odile said, not turning from the rail. "Bess left you a bowl under a cloth. It will be glue by now."

"I ate."

"You didn't." She set down her cloth and turned. "You never eat when your mind is chewing on something else. Sit down, child, and tell me what it is, or I'll stand here until Vespers and we'll both be miserable."

Wendell sat, on the third pew, hands folded in their lap like a penitent who hadn't decided what they were penitent *for*. The evening light came through the east window in that flat amber the autumn gave you, laying stripes across the stone floor, and Odile watched those stripes move with the slow patience of someone who had learned that silence was a tool and not an absence.

"Reyes is alive," Wendell said finally.

The name landed in the chapel the way a dropped chalice lands — a sound you feel in your teeth before your mind has caught up to it. Odile kept her face still. She was good at keeping her face still; it was half her vocation and all her survival.

"So the notice said," she answered. "Declared enemy collaborator. Liaison to their delegation." She let each phrase fall separately, like coins counted out onto a table. "I read the bulletin twice."

"A bulletin," Wendell said, with a bitterness that startled her, "is not a person. A bulletin doesn't have — he used to sing off-key, Odile. He sang off-key on purpose because Tam hated it. He used to steal the ends of Bess's bread loaves before they'd cooled. He sat where you're standing once and told me—" Wendell stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "He told me this chapel was the only honest room in Marrowfield, because nothing in it pretended to be anything but old."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen him. Twice." Wendell's hands tightened in their lap. "Once across the square, in their uniform. Once — last night — in the lane behind the tannery. He says he's ours. He says the figures are false, the casualty reports, both sides cooking the counts, and he knows about—"

Odile raised one hand, palm out, and Wendell stopped. Not because she commanded it; she had no command over Wendell Almeida and never had. But there were words that should not exist in open air, even in a chapel that belonged to no one, even in a town that had been swallowed whole by requisition orders and bayonets at the corners. Some words were only safe underground, beneath floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, written in a hand small enough to hide inside a hymnal.

"He knows," Wendell finished anyway, softly, watching her face. "About the ledger. He knew before I told him."

*There it is,* Odile thought, and felt the cold arrive in her chest, the particular cold that had nothing to do with drafty stone.

***

She made Wendell walk with her after that, out past the chapel yard into the lane where the elms still held their leaves and the sentries' torches burned at the far corner, far enough that two voices could be low and lost among wind. She walked slowly, leaning on her stick more than her knees strictly required, because walking slowly gave a body time to think and gave a companion time to stop talking and start listening.

"Tell me exactly what he said," she instructed. "Not what it meant to you. What he said. Word for word, as near as prayer."

Wendell tried. They were honest, Wendell, painfully honest, and that honesty had always been the finest thing about them and the most dangerous. They repeated Reyes's phrases: *the numbers are wrong on both sides*, *there's a book*, *I can prove my loyalty if you trust me first*. And Odile listened the way she listened to a fevered patient — not to the content of the delirium but to its pattern, its rhythm, the places where the voice reached for something and found nothing.

"Notice," she said at last, "that every sentence he spoke put the burden of belief on you."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean he asked you to trust him *before* he proved anything. That is not how honest men work, Wendell. An honest man says: here is what I know, here is how I came to know it, test me. A handler's man says: prove yourself to me, and I'll consider proving myself to you. It's the grammar of recruitment. I've heard it before." Her stick clicked against the stones, steady as a metronome. "In the last war, in the field hospital at Corvenne, we had a chaplain's assistant who turned out to be feeding troop movements through the laundry lists. Charming man. Everyone loved him. He always asked to be trusted first."

Wendell was silent for ten paces. Then, stubbornly: "Or he's telling the truth and asking the only thing he can ask, because he has nothing else. Because he's alone on both sides of a line and the only people he ever trusted are here."

"Yes," Odile said. "That is also possible. That is precisely what makes this so damnable."

She stopped walking. The torchlight at the corner guttered in the wind; somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything, the way dogs in occupied towns learn to do. She turned and looked at Wendell fully, and she saw them clearly — younger than their years tonight, lit from inside by a joy they were trying to keep leashed, and terrified of her in equal measure, because she could see it, and seeing it meant she might take it away.

"Child," she said gently, "I am going to say a hard thing, and I want you to hear all of it before you hate me. Love makes terrible witnesses."

Wendell flinched as if struck.

"Not because lovers lie," Odile went on, relentless and soft at once, the tone she used for delivering deaths. "Because love *selects*. It edits. You look at that man in the lane and you see the boy who stole bread, the voice that sang off-key, the empty chair at supper for three years. Every memory you own argues for him. And every memory he owns of *you* — he knows which ones to invoke. He knows the promise. Oh, don't look shocked; Bess told me years ago, the night he left, because Bess cannot keep a confidence any more than a sieve can keep water, God preserve her. He knows you swore to hear him out. And a man who knows he holds your promise holds a key to your judgment, whether his heart is true or bought and paid for."

"You think he's a plant." Flat. Wounded.

"I think," Odile said, "that the enemy took our hospital records, our casualty rolls, our supply manifests, within a week of arriving, and that Colonel Hargreave has spent every day since hunting a leak he is convinced lives in this town. I think a liaison officer attached to the armistice delegation has access to both sides' figures — the very figures someone has been disputing. And I think that if I wished to find a hidden ledger of omitted names, the cleverest road to it would not be a search warrant. It would be a dead man's face, coming home."

The dog stopped barking. In the silence Odile heard Wendell breathing, quick and shallow, and made herself finish.

"If he is true, then he is a drowning man and reaching for us is the only rope he sees. If he is false, then he is a hook, and everything he remembers about us is the bait. And I cannot tell which from where I stand, and neither can you, and *that* is the whole of it, Wendell. Not that I believe he's false. That I believe we cannot afford to guess."

"And what would you have me do?" Wendell's voice cracked on it, anger finally surfacing, and Odile welcomed it — anger was honest where joy was not. "Turn him away? Report him? Let him hang as a collaborator without hearing one word?"

"I would have you remember who you are protecting," Odile said. "It isn't yourself. There is a book under our feet with forty names — no, don't startle, I've known since the second winter, I dust the same floorboards you do — and every one of those names has a mother who thinks her child deserted, or drowned, or vanished, because an officer's pen decided grief was bad for morale. If Reyes leads Hargreave to that book, those mothers wait forever. If Reyes is true and we spurn him, the war grinds on another year on top of lies, and how many names does *that* add? Either error costs lives. So we do not get to act from the heart tonight. We must act from evidence. Do you understand me?"

Wendell looked at her for a long moment, and Odile watched the anger drain and something harder settle in behind it — not agreement, never agreement, but comprehension. Wendell understood her perfectly. That was the tragedy of the two of them; they had understood each other completely for fifteen years, and understanding had never once meant obeying.

"I hear you," Wendell said quietly. "And I'm going to meet him again."

"Wendell—"

"In the open. Daylight. The mill path where anyone can see, because if he's true he won't mind being seen, and if he's false he'll want the dark." A ghost of the old stubborn smile. "That's evidence, Odile. You taught me to collect it myself."

They turned and walked back toward the infirmary, and Odile followed at her slower pace, watching their retreating back in the torchlight, and felt the oldest fear of her vocation rise in her throat — the fear not of losing patients, which she had done by the hundred, but of watching the ones she loved walk toward a thing she could not diagnose and could not cut out.

***

That night she did not sleep. She knelt in the chapel instead, though kneeling hurt, before the plain wooden cross some long-dead carpenter had made, and she prayed the way she actually prayed — not with requests, which she considered presumptuous, but with inventory.

*Let him be true,* she offered, as one item among others. *But if he is false, let Wendell see it before it costs the book.*

The candle guttered. She thought about the reliquary behind the altar, empty these thirty years save for a splinter of uncertain provenance, deep enough and dark enough to hold a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. She thought about the order she had received that morning, delivered by a pleasant young clerk with frightened eyes: *all concealed papers to be reported, madam, per the colonel's directive regarding sedition*. She had smiled and said of course, Father would never dream of concealing anything, and the lie had sat in her mouth like a coin, cold and heavy, and she had not yet decided whether to swallow it or spit it out.

Two betrayals possible, she thought. One toward church and crown. One toward family. A woman could spend a whole life avoiding the first and die of the second, or the reverse, and no scripture she knew settled the account.

She rose stiffly, pinched out the candle, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the building settle around her — the old beams ticking, the wind finding the loose pane in the south window, the town holding its breath the way it had held it for a month now.

And then, through that loose pane, faint from the direction of the officers' billets, she heard singing. Off-key. Deliberately, unmistakably off-key, drifting across the square like a signature, like a hand raised in greeting, like bait on a hook glinting in dark water.

Odile stood very still in the black chapel, her heart going hard against her ribs, and understood that whatever Captain Ivo Reyes was — rope or hook — he had already begun to cast his line.
