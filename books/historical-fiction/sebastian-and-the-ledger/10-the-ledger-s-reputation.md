## 10. The Ledger's Reputation

Colonel Edmund Hargreave had learned long ago that a war was not won by armies but by paper. Bullets decided the shape of a line on a map for a season; documents decided it for a generation. He kept this belief the way other men kept religion — quietly, constantly, and with an intolerance for heresy.

So when Private Tammas Wren was brought up from the cells at half past seven in the morning, blinking in the grey light of the requisitioned courthouse like a mole dragged into daylight, Hargreave received him not as an interrogation of flesh but as an audit.

The boy — he could not be more than nineteen, though his hands were already cook's hands, scarred and knuckle-swollen from cauldrons and cleavers — stood before the colonel's desk with his feet slightly apart and his chin down but not bowed. There was a difference between the two, Hargreave noted. A bowed chin was submission. This was something else: a man keeping his face out of the light so no one could read it.

"Sit," Hargreave said, and did not look up from the folder in front of him. He let the silence do its work. In his experience silence broke men faster than shouting, because shouting gave them something to brace against, while silence left them alone with the sound of their own breathing and the arithmetic of their own fear.

Wren sat. The chair creaked. He folded his enormous hands in his lap like a boy at church.

Hargreave let another full minute pass. Outside the window, the occupation of Marrowfield went about its business: boots on cobbles, the clatter of a supply wagon, a sergeant somewhere calling a roll that would be answered in tired voices. The town had surrendered its rhythms to the army's rhythms, and Hargreave found the new music satisfactory. A town that marched when you told it to march was a town that understood its position.

"Private Wren," he said at last, looking up. "Orderly, Marrowfield garrison infirmary. Five years' service." He turned a page he did not need to read. "You have an unblemished conduct record. Not one mark against you. That interests me."

The boy said nothing. His ears had gone red.

"A man with an unblemished record does not get caught carrying contraband through a checkpoint at night," Hargreave continued. "Either your record is a lie, or what you carried is not what I would call contraband at all. Which is it?"

"I don't know what you mean, sir."

"You were carrying a book."

"I was carrying my effects, sir. Personal things."

"In a oilcloth wrapping, at midnight, moving away from the infirmary toward the tannery district." Hargreave closed the folder with deliberate softness. "Private, I have been reading men since before you could shave. You are frightened, which is sensible. But you are not confused, which tells me someone has coached you. Someone has told you precisely how little to say. So let us save ourselves the unpleasantness of the afternoon. Where is the book now?"

"I don't know what book you mean, sir."

It was said without defiance. That was the maddening thing. Hargreave had broken harder men than this in an afternoon — captains, even a major once — and they had all eventually given him either information or rage, both of which were usable. This boy offered neither. He simply sat inside his ignorance like a man inside a stone wall, and the wall had no door.

"The infirmary keeps records," Hargreave tried, changing angle. "Admission rolls, treatment logs, mortality schedules. Those are official papers. They have been requisitioned along with everything else. What I am asking about is anything *unofficial*. Anything Nurse Almeida might keep privately. A personal register. Notes. Memoranda."

Something moved behind the boy's eyes — a flicker, quick as a fish under ice — and was gone.

"No, sir."

"You would know if there were such a thing. You lived in that building. You swept around that man's desk every day for five years."

"Weeks, sir. I've only been posted here since spring."

Hargreave smiled, thin as a scalpel. "Your file says five years."

The red in the boy's ears deepened to something approaching purple. "Then the file's wrong, sir."

*Then the file's wrong.* Said flatly, apologetically almost, by a man who plainly knew that contradicting a colonel's document was itself a flogging offence — and who contradicted it anyway. Hargreave felt the first cold thread of genuine interest wind through him. One did not lie about a service record to protect a book unless the book mattered enormously.

And then, beneath the interest, fainter, like a smell of smoke before any flame is visible: unease.

"Very well," Hargreave said, rising. "We'll continue this after you've had time to reconsider the accuracy of files." He pressed the bell on his desk. "Take Private Wren back. Bread and water. No visitors."

As the guards walked the boy out, Wren paused at the door — just a half-second, just long enough — and looked back over his shoulder. Not at Hargreave. At the wall behind Hargreave's desk, where a map of the northern sector hung. Then he was gone.

Hargreave stared at the empty doorway for a long moment. The boy had looked at nothing. Boys being marched back to cells looked at nothing because they were too terrified to look anywhere. And yet Hargreave's instinct, the instinct that had made his career, whispered that the look had been a message — not to Hargreave, but past him. To whoever might be watching through the observation of the corridor.

*They are organized*, the whisper said. *A cook's boy with no marks on his record, lying about his file, carrying oilcloth at midnight, coached to say nothing. That is not panic. That is a plan.*

He disliked plans he had not authored.

* * *

At eleven, his clerk brought the morning's inventory.

Lieutenant Sorrel was a narrow young man with ink-stained fingers and the anxious precision of someone who had found the one thing in life he was good at and intended to perform it perfectly until death. He had spent four days cataloguing the contents of the Marrowfield garrison infirmary, and he arrived now with two crates of ledgers, registers, and correspondence balanced on his hip and a look on his face that Hargreave had learned to read across twenty years of staff officers: the look of a man bearing bad news disguised as thoroughness.

"The hospital records, sir. As ordered." Sorrel set the crates down. "I've indexed them. There is, ah. There is a discrepancy."

"Discrepancies are why we keep records, Lieutenant. Out with it."

Sorrel opened the top ledger — the official mortality schedule, five years of deaths recorded in the infirmary's cramped clerical hand — and beside it laid a sheaf of copies: the casualty returns Wendell Almeida's infirmary had submitted monthly to divisional headquarters, and the divisional summaries compiled upward from them, the numbers that climbed the chain of command until they arrived, polished and reassuring, on desks such as Hargreave's own.

"Counting the returns against the schedule, sir, the infirmary reported three hundred and forty-one dead over the five years." Sorrel wet his lips. "But the admission rolls, cross-referenced against burial permits and the sexton's register at Saint Brannoc's, indicate—" he swallowed— "four hundred and six."

The number hung in the room.

Sixty-five men. Dead, buried, blessed, and vanished — subtracted somewhere between the infirmary's floor and the army's memory. Hargreave looked at the columns without seeing them. Sixty-five was not a rounding error. Sixty-five was not clerical drift. Sixty-five was a policy, executed patiently, month after month, by someone with steady nerves and beautiful handwriting.

"Some of these will be men transferred to other facilities," Hargreave said. His voice came out level. He was rather proud of that.

"I checked, sir. Transfers are documented separately; none of the sixty-five appear in transfer logs. Some may be misfiled burials. But the pattern—" Sorrel turned pages with careful fingers— "the pattern isn't random. Look at the months, sir. The omissions cluster after engagements where the divisional return was already near the authorised ceiling. After Harrow Ford. After the second push at Cattermole Bridge. It's as though—" the young man hesitated, hearing his own words, then finished anyway, because thoroughness was his faith— "as though someone was trimming the count to fit the report, sir. Rather than fitting the report to the count."

There it was. Spoken aloud, in the flat administrative grammar of an inventory, by a lieutenant too honest to understand what he was saying.

Hargreave kept his face composed. Inside, something old and cold turned over.

Because Hargreave knew exactly whose hand fitted reports to counts. He had signed those divisional summaries himself — hundreds of them, year after year, each one certifying figures he had never audited, trusting the chain beneath him the way a man trusts the bridge beneath his feet. The authorised ceiling was *his* instrument; he had defended it before the General Staff as a matter of morale and supply discipline. A division that admitted its losses stayed within its ration of replacements. A division whose losses crept upward invited inspection, and inspection invited questions about how the losses had been incurred, and some of those engagements — Cattermole Bridge especially, Cattermole Bridge above all — had been conducted under orders whose wisdom Hargreave had defended in writing, vigorously, with his name attached.

If sixty-five men had been quietly erased to keep those reports comfortable, then the erasure had served him. Had flattered him. Had been, functionally, his own work done by another man's pen.

And now a nurse with a private ledger was sitting on the evidence, and the evidence was sitting in *his* occupied town.

"Who else has seen this analysis?" Hargreave asked.

"No one, sir. I brought it straight to you."

"Burn your working notes. Keep only the final index, and lodge that with my aide, sealed. You will not discuss the discrepancy with anyone — not the provosts, not the delegation liaison, not your fellow clerks. If anyone asks what you found in the infirmary records, the answer is: routine, orderly, complete. Do you understand me?"

Sorrel blinked. "Sir — shouldn't the discrepancy be escalated? To the armistice commission, perhaps? They're disputing casualty figures at the talks right now; if our true count differs from the reported—"

"*Burn your notes,* Lieutenant."

The words came out with more edge than Hargreave had intended, and he watched the young man flinch and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what he must look like. Not a commander protecting an investigation. A man protecting himself. Sorrel was not clever enough to see it fully, but he was precise enough to feel it, the way a dog feels weather.

"Yes, sir," Sorrel said quietly, and gathered his crates, and left.

Hargreave stood alone in his office for a long time.

He was not a corrupt man. He wanted that understood, if only by himself, standing in the empty room like a defendant addressing a jury of furniture. Corruption was theft; he had stolen nothing. Corruption was cowardice; he had volunteered for every hard posting of his career. What he had done was manage. A war was a machine that consumed truth along with men — everyone knew this, everyone accepted it — and a man who fed the machine accurate despair, month after month, would have starved it of morale, of reinforcements, of the political patience that kept it supplied at all. The ceiling on reported casualties was not a lie. It was a *load-bearing wall*. Remove it and the whole structure of the division's credibility came down, and with it the armistice position, and with it the country's ability to end this war on terms short of ruin.

That was the argument. It had always been the argument. It was a good argument, built of load-bearing logic in turn, and he had lived inside it for years the way one lives inside a house, forgetting the walls are walls.

And yet.

And yet sixty-five names. He found himself thinking not of the number but of the texture beneath it — mothers, in villages he had ridden through, who had waited for letters that the arithmetic had decided should never come. Somewhere out there were sixty-five families who had been told *missing* when the truth was *dead*, and the difference between those words was a wound that never closed properly. Missing men left wives unmarried to ghosts. Dead men, at least, could be mourned.

Someone had known all their names. Someone had written them down, in secret, for five years, in defiance of every order and convention, because the army would not carry them and someone had to.

*Almeida.*

Hargreave pulled the nurse's file. Thin. Competent. Commendations handled with the grudging brevity the medical service reserved for its best people. A note from the previous garrison chaplain: *"Conscientious to a fault. Keeps his own books — harmless habit."*

Harmless.

He set the file down and crossed to the window. Below, the courtyard of the former courthouse held a line of townspeople waiting for passes — farmers, a widow with two children, an old man leaning on a stick. Ordinary people, waiting patiently on the machinery. Beyond the wall, the spire of Saint Brannoc's stood against a low sky the colour of dishwater.

Somewhere in this town was a book with sixty-five names in it, and a boy in a cell who would not say one word about it, and a household — he had read the reports, the whole improbable devoted household of them — arranged around that book like a garrison around a flag.

And somewhere beyond that, at the stalled negotiating table, enemy commissioners were demanding casualty figures, insisting the published counts were false, insisting — with the particular smugness of men who suspect they are right — that the truth would prove their cause. If that book reached the table, it would not merely embarrass the division. It would confirm every enemy claim, invalidate every figure Hargreave had certified, convert his signature from authority into forgery, and hand the negotiation to the other side wrapped and ribboned. It might, he thought with a sensation like vertigo, prolong the war by months — all those additional dead arriving precisely because a nurse had insisted on counting the last ones honestly.

Or it might simply be justice. The thought arrived unwelcome and he put it down the way one puts down a hot iron.

No. Duty was duty. The record was the record; a state that permitted every subordinate to publish his own corrections dissolved into a mob of memoirists. Whatever the ledger contained belonged, properly, under seal, weighed by competent authority — meaning himself — and if weighing it required discretion about certain prior summaries, well. Discretion was also duty.

The reasoning was sound. He had always been able to make the reasoning sound. That, he suspected, watching the widow below shift her children's weight from hip to hip, was the most damning thing about himself he knew.

* * *

He summoned Captain Voss, his intelligence officer, at three.

Voss was a heavy, patient man with the face of a butcher and the temperament of a librarian, and Hargreave trusted him precisely because he generated no drama. He laid the mortality schedule and the returns side by side on the desk without comment and let Voss read.

When Voss finished, he was quiet for a moment. "The nurse."

"The nurse. Or whoever keeps his books with him." Hargreave tapped the schedule. "This is the original, Voss. These entries are contemporaneous — different ink batches, seasonal paper stock, I'd wager. Whoever wrote this wrote it as the men died. Which means somewhere there may be more. A fuller record. Names, circumstances, dates of death that don't appear here at all."

"You're thinking of the book the boy was carrying."

"I'm thinking that a man who falsifies nothing for five years, in secret, at risk of a firing squad, does not stop at totals. Totals are what officers want. Names are what the guilty need forgiveness for." Hargreave heard his own voice and disliked its heat. He cooled it. "Find the book. Quietly. The household will close ranks if we kick doors — the raid on the infirmary already taught them that. So we go sideways. Debt, drink, vanity, love. Everyone in that building has a seam. Find me a seam."

"And the boy?"

"Leave him in the cell. Hunger makes a man honest, but it works slowly, and I want him whole. He's worth more to me as a promise than as a corpse." Hargreave paused. "One more thing. The liaison officer attached to the enemy delegation — Reyes. Pull his file. Everything. Service history, family, the circumstances of his defection. I want to know who he writes to in this town."

Voss raised his heavy eyebrows a fraction. "You think the leak runs through the talks?"

"I think," Hargreave said, turning back to the window, to the spire, to the town full of patient, dangerous tenderness, "that a book like that doesn't stay hidden in a town this size without friends in high places. And I think the highest place in Marrowfield this month is a chair at the negotiating table."

After Voss had gone, Hargreave remained at the window until the light failed. Below, the widow collected her pass and led her children out through the gate, and he watched them go with the expression of a man doing sums he cannot make come out right — sixty-five names, one book, one signature, and the growing, unspeakable suspicion that somewhere in the dark of this occupied town, in a chapel or a cellar or a kitchen, a page was already written with a name on it that could undo him.

He did not know yet whose name. He would know soon.
