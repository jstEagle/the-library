## 11. Bargaining with Bess

The summons came at half past four in the afternoon, delivered by a corporal who stood in the kitchen doorway as though he feared the room might bite him. It well might have. The ovens were hot, the stockpot was simmering, and Bess Arden was standing at the worktable with a knife in her hand and flour to her elbows, and she had the particular stillness of a woman deciding whether to use one or the other.

"Captain Tam Harrow," the corporal read from his paper, in the tone of a man reading a laundry list, "is to be released into temporary custody pending further inquiry. You are instructed to prepare a meal for Colonel Hargreave, who will dine here this evening."

"Here," Bess said.

"The officers' mess is being repainted." The corporal's ears went pink. He was young — nineteen, perhaps twenty, with the soft chin of a boy who'd been eating army bread too long. "He asked for it to be here. He said—" the corporal consulted the paper again, as if hoping the sentence would have changed, "—he said he wished to take his meal where the cooking was honest."

Bess set down her knife with exaggerated care, laying it parallel to the table's edge like a soldier grounding arms.

"Well then," she said. "Tell the Colonel that honest cooking takes time, and that I'll need an hour, and that if he wants honesty he'll get it, but he may not care for the taste."

* * *

She sent word through the yard in three directions before the hour was out. To Wendell, confined now to the infirmary under house arrest: *the wolf is coming to my table; keep your door shut and your face dull.* To Odile at the chapel: *light every candle you own tomorrow morning and pray loudly enough for the whole street.* And to Tam, by way of the laundress Marta, who carried messages folded into hems because no one searches what they're afraid will come back dirty: *they're letting you go, love, and they'll be watching you walk, so walk slow and stupid and don't look at anyone.*

Then she cooked.

This was not the cooking of necessity. This was cooking as fortification, cooking as architecture, each dish a wall built course by course between herself and the man coming to sit across from her. She made barley soup first, because soup takes time and cannot be hurried and forces even a colonel to eat at the speed of patience. She made it rich — marrow bones browned until the kitchen smelled of iron and sweetness, onions cooked down to amber, a crust of rye floated on top like a raft. Then braised pork with prunes, which took the last of the good meat and she did not begrudge it. Bread still warm. A honey tart, because men like Hargreave always had a sweet tooth they pretended not to have, and a sweet tooth could be spoken to.

While she worked, she thought about power.

She had spent forty years in kitchens, and kitchens taught you things drawing rooms never learned. They taught you that everyone eats, and that everyone can be fed, and that the difference between feeding a person and managing them is only a matter of what you put in the bowl. She had watched generals weep over their mothers' recipes and lieutenants go quiet when the bread reminded them of home. Hunger was the great equalizer; appetite was the great confessor. A man would tell a cook things he'd never tell a priest, because the cook already knew him at his most animal, and there was no dignity left to defend.

Colonel Hargreave wanted something from her. He wanted the ledger — she understood that much, understood it the way you understand weather, without needing it explained. And he believed that offering Tam back was the coin that would buy it. Which meant he thought she loved Tam the way a mother loves, simply and tradeably. He was right about the love. He was wrong about the arithmetic.

* * *

He arrived at seven o'clock exactly, which told her he'd planned it — punctuality as a weapon, the arrival neither early enough to seem eager nor late enough to seem indifferent. Two guards remained outside the kitchen door. He came in alone.

Bess had seen him before, from a distance, on requisition day: a tall dry man with a face like a closed ledger himself, gray going to white at the temples, uniform pressed to the point of aggression. Up close he was worse and better than she'd expected. Worse, because his eyes were quick and intelligent and missed nothing — they went over her kitchen the way an auditor goes over accounts. Better, because he looked tired. Not theatrically tired, not the tiredness men perform to gain sympathy. Deeply, structurally tired, the tiredness of a man who has been carrying his own certainty up a long hill.

"Madam Arden." He removed his cap. Someone had taught him manners once, and the teaching held even now. "I'm told your cooking is the best in Marrowfield."

"You're told correctly." She gestured him to the scrubbed table, where she'd laid a cloth — clean, white, ironed, a small flag of civilization in occupied territory. "Sit. The soup is ready. The rest comes when it's ready, and not before, and if you try to hurry it you'll ruin it and I'll make you eat it ruined anyway."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, maybe, filed away in some drawer of a man long accustomed to locking drawers.

"You are not afraid of me," he observed, sitting.

"I've been afraid of plenty of things, Colonel. Boiling fat. Bad harvests. Fever years. You haven't made the list yet."

"And what does a woman have to do to make the list?"

"Threaten my family." She ladled the soup — a full bowl, generous, deliberate — and set it before him with bread and butter and a spoon polished bright. "Eat while it's hot."

He ate. That was the thing about men like him; whatever else they were, they had been raised to appreciate good food, and the first spoonful betrayed him. His shoulders came down a quarter inch. His eyes half-closed. For three seconds he was not a colonel but a boy somewhere, in some other kitchen, in some other country's war.

"It's very good," he said, and it cost him something to say it plainly.

"My mother's recipe. She fed soldiers too. Different uniforms." Bess sat opposite him with her own small bowl, though she didn't intend to eat much. "You didn't come for the soup."

"No." He took another spoonful anyway. "I came to talk about young Mr. Harrow."

"Tam."

"Tam." He said the name carefully, as though handling evidence. "He's been in our cells nine days. He hasn't given us anything. Not a word about the document we found him carrying, not a word about where it came from, nothing but his name and rank and — repeatedly — complaints about the food."

"He's right to complain. Army food is a crime."

"His silence is remarkable," Hargreave went on, ignoring her. "Most men break by the third day. Most boys break by the second. He's what, eighteen?"

"Nineteen next month."

"Nineteen." He set down his spoon and regarded her across the steam. "You understand my position, Madam Arden. There is a document missing from this town. A record of names — names that appear nowhere in official reports. Names that should not exist anywhere except in the memory of grieving families, and yet someone has been collecting them. Writing them down. Keeping them." He turned the spoon slowly on the cloth. "That document is now material to the armistice negotiations. Casualty figures are being disputed at the highest level. If false numbers are circulating — if someone has been feeding inaccurate counts to enemy delegates — that book is either the source of the falsehood or the proof of it. Either way, I am required to find it. Required by duty, and by the fact that a great many people will die slower deaths if these talks fail."

"That's a fine speech," Bess said. "You've practiced it."

"I have."

"Did you practice the part where you offer me something?"

His eyes lifted. Quick again. Auditor's eyes.

"Tam walks free tonight," he said. "Clean record. No trial, no mark against his name, no shadow following him into any future he cares to build. He becomes, officially, a boy who was never arrested at all." He paused. "In exchange, you tell me where the ledger is."

The kitchen clock ticked. The stockpot murmured on the stove. Outside, one of the guards coughed.

"You've got it backwards," Bess said.

"Have I?"

"You think you're offering to give him back. But he was never yours to give. He's mine. Ours. We raised him in this kitchen since he was fourteen and starving, and you took him, and now you want a reward for returning stolen goods." She rose, unhurried, and went to the oven. The pork needed turning. It did not need turning yet, but her hands wanted occupation, and hands that look busy ask fewer questions. "So let's say it properly: you took something of mine, and you're selling it back, and the price is a book full of dead men's names."

"Dead men's names," Hargreave repeated. "You say that lightly."

"I say it heavily. I've cooked their last suppers, Colonel. Eleven of them, over five years, boys who died in that infirmary whose names never appeared in any report your office signed. I know because I kept count myself, in my head, before I ever knew Wendell kept count on paper. Eleven boys whose mothers got letters saying *wounded, recovering* or nothing at all." She closed the oven door. "So don't talk to me about the weight of names. I carry them in flour and salt."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind the colonel's face. Something old and gated. She saw it and marked it, the way you mark a loose brick in a wall. Then it was gone, bricked over again, and he was all smooth official surface.

"If the ledger contains errors," he said quietly, "then those families were misinformed by whoever recorded them. The book is not mercy. It's confusion, weaponized."

"And if it contains truth?"

"Truth is what reports are for. Officially verified, properly contextualized—"

"Bless you," Bess said, "you sound like a sermon nobody could stay awake through."

* * *

The pork came out golden. She served it with prunes and pan juices and the warm bread, and he ate like a man doing arithmetic with every bite — pleasure on one side of the ledger, suspicion on the other. She refilled his plate without being asked. She complimented nothing of his, praised nothing, flattered nothing directly; instead she flattered the space around him. She told him the chair he sat in had been mended by Tam. She mentioned, idly, that the honey in the tart came from hives Tam tended. She spoke the boy's name into the room every few minutes the way you water a plant, letting the colonel sit in a house saturated with the child he was holding hostage.

And she talked. That was the true weapon — she talked the way rivers erode stone, gently, endlessly, about everything and nothing. About the price of prunes since the blockade. About how the chaplain's candles smoked terribly since the army requisitioned the good tallow. About her late husband, God rest him, who'd been a quartermaster and died believing paperwork was a form of prayer. She watched him listen despite himself, watched the auditor in him relax its grip minute by minute, and all the while she gave him nothing. Not a syllable about floors or reliquaries or loaves of bread. Every question he angled toward the ledger she caught and turned aside with an anecdote, the way you turn a blade with a rolled sleeve.

By the time the tart came, he had stopped asking questions altogether. She counted it a victory, though she knew better than to trust it. Silence in a man like Hargreave wasn't surrender. It was reloading.

"You're very good at this," he said finally, fork resting, looking at the tart as though it had insulted him. "This entire evening. The soup, the talk, the — atmosphere." He gestured vaguely at the kitchen, at the warmth of it. "I came here to interrogate a suspect and I find I've been hosted."

"Yes," Bess said pleasantly. "That happens."

"Why?" It was almost genuine — the question of a tired man. "Why resist? Give me the book, take the boy, and none of you need ever see another uniformed man in this kitchen again. The war ends eventually. Armistices come. What is one ledger against a household's peace?"

Bess poured him a small glass of the plum brandy she saved for fevers and funerals, and set it by his hand, and sat down across from him, and for the first time that night she let her voice drop all its armor.

"Because peace bought with forgotten names isn't peace, Colonel. It's just a quieter kind of war. My mother lived through the last one, and she used to say the killing stops and then everybody agrees to pretend, and the pretending lasts twenty years, and then somebody finds a list." She met his eyes. "Somebody always finds a list."

Hargreave looked at her for a long moment. The candle flame stood perfectly still between them.

"You believe the book will surface regardless," he said. "No matter what I do."

"I believe books are like bread dough, sir. You can punch them down as often as you like. They rise."

* * *

He rose to leave at ten o'clock. At the door he stopped, cap in hand, and did not turn around fully — spoke instead to the doorframe, to the night beyond it, in a voice stripped suddenly of all its varnish.

"The boy complained about the food," he said. "Every day. Nine days." A pause. "It was the worst complaint I've ever heard a prisoner make, and the bravest. He was telling us you'd taught him that complaining is how you stay human." He put on his cap. "Thank you for dinner, Madam Arden. It was the best meal I've had in six years, and I regret the circumstances of it more than you can know."

Then he was gone, and the guards with him, and the kitchen was hers again — cold at the edges where the door had stood open, the candle flames leaning in the draft.

Bess stood a long while among the dishes. Her hands shook now, having waited politely all evening for permission. She let them. Fear delayed was fear doubled, and she owed herself the full payment.

Then she banked the fire, wrapped the leftover tart in cloth, and went to the window where Marta the laundress was passing, right on time, basket on hip.

"For the infirmary," Bess murmured, sliding the bundle through the crack. Under the tart, folded flat, a scrap of paper: *He offered. I refused. He believes the book will surface — and he's right, so it must be far away before he decides to dig. Tell Wendell: move it soon, move it far, and tell no one — not even me.*

Marta nodded and was gone into the dark.

Bess washed the colonel's dishes last, alone, scrubbing the good plates until they sang. And it was only afterward, setting them back on the shelf, that she noticed what her sharp eyes had catalogued all evening without telling her: the colonel had eaten everything she gave him, yes. But he had never once touched the honey tart.

And a man who refuses sweetness, she thought, standing very still in her warm kitchen, is a man who's already decided the meal is a negotiation — or a man who knows something about the house he's sitting in that the cook doesn't.

Either way, dawn was coming. And with it, she suspected, soldiers.
