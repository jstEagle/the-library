## 12. Night Move

The rain had been falling since dusk, a thin grey insistence that turned Marrowfield's cobbled lanes to rivers of oil and lamplight, and Wendell Almeida stood at the infirmary window watching it and thinking about floorboards.

Beneath the third bed from the stove — old Marta's bed, though Marta had gone home to her sister's farm in the spring and no one new had been put there yet — the boards were loose in a way that looked like rot and was not. Five years of names slept under that gap in a ledger bound in green leather gone soft as skin at the spine. Five years of men the officers had struck from their reports like smudges on glass: the drowned, the fevered, the ones who died of wounds that would have embarrassed a colonel to admit his artillery had made, the boys who bled out before they'd learned to shave. Wendell knew every name by weight if not by sight now. Lifting the board and feeling the book beneath it was like pressing a thumb against one's own pulse.

And tonight, of all nights, the pulse felt dangerous.

Tam had been in the cells for six days. Six days of silence from the garrison, six days of Bess sending up trays that came back untouched or not coming back at all, six days of Odile saying nothing at all, which was worse than anything she could have said. And this morning, Corporal Vey — young, freckled, apologetic in the way of dogs that have bitten someone by accident — had appeared at the infirmary door with a paper bearing Colonel Hargreave's seal and informed Nurse Almeida that, pursuant to ongoing inquiries, all rooms under army administration would be subject to inspection without further notice, beginning tomorrow at first light.

*Beginning tomorrow at first light.* The phrase sat in Wendell's chest like a swallowed stone. They had searched once already, in the first week of occupation — drawers emptied onto sheets, mattresses opened along their seams, the linen cupboard reduced to an avalanche — and found nothing, because the ledger had been beneath Marta's floorboard then too, patient as a seed. But searches were like weather; each one taught the searchers something. A second search would be slower. A third would be thorough. Somewhere in that arithmetic was a morning when a corporal with a pry-bar knelt beside the third bed from the stove, and everything ended: the ledger seized, Tam's silence rendered meaningless, five years of names marched off to be burned as sedition.

It could not stay where it was. That much had been clear since morning. Where it *could* go was the problem Wendell had been turning over all day while changing dressings and dosing fevers and smiling at patients who deserved better than a nurse whose eyes kept drifting to the rain.

There were only so many places in an occupied town where a book could hide. Every cellar had been catalogued. Every attic belonged to someone who could be arrested. The millpond, the well, the lime kiln — all of them meant burying the names, and Wendell had promised, silently, over every entry ever written, that these men would not go into the ground twice.

Which left the chapel.

Wendell pressed their forehead briefly against the cold glass. The chapel stood at the end of Chapel Lane, squat and grey-walled, its one bell long ago cracked and silent. It was a poor church serving a poor parish, and Odile had tended it the way she tended everything — fiercely, quietly, and without any expectation that heaven was taking notes. Its reliquary was a battered iron box behind the altar that had not, as far as anyone knew, held an actual relic in living memory. Its crypt was shallow and damp. Its congregation had shrunk to a dozen souls who came more for Odile's voice than for God.

But it was a sanctuary. However threadbare, however occupied — soldiers billeted two streets away, sentries at the market cross — a chapel remained the one building in Marrowfield where a man might kneel and not be asked why. Hargreave's requisition orders covered granaries and hospitals and private homes. They did not, yet, cover altars. Perhaps the colonel believed in God after all. More likely he simply hadn't thought of chapels as containing anything worth wanting.

*Trust him,* said a voice in Wendell that sounded uncomfortably like Odile herself. *Trust her,* Wendell corrected. Trusting Odile was not the question. The question was whether trusting Odile's chapel was the same thing, and whether faith — real faith, the kind Wendell had never managed, the kind Odile wore like a coat she'd outgrown but refused to discard — could hold the weight of a green leather book full of dead men's names.

Behind them, the infirmary breathed. Eleven patients, nine asleep. Bess had gone down to her kitchen hours ago, leaving a pot of barley soup banked in embers and a look on her face that said she knew exactly what Wendell was thinking and wanted no part of hearing it aloud. That was how the family worked: nobody asked, everybody knew, and the knowing was itself a kind of covering fire.

Wendell waited until the sentry at the corner completed his slow circuit and disappeared around the cooper's shed. Then they crossed the ward on socked feet, knelt at the third bed from the stove, and slid their fingers into the seam between floorboards.

The board came up without sound. It had been practiced until it was silent — Wendell had spent an entire afternoon the previous autumn lifting and lowering it, over and over, while Tam pretended to mop the same stretch of floor and Bess pretended not to watch, because the family understood that devotion is mostly rehearsal. The ledger came up wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than a book had any right to be, heavy the way a sleeping child is heavy: with all its weight surrendered to you.

For a moment Wendell only held it. The rain spoke against the window. Somewhere across town, in a cell that smelled of wet stone, Tam was lying awake doing the same thing — holding the memory of the book, weighing what his silence had cost and protected. Wendell had seen him for ten minutes on the fourth day, through bars, escorted, permitted only to confirm he was alive. He had looked smaller and older both at once, the way grief ages people in patches. He had not been asked about the ledger in front of Wendell. He had caught Wendell's eye anyway and given the smallest shake of the head — *I said nothing* — and then, worse, the smallest nod — *and I'd say nothing again* — and Wendell had walked back down the corridor with tears locked behind their teeth like prisoners.

*I'm moving you tonight,* Wendell told the ledger silently. *I'm sorry. You were safe here. Nowhere is safe. So we choose the safest unsafe place, and we pray.*

Wendell had never prayed in their life. It seemed suddenly important to start.

* * *

Chapel Lane at midnight was a river running backwards — rain flowing down its gutters toward the sea, lamplight drowning in puddles, the whole world reduced to shades of wet. Wendell went wrapped in the plain brown oilskin of an orderly, hood up, the ledger strapped flat against their chest beneath it where it rose and fell with each breath like a second heart. They had considered the laundry basket. They had considered the cart route. Both involved other people, and tonight the secret needed to be carried by the fewest hands possible. One set of hands. Their own.

The sentry at the market cross had a brazier and no imagination. Wendell gave him the nod one gives a stranger at night, half-apology for existing, and he returned it with the bored indifference of a man three hours from relief, and that was the whole of the war's attention paid to Wendell Almeida crossing the square with five years of falsified history against their ribs.

Still, the heart would not behave. Every footstep sounded like a confession. At the mouth of Tanner's Row a shutter banged and Wendell flattened into a doorway, breath held, counting — one, two, three — before the night resumed its ordinary noises and let them go. The ledger's edge pressed through the oilcloth with each heartbeat, patient, insistent, *remember me remember me remember me*. As if it needed reminding. As if Wendell could forget a single name in it if they lived to be a hundred.

They thought of the last time they had carried it anywhere — two winters ago, when the roof beam above the storeroom cracked and everything had to be moved, and Tam had carried the ledger out cradled against his chest like an infant, absurdly solemn, and Bess had laughed at him and then, catching sight of his face, stopped laughing, because Tam had said, simply, *it's people, Bess*, and that had been the end of the joke forever. It's people. Four syllables that had become the family's whole theology.

Odile's window was dark, but a candle burned behind the altar — Odile kept a perpetual flame there, had done since the war began, one small defiance of the darkness she claimed was purely liturgical and everyone knew was otherwise. Wendell knocked the particular rhythm on the vestry door: two, pause, three. The rhythm of the family. Bess knocking flour off her hands. Tam drumming on table edges. Odile tapping her spoon against the soup pot. Nobody had invented it; it had accumulated, the way families do.

The door opened on a sliver of candlelight and Odile's face, sharp-boned and sleepless, framed in a grey shawl.

"You came," Odile said. Not a question. She stepped back to let Wendell in, and the vestry closed around them both — incense and damp wool and old paper, the smell of a building holding its breath.

"I had no choice."

"There's always a choice." Odile took the candle from its shelf and led the way into the nave, her shadow enormous on the stone. "You chose to walk through a patrolled town at midnight carrying the single most dangerous object in Marrowfield strapped to your chest. You chose it over every safer cowardice available to you." She glanced back, and in the candle-glow her eyes were tired and fierce and fond. "I'm glad of your choice. I'm noting that it was one."

Wendell unwrapped the oilcloth right there, between the third pew and the font, because some things should not wait for ceremony. The green leather caught the light. Odile looked down at it the way she looked at dying men — completely, without flinching, giving it her whole attention as a form of respect.

"Five years," Wendell said quietly. "Two hundred and eleven names. Some of them officers' own reports contradict the casualty returns — I checked the dates, the companies, the field hospital transfers. If anyone honest ever reads it—" They stopped. Honest was a word that had grown thin lately, worn like a coin passed through too many hands.

"If anyone honest ever reads it," Odile finished for them, "the war changes shape. And if the wrong person reads it, everyone who touched it hangs." She said it without drama, the way she announced penitential seasons. Then she extended both hands, palms up, the gesture she used at the offertory. "Give it to me, then. Or tell me where you want it hidden and give me your reasons, and I'll do it myself either way."

"The reliquary." Wendell's voice came out lower than intended. "Behind the altar. No one searches a reliquary, Odile. Even Hargreave. Especially Hargreave — men like him are careful about appearing to respect holy things. It's part of how they keep the town quiet."

"And if he isn't careful? If he decides God can be requisitioned like grain?" Odile held Wendell's gaze steadily. "I'll ask you plainly, because you deserve plainness: do you trust me?"

"With everything," Wendell said, and heard the truth of it land in their own chest like a bell. "With Tam's life. With Bess's. With yours. With theirs—" they touched the ledger lightly, "—all two hundred and eleven. There is no one else left to trust, and even if there were, I wouldn't. It's you. It was always going to be you."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes — quick, unreadable, gone — a flicker Wendell would turn over and over in memory in the weeks to come, trying to read it, failing, forgiving. Tonight it looked like nothing more than weariness. Tonight it looked like love receiving a burden.

"Then hear my conditions," Odile said briskly, and the moment passed, and the candle guttered, and neither of them moved to shield it. "One: you don't come here again except for services, in daylight, among the congregation. Two: you don't tell Bess where it is. Not because she'd betray it — because she'd come here, and she cannot help being known everywhere she goes. Three—" and here her voice gentled, which was how Wendell knew the hardest condition was coming, "—if I am taken, if they put me where they've put Tam, you will not trade anything for me. Not the book. Not yourselves. Promise me."

"No."

"Wendell—"

"No." The word came out harder than Wendell expected, ringing in the empty nave like the cracked bell that never rang. "We're not doing this. We're not standing in a church agreeing in advance to sacrifice each other. That's what the army does — it spends people. It writes them off. It strikes them from the report." Wendell lifted the ledger between them. "Every name in here is a man the army decided was spendable. I am not going to pre-spend you, Odile. I refuse. That's not a condition, it's the whole point of my entire life."

The silence afterward was very long. Rain filled it. The candle flame steadied, and in its light Odile's face did something complicated — pain and pride wrestling to a draw.

"You always were the stubborn one," she said finally, softly. "In a family of stubborn people, somehow you're the most." She took the ledger from Wendell's hands. "Come. Help me. If we're defying a colonel, we'll defy him properly, together, tonight."

* * *

The reliquary stood behind the altar in its niche, iron banded with oak, older than the parish register, older probably than the town's charter. Together they cleared the false bottom Odile had built herself, years ago, for chalices during the bad summer when looters came through — Wendell remembered helping her plane the boards, remembered the smell of fresh-cut pine mixing with incense, remembered thinking it was a strange hobby for a pacifist. Now the hollow received the ledger like a hand closing around a coal.

Oilcloth first. Then the book. Then a layer of altar linens over it, folded, innocent. Then the false bottom, seated flush, invisible unless you knew to feel for the seam with a fingernail. Odile replaced the few genuine relics the box still held on top — a splinter of something, a scrap of embroidery attributed to a saint whose name had worn off the label — and shut the iron lid, and the lock clicked with terrible finality, a small domestic sound, a sound like a pantry door.

"That's it," Wendell said. Their voice sounded strange in their own ears. "That's done."

"That's done," Odile agreed. She relit the altar candles properly, one by one, and the little sanctuary bloomed with warm light, and for a moment it almost worked — the illusion that holy ground was proof against anything, that iron and oak and God formed a wall the army would not cross.

Then Wendell saw it. Small, easily missed, tucked into the angle where the altar rail met the wall: a boot print in the dust. Fresh. A soldier's hobnail heel, printed clean and deep, pointing toward the sanctuary steps.

Someone had stood here. Recently. Looking at the altar.

"Odile." Wendell's whisper barely carried. "Who comes into your chapel?"

Odile followed their gaze. Wendell watched her see the print, watched her whole body go very still in the candlelight — stillness like deep water, like a woman reading her own sentence — and when she answered, her voice was perfectly level, perfectly calm, and that calm frightened Wendell more than shouting could have.

"A staff officer came on Tuesday," she said. "Measuring. He said the garrison wished to discuss billeting the choir loft." A pause, precise as a knife laid down. "He stayed rather longer than measuring requires."

Wendell stared at the boot print, and the ledger lay twelve inches beneath their feet, and the candle flames leaned in a draft from the door, and somewhere across the sleeping town Colonel Hargreave — or whatever hand he used, whatever clerk, whichever obedient instrument — had already marked this room, had already stood where Wendell stood, had already looked at the altar and thought *what would they hide in there?*

"We could move it again tonight," Wendell breathed.

"No." Odile's hand closed on their arm, firm as a blessing. "No. Moving it in panic is how things get lost and people get caught. It stays. And tomorrow—" she straightened, and the tiredness fell away from her face like a mask, and what remained underneath was the Odile who had buried forty parishioners in the fever year without missing a single Sunday, "—tomorrow I will preach a sermon on the sanctity of hidden things, loud enough to reach the garrison walls. Let them wonder what I mean. Wonder is a wall too, Wendell. Sometimes it holds better than iron."

She walked Wendell to the vestry door, embraced them briefly — brief as communion, complete as sacrament — and pushed them out into the rain.

Wendell went home through the flooded lanes with an unaccountably light chest and a heart that would not stop hammering, telling themselves the boot print meant nothing, telling themselves Odile's chapel had held for centuries and would hold a few weeks more.

Behind them, in the dark nave, the candle behind the altar burned on — and Odile stood alone before the reliquary for a long time, motionless, her lips moving in what might have been prayer.

Or might have been practice. For words she had been ordered to say, and did not yet know how to refuse.
