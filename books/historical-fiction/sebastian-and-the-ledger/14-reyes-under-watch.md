## 14. Reyes Under Watch

The candle in Ivo Reyes's quarters had burned to a stub by the time he finished writing the report that would either save him or hang him, and he found he could not tell the difference between the two outcomes anymore. That was the first lesson of double service: after enough months of living inside a lie, every truth began to feel like a forgery and every forgery like a confession.

He read it over once more. *Subject maintains regular contact with garrison medical personnel. Subject's inquiries into hospital records appear consistent with intelligence-gathering objectives. Recommend continued observation.* Three sentences, each one a small door left open — for himself, if his handler chose to walk through it; against himself, if someone else read them more carefully than he'd written them.

He signed the name they had given him. Not Reyes. Never Reyes, here on this side of the river, in this requisitioned merchant's house with its wallpaper of faded pomegranates and its windows that faced the wrong way, toward the delegation compound rather than toward Marrowfield. Here he was Captain Aldous Vane, liaison officer, seconded from a regiment that had ceased to exist two winters ago, which was precisely why they had chosen him. A dead regiment made a dead man untraceable. He had been someone else so long that some mornings, lathering his face at the basin, he caught a stranger's eyes in the glass and had to say his own name aloud — *Ivo, Ivo* — like a prayer against forgetting.

Outside, the armistice town lay under its third week of rain. Marrowfield had become a strange animal since the delegations arrived: half garrison, half diplomatic enclave, soldiers of two armies drinking in different taverns on opposite sides of the market square, glaring across the cobbles at one another while their generals dined together under chandeliers borrowed from a bankrupt silk merchant. The war had not ended. It had only learned better manners.

Reyes capped his inkwell and sat listening to the rain. Somewhere across the roofs, past the chapel spire he could just see from his window — Odile's chapel, though he would not let himself think of it as hers, not yet — Wendell was awake too. Or asleep, which was better. Asleep was safe.

A knock at his door. Two knocks, a pause, a third. The pattern meant urgency without officialdom, which in this house meant trouble wearing plain clothes.

"Enter."

Corporal Nadezda Sarr slipped in the way she did everything — quietly, thoroughly, leaving no impression behind except the sense that something had been moved. She was his handler's creature, nominally assigned as his clerk, actually assigned as his leash. She carried a folded slip of paper and did not offer it to him directly.

"The Major asks you to come down," she said. "Tonight, if convenient."

"And if inconvenient?"

"Then tonight anyway." Her mouth did something that was almost, but not quite, a smile. "He's in the map room. There's tea. It's terrible tea."

Everything about her was designed to be disarming — the boyish crop of dark hair, the soldier's bluntness, the terrible-tea joke she had told him four times now, always the same way, as if testing whether he laughed differently each time. He had stopped laughing at it three repetitions ago, and had watched her record the fact somewhere behind her flat grey eyes.

"I'll come now," he said.

* * *

Major Elsen kept the map room warmer than any other room in the house, which Reyes understood as a form of hospitality the way he understood everything in this trade: as an instrument. Warm rooms loosened tongues. Cold ones disciplined them. Tonight the stove glowed cherry-red and the tea, indeed, was terrible — stewed to tannin-bitterness in a pot that had never once been scoured — and Elsen stood over the great table with his hands flat on the map of the river valley as though holding the geography itself in place.

"You've been busy," Elsen said, without looking up. He was a soft-bodied man with a scholar's stoop and a voice like dry paper turning, and he had been running agents since before Reyes had learned to shave. People underestimated him for exactly those reasons, right up until the moment he destroyed them.

"Liaison work is busy work," Reyes said carefully.

"Mm." Elsen turned one of the map weights — a brass horse — a quarter-inch to the east, as if correcting history itself. "Our friends across the table have shifted their position on the casualty question. Did you notice? Tuesday's session. They came armed with numbers. Specific numbers. Numbers for Marrowfield, if you can believe our luck, with monthly totals going back five years."

Reyes kept his face still. Five years. The ledger covered five years.

"They're fabricating," he said. "It's a negotiation tactic. Inflated figures justify inflated reparations."

"That is certainly what I told the delegation." Elsen finally looked up, and his eyes were pale and patient and utterly without mercy. "But here is what troubles me, Captain Vane. Their figures are *good*. Too good. Fabrications are usually sloppy — round numbers, arithmetic errors, deaths conveniently clustered where the enemy wants sympathy. These are granular. They distinguish between died-of-wounds and died-of-fever. They name wards. One entry references a boy who died of a chest wound received at Harrow Ford who does not appear anywhere in our own records — because our own records were corrected, you understand, by officers concerned with morale." He picked up the brass horse again, weighed it in his palm. "Someone over there has been keeping honest books for five years. And someone on *this* side of the river has known about it, or should have, and said nothing."

The silence stretched. Rain ticked against the shutters. Reyes poured himself tea he didn't want, because hands that are doing something look less like hands that are hiding something, and drank the bitter swill and set the cup down without a tremor he could detect.

"If such a ledger exists," he said, "it's worth more than this entire delegation."

"Yes." Elsen smiled faintly. "Now you see my interest. And my difficulty." He set the horse down and straightened, folding his hands behind his back, a schoolmaster preparing to assign the lesson that would break or make his pupil. "There are people above me — people whose names you don't need — who believe the casualty dispute is the only thing standing between us and a signed armistice. If those figures stand unchallenged, we negotiate from weakness for a generation. If they can be discredited—" he shrugged, eloquent, "—the whole moral architecture of the enemy's position collapses. So. The ledger. Where is it, Captain Vane?"

"I don't know that it exists."

"I think you do."

"I think," Reyes said, meeting the pale eyes, "that you suspect everyone in this house, including your own clerks, including yourself, because suspicion is the only currency you trust. But I have no knowledge of any ledger. I'm a liaison. I carry messages between men who hate each other and pretend to dine."

Elsen regarded him for a long moment. Then he chuckled — a soft, papery sound, genuinely amused, which was somehow worse than anger.

"There it is," he said. "That's very good. That's the best performance you've given me, and I want you to know I appreciate craft." He came around the map table slowly, his shadow sliding across the valley, across the little painted squares that stood for armies. "Let me tell you what I know instead, and then you can decide how much of your performance to keep. When you were reassigned to me eight months ago, there were questions. A captain captured at Vell's Crossing who surfaces eighteen months later wearing our uniform and asking for liaison duty — questions get asked. Your answers satisfied the review board. Barely. You were given to me on probation, and part of my probationary duty is to find out whether you are ours, theirs, or merely yours." He stopped an arm's length away. Close enough that Reyes could smell the tea on his breath. "Three weeks ago, someone visited the infirmary at Marrowfield. Off the books. No escort logged, no pass filed, and yet the sentries remember a tall officer, our uniform, speaking familiarly with the nursing staff. The description matches you, Captain Vane. It matches you the way those casualty figures match a ledger that officially doesn't exist."

"I was assessing field hospital capacity," Reyes said. "Liaison preparation. The delegation will need—"

"Stop." Elsen held up one soft hand, and Reyes stopped, because there was no performance in the world equal to the moment when a handler stops performing back. "Here is how this goes now. You will find that ledger. You will put it in my hands before the delegations reconvene — nine days, Captain. And when you do, your probation ends, your name comes off the watch list, and you will be exactly what your papers claim: a loyal officer of this army, decorated in absentia, with a future. Refuse, fail, or run—" Elsen's voice stayed gentle throughout, which made each word land like a laid stone, "—and I will have you arrested as a collaborator by Friday, tried by Sunday, and shot on the parade ground Monday morning with your real name read aloud to the firing squad, so that everyone who ever loved you will know precisely what you became."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below, a door closed; Sarr, excusing herself from a conversation she had already heard the whole of.

"And if I bring it to you," Reyes said quietly, "and it names your own officers' falsifications — Hargreave's reports, the corrected counts — what happens to the book then?"

Elsen's smile returned, thin as paper. "That depends entirely on whose hands hold it first. Which is why I am telling you: mine. First." He gathered his papers, tapped them square. "Nine days. Good night, Captain. Mind the stairs — the third step lies about its height. Like most things in this house."

* * *

Reyes walked back through the sleeping house with the lantern turned low, and every corridor seemed longer than it had that morning, every door heavier. He passed Sarr's room and saw lamplight under her door and knew she was writing tonight's observations — *subject met with Major at 2200 hours; subject's manner composed; subject asked about disposition of recovered documents* — and he hated her a little and understood her completely, because she was doing exactly what he did: surviving inside a machine that ate its servants.

In his own room he barred the door, though a bar was a courtesy, not a defense, and sat down at the desk where his false report still lay drying. He looked at it for a long time. Then he took a fresh sheet, and beneath the pomegranate wallpaper's fading pattern, by the light of the guttering candle, he wrote the true report — the one no one would ever read, addressed to no one, in a cipher of his own invention that consisted simply of honesty:

*They know. Sarr has been reporting my movements since the infirmary visit. Elsen suspects the ledger exists and knows I know. Nine days. If I find it, I betray Wendell and everyone the ledger protects. If I don't, I die under my own name. There is no path through this that doesn't cross someone.*

He stared at what he'd written until the ink shone dry, then fed it to the candle flame and watched it curl, blacken, and go to ash in the saucer. The smoke smelled of cedar and ruin.

Through the window, past the wet roofs, the chapel spire stood black against the deeper black of the sky, and beneath it, invisible, Marrowfield slept — Bess in her kitchen with her banked fires, Tam wherever they were letting him sleep now, Odile above her chapel, and Wendell, Wendell with the floorboard and the book and the five years of names that had cost more to write than any general's dispatches. He thought of the promise, made in peacetime over bad wine in Bess's kitchen: *if they ever call me enemy, hear me out before you believe the report.* He had asked for that promise thinking it would never be needed, the way one buys insurance against lightning. Now the lightning was here, and it had struck him in both directions at once — condemned by the army that owned his body, trusted — perhaps still trusted — by the family that owned whatever remained of his soul.

And the trap was closing from both sides with a symmetry that felt almost deliberate: Elsen wanted the ledger to control the peace; Hargreave wanted it buried; the enemy negotiators wanted it brandished; and the only person who wanted it used *honestly* was a nurse with ink-stained fingers who had once promised to hear him out.

Nine days. He pressed his forehead against the cold window glass and watched his breath bloom and fade on it, a small ghost making its small argument for existence.

Then, below, at the edge of the market square, where the lamplight thinned — movement. A figure in a kitchen apron, unmistakable even at distance by the roll of her shoulders and the set of her jaw, crossing the wet cobbles toward the delegation compound at an hour no cook had business walking. Bess. Carrying a covered basket, and pausing — deliberately, unmistakably — to glance up at his window before disappearing through the tradesmen's gate.

His heart went hard and loud. She couldn't have known which window was his. She couldn't possibly—

Unless someone had told her. Unless a signal had traveled the other way, bread and ink and faith moving through occupied streets faster than any courier's pass.

Reyes pulled on his coat. Whatever was in that basket, whatever message the found family had risked sending him through the teeth of Elsen's watchers, it would be gone by morning — confiscated, or worse, traced. He had nine days to find a ledger he must never deliver, and apparently until dawn to learn why Bess Thackeray was walking into the wolf's house carrying bread.

He blew out the candle and opened the door onto the dark.
