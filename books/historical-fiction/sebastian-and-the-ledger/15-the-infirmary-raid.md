## 15. The Infirmary Raid

The knock came at half past five in the morning, when the light through the infirmary windows was still the color of watered milk.

Wendell was already awake — had been awake since four, in fact, lying in the narrow cot behind the dispensary and listening to the garrison town breathe its new, wrong breath. Marrowfield had always woken in stages: first the bakery chimney, then Bess's kettle, then the groan of cart wheels on the bridge stones. Now it woke to boot-heels. To the clank of a sentry shifting his rifle against the doorpost of a building that had never before required one. The town had learned a new rhythm overnight, and Wendell's body, treacherous thing that it was, had refused to sleep inside it.

So when the knock came — three blows, hard, official, nothing like Tam's apologetic double-tap or Odile's patient churchly rap — Wendell was already dressed, already standing, already arranging their face into the mild, unremarkable expression they had spent five years perfecting for exactly this species of man.

"Open in the name of Colonel Hargreave," said a voice through the wood. "By order of the Provisional Authority."

Wendell opened the door.

There were six of them on the step, and more coming up the lane behind — Wendell counted eight, ten, twelve, a gray-green river of them flowing through the gate that Bess had painted white only two springs ago, back when paint was something you could still buy. At their head stood a lieutenant with a face like a folded map, all creases and no country, holding a paper he did not offer to show.

"Nurse Almeida." He said it as though reading it off a list, which he almost certainly was. "The infirmary is to be searched. All persons will remain in the main ward. All records, correspondence, and written materials are subject to seizure."

"And my patients?" Wendell asked. Their voice came out level. They were quietly proud of that, and quietly ashamed of how much practice it had taken.

"The wounded are not the object of this operation."

*Everything is the object of every operation,* Wendell thought. *That is what an operation is.* But they only stepped aside, and the soldiers came in with the morning.

* * *

They began with the dispensary, because that was where the paperwork lived — or where the paperwork they could understand lived. Two soldiers emptied the supply cabinet onto the floor while a third went through the requisition book with a pencil, licking his thumb to turn pages, leaving small gray smudges like bruises across the columns. Wendell watched from the doorway of the ward, hands folded, heart going like a trapped bird against the cage of their ribs, and made themselves catalog the damage instead of feel it: the opium tincture spilled, the lint boiled yesterday now trodden into the boards, Dr. Merrow's old anatomy atlas thrown down open, spine cracked, its colored plates of the human interior exposed to boots and dust.

Behind Wendell, the ward breathed. Fourteen patients. That was the count Wendell had made before opening the door, the way other people counted exits in a burning building. Fourteen men in the long room, most of them too hurt to stand, several too hurt to sit, and every single one of them awake, because word traveled through a hospital faster than through any wire: *they've come for her.* For him. For them — Wendell had stopped, years ago, bothering to guess which pronoun the soldiers would settle on; they had used all three in the course of the requisition, sometimes in a single sentence, and Wendell had long ago decided that being illegible to the army was a small, private victory.

Private. The thought snagged, as it always did, on the ledger — on the chapel, on the reliquary, on the little lead-lined box beneath the false bottom where Saint Ferrell's fingerbone had slept for two hundred years until Odile moved it to the drawer with the vestments and gave its place to five years of names. Safe. Odile had sworn it safe. And yet here came the army with its folded-map face and its gray river of men, and Wendell understood with sudden, sick clarity that safety was not a place you put something. It was a story you told yourself while you waited to find out if you'd been believed.

The lieutenant appeared at Wendell's shoulder. "Your private papers, Nurse. Where do you keep them?"

"I'm a hospital nurse, Lieutenant. My papers are the ones your corporal is smudging."

"Private papers. Letters. Diaries." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Accounts."

*Accounts.* The word dropped through Wendell like a stone through still water. They had known — of course they had known since the requisition, since Hargreave's clerks inventoried the hospital records and found gaps shaped like names — but knowing a hunt exists is different from hearing the hounds. Wendell kept their eyes on the corporal's pencil moving down the requisition column.

"A ledger," the lieutenant said. "We have reason to believe this facility maintains an unofficial record. Casualty figures not entered in the official reports."

"We keep no such thing."

"You'll forgive me if I don't take a nurse's word over a colonel's intelligence."

"You'll forgive me if I don't help you look for something that doesn't exist."

Something flickered in the creased face — not anger; Wendell had learned to read the difference. Anger was loud. This was quieter and worse: the satisfaction of a man who has expected obstruction and can now report it. "Then we'll search thoroughly," he said, "and see what doesn't exist turns up," and he snapped his fingers, and the soldiers began on the ward itself.

* * *

They went through the patients' things first, because patients' things are small and easily scattered. A soldier shook out Corporal Dunn's knapsack and a photograph fell out — Dunn's mother, or someone's mother, her face worn pale from handling — and he stepped on it without looking, and Wendell heard, behind them in the ward, a sound that was not quite a word. Private Ellery, both legs gone above the knee, turned his face to the wall. Tam, who had been released from the cells only nine days ago and who flinched still at raised voices, planted himself by the stove with his arms crossed and his jaw set, and looked so much like a boy guarding a treehouse that Wendell wanted, absurdly, to laugh and weep in the same breath.

The search moved inward. Mattresses probed with bayonets. Floorboards tested with the flat of a spade. One soldier took the wall clock apart because it ticked, which seemed to offend him. Another pulled the linen shelf down in an avalanche of sheets and stood in the middle of it looking faintly embarrassed, as though the sheets had ambushed him.

And through all of it, the ward talked.

That was the thing Wendell would remember afterward — not the bayonets, not the cracked atlas, not the lieutenant's folded-map patience. The talking. Because a hospital is not a building; it is an organism, and its immune system is conversation, and the patients deployed theirs with a fluency no officer could match.

"They're after the colonel's whiskey, I heard," announced Sergeant Boil, whose fever made him loud and whose volume made him useful. "Stashed it in the dispensary myself, back in March. Under the quinine."

"There's no whiskey under the quinine," said the soldier on his knees there, wearily.

"That's what you'd think," said Boil, with the serene confidence of a man at forty degrees, "but March was a strange month."

In the corner, old Hobb — shot through the hip at the crossing, seventy years old and shameless — engaged two soldiers in an exhaustive description of a tunnel he claimed ran from the coal cellar to the millpond, dug, he said, by smugglers, or possibly monks, and certainly wide enough for a man carrying documents. It existed nowhere except in Hobb's considerable imagination, but he described its dimensions with the authority of a man who had personally widened it, and the soldiers, who were nineteen and twenty-two and had been told stories about tunnels all their lives, actually went and looked.

Ellery turned from the wall and offered, unprompted, that the chaplain kept papers, lots of them, sermons and such, locked in the vestry — technically true, generously true, true enough to pull two men out of the ward and into the chapel, which was the last place Wendell wanted anyone and the first place they needed them distracted from. Wendell caught Ellery's eye across the room. The boy — twenty-three, legless, face like ash — gave back a look of such steady, deliberate complicity that Wendell's throat closed. *He knows,* Wendell realized. Not everything, perhaps, but enough. Enough to lie for it.

The overlooked, shielding what they loved with the only weapons they had: misdirection, fever, fiction, and time.

* * *

It was past noon when the lieutenant came back to Wendell with his creased face arranged into apology. They had found, he reported, nothing. No unofficial ledger. No suspicious correspondence. Only a hospital full of madmen and liars — he did not say this unkindly, which somehow made it worse — and a chaplain's vestry smelling of incense and candle wax, and a coal cellar containing coal.

"The search will continue," he said. "Tomorrow, perhaps, the upper rooms. In the meantime—" and here he unfolded, at last, the paper he had carried all day, "—you are confined to these premises. You may attend patients within the building and within the yard. You may not leave. Your orderly—"

"Tam is needed here."

"—your orderly is likewise restricted. A guard will be posted at the gate." He hesitated, and for one moment the map-face unfolded into something almost human — tired, young, uncertain. "For what it's worth," he said, lower, "the colonel believes this ledger of yours is sedition. But my grandmother died at Vellcross, and her name isn't on any list I've ever seen. So." He folded himself shut again. "Guard at the gate. Good afternoon, Nurse."

Wendell stood in the wrecked ward and listened to the boots recede, and only when the last one cleared the gate did they let their shoulders drop from around their ears.

Bess emerged from the kitchen passage carrying a tray she had been holding ready for hours — bread, broth, the particular tea she brewed for shock — and set it down without a word, and then, because she was Bess, wrapped Wendell in an embrace that smelled of flour and woodsmoke and held on until Wendell's breathing remembered its own business.

"They didn't find it," Wendell said into her shoulder.

"No."

"Odile—"

"Is fine. She sat in the chapel the whole time praying so loudly the soldiers couldn't think, and then she gave them all a blessing on the way out that I'd swear made two of them blush." Bess pulled back and held Wendell's face in her strong flour-dusted hands, and her eyes, usually so quick with mischief, were grave. "But love, listen. They didn't find it today. Today they were clumsy and our people were clever. Tomorrow they'll be less clumsy. And you're caged now — you know that, don't you? This house arrest, it's not punishment. It's a fence. They're keeping you where they can watch you, so they can follow whoever comes to you."

Wendell knew. Had known since the lieutenant said the word *confined*, really — had felt the shape of the trap even while walking around inside it. If Reyes came, they would be followed. If a message went out, it would be traced. Every path from the infirmary now led through a guard's eyes.

"Then we need another way," Wendell said slowly. "A way that doesn't come through me at all."

Bess smiled, and there was the mischief, banked coals under the gravity. "Love," she said, "I've been feeding an occupied garrison for three months. There is no gate in this town I can't walk a loaf of bread through."

Outside, the evening bell rang from the chapel, and fourteen men who should not have been able to hold a secret held one, and the guard at the white-painted gate stamped his feet against the cold, watching the front door — while behind the infirmary, in the yard, Bess's kitchen chimney began, innocently, to smoke.
