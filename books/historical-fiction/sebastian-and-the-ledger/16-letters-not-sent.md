## 16. Letters Not Sent

They released Tam on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before the lamps were lit, as though shame were best conducted by daylight's absence.

He came through the infirmary gate the way water comes through a cracked wall — slowly, finding every weakness, unable to stop himself. His uniform was his own again but it hung wrong on him, as if the cells had taken something out of his shoulders and never given it back. The left side of his face was still swollen where they had worked him over on the third night, the one they thought would be productive. It hadn't been. He'd counted floorboards instead. Four hundred and eleven of them, give or take, because counting was a kind of prayer and prayer was the only thing they couldn't confiscate.

Bess saw him first from the kitchen window. She didn't run out — Bess never ran, she said running was for people who hadn't learned patience — but by the time Tam reached the step there was a bowl in her hands and her apron already dusted with flour, though the bread had been baked hours ago. She had flour waiting for him. That was Bess.

"Sit," she said.

"I can work."

"You'll sit."

He sat. The soup was barley and leek, the same soup she'd made the day he'd arrived at Marrowfield five years ago with frostbitten feet and a name nobody could pronounce properly. She had learned it properly within a week. *Tamás Ferenc Orbán*, she pronounced now, silently, watching him eat, the way you watch a fire you're afraid will go out.

Wendell came at dusk, moving carefully because house arrest meant soldiers at the gate and soldiers at the gate meant every movement measured. They stood in the doorway a long moment, looking at each other across the ward's lamplight, and neither of them said anything about the cells, because some rooms you don't enter unless invited, even when you both have keys.

"They let me keep my hands," Tam said finally, holding them up, turning them over like strange objects. "I kept thinking — while they asked their questions — whatever else happens, I've got my hands. I can write. I can knead. I can hold a man's head while he dies so he doesn't die alone." He looked down. "That last one I did less often than I wanted. But I did it."

"You did," Wendell said. "I saw you do it. Forty-one times I watched you sit up all night with men whose names the reports wouldn't carry."

"Forty-three," Tam said quietly. "You missed two. In the fever tent, during the winter push."

The number hung between them. Forty-three names that existed nowhere in the world except one hidden ledger beneath the floor of an infirmary now occupied by the very army that had erased them.

Tam flexed his fingers. "I want paper," he said.

* * *

The paper problem took three days to solve.

Requisitioned towns are strange economies. Everything official belongs to the army — forms, manifests, requisition dockets stamped with Colonel Hargreave's eagle-and-crown — and everything unofficial belongs to the shadows between the stamps. Bess bartered for writing paper the way she bartered for everything: a jar of rendered fat here, a promise of plum preserves there, traded through the laundress who traded through the farrier who traded through a woman in the village whose son's name was in the ledger, though Bess never told Tam that part until much later. She simply appeared one evening with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: forty sheets of good rag paper, six nibs, a stoppered bottle of ink, and a box of matches.

"Matches?" Tam asked.

"For after," Bess said, and would not explain.

He set up in the corner of the linen room, which was the only room in the infirmary without a window facing the soldiers' billet, and which smelled of lavender and carbolic and clean cotton — the smell of care, which he needed the way a drowning man needs a rope. Wendell smuggled him the ledger itself on the second night, wrapped in a bloody bandage that a dying man had genuinely used, so that if it were found it would be evidence of nothing but housekeeping. Tam read it by candle stub, page by page, and wept over entries he had personally witnessed, because reading a death you attended written down in Wendell's careful hand was different from attending it. Written down, the deaths became permanent. Unwritten, they had been weather. Now they were stone.

Then he began to write.

The first letter went to a woman named Marta Kelleher in the town of Ashford Ferry, mother of Private Daniel Kelleher, who had died on the fourteenth of March not of the wound in his shoulder but of the fever that followed, and whose death had been recorded — Tam checked twice, disbelieving — as *returned to duty*. Tam wrote:

*Mrs. Kelleher. You do not know me. My name is Tamás Orbán and I was an orderly at the garrison infirmary at Marrowfield, and your son Daniel was under my care in the last week of his life. I am writing to tell you what the army will not: that Daniel did not die as the record says. He died of fever, gently, with his hand held, on the 14th of March at twenty minutes past four in the morning. He spoke of you. He asked whether the river at Ashford freezes in winter and I told him it does and he said that when the war ended he would teach his children to skate on it. He was brave in the way that quiet men are brave, which is to say completely. I am sorry to bring you grief, but I thought you would rather have the true grief than a comfortable lie. Your son mattered. Someone wrote his name down.*

He read it over four times. Then he read it aloud, softly, to the empty linen room, because letters to the dead's kin should be tested in the mouth the way a bridge should be tested with weight.

Then he folded it, addressed it, set it in the finished pile —

— and stopped.

Because there was no post. Of course there was no post. The requisitioned town's civilian mail had been suspended in the first week of occupation; outgoing letters required an officer's countersignature, and no officer would ever countersign a letter beginning *the record is a lie*. Every letter he wrote would have to wait for peace, or for smuggling, or for some future neither he nor anyone could see. And yet he wrote them anyway. He understood this about himself around the ninth letter: he was not sending mail. He was building a reliquary out of paper. Each letter was a small church built for one dead man, consecrated in ink, and the fact that no one would read it today did not make it less real. Faith, Odile had once said over supper, is the substance of things hoped for. Tam had no theology, but he had barley soup and a candle stub and forty sheets of good rag paper, and he had decided that this was the same thing.

* * *

Some nights the letters came easily, flowing out of him like the soup Bess pressed on him, warm and continuous. Those were the nights he remembered the men alive: Corporal Ellis singing off-key in the convalescent ward; young Prewitt, seventeen, who had never kissed a girl and practiced on the back of his own hand until Tam caught him at it and laughed until his ribs hurt; Sergeant Amadu Diallo, who taught Tam three words of his mother's tongue and made him swear to use them correctly or not at all.

Other nights the pen would hover and hover above the page and nothing would come, because what lived in Tam's hands was not language but touch — the angle of a body to be turned, the pressure that stops bleeding, the exact way a dying man grips a wrist. Language had always been Wendell's gift, and Reyes's, and even Odile's from the pulpit. Tam's gift was the other thing, the wordless thing, and forcing it onto paper was like trying to pour water uphill.

On those nights he wrote shorter letters.

*Your husband Thomas was not afraid. I want you to know that specifically, because fear is the thing the living worry about most. He talked about the apple tree behind your house for three days. He named its branches. I believe he loved that tree more than any man has loved anything, except possibly you.*

And once, only once, he wrote a letter that broke him:

*Dear Miss Averill. Your brother Edmund asked me, near the end, to write to you and say that he forgave you. I don't know what for. He said you would know. He made me repeat it back three times so I would get it exactly right: "Tell her I forgave her years ago and I only wish I'd said it while I could." I am sorry. I hope knowing brings you something better than pain. If it brings nothing, then I am sorry twice, once for him and once for the world that wasted you both.*

He sealed that one and sat with his forehead against the cool plaster of the wall for a long time, listening to the garrison clock count out an hour that belonged to no one.

* * *

It was Odile who found him burning them.

Not all of them — never the finished pile, which grew week by week inside a false bottom Bess had built into the flour chest, beneath a layer of ordinary flour and above a layer of extraordinary courage. But some letters could not be finished. Some letters had no address, because the family had fled or scattered or been conscripted themselves; some had addressees who were dead by the time Tam reached their names in the ledger, whole households erased as thoroughly as their sons; and some — these were the worst — concerned men whose deaths had circumstances attached that Tam could not write down even in a private letter, because some truths, unloosed into the world without preparation, do more damage than the lie they correct. There was a boy called Fenwick whose last hours involved things Tam had seen but refused to record anywhere, not even in the ledger, not even in memory if he could help it, and for him Tam had started seven letters and burned seven beginnings.

He burned them in the infirmary's copper, the great laundry boiler, feeding the papers into the coals one at a time so the flames took them cleanly. Paper burns beautifully. That is the obscenity of it — how willingly it goes, curling and brightening and lifting its black ghosts up the flue, asking nothing, resisting nothing. Tam watched each sheet surrender and thought: *this is why they burn books. Because burning works.* And then he thought: *but only if you let the burning be the end of it,* and fed in another page.

Odile stood in the doorway in her cassock, hands folded into her sleeves, watching the smoke.

"You're allowed to grieve badly," she said.

"I'm not grieving."

"No," she agreed mildly. "Of course not. You've simply developed a passion for arson."

He almost laughed, and the laugh hurt his bruised ribs, and the hurt felt earned, so he let it happen anyway, a single dry bark of sound. Odile came and stood beside him at the copper and together they watched a letter about a man named Hollis, who had survived the war's worst afternoon only to die of a ruptured appendix that a competent surgeon might have caught, go up in a ribbon of orange light.

"The Church burns things too," Odile said. "Palms into ash. Old vestments. Consecrated hosts gone bad — we drown those, actually, but the principle stands. Burning isn't erasure. Done rightly, it's transformation. You're not destroying their letters, Tam. You're letting the ones that can't fly go to smoke so the ones that can stay clean."

"Is that doctrine?"

"It's Bess's doctrine. I'm borrowing it. She's very persuasive with the ladle."

But her eyes stayed serious above the faint smile, and after a moment she said, quieter: "How many can't fly?"

Tam looked at the finished pile's hiding place, then at the growing heap of ash. "Thirty-one sent-ready. Nine burned. Eleven I can't finish." He hesitated. "One I won't."

"One you won't?"

"There's a man — Corporal Voss. Died in the retreat last autumn. His family lives on the other side of the lines now, in territory they hold. Even if peace comes tomorrow, even if every letter flies, his letter crosses a border." Tam stared into the coals. "And I keep thinking: what if the border never opens? What if the war ends the way wars do, badly, with everyone still angry, and Mrs. Voss dies an old woman never knowing? What if I'm the only person left alive who knows her son said a prayer for her every single night, in a language he was ashamed of, in a hospital bed that smelled of iodine?" His voice cracked on the last words and he hated it and let it crack anyway. "Someone has to know, Chaplain. Even if it's only smoke. Smoke rises. Somebody ought to be collecting it."

Odile put her hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way you steady a bird.

"Somebody is," she said.

* * *

The weeks turned. Winter deepened. The letters accumulated in the flour chest like sediment, thirty becoming fifty, fifty becoming seventy, each one a small act of defiance indistinguishable, to any searching officer, from madness. Tam's hands healed. His sleep did not — the cells had left him a gift of dreams, corridors that lengthened, doors that opened onto questioning — but Bess prescribed chamomile and extra bread and the specific medicine of being needed, and it worked the way her medicines always worked, imperfectly and entirely.

He noticed, in that season, that the occupation was changing shape. The soldiers at the gate grew fewer and stiffer. Officers passed through the infirmary corridors with documents clutched to their chests like sick men clutching blankets. And twice, carrying linens past the administrative office, Tam heard fragments that snagged in his orderly's ear the way a torn edge snags cloth.

"—delegation's adjourned again, third time this month—"

"—casualty figures, that's the sticking point, both sides swearing the other's lying—"

"—Hargreave wants the hospital records cross-checked, every admission, every discharge, he says someone's keeping a private count—"

A private count.

Tam stood in the corridor with an armful of pillowcases and felt the words go through him like cold water through cloth. Somewhere across the town, in a room smelling of sealing wax and self-regard, the Colonel was hunting a ledger. Their ledger. The book of forty-three names plus one hundred and six more, the book Tam had bled for and said nothing for, the book whose every page was a refutation of the figures being argued over at a table where the war's ending was being negotiated — and stalled, and stalled, precisely because both sides were lying with numbers and everyone knew it and no one could prove otherwise.

Except someone could. Someone had proved it, entry by entry, in a hand steadier than Tam's would ever be, and that proof lay under flour in Bess's kitchen while history knocked at the door and found no one answering.

He carried the pillowcases onward. He said nothing to anyone, because saying things was how things got seized. But that evening he took out fresh paper, and instead of a letter to the dead he wrote a note to the living, four lines long, unsigned, and slipped it into the morning loaf the way Bess had shown him — folded small, buried in the crumb, invisible to any knife that wasn't looking.

*To W. — The officers talk. They say the talks are failing over the numbers. They say the Colonel wants every count in this town. Whatever you're planning — plan faster.*

He pressed the loaf into the delivery basket himself, watched Bess carry it out through the gate past the bored sentry who had searched it twice in November and never again since, and stood in the doorway of the kitchen with flour on his hands, thinking about smoke rising, and borders, and how the truest things he knew were locked in a breadbox, waiting for someone brave enough to open the war with them.
