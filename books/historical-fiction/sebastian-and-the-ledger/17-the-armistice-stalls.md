## 17. The Armistice Stalls

The conference room at the Hôtel Verlaine had once been a ballroom, and it still remembered how to be cruel. The crystal chandeliers threw light like interrogation lamps; the parquet gleamed where a thousand dancing shoes had worn it hollow, and now the boots of delegates crossed it instead, carrying folders full of arithmetic that would decide whether the war ended or merely paused to sharpen itself.

Captain Ivo Reyes stood at the edge of it with his hands clasped behind his back and his face arranged into the particular blankness that had kept him alive for two years. Liaison officer, third rank of the delegation. His job was translation — not only of language but of intention, the subtle freight that words carried between men who no longer believed each other anything. He was very good at his job. He had to be. Every day he was good at it was another day his handler did not look too closely at him, and another day the ledger stayed where it was, and another day Wendell Almeida stayed breathing in an occupied town whose name Reyes was not permitted to say aloud.

He thought about Marrowfield the way other men thought about prayer.

* * *

It began, as these things always began, with numbers.

The third session of the week convened at ten o'clock, and by half past eleven the casualty question had risen out of the agenda like a drowned body surfacing. It always did. Every other point — borders, prisoners, grain shipments, the disposition of the river forts — could be argued and deferred and argued again. But the casualty figures were load-bearing. Both delegations had built their positions on them, and both knew, in the way that everyone at this table knew everything and admitted nothing, that the figures were wrong.

"Four hundred and eleven thousand," said Aldous Vey, the enemy delegation's chief negotiator, laying one pale hand flat on his folder as if pressing down something that might otherwise escape. "Confirmed dead. That is the number my government will stand behind."

"And mine," said Councillor Merrick across the table, "stands behind four hundred and sixty-two thousand." He smiled the thin smile he used when he believed himself to be holding the better cards. "Your government's bookkeeping has always been optimistic."

Reyes translated the exchange for his own delegation's junior members in low murmurs, though nothing needed translating. Everyone in the room spoke both languages. Translation, here, was theater — a ritual that let men pause before saying the next unforgivable thing.

The numbers were wrong. Reyes knew they were wrong the way a man knows his own heartbeat is irregular: intimately, privately, with a dread that lived below thought. Four hundred and eleven thousand. He had stood in the infirmary at Marrowfield on the last night of peacetime and watched Wendell Almeida dip a pen and write a name the morning report had already struck from existence — a gunner named Petch, twenty-three years old, dead of a wound in the belly that the surgeons' log recorded as *recovering*. Five years of such names. A private ledger kept beneath floorboards because the official ledgers lied, because the officers who signed the reports preferred their arithmetic clean, because a tidy war is easier to fund than an honest one.

If the true count sat in that room — every omitted name, every falsified line, every son reported wounded-then-recovered who had in fact died in a ditch outside Marrowfield — the negotiation would not stall. It would detonate. Both governments had staked their honor on figures that were lies, and the lies had been told so long and so loudly that the truth could no longer be added to them. It could only replace them. And replacement meant that someone would have to stand up in front of history and say: *we counted wrong, deliberately, for years, and here is the proof.*

No one at this table wanted to be that someone. Which was why, Reyes understood, the talks were not failing over territory or pride or any of the grand nouns in the newspapers. They were failing over a book hidden under a chapel floor in a garrison town most of these delegates could not find on a map.

* * *

The collapse came after luncheon, and it came quickly, the way ice gives way.

It began with a document. Vey's delegation produced it with theatrical reluctance — a captured dispatch, they claimed, listing field hospital admissions for the spring offensive, and the totals in it exceeded, by some nineteen thousand, the figures the other side had tabled as its confirmed dead. Nineteen thousand men who had entered hospitals and never appeared in any accounting afterward.

"These are admissions, not deaths," Merrick said. His voice was still level, but Reyes, standing behind him, saw his hand flatten against the table the same way Vey's had that morning. Men pressed their hands down when the ground moved. "A man admitted may recover."

"A man admitted," Vey replied, "and then vanished from every subsequent report, is not recovering. He is being *managed*." He let the word sit in the air. Around the table, faces performed various degrees of innocence. "My principals ask a simple question. Where are your missing nineteen thousand? Produce them — living, dead, or accounted for — and we proceed to the border question tonight. Fail to produce them, and we must conclude that the figure of four hundred and sixty-two thousand is itself a fiction, and that the true count—" he paused, delicately, "—is higher than either of us can afford to admit."

There it was. Reyes felt the sentence go through the room like cold water through a hull. Because the true count *was* higher. Not nineteen thousand higher — more than that, if Wendell's ledger was even a fraction of what Reyes believed it to be, five years of omissions multiplied across every garrison, every field hospital, every colonel with a reputation to protect. And neither delegation could produce the truth without producing the evidence of its own falsification, and neither could continue lying without the other side knowing, now, precisely what kind of lie it was.

Merrick turned slightly, and his eyes found Reyes. Not accusation — request. *Translate,* the look said. *Find me the honorable version of retreat.*

"The councillor observes," Reyes said, in the other language, addressing Vey's delegation, "that hospital records during active bombardment are subject to disruption, and that a rigorous reconciliation would require access to original registers rather than captured summaries, which may have been altered."

"He says our document might be forged," Vey said flatly, to his own side, and there was a ripple of something that was almost laughter, except that no one laughed, because the joke was true in both directions: the document might be forged, and the figures it contradicted certainly were.

What followed took forty minutes and accomplished nothing, which was its purpose. Delegates spoke of verification commissions and neutral observers and archival access "at an appropriate stage." The word *appropriate* was used eleven times. Reyes counted. Counting kept him sane. Somewhere in the middle of it, a junior delegate from the far province — a young man with ink-stained fingers who reminded Reyes painfully of Tam — asked the only honest question of the afternoon: "Has anyone simply *asked the nurses*?"

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when someone has touched the live wire.

"The medical services," said Merrick smoothly, "are naturally cooperating fully with all inquiries."

Afterward, the sessions adjourned early. Vey gathered his papers with a snap like a door closing. In the corridor, Reyes passed him close enough to smell his tobacco, and Vey said, without turning his head, in Reyes's own tongue: "Tell your masters the clock is running. My principals give it a week. After that, the winter campaign resumes, and everyone in this building knows what that means."

Reyes bowed, as liaison officers bow, and walked on with his heart hammering against his ribs like a prisoner against a cell door.

* * *

That evening he wrote his report to his handler, and this was the trap he had lived inside for months: everything true he could write made him more useful and less safe at once. He described the collapse accurately. He noted the casualty dispute, the demand for original registers, the mention of the medical services. He chose each fact the way a surgeon chooses an incision — precisely, minimally, with awareness that one slip opens everything.

His handler, a soft-spoken man called Ferren who met him in the back room of a stationer's shop, read the report twice and looked up with mild, terrible eyes.

"You've been to that garrison town," Ferren said. "Marrowfield. Before the war. You had leave there — it's in your file."

"I recovered from a wound there," Reyes said. This was even true. "The people were kind."

"People usually are." Ferren smoothed the report flat on the desk between them. "Here is what interests me, Captain. The other side's negotiators are drowning in their own false numbers, and the only thing that could save these talks — or sink them — is an honest register. An original. Kept by someone who wrote down what the reports left out." He tapped the paper once. "If such a document exists, it exists in a place like Marrowfield. Small garrison. Sloppy oversight. Nurses who see everything." A pause, delicate as a scalpel entering skin. "You know the town. Find out whether such a register exists. Consider it a test of your loyalty — which, I should say, is not in doubt, merely unproven. There is a difference."

"There is always a difference," Reyes said, and hated how steadily he said it.

Ferren smiled. "A week, Captain. Coincidentally, the same week the negotiators gave us. History keeps its own schedule."

Walking back through the dark streets to his billet, past shuttered shops and the occasional patrol, Reyes did the arithmetic he had been avoiding. If he reported that no register existed, and one surfaced later — through the armistice record, through Wendell, through whatever reckless beautiful thing that stubborn nurse intended — he would be exposed as having lied, and exposure meant execution, and worse, it would mean he had spent his last weeks making his family's hiding place smaller. If he reported that it existed, Ferren would take it, and the ledger would become one more instrument of one more government's version of the truth, and the names would be folded into somebody's arithmetic instead of given back to the mothers who were owed them. And if he refused the task entirely—

He stopped under a lamp with a broken mantle and stood in its insufficient light.

There was a third path, and it had been forming in him since the night he had seen Wendell's signal chalked on the bakery wall — the small mark, the old mark, the one they used to scratch on doors when one of them was coming home late and the others should keep the stew warm. *I am yours. Wait for me.* He had stood in front of it for ten minutes in enemy uniform while a sentry watched him from the corner, and he had understood that the promise Wendell had made him three years ago — *we'll hear you out before believing the report* — was the only currency he had left that anyone trusted.

The talks would fail within the week unless the true figures reached the table. The true figures lived in one book. The book lived in Marrowfield, under the protection of a cook, an orderly, and a chaplain who had no idea what they were holding — no, he corrected himself; they knew exactly what they were holding. That was the whole point of them. That had always been the whole point of his family: they were the people who knew exactly what things were worth.

He would need to get word to Wendell. Not through channels — channels belonged to Ferren now — but through the old ones. Through bread, if Bess still ran her kitchen the way she ran everything, which was to say completely. Through the chapel, if Odile still left the vestry window unlatched on Thursdays out of habit, and Odile never broke a habit; habits were her liturgy. Through the simple, dangerous act of walking to the edge of the occupied quarter at dusk and looking up at the infirmary's third window, where a lamp was kept burning for the night shift, and waiting to see whether it blinked.

History, Ferren had said, keeps its own schedule. Let it. Reyes had kept schedules too — ward rounds and supper times and the hour when Bess banged the pot and called them all in from the cold. His family had taught him that history was not made in ballrooms by men arguing over numbers. It was made in kitchens and chapels and infirmaries, name by name, meal by meal, by people who wrote down what actually happened and refused, absolutely refused, to call it anything else.

Tomorrow he would begin. Tonight he stood a moment longer under the broken lamp and let himself, for exactly thirty seconds, miss them with his whole chest — Bess's laugh, Tam's nervous humming, Odile's dry blessings, Wendell's pen scratching in the lamplight, writing down the names of the dead so that the dead would stay real.

Then he straightened his collar, became Captain Reyes again, liaison officer third rank, and walked back into the city that was about to decide whether one hidden book would end a war or burn everyone he loved.

Behind him, in the east, the sky over the front held its faint, perpetual bruise-colored glow — the fires of a war waiting to see what the diplomats would do, patient as winter, certain as arithmetic.
