## 19. Wendell Refuses

They came for the answer at half past four in the afternoon, when the light through the infirmary windows went the color of old honey and the ward was quiet in the way that only wounded men sleeping could make it quiet — a silence full of breathing, of bandages rustling, of the small honest sounds of bodies mending or failing to mend.

Wendell was at the dispensary counter, counting out laudanum drops with the pipette held steady between finger and thumb, when Colonel Hargreave walked in without knocking. He had a way of entering rooms as though they had been built around him retroactively, as though walls and doorframes were accommodations to his presence rather than the reverse. Two soldiers remained outside on the step. That was new. Last time he had come alone, confident enough in his own authority not to need witnesses. The soldiers on the step meant he expected this visit to become a matter of record.

"Miss Almeida." He used the honorific like a man laying down a card he knew to be false but useful anyway. Wendell had long since stopped correcting him. Names were currency now; Wendell spent them carefully and gave nothing away for free.

"Colonel." Wendell set the pipette down, stoppered the bottle, and folded their hands on the counter — a gesture learned from Odile, who said that stillness made liars nervous and honest men patient, and one never knew which one stood before you. "You'll want to speak quietly. Half my patients have head wounds."

"I'll be brief."

"You said that last time."

"And I meant it last time." He crossed the ward floor with his boots striking each board like a gavel, pausing at the cot nearest the window where young Private Ellery lay with his jaw wired shut and his eyes tracking the colonel with the flat wariness of a bird watching a cat. Hargreave looked down at him without expression, then moved on. Wendell watched him do it and understood something: the man saw cots, not men. Saw figures. A ledger of his own, kept in his head, all of it in columns he had invented himself.

He reached the dispensary counter and stood on the far side of it, which put a yard of scarred pine between them, and Wendell thought: *he wants the counter there too. He thinks furniture will do what armies couldn't.*

"You've considered my offer," he said.

It wasn't a question, but Wendell answered it as one. "I've considered it every day since you made it. Usually while I'm changing dressings. Sometimes while I'm holding a boy's hand so he doesn't die thinking no one is holding it."

Hargreave's face did not move. It was a remarkable face in its way, Wendell had come to think — not handsome, not cruel, exactly, but *sealed*, built by years of command into a surface that weather could not touch. Only his eyes worked behind it, and they were working now, moving over Wendell's face, reading for cracks.

"Then you know what I'm offering," he said. "Your freedom. Full restoration of your movement through the town. Orderly Tamasic released with a clean record — no mark against him, no file, no consequence. And the infirmary continues under your direction, staffed as you see fit, supplied beyond your current allotment." He let that sit a moment. "All of it. For one book."

"One book," Wendell repeated softly.

"Which contains nothing but names. Names of dead men, Miss Almeida. Men who are past caring whether anyone writes them down." He leaned forward slightly, and for the first time something almost human moved across the sealed surface — not warmth, Wendell decided. Impatience. Perhaps something adjacent to it. "I understand why you keep it. I do. I've read enough of the seized correspondence to understand the sentiment. You believe you're doing a kindness. An act of witness." His mouth thinned. "But kindness has a cost, and right now the cost is being paid by a nineteen-year-old orderly sitting in a cell because you won't part with ink and paper."

There it was. Wendell had known it would come — had felt it circling since the first offer, the way one feels weather coming in an old wound. They had prepared for it, rehearsed answers in the dark of the night, lying awake in the room above the ward while Tam sat in the cells and Bess paced her kitchen and Odile prayed with a face like a closed door. But preparation, Wendell discovered, was nothing against the actual sound of Tam's name in this man's mouth, used this way — as ballast, as leverage, as a weight on the scale opposite a book.

"His name is Tamasic Oduya," Wendell said. "If you're going to spend him, spend him accurately."

Something flickered in the colonel's eyes. A point scored, small but real.

"He is nineteen," Wendell went on, keeping their voice level, low, pitched under the sleep-breath of the ward. "He was carrying a book through occupied streets at midnight because I asked him to, because I was afraid and he wasn't, or because he was and went anyway, which is braver. He has told you nothing. He will tell you nothing. And you know it, Colonel — that's why you're here instead of sending a clerk. Because the clerk failed, and the interrogation failed, and now the only lever left is standing in front of me wearing a colonel's coat and calling itself mercy."

"I am offering mercy."

"No." Wendell shook their head slowly. "You're offering a trade and dressing it in mercy's clothes. Mercy doesn't come with conditions. Mercy doesn't say *and also give me the thing that damns you*. What you're offering is ransom, and we both know whose ransom it is — his or mine, whichever breaks first."

For a long moment the colonel said nothing. Outside, a cart rattled past on the cobbles, and somewhere down the lane a woman was calling a child home to supper, her voice carrying the ordinary music of a town pretending to be itself. In the ward, Private Ellery turned his head painfully against his pillow, still watching, still listening. Wendell knew the whole ward was listening. Wounded men heard everything; fear had given them all the ears of the hunted.

"The book," Hargreave said at last, and his voice had dropped too, gone quieter, which Wendell understood was more dangerous than any volume. "Do you know what it does? Do you understand what happens if those numbers leave this town?" He tapped one finger on the counter, once, twice — a man counting. "The armistice delegation sits in that house on the hill arguing about casualty returns like merchants haggling over grain. Both sides inflating, deflating, accusing. Every figure disputed. Every death contested. And you hold a document that would settle the argument — or blow it apart entirely, depending on who reads it and how. Either way, the talks collapse, the war continues through another winter, and another winter means thousands more names. Thousands more entries for your little book." His eyes were very steady. "Is that your kindness, Miss Almeida? Is that your witness? A longer list?"

It was a good argument. Wendell would grant him that. It was even, in its cold arithmetic, partly true — Wendell had lain awake turning over exactly that question, the terrible arithmetic of it, one book weighed against a winter. But the argument had a flaw in it, and the flaw was the man himself, and Wendell had finally, in these long occupied weeks, found the words to name it.

"May I tell you what the ledger says?" Wendell asked. "Not what's in it. What it *says*. On the inside cover, where I wrote it five years ago, before any of this, when it was only a notebook and Marrowfield was only a garrison and the war was something that happened elsewhere."

Hargreave's jaw tightened fractionally. "I didn't come here for a recitation."

"You came here to take the book. You may as well know what you're taking." Wendell lifted their hands from the counter, unfolded them, laid them palm-down again — steadying themselves the way one steadies a hand on a railing in high wind. "*For the ones the reports forgot,*" they said. "*So that someone, someday, can tell their mothers the truth.* That's all it is. That's the whole of it. Not sedition, not intelligence, not a weapon — though you've all treated it as all three. Five years ago I started writing down the names of men your officers struck from the casualty rolls because it was administratively inconvenient to admit they'd died of fever in a supply depot, or been shot by their own sentry, or bled out waiting for a surgeon who'd been reassigned. Their families got letters saying *missing* or *wounded* or nothing at all. Mothers waited. Some of them are waiting still." Wendell's voice did not rise. It had gone somewhere below argument, below persuasion, into the flat country where certain truths live. "You call that a leak feeding enemy negotiators. I call it a mother in Harrowgate who deserves to know her son died on the ninth of March and not in some battle he never saw. And I will not trade that truth to you, Colonel, not for Tam's clean record, not for my freedom, not for this whole infirmary built of gold."

The silence after was long. Down the ward, a man coughed in his sleep and subsided.

"You'd let him rot," Hargreave said quietly.

"I'd let *you* carry him," Wendell said. "That's different. His chains are yours. You forged them. And I promise you, Colonel — I will spend every hour of every day getting them off him, by any means I can find that don't require me to sell the dead. But I won't buy his freedom with theirs. He wouldn't ask me to. Ask yourself why a nineteen-year-old boy in a cell won't give you the book even to save himself, and then ask yourself what's in it that's worth that."

Something moved behind the colonel's eyes then — quick, involuntary, quickly buried. Wendell had seen it before, in other officers, at other moments: the flicker of a man hearing a truth he had organized his entire mind to avoid. Hargreave recovered fast. He straightened, squared his shoulders, and the seal came down over his face again, complete, final.

"Then you leave me no alternative," he said.

"No," Wendell said. "I suppose I don't."

"This facility is requisitioned effective immediately. It will be converted to a forward clearing station under direct military command." He said it in the tone of a man reading a bill of lading. "The civilian staff will be conscripted to the Army Medical Corps, front-line attachment. You, Miss Almeida, will serve at the dressing stations. First line." He let that land. Everyone in Marrowfield knew what first-line dressing stations were. Everyone knew their life expectancy measured in weeks, sometimes days. "Orderly Tamasic remains in custody pending review. Cook — Bess Marsh — will be assigned to field kitchens. The chaplain—" and here he paused, just slightly, and Wendell felt the pause like a blade laid flat against the skin, "—the chaplain's status is under separate consideration."

"What does that mean?" Wendell's voice cracked at last, the level breaking. "What does *separate consideration* mean?"

"It means what it says." Hargreave took a folded paper from inside his coat and set it on the counter between them. Orders. Official stamps. The bureaucratic machinery grinding forward, indifferent as weather. "You have until dawn to prepare your patients for transfer. Transport arrives at six." He turned to go, then stopped, half-turned back, and for one unguarded moment looked at Wendell with something that might, in a better man, have been regret.

"You're a brave person," he said. "I mean that. Bravery is wasted on the wrong causes more often than not, but it's bravery." He paused. "Give me the book tonight and none of this happens. Not one word of it. I'll tear up these orders myself."

And there it was — the last door held open, the final kindness-shaped trap. Wendell looked at the paper on the counter, at the stamps, at the neat clerk's hand consigning Bess to field kitchens and Odile to *consideration* and Wendell to the mud and the shells, and felt the fear arrive fully at last, rising up through the floorboards and the soles of the feet and the bones of the legs, the animal terror of a body that understood exactly what front-line duty meant. Fear was not the absence of courage. Wendell had learned that at the bedsides of dying men: fear was what courage was *made of*, the raw ore of it, smelted down in the moment of choosing.

"I have watched men die for five years," Wendell said. "Most of them died of things that could have been prevented — delay, indifference, lies in the reports that sent them badly supplied into battles nobody needed to fight. I have written down every name they tried to erase. And I am telling you, Colonel, with all the spine I own: I will stand in your front-line dressing station, and I will bind your soldiers' wounds, and I will do it well, because that is my work and I am good at it. And every night, wherever I am, whatever you take from me — the names will survive. Because you cannot search a thing that lives in people. You cannot seize what forty families already carry in their hearts. Burn the book and I'll write it again. Arrest everyone who's read it and it will still be true. Truth is the one contraband you'll never confiscate, because it isn't kept anywhere. It's kept in *everyone*."

Hargreave looked at them for a long moment. Then he picked the orders back up off the counter, folded them along their creases with precise, economical movements, and tucked them away inside his coat.

"At dawn, then," he said, and walked out.

The two soldiers on the step fell in behind him. Boots on cobbles, fading. The honeyed light lay across the ward floor, across the sleeping wounded, across everything that was about to be taken away.

Wendell stood at the counter with their hands flat on the wood and let the shaking come now that it was safe to shake, shoulders trembling, breath ragged, alone for perhaps ten seconds — until the dispensary door opened and Bess came through it, flour to her elbows, having heard every word through the wall she had been pressed against. She said nothing. She simply came and stood beside Wendell and put one floury arm around their shoulders, and together they looked out at the ward, at the men who would be shipped at dawn to some other building, at the life they had built being dismantled by a signature.

"Well," Bess said at last, in the voice she used for kneading dough and facing down colonels, "if they want us at the front, they've made their first mistake." Her grip tightened. "Because the front, love, is exactly where our Captain Reyes is."

Wendell turned to look at her — and saw, in Bess's eyes, the beginning of a plan.
