## 21. The Reliquary Empty

The chapel smelled of candle wax and cold stone, and Wendell had loved it once.

That was the thing about occupation — it did not take your love away; it only made loving dangerous. Wendell still loved the little garrison chapel with its crooked bell tower and its windows of green and amber glass that turned the afternoon light the color of pond water. They had been married in a church like this one, or near enough — not Wendell personally, but their parents, back when names were simple things handed down like spoons. They had knelt here through five years of funerals, Odile's voice steady over the boxes, saying the words the army would not say for men whose names never made the reports.

And now the ledger lay folded in oilcloth beneath the reliquary of Saint Ferren, six inches of hollow oak behind a saint's gilded shinbone, and Wendell could not walk past the building without their heart climbing into their throat like a cat seeking high ground.

They came at evening, as agreed. Not by signal — there had been no time to arrange signals, no bread loaves, no chalk marks on doorposts. Only Odile, three nights ago, standing at the infirmary door with snow in her gray hair, saying in her plain way: *Come to Vespers on Thursday. You have missed too many services, and God will notice.* And then, after a pause long enough to hold a whole sentence inside it: *God, and possibly others.*

Wendell understood. That was the terrible gift of their found family — they had learned each other so thoroughly that entire conspiracies could be conducted in the space between two remarks about attendance.

Thursday. Vespers. The reliquary.

Wendell dressed carefully: the nurse's gray wool, unremarkable, the collar high. They told Bess they were going to pray. Bess looked at them over a bowl of rising dough, flour to her elbows, and said, "Say one for Tam," which meant *I know*, and "Take the long way round," which meant *be careful*, and "There's seedcake when you're home," which meant *come back*. Five years of shorthand, compressed to flour and yeast.

The long way round took Wendell past the colonel's headquarters, because the long way round was the only way that did not pass the infirmary, where Wendell was technically under house arrest, and past the infirmary there were always soldiers now, and a soldier who saw a prisoner walking freely toward the chapel might ask questions that ended in cells. But the route along the millrace road — that was different. There, Wendell was just another gray figure bent against the wind, another civilian with a prayerful stoop, and the sentry at the corner had learned that nurses went to Vespers and that stopping them was more trouble than letting them pass. Hargreave wanted his town quiet. Quiet was easier to govern than grief.

Still, Wendell's hands would not stay warm. They tucked them into their sleeves and counted their breathing the way they counted it over dying men — four in, four out — and told themselves the fear was only weather. It would pass through. It always passed through. What remained afterward was whatever you had managed to hold onto while it blew.

* * *

The trouble began before the chapel door.

Wendell saw him from thirty paces: a soldier, young, posted not at the chapel entrance but across the lane, in the lee of the cooper's shuttered workshop, positioned with the artless carelessness of someone who has been told to look casual by a man who understands casualness professionally. He held a clay pipe he did not smoke. His eyes moved over everyone who approached the chapel door, and each time someone entered, his lips moved slightly, counting.

Wendell stopped at the pump at the lane's end, as though to drink. The water was iron-cold and tasted of winter. Over the rim of cupped hands they counted heads going in: the Widow Marsh, old Cobb with his stick, the Hollis girls, a handful of others — the faithful remnant that war had not yet frightened off or frozen out. Twelve souls. Thirteen with themselves.

And the soldier counting every one.

So Odile knew. Of course Odile knew — she had seen him too; she must have, she saw everything that moved within a mile of her church. Which meant the retrieval could not happen the way they had planned, in the hush after the last hymn when the congregation drained away and the chaplain lingered to snuff candles. A man outside was counting. A man outside would note when the crowd thinned to nothing and only the chaplain remained, and if a nurse remained also, lingering, kneeling at the reliquary rail a moment too long —

Wendell finished their water. Their heart had gone from climbing to hammering, a dull drumbeat against the ribs, and they made themselves breathe four and four until it slowed. Then they crossed the lane and went into the chapel, into the warmth of forty candles and the low murmur of Odile's voice leading the opening psalm, and they did not look at the reliquary, they did not look at it, they fixed their eyes on the altar cloth — white linen, embroidered years ago by hands now dead — and they prayed, actually prayed, without irony, because terror will make even a lapsed soul sincere.

*Let this be ordinary,* they prayed, in the old childish formula. *Let me be invisible. Let me be nobody.*

It struck them, somewhere between verses, what a strange prayer that was for a person whose whole life's work had been the opposite of invisibility — whose secret craft was making visible every name the army erased. Somewhere in the walls of the world there was perhaps a ledger of prayers too, and someone keeping count of the ones that went unanswered, and Wendell thought: whoever you are, write mine down anyway. Write down that I asked.

* * *

Vespers, when it came, was a masterpiece.

Wendell realized it gradually, the way one realizes a patient is improving — not all atonce but detail by detail, each small sign accumulating into certainty. Odile had chosen the psalms for travelers and the exiled, *Quomodo cantabimus* — how shall we sing the Lord's song in a strange land — and her voice carried it with a weight that made several of the congregation glance up. She had lit every candle, all of them, the full stand, though wax was rationed and she was famously thrifty with it; the chapel blazed like a festival night. And she had arranged the service long — two psalms instead of one, the longer Magnificat, a homily, announced with a gentle apology: *Forgive the length tonight, friends. Some things ought not be hurried.*

Some things. Wendell kept their head bowed and their face arranged into piety and marveled. Odile was buying minutes. She was filling the chapel with light and sound and sanctity so that no one would leave early, so that the counting soldier across the lane would see a full, unhurried, entirely ordinary service, and so that — Wendell's stomach tightened with understanding — so that when the moment came, it would come hidden inside ritual, wrapped in the one thing no soldier dared interrupt: worship in progress.

Odile preached, briefly. Her text was the raising of Lazarus, and her theme was the stone rolled away from the tomb, and how some stones are rolled away by angels and some must be rolled away by friends, sweating, straining, in broad daylight, with everyone watching — and the widow Marsh wept quietly at the beauty of it, and the Hollis girls held hands, and Wendell sat with their heart in pieces, thinking: *she is telling me the plan in front of God and the army both.*

Then communion. Odile distributed it herself, moving down the rail, and Wendell understood at last why the candles, why the length, why everything. Communion meant the congregation would kneel in a line, one by one, faces bowed, hands cupped, and Odile would move among them with the paten and the chalice, and the sightlines of a watching soldier through a doorway would be broken by pillars and shoulders and candle-stands — and in that moving, that slow procession of the sacred, something small and flat and wrapped in oilcloth could travel from the hollow behind a saint's gilded leg to the wide pocket of a chaplain's vestments, and from the vestments to the hands of the last communicant, the one who knelt alone at the end, the one who had come to pray.

Wendell rose when their turn came. Their legs felt borrowed. They walked to the rail and knelt beside old Cobb, and Odile stood above them with the chalice, and for one moment her eyes met theirs — calm, gray, absolutely without fear, or wearing its absence the way she wore everything, as a garment chosen deliberately for the occasion — and she said the words of institution in her level voice and gave Wendell the cup, and beneath the cup, balanced on the paten's edge, her fingers brushed Wendell's wrist twice. Once. Twice. *Done. Done.*

The reliquary was empty. Or rather, it was relieved of its burden, and the burden had traveled six feet through candlelight into the hands of a woman of God, and Wendell received the body and blood of their faith with a ledger-shaped weight pressing against their own ribs where Odile had slipped it into the inner pocket of the nurse's coat during the kiss of peace, so smoothly that Wendell had felt nothing but a hand upon their shoulder, a blessing, a touch like any other touch.

* * *

Afterward, the congregation left slowly, as congregations do, gathering in the porch to gossip about the cold and the war and Mrs. Hollis's daughter's engagement. Wendell stayed to help snuff candles, because staying was expected of them — the nurse helping the chaplain, an old domestic picture, harmless as bread — and only when the last parishioner had gone out into the blue dusk did either of them speak.

"He'll report a well-attended service," Odile said, pinching out a flame with finger and thumb, unflinching. "Thirteen souls, one soldier counting. I gave him a good show."

"You knew he was there."

"I've known since Monday. They rotated him in with the new conscripts — a boy called Pell, if it matters. He crosses himself wrong, poor lamb, thumb before fingers, so I expect he's new to his orders as well as his prayers." She snuffed another candle. The chapel dimmed around them, gold collapsing into shadow, until only the lamp near the sacristy remained. "I decided that if I was going to be watched, I would give the watcher something worth reporting. Piety is excellent camouflage, Wendell. Nobody suspects a full stand of candles."

"The reliquary—"

"Was emptied Tuesday night. I told you to come today because I wanted the handoff done under cover of the mass, not in an empty church where a boy with good eyes might see you kneel in the wrong place." She turned from the candles and looked at Wendell fully, and in the lamplight her face was tired in a way Wendell had never seen — tired down to the bone, tired the way people are tired after carrying something heavy very far. "You think I didn't understand what I was doing? I have kept that book safe for nine days. Nine days of knowing it was behind my own altar, six feet from where I consecrate the host, and every morning I said the words *this is my body* over a box of other people's dead." Her voice did not rise. It never rose. It simply grew quieter and heavier, like a bell being carried rather than rung. "I lied in my report to Colonel Hargreave. Did you know that? I wrote that I had searched the chapel and found nothing. My first lie in forty years of ministry, and I wrote it in a fair hand and signed my name."

"Odile—"

"Don't thank me yet." She held up a hand. "Because here is what I have learned, these nine days, and I want you to hear it plainly, since you are the one carrying the thing now. A lie is a door. You open it once, thinking you are stepping through to save someone, and then you discover the door does not close behind you. It stays open. And through it comes everything else — the next lie, the next concealment, the next risk taken for love. I am complicit now, Wendell. Truly complicit, not in the soft way I have always been complicit, burying the dead kindly and saying their true names over their graves. In the hard way. If they find that book on you, they will ask who helped, and I will lie again, and lying again makes it easier, and that is how a chaplain becomes a criminal — not by falling, but by walking, one honest-seeming step at a time."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the dusk had gone the color of slate, and somewhere down the lane the counting soldier would be writing his report: *service attended by thirteen civilians, duration one hour twenty minutes, nothing irregular.*

"I'm sorry," Wendell said, and meant it so wholly it hurt.

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." Odile almost smiled. "Be accurate the way your book is accurate. That is the only penance I want from any of us — that when this ends, however it ends, we can say exactly what we did and exactly why. Now go. The boy counts departures too, and a nurse lingering late with the chaplain is a story he may invent details for."

She embraced them then, briefly, fiercely, smelling of candle smoke and incense and the cold outdoors, and pressed her lips to Wendell's forehead in benediction, and whispered against the skin, so softly it was barely breath: "Bess has the cart ready Thursday, market day. Whatever you are planning — and don't tell me, I sleep better ignorant — tell Bess. The family moves together now. That is the whole of my sermon, and it cost me everything, so mind you keep it."

* * *

Wendell walked home the long way, past the dark millrace, the ledger flat and warm against their chest as though it had a heartbeat of its own.

Twice they stopped, once at the bridge and once beneath the bare elms, and put a hand inside their coat just to feel the oilcloth, just to confirm it was real. Each time, the same vertigo: *it is here, it is with me, thirteen souls and one boy named Pell and one chaplain's first lie stand between this book and the fire.* Hargreave wanted it to burn it. The enemy handler wanted it to win a negotiation. Reyes wanted it — Reyes wanted it for reasons Wendell still could not weigh, love and suspicion tangled past separating. Everyone wanted it, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread, and it held more names than a regiment.

At the elms Wendell paused longest, listening to the creak of bare branches, and let themselves feel, for one unguarded minute, the gratitude — vast and frightening, gratitude with edges. Bess with her cart. Tam, released and silent and changed. Odile with her first lie signed in fair ink. And beyond them, unseen, unknown, the patients who had lied to soldiers, the families who would someday receive their names. A whole invisible congregation, assembled without anyone's consent, bound by a book none of them had read.

We are all complicit together now, Wendell thought. May it be the kind of complicity that history forgives.

Then the minute ended, because minutes of grace always do, and Wendell buttoned their coat and walked on — and did not see, at the bend where the millrace road met the lane, the figure detach itself from the cooper's wall and follow at a distance: not the boy Pell, but a man in a greatcoat with a staff officer's bearing, who had arrived that afternoon on the evening coach and who watched the nurse's gray shape all the way to Bess's door, noting it in a small book of his own before turning toward the colonel's headquarters, where a lamp still burned.

Someone had come looking for the ledger. And now he knew exactly which door it slept behind.
