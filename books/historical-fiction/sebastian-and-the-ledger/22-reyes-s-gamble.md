## 22. Reyes's Gamble

The report was due by dawn, and Ivo Reyes had learned that lies, like sutures, held best when they were small and neat.

He sat at the desk in his quarters — *quarters*, the delegation called them, though they were a converted linen room in the commandeered customs house, narrow as a coffin and smelling faintly of camphor and old rope — and he looked at the two documents before him the way a man looks at two roads, knowing one of them ends at a wall with riflemen standing against it.

The first document was genuine. It was the search inventory from the chapel of Saint Aldhelm's, compiled by Corporal Vasse under orders from Colonel Hargreave's staff, countersigned by the enemy liaison office because the chapel sat on the seam between occupied and parleyed territory, and everything on that seam required both signatures to be legal. Vasse had searched it thoroughly. He had opened the vestry cabinets, pulled the cushions from the kneelers, tapped along the wainscoting, climbed into the belfry and come down gray with pigeon filth. He had found nothing.

Which was itself a problem, because Ivo knew — as surely as he knew the sound of Wendell Almeida breathing in the dark beside him three years ago, as surely as he knew the weight of Bess's hand on the back of his neck when she'd pulled him down to kiss his hair like a mother sending a boy off to school — that there had been something to find. The ledger had gone into the chapel. He would have staked his life on it, and in fact he was staking considerably more than that.

The second document was the one he intended to file. It said nearly the same thing as the first, with one difference: where Vasse had written *reliquary — sealed, key unlocated, not forced*, Ivo wrote *reliquary — opened, contents inventoried, no papers found*.

A small lie. A neat suture. And an executionable one.

He understood the arithmetic of it with perfect clarity, which was somehow worse than if he'd been able to deceive himself. If anyone ever checked — if Vasse was asked directly, if the reliquary was re-searched, if a single clerk in Hargreave's office compared the corporal's field notes against the filed copy — then the discrepancy would be found, and discrepancies in liaison reports during armistice negotiations were not treated as clerical errors. They were treated as treason by whichever side the traitor was closest to, and since Ivo wore the enemy's uniform and ate at the enemy's table and slept under the enemy's roof, the enemy would have him. A drumhead court, a wall, a shallow grave behind the customs house, and his name added to some other ledger somewhere, some list of men whose deaths were too inconvenient to report honestly. The irony of that was so clean it was almost funny. He did not laugh.

He dipped his pen anyway.

His hand was steady. That surprised him a little. Three years ago — no, four now, God, four — his hands had shaken over less. Over a card game lost to Tam, over a letter from home he'd been afraid to open. The infirmary family had taught him steadiness the way they taught everything: not by preaching but by doing. Wendell's hands never shook over a wound, no matter how bad, because shaking helped nobody. You were allowed to shake afterward, in private, into Bess's shoulder, but not while you worked. So he worked.

Outside, Marrowfield lay under its third week of occupation, and the town had developed the particular silence of places where soldiers walk. Not true quiet — there were boots on cobbles, the clink of tack, the low continuous murmur of men who have been given authority and don't know what to do with it except talk about it — but a hush underneath, the sound of a population holding its breath. From his window he could see the infirmary's chimney, and below it, past the roofs, the thin thread of smoke that meant someone was baking at an hour no lawful kitchen baked. Bess. Working before dawn, as she always had, though now her loaves carried more than flour and water. He'd received two messages in bread so far, folded oilcloth wedged into the crumb like a stone in a plum, and each time he'd torn the loaf apart with fingers that finally, finally shook.

*I am well. They watch me but cannot close my mouth. O is braver than all of us. Come to the mill when you can. — W.*

That last line had kept him alive through two weeks of suspicion from his own delegation, through Handler Merek's soft-voiced interrogations, through the growing certainty that everyone in this war wanted him dead for slightly different reasons.

He finished the falsified entry, blotted it, and set it beside Vasse's original. Then he did what he always did now before committing anything to paper: he read it as Merek would read it.

Merek was the delegation's intelligence officer, a spare, gray man with the patient manner of someone who has spent thirty years watching other people make mistakes, and who has concluded that waiting is cheaper than acting. Merek did not believe Ivo was loyal. Merek believed Ivo was *useful*, which was different and, for the moment, sufficient. Two weeks ago Merek had laid it out with terrible gentleness, over tea neither of them drank: *You were one of them, Captain. Everyone knows it. Your file says you died at Corvenne Bridge, and yet here you sit. Either you are the most fortunate soldier in this army, or you are exactly what you appear to be — a man who changed sides. Prove otherwise. Find me the ledger.*

So the trap had two jaws. Hargreave wanted the ledger destroyed or buried, because it documented the colonel's own falsifications — Ivo had pieced that much together from the shape of the man's searches, the feverish thoroughness around records, around names. Merek wanted the ledger seized, because a document proving the other side lied about its dead was worth ten divisions in negotiation. And Ivo stood between the jaws holding a pen and a piece of paper, writing a lie to keep a book hidden that both sides would kill to possess, for the sake of a person whom both sides considered either a suspect nurse or an irrelevant one.

Love justifying treason. He turned the phrase over in his head as he sanded the ink, and found it didn't shame him anymore. Let the historians call it treason. Treason was only loyalty pointed the other way, and he had chosen his side years ago, in a warm kitchen, over burned onions, when five people who had no blood in common decided they were a family. Everything since had merely been paperwork.

* * *

Merek summoned him at midmorning, which meant Merek had already read the report, which meant the next hour would be a fencing match conducted entirely in courtesies.

The intelligence office occupied the customs house's best room, with tall windows overlooking the river quay, and Merek arranged himself in the light the way certain men arrange themselves — visible, unhurried, letting you see his hands. There was tea. There always was. Ivo had never once seen him drink it.

"Captain Reyes." Merek gestured to the chair. "Your chapel report."

"You'll forgive me if I've grown tired of chapels," Ivo said, sitting. "Corporal Vasse found enough pigeon droppings to fertilize the whole valley."

"Mm." Merek drew the report across the blotter and regarded it without reading it, which was its own kind of message: *I have already read it twice.* "You countersigned the reliquary entry personally."

"I inspected it myself. Vasse couldn't locate the key, and I didn't want a forced entry on record without a senior signature. The parish priest's family still has standing here. Property damage creates grievances, and grievances create incidents, and we're here to prevent incidents."

"A careful answer."

"It's a careful war, sir. On this street, at this moment."

Something flickered at the corner of Merek's mouth — approval, or its impersonation. He turned the report face down. "Tell me about the reliquary."

"Oak, iron-banded, older than the town. Empty except for dust and a saint's fingerbone in a silver case. We left the bone. I'm told the locals get upset about bones."

"And the papers?"

"There were no papers."

"No," Merek agreed softly. "There weren't." He let the silence do its work. Merek's silences were better equipped than most men's arguments. Ivo counted floorboards and waited him out. "Colonel Hargreave's staff believes otherwise. They believe a document of considerable size passed through that building. Their conviction is based on— well. Sources." A pause calibrated to tell Ivo there *was* a source, and that Merek knew its nature, and that this knowledge was currency being displayed rather than spent. "If their conviction proves correct, and if it emerges that a liaison officer certified the search of the very place the document was hidden —"

"Then you should ask why your own corporal missed it," Ivo said. "I countersigned his findings. His findings said the reliquary was empty. If papers were later found there, the failure belongs to the search party, not to the signature."

"Vasse is twenty years old and writes letters to his mother every Sunday. He will say whatever he is told to say, Captain, when the time comes. As will we all." Merek folded his hands. The light through the window caught the gray at his temples and made it almost white. "I'm not accusing you. I want to be clear about that. Accusation is a tool for when persuasion has failed, and I find persuasion cheaper. I'm telling you that the clock is running. The talks collapse Friday if the casualty figures aren't reconciled. Both delegations know there's a private count out there somewhere — your side's deserters talk, ours talk, and the numbers don't agree, and everyone in that room suspects everyone else of arithmetic as a weapon. Whoever produces the true figures first owns the moral high ground for the rest of the century." He leaned forward, and for the first time his voice lost its clerical smoothness and became simply a tired man's voice. "Find the ledger, Captain. Before the colonel does. Because if Hargreave finds it, it burns, and if you find it, we might end this war while there's still a country left to end it in. Those are the only two outcomes I can see from where I sit. Choose the one you can live inside."

Ivo rose, saluted with the crispness of a man with nothing on his conscience, and walked out through corridors that seemed, today, narrower than usual.

Only when he reached the yard did he permit himself the thought that had been sitting behind his teeth through the entire interview: *Merek doesn't want the truth. Merek wants the ledger. Those are different things.* The true figures would indict both armies — the ledger made no distinctions, that was its whole terrible holiness; Wendell had written down every name the officers struck, from every uniform, every regiment, including, Ivo now suspected, names that would burn the men reading them. No. Merek wanted a weapon shaped like a book. Which meant that delivering the ledger to Merek was only a slower way of destroying it.

And that thought led, as it always led now, down the hill, past the shuttered draper's, to the infirmary with its smoking chimney, and to the question underneath all his questions: what would Wendell do with it? What had Wendell *been* doing with it for five years?

Keeping faith with the dead. Nothing more complicated and nothing less. The ledger wasn't a weapon. It was a promise, written in a steady hand, one name at a time.

He would have to steal a weapon from the only person who saw it as a promise, or persuade that person to hand it to men who would forge it into something else, or find a third road that probably didn't exist. He walked toward the infirmary under house-arrest guard — they still let him visit, officially to assess the medical situation for the delegation, unofficially because surveillance enjoyed itself — and rehearsed, once again, the sentence he could not yet bring himself to say aloud: *Wendell. Give it to me, and trust me, and if I'm wrong, hate me forever, but give it to me.*

* * *

The visit went wrong in the first minute, which was how Ivo knew something had happened.

Wendell met him in the infirmary's front room, where the requisitioned space still managed to smell like carbolic and lavender if you breathed deep, and where two patients played cards with studied deafness. Wendell looked — Ivo catalogued it automatically, the old triage habit — exhausted but upright, eyes clear, hands quiet. Not broken. But there was a new quality in Wendell's face, a stillness like the air before weather, and when their eyes met, Wendell gave the smallest nod, the one that had meant, in the old days, *come, I need you, don't ask yet.*

"The delegation wished an assessment," Ivo said loudly, for the guards and the card players. "Sanitation, supplies, convalescent capacity."

"Of course, Captain." Wendell's voice was level, official, and beneath it ran something else, something almost bright. Not fear. He knew all of Wendell's fears by now, had watched them move across that face like clouds. This was different. This was closer to awe. "We'll begin with the ward. Mind the floorboards near the third bed; the wood's rotten. It's been giving us trouble all month."

Floorboards. Third bed. The old code — Tam had invented it years ago for smuggling brandy past Odile, and Odile had known about the brandy the entire time, and none of them had ever told Tam. Ivo kept his face professionally blank and followed Wendell down the ward, past rows of cots, past the window where afternoon light fell in long dusty bars, and when Wendell knelt at the third bed to adjust a pillow, Ivo crouched beside them as if inspecting the floor, and Wendell spoke with lips barely moving, gaze fixed on the boards:

"The last page. Ivo, the last page — I never told anyone what's on the last page, because until yesterday I hadn't understood it myself." A breath. "It's Hargreave. It's his son. His own boy is in my book — died at Corvenne Bridge, in the retreat he ordered, and the report struck the name, and I wrote it down, and I never knew whose name it was until the requisition lists came through and I matched the dates." Wendell finally looked up, and their eyes were wet and blazing at once. "The one man who wants this book burned more than he wants the war ended is the one man with a name inside it. Do you understand what that means?"

Ivo understood several things at once, and they arrived with the sensation of a floor giving way. He understood that Wendell had just handed him the most dangerous sentence spoken in Marrowfield since the shelling stopped. He understood that if Wendell was right, Hargreave's searches were not policy but desperation, and desperate men with armies at their backs did not negotiate. He understood that Merek would pay any price for this information and that telling him might buy Ivo his reputation and cost the ledger everything.

And he understood — with a clarity that felt almost like peace — that he had already decided. Somewhere between the falsified report at dawn and this rotten floorboard, the decision had made itself. He would not sell this. He would not spend it. He would carry it the way Wendell carried the book: as a promise, not a weapon.

"Then we don't give it to anyone," he said quietly. "Not Hargreave, not Merek, not the delegations. We take it to the table ourselves, when the time comes, and we make them hear every name — his son first." His hand found Wendell's, briefly, in the shadow between the beds. "But that's Friday's fight, and today is Tuesday, and today I have committed treason on a search report to buy us the days between. So I need to know one thing, Wendell, and I need you to be certain: when the time comes — the table, the reading, all of it — can your family move that book faster than an army can march?"

From the doorway came the smell of fresh bread, and Bess's voice, cheerful and dangerous: "Captain Reyes. You'll stay for supper. I've baked far too much again — it's the flour, it never keeps, and I'd *hate* for it to go to waste."

Behind her, in the corridor, boots were coming down the stairs — too many boots, moving too quickly, and among the voices calling for the commanding nurse, Ivo heard one he knew from Hargreave's own offices, and it was saying a word he had hoped never to hear aimed at this house.

The word was *chapel*.
