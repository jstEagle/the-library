## 23. What the Last Page Says

The safehouse was a hay loft above a cooper's shop that had not made a barrel since the second winter of the war, and it smelled of dust and old shavings and the sweet rot of apples someone had stored badly and abandoned. Wendell sat cross-legged in the corner farthest from the trapdoor, where the light came down through a gap in the slating in a single dusty blade, and held the ledger on their knees for the first time in nine days.

Nine days. It felt longer. It felt like the book had been away from them for years — passed hand to hand through Bess's kitchen network like a relic, wrapped in oilcloth, hidden in a flour sack, buried once beneath a crate of turnips while soldiers walked past close enough to touch the crate. Now it was here again, warm from the ride against Bess's side under her shawl, and Wendell found their hands were shaking as they untied the cord.

*You're safe,* they wanted to say to it, the way one spoke to frightened patients. *We're both safe. For now.*

They did not open it immediately. That was the strange discipline of grief, Wendell had learned: you could hold a thing and still not be ready to look at what it held. Around them the loft creaked with the ordinary sounds of an old building settling, and below, faintly, Bess's voice rose through the floorboards as she charmed the cooper's widow into letting them stay another night without asking a single question. Reyes stood at the small round window with his back to the room, watching the road in the way he had learned to watch roads — not looking at anything, seeing everything.

Wendell opened the ledger.

* * *

It began, as it always began, with a smell rather than a sight: ink and tallow smoke and the ghost of carbolic, the whole infirmary folded up inside the paper like flowers pressed in a hymnal. The early pages were careful. Almost timid. Five years ago Wendell had been a different creature — a nurse who believed the reports would correct themselves, who wrote the first names down only because it seemed impossible that no one else would, who told themselves it was temporary, a private accounting until the army remembered its own arithmetic.

*Private Aldous Merrick, 3rd Battalion, died 14th of Thawmonth. Cause given: fever. True cause: wound to the abdomen, left untreated two days. Report signed by Lt. Col. Vane.*

*Corporal Edda Solloway. Died 2nd of Seedmonth. Report says returned to duty. I held her hand when she went. She asked after her sister's dog.*

Page by page the handwriting changed. The letters grew smaller, more compressed, as though Wendell had understood even then that space in this book was finite and the war was not. The dates ran together. The causes multiplied. And threaded all through it, in every margin, the small annotations that had become the ledger's second language: *mother notified by me, not by the army.* *He was seventeen; he said he was nineteen.* *She sang, at the end. I did not know the song.*

Wendell read slowly. This was not reconnaissance. They knew every name here; they had earned each one, paid for each one in candle-oil and sleeplessness and the specific loneliness of knowing something true that everyone in authority preferred false. But reading it straight through, in hiding, with a man in enemy uniform ten feet away who might be their salvation or their ruin or both — reading it now was different. Now the book felt less like a record than a body. Something that could be wounded. Something that had already been hunted.

"You're crying," Reyes said quietly, without turning from the window.

"No," Wendell said, and then, because lying to him about small things seemed suddenly obscene when the truth between them was so large and unsorted, "Yes. I don't know yet which name."

That was honest. There were four hundred and eleven names, and any one of them could do it. Tam, if they let themselves think how close he had come to dying in those cells for a book he'd never been allowed to read. Odile, locked now in some room in her own chapel's shadow, serene because serenity was the only weapon they'd left her. Any of them. All of them.

Reyes turned then. In the slanted light his face was half gold, half shadow, and he looked — not young again, nothing could give him that back — but like the man from the peacetime suppers, the one who used to steal bread off Bess's tray and get away with it because he laughed while he did it. "Read me one," he said. "If it helps. Read me one that doesn't hurt."

"There isn't one."

"I know." He crossed the loft and sat down across from them, folding his long frame onto a barrel with the ease of a man who had spent recent years sitting wherever sitting was possible. His uniform — their enemy's grey-blue, the collar brass dull — looked wrong in this light and right in every other way, and Wendell had stopped flinching at it sometime around the fourth day and hated themselves a little for that too. "Then read me one that hurts least."

Wendell looked down. Their thumb had stopped, without their noticing, near the end of the book — near the last pages, which they had somehow skipped, the way one steps around a loose stair in the dark out of old habit. The final entries. The last weeks before requisition, written fast, the ink hurried.

They turned to the last written page.

* * *

The last page was not the last page of paper. Wendell had always kept three or four leaves blank at the end, a superstition they had never examined — as though leaving room meant leaving hope, as though the war would see the empty space and understand it should stop there. So the final entry sat alone on a leaf with whiteness beneath it, and perhaps that was why it stood out so sharply, why Wendell saw it whole before they could protect themselves from it.

The date was eleven months ago. Late autumn. The fighting at Harrow Fen, the engagement the dispatches had called a *successful holding action*, the week the infirmary took in sixty-one men in thirty hours and Wendell stopped sleeping in anything resembling a bed.

They remembered that week. They remembered it the way the body remembers a fever.

But the entry —

*Captain Elias Hargreave, liaison staff, attached forward observation. Died 19th of Frostmonth, wounds sustained at Harrow Fen. Cause given in report: killed in action, body not recovered. True cause: brought in alive at dusk, shell fragment, chest and left arm. Recognizable. He begged us not to send word ahead — said his father would come, and his father coming would be worse than anything. Died before dawn. Buried in the east row, unmarked per his request. No report filed. Officer casualties were being held back that month for morale purposes. I recorded him anyway. He asked me to remember him as a nurse does, not as a son does. I have tried to do both.*

Wendell read it twice. Then a third time, the words refusing to assemble into sense, sliding apart like wet rope.

Hargreave. The surname was common enough. Half the province probably had a Hargreave in it somewhere, a miller or a schoolmaster or a dead captain. It needn't mean—

But *liaison staff*. And the colonel's file, glimpsed once on the seized documents table before Wendell had been ushered out: *Col. A. Hargreave, Provost Command, Marrowfield District.* And Tam's account of the interrogation, delivered in a flat voice that had frightened Wendell more than weeping would have: *he talked about duty, and then he talked about his boy. He got soft when he talked about his boy. Like it was the only part of him that wasn't a lie.*

"Elias," Wendell said aloud. The name came out strange, unpracticed. They had written it eleven months ago and never spoken it since; names in the ledger lived in ink, not air.

Across the loft, Reyes had gone very still. He was good at stillness — it was a soldier's gift and a spy's necessity — but this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a man watching a bridge take weight. "Whose name is that?"

"The last entry." Wendell heard their own voice as though it belonged to someone in another room. "Ivo. The last name in the ledger. The one the officers struck from the reports." They looked up. "It's Hargreave's son."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. The blade of light had crawled along the floorboards; somewhere below a dog began barking at nothing and thought better of it. Wendell became aware that they had not breathed properly since reading the entry, and made themselves breathe, the way they'd teach a panicked patient: slow, counted, the body obeyed even when the mind would not.

"Tell me again," Reyes said. Not because he hadn't heard. Because hearing twice made it real, and real things could be planned around.

"Captain Elias Hargreave. Forward observation at Harrow Fen. Brought in alive — I remember the night now, Ivo, I remember *him*, I didn't know the name meant anything then, he wouldn't give more than rank. He was lucid for six hours. He knew it was bad. He made us promise not to send word to his father." Wendell's hands had gone cold around the ledger's edges. "Because his father would come. Because his father coming would be worse than anything. He knew his father, you understand? He knew exactly what his father would do, and he chose to die unnamed rather than set that machinery moving."

"And the report?"

"Held back. Officer casualties suppressed that month — morale, they called it. I've seen it done a dozen times. Colonels sit on the names of their own dead like misers counting coin." Wendell heard the bitterness arrive in their own voice and did not chase it out. "Except this colonel wasn't just sitting on a name. He was sitting on his *son*. And he never collected. Eleven months. He has spent eleven months believing—" Wendell stopped. Started again, more carefully, because this was the hinge of it and hinges deserved precision. "Or not believing. Either way. Either he thinks his boy is missing, body unrecovered, maybe alive in some prison column — and every day of that hope is a wound he dresses with more zealotry. Or he suspects, and he cannot confirm it, and the not-confirming is eating him alive. That is why the infirmary. That is why the raids, the arrests, the obsession with a nurse's book of names. It was never sedition he was hunting. It was a date. A place. A row of earth."

Reyes had risen during this without appearing to decide to rise. He stood at the window again, but differently now — not watching the road, watching nothing, his reflection ghosted in the glass with its enemy collar and its face that Wendell loved against all evidence and instruction.

"Do you understand what you're holding?" he said at last.

"A ledger."

"You're holding the one sentence that can stop Colonel Augustus Hargreave mid-stride. You're holding the thing he has torn a district apart to find, and he doesn't even know what it looks like." Reyes turned. The light caught him full in the face now, and Wendell saw something they had not seen in him since the mill road: not fear, exactly. Awe. The awe of a man realizing the weapon he'd been carrying was heavier than he'd known. "Every falsified report, every suppressed count, every figure he's cooked to keep the talks stalled and the war fed — all of it rests on a man who believes, or needs to believe, that his son's death was clean and honorable and *recorded*. And your book says otherwise. Your book says his son died begging, unmarked, in a charity ward, filed under nobody." He shook his head slowly. "Power's weakness isn't armies, Wendell. It's this. It's grief with nowhere to go. It's a father who can survive losing everything except not knowing."

"Then we could use it." Wendell said it flatly, testing the words the way one tests ice. "Take it to him. Trade it. Odile for the ledger, Tam's record cleaned, the raids ended—"

"No."

"—or better, force it. Tell him what happened to his son unless he lets the true figures reach the armistice table—"

"*No.*" Reyes crossed the loft in three strides and crouched in front of them, and his hands closed over theirs over the ledger, gentle as a tourniquet. "Listen to me. I've watched powerful men break, Wendell. It doesn't look like surrender. It looks like a man with nothing left to lose, and a man with nothing left to lose burns whatever he's standing in. If Hargreave learns his son's death was hidden — *by his own command structure*, by the machine he has served and falsified for — do you know what he does? He doesn't repent. He doesn't sign an armistice with clean eyes. He escalates. He burns Marrowfield to keep the story from existing. Or worse — he takes the ledger and feeds it page by page to a fire, because if his grief can't stay private, no one's truth survives either." His grip tightened fractionally. "A secret like this is only a lever if the man on the other end still has something to stand on. Hargreave's whole footing is the fiction that he's righteous. Take that away carelessly and you don't disarm him. You detonate him."

"Then what do we do with it? Sit on it?" Wendell pulled one hand free — not in anger, only to gesture, only because their body needed to argue even while their mind lagged behind. "People are in cells, Ivo. Odile is in custody for *us*. Tam was shot. The talks are deadlocked over numbers this book could settle tomorrow. And you want me to treat the last page like a live shell and bury it in a turnip crate?"

"I want you to treat it like what it is," Reyes said. "Not a threat. A testimony. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." He paused, and Wendell watched him choose his next words with the visible care of a man crossing a frozen river. "When I deliver this ledger — and I will deliver it, to the armistice table itself, where both delegations have to sit and listen whether they want to or not — the last page goes in last. Read aloud. In order. Four hundred and twelve names, ending with Elias Hargreave, so that when his father hears it, he hears it in front of witnesses. In front of history. Not in a dark room with a nurse and a traitor and a knife's length of privacy. Grief can be blackmailed, Wendell. Grief witnessed can only be honored or disgraced. Let him choose which."

Wendell stared at him. Somewhere in the walls a beam ticked, cooling. *Four hundred and twelve.* They had said the number without thinking, and it had landed in the loft like a stone dropped down a well.

"You've known," Wendell said slowly. "About the ledger's weight. Since the midnight meeting, since you told me you needed true casualty figures. You've known what the last page could do to him."

"I suspected the book would contain things powerful people had buried." Reyes almost smiled, and it was the tired, rueful version, the one with the peacetime supper underneath it. "I didn't know it contained *him*. God, Wendell — I didn't know that." He looked down at their joined hands, at the ledger beneath them, and his voice dropped. "Eleven months. His father has commanded this district for eleven months with that wound undressed. Every cruelty he's committed — Tam's cells, Odile's arrest, the raids — some part of it is a man tearing through the world looking for a grave. And the grave was in your handwriting the whole time."

The words settled over Wendell with a weight that was not entirely terrible. That was the strangest part. Terrible, yes — a son begging not to be named, a father hunting blindly, a family — *their* family — paying in blood and cells for the space between those two facts. But underneath the horror lay something else, something that felt almost like the moment a fever breaks. Because the ledger had always been an accusation, and accusations harden the accused. But this — this was also a *gift*, the only kind Wendell had ever had it in their power to give: the location of a boy. A row of earth in the east field. A place to stand and grieve.

"He asked to be remembered as a nurse remembers," Wendell said. "Not as a son."

"Nurses remember truly," Reyes said. "Sons get the story the family can bear. He knew that. He was protecting his father from himself, and look what it bought." He exhaled. "It ends at the table, Wendell. Both of them — father and son, lie and truth — in front of the delegates, in front of the record. It's the only ground where that page can't be burned, buried, or bargained. And it has to happen soon. The talks reconvene within the week. If the deadlock holds past that, both sides go back to the line, and everything we've bled for becomes kindling."

Below them, the stairs creaked. Bess's voice preceded her up through the trapdoor, pitched low and urgent in the code she thought they hadn't decoded weeks ago — *bread's risen wrong, need to speak to the baker* — and then her head appeared in the square of fading daylight, flour to the elbows, eyes sharp.

"Rider came through," she said. "Chapel bell's to ring at noon tomorrow — full peal, unscheduled. Odile's doing, or done *for* her, I can't tell which. And Wendell—" She hauled herself fully into the loft, breathing hard, and her gaze went straight to the ledger on their knees as though she could read it through the cover. "There's word going round the garrison. Quiet-like, the way the dangerous words travel. That the colonel's ordered every priest and chaplain in the district to preach Sunday on *duty and silence*. Every one of 'em. Except—" her jaw set, "—except folk are saying he means it as a leash. And folk are saying that if anyone can turn a leash into a warning, it's her."

Wendell looked down at the last page. At the blankness beneath the final entry, waiting. At the name that could end a war or start a conflagration, depending entirely on whose mouth it came out of, and when, and in front of whom.

"Noon tomorrow," they said. "Then we'll know whether Odile is preaching to the congregation—" they closed the ledger gently, the way one closed a patient's eyes, "—or preaching to us."
