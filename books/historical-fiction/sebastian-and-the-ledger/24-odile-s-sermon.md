## 24. Odile's Sermon

The chapel smelled of candle wax and cold stone, and Odile loved it best at this hour, before the congregation arrived, when the light came through the east windows in long amber blades and lay across the floor like something patient. She had lit the candles herself. She always did. It was a small vanity, perhaps, but she had earned her vanities in thirty years of service, and this one cost nothing but an earlier rising.

Below her, in the reliquary, behind the iron door with its worn saints, the ledger waited.

She had not opened the reliquary since the night she hid it there. She made herself not open it. That was part of the discipline — not the discipline of the office, which was easy, a matter of hours and vestments and the proper words said over bread and wine, but the other discipline, the one that had no rulebook: the discipline of carrying a secret so heavy that your body began to arrange itself around it, the way a tree grows crooked around a stone.

*It is only paper,* she told herself each morning, folding her hands. *Ink on rag paper. Forty pages of names.*

But she knew better. She had been a chaplain through two campaigns before Marrowfield. She had seen what names did when they were spoken aloud — how they moved through a room like weather, how they could flatten a man or raise him from his knees. Ink on paper. So was a fuse.

She heard them coming up the lane now, the sound of boots on gravel, more boots than usual. The garrison had taken to attending service in numbers since the requisition — some out of genuine piety, most because Colonel Hargreave had issued a memorandum encouraging attendance, the way he encouraged everything, with a tone that made encouragement indistinguishable from order. They would fill three pews today, maybe four. Officers with their polished buttons catching the candlelight like small complacent suns. Enlisted men at the back, awkward in their holiness, glancing at the doors.

Odile stood in the sacristy and looked at herself in the little mirror there, cracked diagonally so that her face seemed split into two versions of itself, one slightly above the other. Which one would preach today? She supposed both. That was the trouble with a lie, even a righteous one — you became two women, and you had to keep them both fed.

She had lied exactly once in her life before last week. She could still remember the first, though she had been seven years old and it had been about a stolen plum, and her mother had known instantly, mothers being the original inquisitors. This second lie was larger. In her report to the military chaplaincy — the report she was required to submit weekly now, listing all materials held on church premises, all papers, all documents — she had written the words *nothing of note*, and she had signed her name beneath them, and her hand had not even shaken, which frightened her more than trembling would have.

*Nothing of note.* As if forty names were nothing. As if the dead could be filed under a heading so bland it barely existed.

She straightened her stole. White for ordinary time, though nothing about this season felt ordinary. And then she went out into the chapel, and the congregation rose, and the service began.

* * *

They sang the opening hymn badly, as soldiers do, loud where they should be soft and soft where they should be loud, and Odile found she did not mind. There was a kind of honesty in bad singing. The officers in the third pew sang loudest of all — Captain Merrick, who had overseen the ransacking of the infirmary; Lieutenant Voss, young and pink-cheeked, who had been present when Tam was taken and had afterward looked, for one strange moment, as though he might be sick. She noted them the way she noted everything now: cataloguing, filing, weighing. It was not a habit she admired in herself. It was the habit of a woman living under occupation, and the occupiers wore her own nation's uniform, which was the strangest occupation of all.

The lessons were read. She had chosen them carefully, days ago, back when the choice had felt abstract. Now they landed in the chapel like stones dropped into still water.

The first was from the prophet: *"The slain of the Lord shall be many, cast out from one end of the land unto the other, and they shall not be lamented, neither gathered nor buried."*

She watched the word *lamented* move down the pews. Watched Captain Merrick's jaw tighten. Watched a private near the back — a boy from the marsh country, she thought, with the flat vowels of it still in his speech — look down at his hands.

*"They shall not be gathered,"* the reader continued, *"nor buried; they shall be as dung upon the face of the ground."*

No one shifted. No one coughed. The silence had the particular quality of silence in rooms where everyone knows something is being said to them.

The gospel was shorter, simpler. The sparrow. Not one of them falls to the ground without your Father knowing; the very hairs of your head are numbered.

Numbered. Odile almost smiled at the audacity of providence. *Numbered.* Somewhere below her feet, wrapped in linen cloth against the damp, a book of numbering sat in the dark among the relics of saints who had mostly died the way the prophet described — unmourned, unburied, struck from records by empires that preferred their histories tidy.

She ascended the pulpit.

* * *

She had preached perhaps two thousand sermons in her life. Most of them, she admitted privately, had been adequate — sound doctrine, decent structure, the occasional image that lifted. A handful had been good. None, she suspected, had ever been dangerous.

She looked out at them. The officers. The enlisted men. The townsfolk scattered among them — Widow Crenshaw with her black gloves, the miller's family in their Sunday wool, old Piotr from the tannery who never knelt properly because of his knees. Wendell was not there — house arrest kept them home — and Bess was not there, because someone had to keep the kitchen burning, and Tam was not there, because Tam was released but changed, moving through the infirmary like a man walking through shallow water, careful where he put his weight. Her family was absent, and so she would preach to strangers, and trust that words travel farther than their speakers intend.

"In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit."

"Amen," they said, and settled.

"I want to speak to you today," she began, "about forgetting."

A small rustle. Sermons about forgetting were usually sermons about forgiveness, and forgiveness sermons made people comfortable, comfortable the way a warm bath is comfortable, right up until the water goes cold.

"Forgetting," she said, "is a respectable vice. We do not call it a sin. We call it *moving forward*. We call it *healing*. We call it *not dwelling on the past*. And there is truth in those words — I have buried too many soldiers to believe that grief should be carried raw forever. Memory must be tended like a wound, cleaned and dressed, or it festers. I know this. I have done the tending."

She let her eyes rest on the third pew. Not accusingly. Gently. Gentleness, she had learned, was far harder to defend against than fire.

"But there is a forgetting that is not healing," she went on, "and we have grown very skilled at it in these years of war. It is the forgetting we perform *for others*. When a man dies at the front, somewhere a clerk writes his name in a book, and that book travels, and eventually a woman in a kitchen hundreds of miles from here reads that name and falls to her knees among the flour sacks. That is what the book is *for*. That is its whole purpose. Not strategy. Not glory. A woman, falling to her knees, because someone told her the truth."

Lieutenant Voss had gone very still.

"And when that book is wrong — when a name is left out —" she paused, and the pause did the work of ten paragraphs, "— the woman does not kneel. She waits. She sets a place at the table. She tells her neighbors her son is delayed, that the post is slow, that any day now. And she waits. Years, sometimes. Because the book said nothing, and books are believed."

Somewhere in the chapel a baby began to fuss and was hushed. Odile waited for the hushing to finish. She had learned long ago that a preacher who fears silence has already lost.

"You will say to me — *Chaplain, surely such omissions are rare. Surely errors happen in any great undertaking.* And I will tell you: yes. Errors happen. Hands shake, ink smears, clerks grow tired. But there is a difference between an error and an omission, and the difference is intention, and intention, thank God, leaves fingerprints."

She saw Captain Merrick lean slightly toward the man beside him, murmuring something. Let him murmur. She had not yet said anything that could not be defended as general exhortation. The blade was still inside the sheath. But every eye in the chapel had begun, without quite knowing why, to follow the shape of it.

"Our Lord," she said, "was asked once about a tower that fell in Siloam and killed eighteen people. Do you remember His answer? He did not say, *these things happen*. He did not say, *the accounts of the fallen are disputed*. He named the number. Eighteen. He counted them, and He spoke the count aloud, in public, in Jerusalem, with powerful people listening." She spread her hands. "Eighteen. Not seventeen. Not 'some.' Eighteen. Because a number is a form of love. To count the fallen is to say: *each one of you was one, and one mattered, and I refuse to round you off.*"

The boy from the marsh country was crying now, silently, the tears simply running, and he made no move to wipe them. Beside him an older soldier — a sergeant, grey at the temples — put one hand briefly on the boy's shoulder, then took it away, as if even comfort required permission now.

"There are those who think a name written down is a small thing," Odile said. "Paper burns. Books are lost. Empires rewrite their own histories and call it scholarship. All true. But I will tell you what I have learned in thirty years of burying men whose names I read from this book—" she touched the lectern, "—paper is patient. Paper waits. A lie can outrun the truth for a year, for five years, for a generation — but the truth does not tire, because the truth does not need to eat or sleep or justify itself. It just sits. In whatever drawer. Under whatever floorboard. Behind whatever door." 

She had not meant to say *behind whatever door*. She heard it leave her mouth and felt the danger of it, and also — God forgive her, God forgive her — the pleasure. It was like stepping off a ledge and discovering mid-fall that she could fly.

"And one day," she said quietly, "someone opens the drawer."

* * *

She brought them to the prayers next, and here she did her finest work, if she said it herself, and she did, because no one else would.

"I ask your intercessions," she said, "for the souls of the departed of this war. For the named and the unnamed alike. For those whose deaths were witnessed and recorded, and for those who died unwitnessed — in ditches, in field hospitals, in the mud between lines — whose passing no clerk set down, whom no report remembers, whom the ledgers of armies pass over in silence."

*Amen,* said the congregation.

"For the mothers who wait," she said. "For the fathers who have stopped waiting and do not know why. For every household keeping a place set at a table for someone the paperwork says never died — because the paperwork said nothing at all."

*Amen,* softer now.

And then, and this was the moment she would carry to her own grave whatever grave she got, she raised her voice to full liturgical register, the voice she used for the absolution, the voice trained to carry into the farthest corner and leave nowhere to hide:

"Let us pray especially for those who hold power over records. For colonels and clerks and quartermasters. May they remember that every line they write or fail to write will one day be read aloud. That there is no page so official that God cannot turn it over. That the dead have patience, and the truth has allies, and *nothing that is hidden shall not be revealed*."

Silence.

Then: "Amen," said the boy from the marsh country, loudly, wetly, and after him the sergeant, and after him old Piotr from the tannery in his terrible accent, and then the townsfolk, and then — slowly, uncertainly, unable to determine whether refusing would itself draw attention — the officers. Even Captain Merrick. Even Lieutenant Voss, whose *amen* came out cracked down the middle.

Odile bowed her head over the prayers and felt, beneath the stone floor, beneath the crypt, behind the iron door of the reliquary, the presence of the ledger like a heartbeat under her feet. *Forty names,* she thought. *Forty, and the last one heavier than all the rest combined, though none of them know it yet.*

She finished the service. She gave the blessing. The congregation filed out into the pale morning, and the officers' boots rang on the flags, and Widow Crenshaw squeezed Odile's hand at the door and whispered, "Well preached, Chaplain," in the tone of a woman who understood perfectly well what had just happened and intended to enjoy it.

* * *

Alone in the chapel afterward, snuffing the candles one by one, Odile allowed herself, at last, to shake.

Her hands trembled so badly that the taper rattled against the brass, and she set it down and stood in the half-dark and breathed. The sermon was out of her now. It could not be taken back. By evening, someone would repeat its phrases to someone, and by tomorrow someone would carry them to Colonel Hargreave, and Hargreave — who was not stupid, who was many things but not stupid — would hear a chaplain preaching about omitted names and hidden pages and would ask himself the question she had spent two weeks praying he would not ask.

*Where is she pointing?*

He might not reach the reliquary. But he would reach Odile. And when he reached her, he would find a woman who had lied once in her life before last week and discovered, to her horror and her strange liberation, that she could do it again.

She knelt at the altar rail, not to pray exactly — prayer had gone somewhere beyond words — but to be near the altar the way you stand near a friend when you have news too large for sentences. Below her, the ledger slept among the saints.

"Forgive me," she said to the empty chapel, to God, to the forty, to the one whose name she had not yet read and already grieved for. "I am going to make them listen. Whatever it costs."

She rose, straightened her stole, and blew out the last candle.

Behind her, in the doorway — she had not heard him come in, and her heart seized — stood Lieutenant Voss, cap in his hands, pink-cheeked and miserable.

"Chaplain," he said. His voice was low. His eyes went once to the reliquary, quickly, the glance of a man who has noticed something he wishes he hadn't. "The Colonel requests your presence. Tomorrow morning. He says—" and here the young man swallowed, "—he says he'd like to discuss your *report*."

The word hung between them like smoke.

Odile folded her hands, and found they had stopped shaking.
