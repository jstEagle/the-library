## 26. Chapel Sealed

Colonel Edmund Hargreave did not sleep. He had learned long ago that sleep was a currency, and that men who spent it carelessly woke poorer than they knew. So he sat in the requisitioned study of the Marrowfield garrison house — a room that still smelled faintly of someone else's pipe smoke, someone else's ordinary life — and he watched the window turn from black to iron to the color of old bone.

Dawn came slowly to Marrowfield in October. The mist rose off the river first, then off the marshes beyond it, and for perhaps twenty minutes the whole town hung suspended in white, as though the war had never touched it and the world were being made new. Hargreave had once found such mornings beautiful. He could no longer remember when.

On the desk before him lay the clerk's report, read so many times the creases had gone soft. *Chapel of St. Aldhelm. The chaplain Odile Ferrant has been observed entering the reliquary recess on multiple occasions outside the hours of service. She was seen on Tuesday last carrying an object beneath her cloak, rectangular, of sufficient weight to cause her to favor her left side.*

Rectangular. Of sufficient weight. He did not need the clerk's careful circumlocutions. A book. The book. Five years of names his own hand had struck from five years of reports, written down by some nurse with a private passion for accuracy, circulating through his garrison like infection through a wound.

He thought of Tam — the orderly, the boy with the broken fingers who had sat in his cells and answered every question with the same maddening, gentle blankness, *I don't know, sir, I only carried what I was told to carry* — and felt again the peculiar rage of a man striking at water. He had interrogated deserters who wept. He had interrogated spies who bargained. He had never interrogated a boy who seemed genuinely unable to understand why telling the truth might be asked of him as a separate thing from doing right by his people.

The boy knew nothing. But the household knew everything. And the household orbited, as all such households orbited, around two points: the nurse Almeida, now under house arrest and watched with a thoroughness Hargreave had personally specified; and the chaplain, who moved freely because God moved freely, or so the convention went, and whom no one had yet dared to stop and search because stopping a chaplain required paperwork and paperwork required reasons and reasons, in this particular matter, were things Colonel Hargreave could not afford to write down.

But dawn searches required no reasons. Dawn searches required only soldiers, and soldiers were the one commodity the war had made cheap.

He rang the bell. Sergeant Pryce appeared with the promptness of a man who slept in his boots, which, Hargreave suspected, he did.

"The chapel," Hargreave said. "At first light. Full search. Every pew lifted, every flagstone sounded, the reliquary opened, the sacristy turned out to the walls. Father Anselm is to be kept out of the way — courteously. If the chaplain objects, she may object to me directly."

"And if we find nothing, sir?"

Hargreave looked at him. Pryce had been with him since the first winter, and there were perhaps three men in the army who could ask him a question in that flat, honest tone and keep their careers. "Then we find nothing," he said, "and we search again. Books do not evaporate, Sergeant. Paper burns, paper rots, but paper does not evaporate."

"No, sir." A pause. Pryce's face did something complicated and then did nothing at all. "Only — begging your pardon — the town's saying otherwise. They're saying the book walks. That it goes where it likes and hides where it likes, and no soldier's laid a hand on it yet."

"The town," said Hargreave, "says a great many things."

"Yes, sir." Another pause, longer. "It's just that the town keeps being right, sir."

Hargreave dismissed him with a flick of two fingers and sat alone with the grey light and the soft-creased report, and permitted himself, for exactly sixty seconds by the regimental clock on the mantel, to feel what he actually felt, which was fear.

Not of the book itself. Names on paper were names on paper; dead men stayed dead whether their deaths were reported or recorded or whispered over kitchen tables. No — what he feared was simpler and worse: that the book existed, that it was real, that somewhere in this damp occupied town his own signature — or rather, the absence of his own signature, the elegant negative space where his signature should have been — was sitting in the dark waiting for him. He had falsified reports the way other men balanced ledgers: carefully, systematically, out of conviction that the truth, properly managed, served the war better than the truth unmanaged. Casualty figures fed the enemy negotiators. Inflated losses weakened the armistice position; concealed losses strengthened it. Every number he had buried was a brick in the wall of the peace he intended to build, and he had built it believing — still believing — that history would thank him for the wall and never ask about the bodies in the foundation.

Except that the bodies had a nurse. And the nurse had a book. And the book had a habit, according to the town, of walking.

The clock ticked past the minute. He rose, buttoned his coat, and went down to watch his soldiers desecrate a church.

* * *

They came up through the mist in single file, boots silent on the wet cobbles, and Hargreave walked behind them not because a colonel should lead a search party but because he could not bear to wait elsewhere for news of himself.

The chapel of St. Aldhelm was a small, stubborn building, older than the garrison, older than the war, its flint walls furred with lichen, its bell rope hanging slack. Inside, candle-smoke and beeswax and cold stone. Hargreave stood in the doorway while Pryce's men spread through it with the terrible gentleness of trained vandals: pew cushions lifted and replaced, hymnals shaken spine-down, the vestry drawers run through with gloved hands and closed again in their proper order, because Pryce understood, as Hargreave had taught him, that courtesy was a weapon too.

Father Anselm, the parish priest — a stooped old man with ink-stained fingers and the resigned air of clergy everywhere whose buildings had become military property — stood by the font wringing his hands. "Colonel. Is there some trouble? Has something happened?"

"A routine matter, Father. You'll be compensated for any damage."

"Damage." The old man said the word as though tasting a foreign coin. "You misunderstand, Colonel. It isn't the damage. It's the looking. A man can survive having his house searched. It's harder to survive knowing the searchers believed he'd hidden something shameful in God's house."

"God's house," said Hargreave, "has hidden a great many things this year."

He heard the words leave his mouth and heard, beneath them, the other sentence they carried — *I have hidden things in God's house myself, or nearly; I have hunted a book into sanctuaries and reliquaries and the folds of cloaks* — and hated the chaplain Ferrant for making him hear it.

She was not present. He had expected her to be present. Chaplains of her reputation — and she had a reputation; even his own clerks spoke of her sermons with a respect they extended to no officer — did not abandon their churches to soldiers. Yet the sacristy was empty, her vestments hung in their neat row, her prayer desk bare, a half-finished cup of tea gone cold on the windowsill as though she had stepped out minutes ago and meant to step back in.

"She was here at four o'clock, sir," said one of the privates, a young man named Ellery who had a talent for noticing things Hargreave both valued and distrusted. "Lamp burning in her rooms above. I saw the light when we mustered."

"Where is she now?"

"No one's seen her, sir. Not the verger, not the priest."

A cold thread worked its way down Hargreave's spine and settled in his stomach. A woman who fled before a search was a woman with something to lose. A woman who vanished entirely was a woman executing a plan.

"Pryce. The reliquary."

The reliquary of St. Aldhelm stood behind the altar: an iron-bound oak chest on a stone plinth, three centuries of candle grease pooled and hardened at its base. Two soldiers lifted the lid. Inside lay the usual furniture of small-town sainthood — a fingerbone in a silver case gone brown with age, a scrap of embroidered silk, folded linens, a chalice wrapped in wool.

And beneath the linens, pressed flat against the bottom of the chest, a rectangle of empty space in the dust, sharp-edged, unmistakable. The ghost of a book.

Pryce looked at Hargreave. Hargreave looked at the space, and felt the strange double sensation he had come to know in this affair: triumph and humiliation arriving simultaneously, holding hands.

"She had it here," he said quietly. "It was here. Hours ago."

"Yes, sir."

"Which means she knew we were coming."

Ellery shifted his weight. "Begging pardon, sir. Or it means she moves it regular. Nightly, like. Like a woman putting out the cat." He flushed at his own boldness. "My gran used to move her savings nightly. Never kept it anywhere two nights running. Said a hiding place was like a heartbeat — you could set a watch by it, and then it wasn't safe anymore."

Hargreave stared at the young soldier. Out of the mouths of grandmothers. A hiding place like a heartbeat. If the chaplain moved the book nightly — if this whole network, cook and orderly and nurse and priest, treated concealment not as a secret to be kept but as a discipline to be practiced — then no raid would ever find it, because no raid could ever be fast enough. You did not catch a thing that moved. You caught the people who moved it.

That was the thought, and it arrived with the clarity of ice water: he had been chasing a book for weeks, and the book had been winning, because books could not be arrested and people could.

"Search her rooms," he said. His voice came out level, which cost him more than shouting would have. "Thoroughly. Then seal the chapel. No one enters without my written authority — including Father Anselm. Especially Father Anselm."

The old priest drew himself up to the full height of his stoop. "Colonel, this is a house of God—"

"It is," said Hargreave, "a building containing a hollow space where a seditious document recently lay, and until I am satisfied otherwise, it is also a military storehouse. You may take it up with God, Father. I'm told He hears complaints."

He climbed the narrow stair to the chaplain's quarters while the search rattled below him. Her cell was exactly what he had expected: a cot, a desk, a shelf of theology, a window overlooking the churchyard where the mist was burning off the graves. On the desk lay her sermon notes for Sunday, written in a small, upright, disciplined hand.

He should not have read them. He read them anyway.

*What is a name?* the notes began. *A name is the smallest unit of a human being that survives them. Armies count numbers. God counts names. When we refuse to speak a name, we do not protect the living from grief — we rob the dead of the only property they carried out of the world.*

Below it, in the same hand, smaller: *They think a book is a thing. A book is a congregation. Every name in it is a person who once had a mother who said it aloud every morning, and mothers do not forget, and neither shall we.*

Hargreave stood in the small clean room holding another man's son's memory in his head — no. Holding no one's memory. Holding nothing. He put the papers down precisely where they had lain, squared to the desk edge, and found that his hands were not entirely steady, and was grateful no one was there to see.

His own son's name was not in any ledger. He had made certain of that. He had made certain of it in ways that had cost him more sleep than the falsified reports ever had, and if anyone had asked him — no one had ever dared to ask him — he would have said that what he did with the casualty figures and what he had done in that other matter were different things entirely, one strategy and one mercy, and that a man who could not tell the difference deserved to lose everything.

No one had ever asked. That was the privilege of rank: no one asked, and so you never had to answer, and so you never had to find out what the answer was.

He went back down. The search had produced the hollow in the dust, three candles of suspiciously recent manufacture, and a loose floorboard beneath the third pew from the back that covered a cavity smelling of lamp oil and nothing else. Another ghost. Another absence shaped exactly like the thing he wanted.

"Sir." Pryce appeared at his shoulder. "Still no sign of the chaplain. Verger says she went out before dawn, alone, toward the river lane. Carrying her market basket, he thinks."

Her market basket. Hargreave closed his eyes briefly. Rectangular. Of sufficient weight. A book fits very nicely in a market basket, under a cloth, beside the onions, in the hands of a woman no soldier thinks to stop because she is a chaplain and chaplains are furniture, chaplains are background, chaplains are the one category of person the entire apparatus of occupation agrees not to see.

She had walked the book out of the church under his nose before his search party had finished forming ranks, and she had done it at four o'clock in the morning, calmly, with a basket, the way her cook presumably baked bread, the way her nurse presumably changed dressings — as work. As ordinary labor. These people had turned resistance into housework, and housework, Hargreave was beginning to understand, was the one army he could not defeat, because it did not drill, did not parade, did not break, and replenished itself at supper.

"Find her," he said.

"Sir?"

"The chaplain. Find her, bring her to me — alive, unharmed, respectfully. If she resists, restrain her gently. If she preaches at you, Sergeant, you will stand there and listen to every word and then bring her to me anyway." He heard his own voice sharpening and did not trouble to smooth it. "She knows where that book is going. She may not know the final destination, but she knows the road, and roads have two ends."

They found her within the hour, because of course they did; she had not truly been fleeing, only delivering, and a delivery ends at a doorstep. The verger's information, the river lane, a fishwife who remembered a chaplain pausing at the bridge — the town talked, always, endlessly, and its talk was loyal to no one, and it led Pryce's men to the mill road, and the mill road to a cottage where Odile Ferrant was discovered sitting in a kitchen drinking tea with an elderly widow, her market basket empty on the table, her expression composed, her hands folded, as though she had been expecting them since before dawn.

Which, Hargreave realized, she probably had.

* * *

She sat across from him in his study that afternoon, in the same chair where Wendell Almeida had sat and refused him, hands still folded, back straight, wearing her gray clerical dress with the small worn collar, looking less like a prisoner than like a woman who had granted an interview.

"You understand," Hargreave said, "that I have no wish for this."

"I believe you," she said. "That is the saddest part."

"I have a duty—"

"To your reports," she said. "Yes. I have read your reports, Colonel. I read everything the garrison publishes; it is part of my work, knowing what my congregation is being told to believe." She let that sit between them for a moment. "Your reports are beautifully written. Very few deaths in them. One might almost think this war was being won politely."

"You are concealing a seditious document."

"I am keeping a register of the dead," she said, "which my church has done since before there was an army, and will do after there is none. If the register is sedition, Colonel, then so is the burial service, and you are welcome to arrest the whole of Christendom. You will need a larger cell block."

"The book is not in the chapel."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It is not in the chapel."

"Where is it?"

"I don't know."

He leaned forward. "Madam—"

"Colonel." Her voice remained gentle, and that gentleness was somehow worse than defiance; it was the voice of a woman speaking to a man she pitied. "I am going to tell you something true, and you may make of it what you like. I do not know where the ledger is. I have not known where it is since Tuesday. I know who moved it and I know why, and I will not tell you either of those things, but the location itself — that I honestly cannot give you, because I was not trusted with it." She smiled, and there was real warmth in it, which appalled him. "You see how it works? We hide things from each other too. Love does not require omniscience. It requires only that each of us be willing to be the one who pays."

"And you are willing."

"Oh, yes." She said it simply, without drama, the way one confirms an appointment. "I have been willing since the first funeral I conducted for a boy whose name your reports declined to contain. Do you know what I said over his grave, Colonel? I said his name. Just his name, and the prayers, and then his name again at the end, so it would be the last word in his ears and the first word in God's. There were officers present. None of them stopped me. I have sometimes wondered whether that was mercy or merely that none of you were listening."

He had not been listening. He had attended eleven funerals in Marrowfield, standing at the back in his greatcoat, checking his watch, thinking about supply lines.

"Chaplain Ferrant," he said, and was disturbed to hear how tired his own voice sounded, "I am offering you one chance. Tell me where the book is, and you walk out of here tonight. Refuse, and you spend the armistice negotiations in a cell, and when the histories of this war are written, you will be a footnote at best."

"What a curious threat," she said. "Footnotes are where the truth usually lives, Colonel. The main text belongs to men like you." She unfolded her hands at last and laid them flat on her knees, and looked at him with an expression he would carry for the rest of his life, though he would never describe it to anyone: not anger, not contempt, but a kind of sorrowful attention, as though he were a patient and she had already read the chart. "Write your footnote about me. I promise you the main text is already being drafted, and you will not enjoy your chapter in it."

He signaled to the guard at the door. She rose without being told twice, gathered her gray skirt in one hand, and paused at the threshold.

"For what it is worth," she said, not turning around, "I will pray for you tonight. By name. Everyone deserves to be somebody's entry."

The door closed behind her.

Hargreave stood alone in the study with the grey afternoon dying outside and the hollow shape of a missing book printed on the inside of his eyelids. He had the chaplain. He had the cook under watch, the nurse under arrest, the orderly released and followed, the whole family mapped and marked — and the book was still walking, somewhere ahead of him, in a market basket, in a bread loaf, in a pocket, in the dark, moving nightly like a heartbeat.

Somewhere in the occupied streets below, a church bell he had not authorized began to ring vespers, and the sound of it went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor: the whole town praying aloud, by name, for everyone he had erased.

He sat down at his desk and gave the order he had been avoiding for three days.

"Double the guard on the mill road," he told Pryce. "Whatever comes out of the marshes at night — a cart, a coffin, a wedding party — it stops. Everything stops."

Because the book was going somewhere. And he intended to be standing at the end of the road when it arrived.
