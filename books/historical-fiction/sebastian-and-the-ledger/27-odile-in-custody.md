## 27. Odile in Custody

They took her at first light, before the bells.

Odile had been awake since four, as she always was, kneeling on the cold stone of her cell-like room behind the chapel with the breviary open in her hands. She heard the boots in the courtyard—there was no mistaking them; soldiers' boots had a rhythm all their own, a drumbeat without music—and she had time to do three things. She folded her spare cassock and laid it on the cot so the room would look tended. She kissed the small wooden cross that had hung over her mother's stove in Vellacourt before it hung over anything else. And she prayed, briefly and without flourish, not for deliverance but for accuracy: *Let me say only what is true, and let what is true be enough.*

Then she went down to meet them herself, opening the chapel door before they could break it, because broken doors were a kind of lie too, and she had resolved—standing in the grey dark, watching frost feather across the window glass—that from this hour forward she would tell no more lies except the ones already spoken, and those she would carry like stones in her shoe until God saw fit to take them from her.

There were six of them. A sergeant with a face like a closed ledger—she thought the comparison and almost smiled—and two privates who would not meet her eyes, which told her they still had consciences, and which meant they could still be spoken to. The other three were strangers, staff officers by their bearing, men who had learned early that looking at things directly was a professional hazard.

"Chaplain Odile Ferren?" the sergeant asked, though he knew. It was the ritual of authority, this asking of questions whose answers were already written down somewhere. She understood rituals. She honored his.

"I am."

"You'll come with us."

"Yes," she said, and picked up her coat, and did not look back at the reliquary, because looking was its own confession, and the reliquary was empty anyway—empty since yesterday's staged prayer service, empty since Bess's cart had rolled out through the marsh fog with a false bottom full of names. Let them search. Let them turn the chapel inside out like a pocket. There was nothing there but saints' bones and candle wax and the smell of forty years of other people's grief, and no army on earth had yet learned how to arrest a smell.

* * *

The cells beneath the garrison headquarters had been wine cellars once, before the war drank everything else. Odile knew this because she had known Marrowfield before the war, before the requisition, back when the building's owner—a fat, cheerful grain merchant named Pelloquin—had donated casks of red to the infirmary every Michaelmas and called it charity when it was really just surplus. Now the racks stood empty along the walls, and the iron rings where casks had been chained held instead the wrists of prisoners, and the air smelled of stone and rust and the particular staleness of fear that has been left standing too long in one place.

They put her in the third cell. She noted this—the third, on the left, beside a drain channel—and found the noting itself comforting. Habit was a form of prayer. Counting was a form of psalm.

The first hours they gave her nothing, which she recognized as technique. Hunger and boredom were instruments; she had watched interrogators work in the field hospitals, years ago, when the front had been closer and prisoners had passed through their hands. So she sat on the bench—they had left her a bench, which she chose to read as either courtesy or oversight—and she recited the offices silently, Matins through Prime, letting the old words move through her like water through a channel cut long ago, wearing the stone smooth.

The words were the point. Not their meaning exactly—meaning could be argued with, twisted, made to serve—but their weight. *Deus in adiutorium meum intende.* O God, make speed to save me. She had said it ten thousand times in ten thousand rooms, over dying men and dead boys and officers who wanted blessings for battles that would unmake those same boys, and the words had never once changed, and in their changelessness was the whole argument against the men upstairs. Armies moved borders and rewrote reports and declared living men enemies and dead men alive-on-paper. But *Deus in adiutorium meum intende* was the same today as it had been when some monk scratched it into wet vellum eight hundred years ago, and it would be the same tomorrow, after whatever they did to her in this cellar, and that sameness was a fortress no colonel could requisition.

Near midday—she judged by the thin stripe of light that crossed the high barred window and crawled along the wall like a slow animal—boots came down the corridor, unhurried, and stopped at her door.

She knew before he entered that it would be Major Corvane. He had been present at the raid on the infirmary, standing in the doorway with his gloves on, watching soldiers pull apart Tam's cot with the expression of a man auditing an account he suspected of fraud. He had a voice like dry paper and the habit of beginning sentences with your name, as though familiarity were a lever.

"Chaplain Ferren." He came in carrying a folding chair, set it up facing her bench, sat, and crossed his ankles. Everything about him was arranged to suggest patience, which meant he had very little. "Thank you for making this easy."

"You're welcome," she said. "Though I don't recall being asked."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth—not quite amusement; amusement cost money and Corvane spent nothing. "You've been in custody"—he consulted a card he didn't need—"eleven hours now. In that time you've eaten nothing, drunk nothing beyond the water provided, and answered no questions. I'm told you spent the morning moving your lips."

"Praying."

"For whom?"

"For you," she said, and let it sit. "For the sergeant who arrested me, who has children in Vellacourt and a bad knee he favors going downstairs. For the two young privates who couldn't look at me, which means they haven't yet done anything they need to avoid seeing. And for you, Major, because yours is the hardest situation in this building, though I doubt anyone has told you so."

He studied her the way he'd study a map of ground he intended to take. "And what situation is that?"

"The situation of a man ordered to find something that isn't where he's been told it is." She folded her hands. "I can't help you, Major. Not because I'm defiant—I've been defiant before, and it's a poor companion, it keeps you up at night—but because there is simply nothing here to give. No ledger in my keeping. No papers. No confessions. You searched the chapel. What did you find?"

"We found," Corvane said mildly, "a reliquary recently disturbed. Dust displaced. A lock oiled within the month."

"You oil locks in damp weather or they seize. Ask any sexton."

"We found a prayer service convened at short notice, well attended, during which the reliquary stood open on the altar."

"People pray near relics. That's rather the tradition."

"We found," he said, and now the mildness thinned enough to show the wire underneath, "that you filed a report stating the reliquary contained nothing but the remains of Saint Adhelm, whereas your own inventory—filed four years ago, before the occupation—lists, alongside the saint, a processional cross, two altar linens, and a silver pyx. The pyx is missing, Chaplain."

Ah. So that was the shape of the net. They couldn't produce evidence, so they would manufacture the appearance of it and see what flapped. Odile felt something in her chest that was not fear—it took her a moment to name it, and when she did she nearly laughed. It was *relief*. Relief, because a man who must build his case out of a missing communion box was a man who had lost the trail entirely. Bess's cart was miles away, under marsh reeds, and every question Corvane asked was a question pointed at an empty room.

"A silver pyx," she repeated. "Major, do you know what a pyx is?"

"It holds consecrated hosts."

"It does. And do you know what happens if a pyx goes missing?"

"Tell me."

"The priest responsible reports it to her bishop, does penance, and replaces it out of her own stipend—which, if you've seen a chaplain's stipend, Major, will explain why the replacement took four years." She opened her hands. "It was stolen, likely by one of the deserters who sheltered in the chapel bell tower in the spring of '43, whom I fed and did not report, and for that failure I am genuinely sorry, though not sorry enough to have reported hungry men to a firing squad. If you wish to charge me with theft, charge me with theft. If you wish to charge me with feeding deserters, charge me with that. Both are true. Neither is sedition."

Corvane regarded her for a long moment. Somewhere above them, a door slammed; the building had a pulse of doors and boots, and Odile listened to it the way she had once listened to patients' hearts, cataloguing rhythm, noting irregularities.

"You're very good at this," he said finally.

"At what?"

"At giving me truths that are useless. Theft, deserters, a stolen pyx. You hand me a handful of small honest coins hoping I won't notice you're not paying the debt."

"And what," she asked quietly, "is the debt, Major? Say it plainly. We're both adults in a wine cellar. Say what you believe I've done."

"You concealed documents from a lawful requisition. Documents containing falsified casualty figures, circulated to enemy negotiators, materially damaging the war effort of—" he paused, and she watched him decide whether to finish the sentence honestly, and watched him decide against it, "-the Crown."

"There," she said softly. "Was that so hard?"

"It wasn't an accusation. It was a hypothesis."

"No. Hypotheses are tested. Accusations are *believed*, and you believed it before you walked down those stairs, because Colonel Hargreave believes it, and you are paid to believe what he believes." She leaned forward slightly, and saw the two guards by the door shift, and spoke to her next words partly to them. "Major, I will tell you what I told the Colonel's men when they emptied my chapel, and what I will go on telling you until one of us dies of it: I have kept no documents. I have concealed nothing. What I *have* done is bury ninety-one soldiers whose names never appeared in any report this building issued, and I buried them with their names spoken aloud, because I believe—this is doctrine, Major, you may look it up—that a soul without a name cannot properly rest, and a nation without accurate graves cannot properly stand."

"Ninety-one," Corvane said. His pen had not moved. She noticed that; she noticed everything now; fear sharpened the eyes even as it clouded the mind, and hers had been sharpening for days. "That's a specific number."

"It's the number in my burial register. Which is public, Major. It sits on a lectern in the chapel and anyone may read it. Dates, plot numbers, names. It has sat there through the entire occupation, in plain sight, because I hide nothing that is true." She paused, and then—because serenity was not the same as passivity, and because she had spent a lifetime learning the difference between a scalpel and a sword—she added, gently: "Perhaps you should compare it to the casualty reports. You have access. I don't. See if the numbers agree."

Something moved behind his face, quickly, like a fish under ice. He wrote one line, closed the notebook, and stood.

"We'll resume this afternoon."

"I'll be here," Odile said. "God willing, and the provost permitting."

At the door he turned. "The pyx, Chaplain."

"Reported to the diocese, March 1843. You're welcome to write to Bishop Anselm yourself. He answers slowly, but he answers."

* * *

They resumed it four times that day, and twice the next, and Odile began to understand that she was not being interrogated so much as *worn*, the way water wears stone, and that her defense was therefore not brilliance but hardness—being the kind of stone that takes centuries. Each session Corvane varied the approach. First the reasonable man, offering her tea she declined (she trusted nothing from their kitchen; also, fasting clarified). Then the offended patriot, wounded that a woman of the cloth would aid the enemy. Then, most dangerous, the tired man, rubbing his eyes, saying quietly, "Look, Chaplain, between us—the Colonel needs a culprit. Give me something, anything, a name, a date, and I'll shape it so it doesn't touch you. You have people in this town. You know what happens to institutions that fall out of favor. Your infirmary. Your patients."

That one reached past her defenses and touched something raw, and she was grateful afterward for the discipline that had made her bow her head before answering, so that the flicker in her face happened while hidden.

"My patients," she said, "have survived shellfire, typhus, amputation without chloroform, and the current cook's opinion of salt. They will survive the Crown."

But that night, alone in the cell with the stripe of moonlight for company, she allowed herself the luxury of fear for precisely the length of one psalm—the long one, the lament, *By the rivers of Babylon*—and no longer. Fear was a visitor; you fed it tea and sent it home. What she did not send home was the accounting, because conscience demanded audit even in captivity, especially in captivity.

She reviewed the lie. The report she'd filed saying the reliquary held nothing but the saint—that had been a lie, her first in forty years of ordained life, spoken with a steady voice while soldiers stood three feet away, and it had worked, and Wendell was free because of it, and the ledger was under marsh reeds because of it, and somewhere Captain Ivo Reyes—who had sat at this family's table and sung badly and wept at Bess's stories—was walking through enemy lines toward a rendezvous none of them could yet imagine.

*One lie,* she thought, *weighed against ninety-one names.*

And the old theology rose up in her like a tide, the theology she'd taught novices: that sin was not a matter of arithmetic, that a good outcome did not launder a bad act, that she had lied and would answer for it, and that she would answer for it *gladly*, because the alternative—the truth as Hargreave practiced it, the truth bent into whatever shape power required—had killed more souls than any lie of hers ever would. There were lies that served death and lies that served life, and God, Who was neither a clerk nor a colonel, weighed intention, not ledgers.

*Forgive me the method,* she prayed. *Not the purpose. The purpose I'd defend before the throne itself.*

She slept, eventually, on the bench, with her coat for a blanket, and dreamed of the chapel in peacetime: sunlight, bees in the ivy, Pelloquin hauling his casks, everyone alive, everyone unnamed by any war.

* * *

On the third day they changed tactics again, and this time the tactic was a person.

A girl brought her breakfast—thin bread, weak tea, an apple, which was new—and lingered setting the tray down with the elaborate slowness of someone working up courage. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Laundry detail by her reddened knuckles. She wouldn't look at Odile, then looked, then looked away.

"They say you're a spy," the girl whispered.

"They say a great many things. What's your name?"

"Maret."

"Maret. Eat half my apple; you look half-starved, and lying to a prisoner about having brought extra won't damn you."

The girl almost smiled—almost—and then the smile fell off her face like a dropped plate. "They're saying things in the kitchens," she said, very fast, barely voiced. "About an exchange. That the Colonel's furious because the talks have started up again, at the river mill, and that the liaison officer—the one in the enemy uniform, the traitor one—he's offered something. Offered himself and something else, something they want, in trade. For you." Her eyes were enormous. "For you and the orderly. That's what Marta heard through the serving hatch, that the exchange is for the chaplain and the orderly, and the meeting's to be at the mill, and—" Boots sounded in the corridor above, and Maret straightened as if yanked by wires, snatched up the tray, and fled, leaving the apple.

Odile sat very still.

The stone of the cellar pressed in around her, familiar now, almost companionable, and the stripe of light began its slow crawl along the wall, and she did the thing she had trained herself to do in forty years of watching men die: she separated what she knew from what she feared.

What she knew: a frightened child had carried rumors out of a kitchen, and kitchen rumor was water—shaped by whatever vessel it ran through. What she feared: that it was true, that Ivo had offered a trade with himself at the center of it, which meant he had walked into Hargreave's arithmetic as deliberately as she had walked down to the courtyard with her coat over her arm, knowing what such trades were worth, knowing what Hargreave did with collateral. A mill meeting. Hidden soldiers on both sides—she'd bet her breviary on it; Hargreave had never met a negotiation he hadn't tried to convert into an ambush. And Ivo Reyes, declared traitor to both armies at once, walking into the space between them carrying the one object that could hang every person in this story.

She should feel terror. She examined herself and found, instead, under the fear, something worse and better: certainty. Because she had heard the promise too, three years ago, that drunken soft night on Bess's steps—Ivo's voice slurred and deadly serious, *if they ever call me enemy, hear me out first*—and she had watched Wendell keep it, and she had warned Wendell that love made terrible witnesses, and now love was proposing an armed exchange at a ruined mill and Odile was in a cellar unable to lift a finger except—

Except that prayer lifted fingers. Except that a chaplain in custody was not a silent woman; she was a woman with one instrument left, and it was the pulpit, and they could not stop her mouth on Sunday without confirming everything they claimed not to believe.

The guards came for the tray. One of them, the younger private, the one who still couldn't meet her eyes, hesitated at the door.

"Chaplain," he said, awkward, official. "Sunday services. The Colonel says—says you'll be permitted to preach. Under escort. He wants the garrison to see normal order maintained." A beat. "His orders."

"Thank you," Odile said gravely, as though this were generosity.

When the boots faded she bowed her head over the apple she did not eat, and began, silently, to compose the sermon—on names, on forgetting, on the God who counted sparrows and numbered hairs and wrote His faithful ones' names in a book that no colonel could requisition—and she prayed as she composed, for Ivo walking toward a mill full of hidden guns, for Wendell deciding God-knew-what behind house-arrest walls, for Bess driving marsh roads with a book of the dead under her seat boards, and for the boy Maret's age who had betrayed the chapel for love of his brother, because betrayal born of love was still love, and someone had to say so out loud before Sunday.

*Let me say only what is true,* she prayed, the old prayer, amended now, deepened. *And let what is true be a weapon.*

Outside, far off toward the river, she imagined she could hear the mill's broken wheel turning, though it had been dry for years.

Turn, she thought. Turn.
