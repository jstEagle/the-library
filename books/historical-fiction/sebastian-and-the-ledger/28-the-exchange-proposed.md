## 28. The Exchange Proposed

The delegation's quarters had been a wool merchant's house before the war, and it still smelled faintly of lanolin and old money, as if the building itself could not quite believe what was happening inside it. Ivo Reyes sat in the merchant's former study, beneath a portrait of a man who had grown rich selling to both sides of the conflict, and waited for his handler the way a man waits for a surgeon: knowing exactly how much it will hurt, and going anyway.

The lamp guttered. Outside, Marrowfield's occupied streets were quiet in the way of places that have learned not to make noise after dark. Somewhere beyond the rooftops was the garrison hospital, and somewhere in that hospital — or the chapel, or Bess's kitchen, or God knew where — was a ledger with forty-one names in Wendell Almeida's careful hand. Forty-one men the reports said had never died.

And Odile Marchand was in Colonel Hargreave's cells.

Reyes had known for two days. The news had come to him sideways, as all true news came in an occupied town — a laundry list with a certain crease in it, a phrase about *the chaplain who would not confirm*, passed from a laundress to a corporal's wife to a clerk who owed Reyes a small kindness and could be paid with a smaller one. He had read the words by candlelight and then sat very still for a long time, because Odile, who had once talked a drunken sergeant out of hanging himself with his own belt, who had buried more soldiers than any bishop in the province and remembered every face, was sitting in a stone room refusing to say yes or no to questions about a book she might not even know the location of.

*She doesn't know where it is,* he thought. *Wendell moved it without telling her. That's why they can't break her. You cannot surrender what you never held.*

It should have comforted him. Instead it made the arithmetic worse, because Hargreave was not a man who tolerated a locked door. He would simply begin removing things from behind it until the door stood open, and the first thing he would remove would be Odile.

The knock came at ten minutes past eleven — three soft raps, a pause, two more. Voss entered without waiting, which was its own message. Major Edric Voss had been Reyes's handler since the second month of his double life, a narrow man with a narrow face and the patient eyes of someone who kept ledgers of his own, though his entries were made in favors rather than ink. He wore civilian wool tonight, grey on grey, and he closed the study door with the delicacy of a man entering a sickroom.

"You sent for me," Voss said. "That's unusual. Handlers summon; assets request." He settled into the chair opposite without being asked, folding his long hands over his knee. "So either you've finally decided to betray us, or something has frightened you badly enough that pride stopped mattering. I confess I've wondered which it would be."

"Both," Reyes said. "In a sense."

Voss's eyebrows rose a fraction. He did not speak. He never wasted silence; he let other men fill it, and Reyes had learned years ago to refuse the gift.

"Odile Marchand is in Hargreave's custody," Reyes said instead. "Arrested two nights ago when a search of the garrison chapel turned up nothing. She's charged with concealment of seditious documents. If she doesn't confirm anything, she'll be held indefinitely. If she does—" he let it hang "—she'll be shot. Either way, Hargreave wins unless someone changes the shape of the board."

"And you propose to be the someone." Voss's voice was mild. Too mild. "You realize, Captain, that your concern for a garrison chaplain in enemy custody is precisely the sort of thing my superiors would find interesting. A liaison officer agitating himself over prisoners. It suggests attachments. Attachments suggest divided loyalties. Divided loyalties suggest—"

"I know what they suggest," Reyes said. "I'm the one who taught you the word *collaborator*. You've been writing it next to my name for three years whenever you needed leverage." He leaned forward. "But hear me out, because this isn't sentiment. This is the armistice. And you want the ledger even more than I do."

Something shifted in the room then, subtle as a change in air pressure. Voss went very still — the stillness of a man whose cards have been glimpsed.

"Go on," he said.

"The talks are dying," Reyes said. "Don't pretend otherwise; I sit at the edge of that table every day and watch the numbers kill each other. Your delegation says their dead number such-and-such. Hargreave's reports say less. Both sides accuse the other of inflating losses to win concessions. And underneath all of it there is one book that contains the actual count — the count Hargreave falsified, the count your own people suspect exists, because half the deserters who crossed your lines last autumn spoke of a nurse who wrote down names." He watched Voss's face, reading it the way he'd learned to read faces across interrogation tables. "Whoever holds that ledger holds the truth of this war's cost. At a peace table, truth is ammunition. You know it. Your superiors know it. Why else has your intelligence service spent eight months trying to confirm its existence?"

Voss unfolded his hands, refolded them. When he spoke, his voice had dropped its performance entirely, and that was somehow more frightening than anything he'd said before. "Suppose I grant that we're interested. What is it you're proposing?"

"A trade. Publicly brokered. The ledger, delivered into the hands of both delegations simultaneously — read into the record of the negotiations, every name, every figure, so that neither side can bury it afterward — in exchange for Odile Marchand's release and the resumption of talks on honest figures." Reyes kept his voice level, but beneath it his heart was hammering like a fist on a door. *Say it plainly. Give him nothing to misread.* "Hargreave gets exposed, yes. That's the price of doing business with him, and your people have always known he was rotten; they simply found him useful. The chaplain walks free. The numbers get corrected. The armistice stops drowning in disputed arithmetic and actually happens."

"And you'd arrange delivery of this book yourself." Voss tilted his head. "Through your contacts among the infirmary staff. Through Nurse Almeida."

"Through channels I control, yes."

"Which means you'd be placing yourself at the center of the exchange. The one man both sides could shoot with a clear conscience." Voss smiled thinly. "You understand that I have to point that out. If you die mid-delivery, we lose the asset and possibly the book."

"If I die mid-delivery, the book still gets delivered — because I won't be the only one carrying it. But I'd rather not die, and I'd rather you didn't treat my death as a line item." Reyes rose and walked to the window, needing the movement, needing to look at something that wasn't Voss's patient face. Below, a sentry paced the wall of the requisitioned granary, breath steaming in the cold. "Major. Ask yourself why I'm really here. I could have run with what I know. I could have sold the ledger's location to Hargreave and bought my life back twice over. Instead I'm handing you the single most valuable document on this continent and asking, in return, for one old woman and a ceasefire. Weigh that."

"I am weighing it," Voss said quietly. "That's what frightens you, isn't it? Not whether I believe you. Whether I believe you *completely*."

Reyes didn't answer. Because it was true, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third man at the table.

Because here was the thing Reyes had not said, the thing coiled tight under his ribs where even honesty couldn't reach it: Voss believed him *too* well. Believed in the ledger, believed in the trade, believed with an eagerness that sat wrong against everything Reyes knew of his handler's habitual caution. Three years of double life had taught Reyes to smell a trap, and what he smelled now wasn't falsehood — Voss's interest was real — but *appetite*. An appetite that had been waiting, perhaps, for Reyes to walk into this study and hand it a reason to exist.

They wanted the book. Of course they wanted the book. And the cleanest way to take a book from a man who guards it well is to authorize him to carry it somewhere.

*You see the trap,* Reyes told himself, standing at the dark window while Voss watched him with those patient eyes. *You see it plain as a snare-wire across a game trail. And you're stepping into it anyway, because Odile is in a cell and the talks are dying and Wendell trusted you with a promise made over bad wine three years ago.*

*Walk in with your eyes open. That's all courage ever was.*

"I'll need authorization in writing," Reyes said, turning back. "Terms of the exchange. Safe passage for myself, the courier, and the document. Marchand released unharmed within twenty-four hours of confirmed receipt. Talks resumed within the week on corrected figures. Signed by someone with rank enough to make it stick — not you. You don't have the authority to guarantee any of this, and we both know it."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "I don't. Which is why I'll need to take your proposal up the chain. Give me until tomorrow night." He rose, smoothing his grey coat, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch. For a moment something almost human moved behind his eyes — or something wearing humanity's coat.

"For what it's worth, Captain," Voss said, "when this is done, whatever way it's done, you may be the only man in this war who ends it holding something worth having. Whether anyone lets you keep it is another matter entirely."

He left. The latch clicked. Reyes stood alone under the dead merchant's portrait and listened to his own heartbeat slow by degrees.

*Authorized,* he thought. *By this time tomorrow, authorized. Every clause signed, every promise made in ink that smells of lamp-oil and lies.*

And somewhere across the sleeping town, in a cell that smelled of straw and stone, Odile Marchand was keeping her silence like a held note, and Wendell Almeida was lying awake deciding whom to trust, and Bess was probably awake too, kneading something, plotting something, turning flour and fury into a plan.

Reyes blew out the lamp and let the dark come.

*One day,* he thought. *One day to move the book, warn them all, and walk into the mill with my eyes open.*

He had promised Wendell they would hear him out. Now he would ask them to hear him out one final time — and pray that love, which had made such terrible witnesses, could make, just once, a terrible good conspirator.
