## 29. Wendell's Decision

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow by the time Wendell finished counting.

Not money — they had never had money worth counting — but names. Two hundred and eleven of them, entered in a hand that had grown steadier over five years, in ink that had gone from shop-bought to oak-gall brewed on Bess's stove when the requisitions cut off everything else. Wendell turned the pages slowly, the way one turns the pages of a psalter, and let the ledger do what it always did: remind them why it existed at all.

*Alcott, Merrin. Fever, third week of March. Not listed.*

*Brannock, Ede. Shell fragments, both legs. Died before transport. Not listed.*

*Carrow, the boy called Sparrow because no one could ever learn his true name. Not listed.*

Two hundred and eleven lines. Two hundred and eleven small rebellions against the army's arithmetic, which held that a death unreported was a death that had never happened, and a war without deaths was a war going well.

Wendell closed the book and pressed both palms flat against its cover, feeling the leather's warmth as though it were a living thing, which in every way that mattered it was.

Reyes's note lay beside it, delivered inside a loaf two nights past, Bess's flour still ghosting the paper's edge. *The mill on the Alder road, Thursday, after moonset. Bring what we both know you have. Ivo.* Fourteen words, and beneath them, in a smaller hand, almost an afterthought: *You promised to hear me out. This is me asking.*

The promise. Three years old now, made over cheap wine while Reyes sat with his boots on the kitchen bench and his laugh too loud for the hour, asking Wendell for the one thing a soldier is never given: the benefit of the doubt. *If they ever say I've turned, hear me first. Before the report. Before anyone.* And Wendell had promised, because it was Reyes asking, because the kitchen had been warm, because promises made in peacetime are always cheaper than the ones collected in war.

Except this promise had come due with interest.

Wendell rose and went to the window. The street below was empty in the way occupied streets are empty — not vacant but *watched*, every shuttered window an eye, every length of shadow a place where a patrol might stand. Somewhere down the lane a boot scraped stone, paused, moved on. Marrowfield had learned to breathe quietly.

Thursday was tomorrow.

They had been turning the question since the note arrived, turning it the way Odile turns a psalm, looking for the angle where it stops hurting. It hadn't come. The question had only two faces, and both of them looked like grief.

If Wendell kept the ledger safe — buried again beneath the floorboards, or ferried out through the marsh to some hiding place only the family knew — then the book survived. The names survived. But Reyes would walk into that mill alone, carrying nothing but his word, and if his word was not enough — if his handlers had sent him as bait, if Hargreave had gotten to him, if any of the hundred betrayals the war had taught them all to expect had finally found its mark — then Reyes would die at that mill having never been heard out. And Wendell would have broken the promise not from malice or fear but from arithmetic, which is how most promises die.

And if Wendell brought the ledger — carried it out of the town, across open country, into a meeting where two delegations circled each other like wolves and Hargreave's soldiers were certainly already positioned in the treeline — then the book might be seized. Burned. Lost to a colonel who needed its last page destroyed more than he needed his next breath. Two hundred and eleven names reduced to ash, and every family who would have wept over their line of ink left instead with the army's version: that their dead had never existed at all.

Safe book, broken promise. Kept promise, lost book.

Wendell had lain awake three nights discovering that these were false choices, and it was Tam — of all people, Tam, released from the cells with his hands still trembling and a silence behind his eyes that might never fully lift — who had shown them the flaw.

He had come to the infirmary that morning, ostensibly to check on the fever cases, actually because he could no longer bear the idleness of convalescence. He had stood at the foot of a patient's bed changing a dressing with his old competence, and when Wendell came near he had said, without looking up, without pausing:

"You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Making it yours alone." He tied off the bandage, neat as a knot in a shoelace. "Bess drove a cart through a checkpoint last week with the ledger under the turnips and her heart going like a drum, and she never told you until it was done. Odile lied to a colonel's face and hid your book in a box of saints' bones. I spent six days in a cell and gave them nothing but my name and my mother's recipe for barley soup, which they didn't ask for anyway." He set down the basin and finally met Wendell's eyes, and there was something new in his face — not the boy who had been caught moving a bundle through occupied streets, but someone the cell had forged. "Whatever you're deciding, you keep deciding it like you're the only one in the room. You're not. You never were."

Then he had picked up his basin and gone on to the next bed, and Wendell had stood in the middle of the ward feeling the truth of it land like a dropped weight.

That was the flaw in the choice. Wendell had been weighing the promise against the safety of the ledger as though those were the only two things on the scale, as though the family were ballast rather than hands. But Bess had smuggled once already. Odile had lied once already. Tam had endured once already. The found family was not an audience for Wendell's sacrifice; it was a machine, built over years of suppers and shared grief, for exactly this kind of work.

Which meant there might be a third option. Which meant the question was no longer *which thing will I lose* but *what am I willing to risk, and who is willing to risk it with me.*

* * *

They gathered that evening in Bess's kitchen, which had become, by unspoken accord, the war room of the overlooked. The shutters were closed. A kettle sang on the stove because Bess insisted that no conspiracy should be conducted without tea — "It steadies the hands and gives everyone something to do besides panic" — and the four of them bent over the table like women and men bending over a wounded man, which in a sense they were.

"Tell it plain," Bess said. She had her arms folded, forearms floured to the elbow, and she looked less like a cook than like a general who happened to know forty ways with potatoes.

So Wendell told it plain. The mill on the Alder road, after moonset tomorrow. Reyes's request. The probability — Wendell refused to soften it — that the meeting was watched from both sides, that Hargreave knew or would soon know of it, that whatever happened in that mill, soldiers would be within a whistle's reach of it. And the ledger, and the promise, laid out on the table between them like the two halves of a broken plate.

"You can't take it," Odile said. Not harshly. She said it the way she said hard things in sermons: gently, so the hardness arrived alone, without company. "If Hargreave takes that book, two hundred and eleven families never learn. If he burns it, they never learn *and* he sleeps soundly. That book has done its work in hiding, Wendell. Let it go on hiding."

"And Reyes?"

Odile's mouth tightened. She had never forgiven Reyes for coming back in the wrong uniform, and Wendell suspected part of her never would, because forgiveness would require believing him, and belief was the one currency her faith did not print freely. "Reyes made his choices."

"He asked to be heard out. Three years ago, before any of this. He asked *me*, and I said yes." Wendell heard their own voice go quiet, which was how they knew they had reached the bottom of themselves. "I have kept every name in that book because the officers wouldn't. How is this different? How can I write down that every forgotten person deserves remembering, and then forget the one promise anyone ever asked of me?"

The kettle sang. Nobody moved to pour.

It was Tam who spoke. "Both, then."

Everyone looked at him.

"Take the book and keep the promise. They're not the same errand, that's all." He leaned forward, and Wendell saw that he had been thinking about this for days, turning it over in whatever dark room the cells had left in him. "Hargreave expects you to choose. He's built his whole trap around you choosing — bring the book and he seizes it, leave it and Reyes hangs alone. So don't choose. Bring the book, yes, but don't bring it *to the mill*. Bring it near the mill. And have a way for it to be gone before anyone whistles."

"The cart," Bess said.

"The cart," Tam agreed.

And there it was, opening up between them like a door: Bess had driven the marsh road a dozen times in the past month, hauling turnips and grievances past checkpoints staffed by boys who saw a farmwife and stopped looking. She knew which ditches held water and which held shadows. If the ledger traveled separately — if it went out ahead of Wendell, hidden in the false bottom Bess had built for cheese, and waited somewhere along the Alder road within reach of a running man — then Wendell could walk into that mill with empty hands and a full promise. Reyes would be heard out. The book would be beyond seizure. And if everything went wrong — Wendell made themselves look at this squarely, because resolve that doesn't look at failure is just hope wearing armor — if everything went wrong, the book would survive even if none of them did.

"It's still a gamble," Odile said.

"Everything since the requisition is a gamble," Bess said. "The only question is whether we gamble scared or gamble together." She unfolded her arms and planted her fists on the table. "I'll drive. I've driven past those boys so many times now they wave at me. One more trip."

"If they search the cart properly—"

"They searched it properly the first time and found turnips. They haven't searched properly since. Soldiers get lazy with grandmothers; it's the one mercy the war allows us." But her voice roughened on the last words, and Wendell understood that Bess was afraid — had been afraid every single time, and drove anyway, and would drive tomorrow, because that was who she had decided to be somewhere in the years before any of them had needed her to decide it.

Odile was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, it was to Wendell, and her voice had changed — softened into something that was almost, though she would never name it, a blessing. "You understand what you're risking. Not just the book. Yourself. If Hargreave takes you at that mill, there may be no exchange, no bargain, no clever cart. You may simply be gone."

"I know."

"And you go anyway."

"I go anyway." Wendell looked around the table — Bess with her floured forearms, Tam with his cell-forged stillness, Odile with her cracked-open conscience — and felt something settle in their chest that was not peace exactly, but was its cousin: the certainty of a decision finally made after long wandering. "That book began because I couldn't stand watching people be erased. I'm not going to end it by erasing myself from the story. Whatever happens at that mill, I want to be the one who chose it. All of it. The promise and the book and the risk. I want to walk in there knowing exactly who I am."

"And who is that?" Odile asked, softly, the way she asked questions in confession.

Wendell considered. Five years ago they would have answered *a nurse*, and meant it as a wall. Now—

"Someone who keeps what matters," they said. "Names. Promises. People."

* * *

Preparations took the rest of the evening and none of it felt like enough.

Bess rebuilt the cart's false floor with fresh slats and fresh nails, working by feel in the dark of her pantry, swearing gently at splinters. Tam walked the route to the Alder road twice and returned with a map drawn in his head and transferred to the back of a laundry list: the ford, the fallen willow, the stretch where the road ran high enough to see the mill from half a mile off. Odile sat apart with her breviary and did not pray so much as rehearse, Wendell thought — rehearsing whatever she would need to say or not say if dawn came and the family did not return.

And Wendell packed.

There was little to pack. The ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into its parcel with waxed thread. A change of shirt, because Reyes would laugh otherwise — *five years a nurse and you still can't travel light* — and because laughter might matter tomorrow more than ammunition. And at the bottom, folded small, the page Wendell had copied out and nearly burned a dozen times: the final entry, the name that could end the war or condemn everyone they loved, transcribed in case the original was taken. Insurance. Or treason, depending on whose court weighed it. Wendell no longer believed those were different things.

Near midnight, Wendell sat alone with the ledger one last time before it went into the cart, and read the last page again. Not the dangerous name — Wendell knew that name by heart now, had dreamed it, had woken with it on their tongue tasting of smoke — but the space below it. The blank lines waiting. For five years the ledger had been a record of the erased, and Wendell had assumed it would end when the war ended, whenever that merciful hour came.

But ledgers, like promises, don't close themselves.

Wendell thought about the last line. About what it should say, someday, when there was a last line. Not yet. But soon. And it would not be a name struck from a report; it would be a name chosen, claimed, written down at last by the one person with the right to write it.

"That's who I am," Wendell murmured to the empty room, to the book, to whoever might read it in fifty years. "I'll tell you when it's over."

They closed the cover and carried it down to Bess.

* * *

Moonset was at half past two. At half past one, the cart stood loaded in Bess's yard, and the family stood around it in the cold, saying the goodbyes that none of them would say aloud.

Bess embraced Wendell fiercely enough to crack a rib, or so she claimed afterward through her own tears. Tam pressed something into Wendell's hand — a spare dressing kit, rolled tight, soldier-practical — and said only, "Come back and I'll teach you to tie off a bandage properly." Odile took Wendell's face in both hands, thumbs at the cheekbones, and gave the blessing she used over the dying, which alarmed them both until Wendell understood: it was not a blessing for the dying. It was her strongest one. She simply did not believe in spending less than her strongest on anything.

"For all the names," she said.

"For all the names."

Bess climbed onto the cart. The ledger rode beneath the turnips, above the false floor, in the dark Wendell had trusted to no one else on earth — and found, standing in the yard watching the wheels begin to turn, that trusting was the whole of it. Not the plan. Plans failed. Trust was the thing itself: handing two hundred and eleven souls to a woman with a cart and watching her drive into the dark without running after.

The cart vanished down the lane. Wendell stood a moment longer in the cold, listening to the wheels fade, feeling the absence of the book against their chest like the ache of a missing tooth.

Then they turned toward the Alder road, and began to walk.

Empty-handed. Full of promise. The mill waited three miles east, and the moon was going down, and somewhere ahead of them — alive or not, honest or not, theirs or not — Reyes was walking toward the same hour from the other side.

Wendell quickened their pace. There was just time to arrive before the dark lifted, and everything after that would belong to the morning.
