## 30. The Mill Meeting

### Wendell

The mill had been dead for six years, but it still smelled of its working life — rotting grain and river damp and the ghost of oil in the great wooden gears that stood frozen mid-turn like the ribs of some beached leviathan. Wendell came to it through the marsh path, the ledger bound against their chest beneath a nurse's coat two sizes too large, and felt every step of the last month in their knees.

Bess had packed bread that morning as though a loaf could hold a prayer. She'd pressed it into Wendell's hands without meeting their eyes, because if she looked at them she would say something true, and true things were dangerous before dawn. *Come back for supper*, was all she'd allowed herself. As though supper were a country they might still reach.

*You come back too*, Wendell had said.

She'd snorted. "I've buried better men than whoever's waiting in that mill." Then, quieter, her flour-white hands gripping Wendell's wrists: "Both of you. I'm not burying either of you."

Now the mill rose out of the fog, and Wendell counted windows the way Odile counted beads — three broken on the north face, one boarded, one dark and whole. The dark whole window was where Reyes would be. He had said so in the message baked into Thursday's loaf: *the window that still holds glass, because I want to see you coming.*

Tam should not have come. Tam, whose shoulder still carried Hargreave's bullet, who walked with a stick and a set jaw, had insisted on taking the high ground above the mill road with Bess's field glasses and a whistle. "You'll need someone watching your back who isn't in love with you," he'd said, and Wendell had not had the strength to argue, only enough to make him promise — *promise, Tam* — that he would run at the first wrong thing. They both knew the promise was worth nothing. The Almeida family had never once kept the sensible promise.

Wendell stopped at the edge of the yard. Fog lay over the millpond like a sheet drawn up to the chin. Somewhere in it were soldiers — Reyes had warned them of that much, in the flat careful language of a man writing under his handler's eye: *there will be an escort. Do not come unarmed with trust.* And somewhere else, presumably, were Reyes's own delegation's soldiers, and somewhere else again — Wendell's stomach turned — Hargreave's, because a man like Hargreave did not let an exchange happen in his own garrison's shadow without wanting his hands on it.

Three sets of hidden guns, then. One book.

"Almeida."

The voice came from the mill doorway, and it was worse than any ambush, because it was warm. Ivo Reyes stood in the frame with fog behind him and lamplight from within gilding one side of his face, and he looked — God help them all — *well*. Fed. Rested. An officer's coat that fit him, in a uniform Wendell had spent five years learning to hate.

"You look terrible," he said.

"You look like a poster," Wendell said, and heard their own voice crack down the middle, and hated that too.

He crossed the yard in eight strides — Wendell counted, because counting was what nurses did instead of weeping — and stopped just short of touching. That restraint was its own confession. Five years ago he had hugged like a man trying to fold people into himself. Now he stood with his hands open at his sides, palms out, as if to show they held nothing.

"I know how this looks," he said.

"It looks like you're wearing their buttons."

"I am wearing their buttons." A breath. "And underneath them I'm wearing everything you gave me, so don't—"

"Don't what?" Wendell stepped closer, close enough to smell river water and gun oil and, beneath it, impossibly, the tallow soap of the infirmary, as if he still used it, as if he'd carried bars of it across a war. "Don't believe the notices? Odile says love makes terrible witnesses. She says I'll hear what I need to hear."

"Odile," Reyes said softly, "is the reason I'm alive. Did she tell you that? Autumn of '19, the retreat at Corvel. A chaplain dragged a half-dead lieutenant out of a ditch because the ledger said he existed when the army said he didn't." His eyes went bright and fierce. "You wrote my name, Wendell. When everyone else struck it. So no — don't believe the notices. Believe the ledger."

The ledger burned against Wendell's chest like a second heart.

"Then let's do what we came to do," Wendell said, "before anyone's aim improves."

### Reyes

Reyes had planned this hour forty times and it had never once gone like this. In the plans there was procedure: the book produced, inspected, countersigned; the prisoner released; lines spoken about the armistice that would sound, later, in reports. In the plans he did not stand four feet from Wendell Almeida wanting, absurdly, to ask about the hens. Whether Bess still named them after generals. Whether the big table still wobbled on a folded hymnal.

In the plans he also did not have Maren's signal burning in his pocket — the folded paper his handler had given him that morning with two words on it: *proceed, prove.* Meaning: deliver the ledger or die proving you could have. Meaning: we will have rifles on that mill whether or not you walk out.

And meaning, thirdly, which Reyes kept in a locked room behind his eyes: whatever happened here, Ivo Reyes was now a traitor to somebody. The only question worth asking was *to whom*, and he had answered it three weeks ago in a marsh at midnight when Wendell had said his name like a diagnosis.

"The exchange is simple," he said aloud, because procedure was a raft and he meant to cling to it. "The ledger comes into my custody. Odile walks out of Hargreave's cells by nightfall — my delegation has the leverage for it, the trade papers are signed. You come with me to the delegation's line, under my protection, until the figures are read into the record. After that—" he faltered. After that was the tribunal, the tribunal that was supposed to save him, the record that was supposed to defend him, and none of it felt real next to the wobbling hymnal-table and the fog.

"After that," Wendell said steadily, "you stop lying to me in a mill doorway."

"I have never lied to you."

"You told me once that peacetime would feel strange. You said we'd all get fat and bored and happy." Wendell's mouth trembled around something that wasn't quite a smile. "That was a lie, Ivo. It was a beautiful lie and I've kept it five years like a pressed flower, and I want it back."

He had no answer for that. He took out the trade papers instead — the signed instrument, the enemy seal, the thing his handler believed was his loyalty made legible — and held them out.

Wendell didn't take them. Wendell reached into the oversized coat and drew out the ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the green string Bess used for the herb bundles, and Reyes felt the sight of it go through him like cold water — because it was smaller than its reputation. Smaller than the war it had been quietly undoing. Five years of names in a hand he knew, in ink that had faded to brown, and the last page unopened since the night Wendell had read it alone and gone white as the fog.

"Conditions," Wendell said, in the voice they used for surgeons who wanted to cut without explaining. "One: I keep the ledger until Odile is physically standing in front of me. Not papers. Her."

"That's not—"

"Two." Wendell's voice didn't rise. It got flatter, which was worse. "When it goes to the table, it goes whole. Every page. Whatever's on the last page gets read out loud with all the rest. If you or your handlers plan to lose that page between here and the armistice tent, say so now, and I will turn around and walk back into that marsh, and you can explain to both armies why their peace drowned."

Reyes looked at the ledger. At the person holding it. At the dark whole window above them, where he knew Voss's marksmen were settling in, and thought about the other windows he couldn't see, and about Hargreave, who had ordered a chapel searched at dawn and found nothing and arrested a chaplain out of pure spite, and who would not have stayed home tonight.

"Wendell," he said carefully, "what's on the last page?"

Something moved behind their eyes — grief and purpose welded together, load-bearing.

"A name," they said. "A name that ends this, or a name that ends us. Possibly both. I won't know which until it's said out loud in front of witnesses who can't unsay it."

"And you're willing to carry that into a room full of rifles."

"I carried five hundred others into rooms full of rifles," Wendell said. "Every time I wrote one down, knowing what the officers did to people who kept records. This one's no heavier. It's just mine now."

Reyes took out his watch — habit, cover, a way to keep his hands busy — and in the act of closing it saw, across the yard, past Wendell's shoulder, the thing he had been dreading since midnight.

A lantern. Brief, hooded, moving along the millrace embankment where no one in either delegation was posted. Then gone.

His own escort was at the north wall. Maren's men were in the trees south of the pond. Which meant the lantern belonged to a third party, and there was only one third party left.

"Wendell," he said, very quietly, very evenly, the voice he'd used over dying men because panic is contagious. "Take my arm. We're going inside. Now."

"What did you see?"

"A light where nobody should be." He was already turning them toward the doorway, already doing the arithmetic he hated — Tam on the ridge, Bess with the cart in the alders, the marsh road the only way out and it ran straight past the embankment. "How many people know you came here tonight?"

"Four," Wendell said. Then, with the particular stillness of a person arriving somewhere terrible: "Ivo. Four *trusted* people. But Hargreave knows the exchange terms — Odile's release papers went through his office."

"Yes."

"Who countersigned them?"

Reyes closed his eyes.

Inside the mill, dust hung in the shafts of grey light like the building was exhaving its last breath. Somewhere overhead, in the loft where the grain had once poured down through wooden chutes, a board creaked under a weight that was not the wind.

And out on the millpond, the fog began to move against the wind — parting, folding back — the way fog does when a long line of men stands up out of it.

"Hargreave," Reyes breathed, and pulled his pistol, and the first shot cracked across the water before either of them could reach the door.
