## 31. Ambush

Colonel Edmund Hargreave had learned long ago that the truth of any battle was decided before the first shot was fired — in the choosing of the ground, in the counting of men, in the patience to let your enemy walk into the shape you had drawn for them. He had been a young subaltern at Callow Ford when he first understood this, watching a company of irregulars march confidently into a river valley that his own engineers had spent three weeks preparing for them, and he had felt then — as he felt now, standing in the cold shadow of the derelict mill on the Vane Road — that peculiar and shameful pleasure of watching fate unfold exactly according to one's own handwriting.

The mill had not ground flour in nine years. Its wheel stood frozen in the race, furred with moss, and its great timber frame leaned against the prevailing wind like an old drunk leaning on a wall. Through the shattered clerestory the morning light fell in dusty ladders, striping the floor where grain had once been measured out in honest hundredweights. Hargreave found the place fitting. Everything about this affair — the ledger, the nurse, the traitor Reyes — was a question of measurement, of accounts kept and accounts falsified, and here they would all be settled in a building built for weighing.

He stood at the north window with Major Quill at his elbow and swept his glass across the approach road one more time.

"They'll come from the east," Quill said. "Reyes's message said half past eight."

"Reyes's message said what we wanted it to say," Hargreave murmured. "That is rather the point of messages."

It had taken four days to arrange. Four days of careful, patient work conducted through channels so indirect that no single clerk held the whole of it in his head: a forged order passed through a quartermaster who believed he was serving the delegation; a reply intercepted; a counter-reply planted. Captain Ivo Reyes believed he was meeting Wendell Almeida at the mill to trade the ledger for the chaplain's release and a corridor through the lines. In truth, Hargreave had known of the meeting almost before Reyes had proposed it — because Hargreave had written it into being, feeding the arrangement through the very courier network the cook woman thought she had built inside her kitchen.

That had been the most instructive discovery of the entire miserable season. Bread loaves. Messages folded in waxed paper baked into the crumb, carried by farm carts along the marsh road. He had almost admired it. A colonel learns to admire competence even in vermin; it is a professional courtesy.

And now every thread of it ended here, in a ruin, on a grey morning in the last week of a war everyone was tired of winning.

"Positions," Hargreave said quietly.

Quill lifted two fingers. Below, among the fallen timbers and the rusted gearing, twenty men adjusted themselves by inches. Six in the mill itself, behind the grain bins and the collapsed hoist. Eight in the drainage cut along the south side, prone under sacking. The rest in the copse beyond the weir, covering the road. Two snipers in the broken kiln with clean sightlines to both doors. No banners. No colours. If it went wrong, these were partisans, irregulars, deniable men — though Hargreave did not intend it to go wrong.

There was one more element, and it was the element that made the whole design elegant rather than merely brutal. Among the six inside the mill, dressed in a dead soldier's greatcoat with his collar turned up, was Corporal Fenwick — formerly of the enemy delegation's escort detail, lately persuaded by circumstances involving a gambling debt and a sister in Hargreave's custody — whose task was simple: stand where Reyes could see him wearing the wrong uniform at the wrong moment, and let the man hang himself. When the delegations later asked how their liaison captain had died, the answer would be plain. Traitor to both sides. Caught in the act of selling military intelligence — casualty figures, no less — to a hostile agent. Shot resisting arrest.

Hargreave had rehearsed the report in his head during the ride out. *Captain Reyes was observed passing documents to an unidentified party at an abandoned mill. On approach, he fired first. He was killed in the exchange. The documents were recovered.* Clean. Final. And the ledger — whatever the ledger actually contained, whatever name it held that made him wake some mornings with his heart going like a drummer boy's — the ledger would be back in his hands, and then in his stove, and then in ash, and then nowhere.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing that had eaten him alive since the clerk Prewitt had come pale-faced into his office with the interrogation transcripts. Five years of names. Names his officers had struck from the reports at his instruction — struck for morale, struck for efficiency, struck because a war cannot be run on grief — and one name above all others, one name that sat somewhere in that book like a round in a chamber.

*Thomas.*

His son had died fourteen months ago at the retreat from Harrow Beck, in a dressing station not unlike the one at Marrowfield, of a wound that should not have killed him, in circumstances the official record described with a delicacy that amounted to a lie. Hargreave had signed the falsification himself. He told himself — he had told himself every day since — that it was mercy. That Edith need never know her boy had lingered three days calling for a mother who had been refused passage to him. That the omission was a kindness performed by a father who happened also to be an officer.

But a kindness hidden in a ledger is still evidence. And if Almeida read that page aloud — if the name entered the armistice record, if Edith learned that Thomas had died badly and alone while his father burned the paperwork of other mothers' sons — then everything Hargreave had built, everything he had protected, would come down around his ears not with a shell-burst but with a whisper.

So: the mill. The trap. The fire.

"Horses at the crossroads," Quill reported. "Two riders on the marsh road. It's them."

Hargreave raised his glass. The mist was lifting off the millpond in rags, and through it came two figures — small at this distance, moving carefully, leading a cart horse. One tall and slight, wrapped in a nurse's grey cloak, carrying a leather satchel slung across the body like a rifle. The other broader, limping slightly, with the set of the shoulders that Hargreave recognized from the infirmary surveillance reports. Tam Weller. The orderly. The one who had sat in a cell for nine days saying nothing but *I'm sorry* to every question put to him, until Hargreave had released him precisely because a released man can be followed.

They had brought the orderly. Sentiment, or insurance, or both. Hargreave felt something that was not quite pity move in him and die quickly.

"And the third?" he asked.

"Not yet visible, sir."

"He'll be there. Men like Reyes always arrive separately. It lets them pretend they might still run." He lowered the glass. "Wait for the exchange. Wait until the book changes hands. Not one shot before. Is that understood at every position?"

"It is, sir."

Because that was the whole art of it. A ledger seized from a living hand is testimony; a ledger recovered from a corpse is salvage. Let them meet. Let the nurse hand the book over — Hargreave needed to see with his own eyes which satchel, which pocket, which fold of clothing held five years of his sins. Let Reyes take it. And then, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, end both of them and take it back.

* * *

From inside the mill, wedged behind a bin of spoiled barley, Hargreave watched them enter.

It was strange — he had imagined Wendell Almeida so often these past weeks that seeing the actual person produced a jolt, like hearing one's own voice played back. Smaller than expected. Younger. With the unhurried economy of someone accustomed to walking through rooms full of dying men without hurrying or slowing. The nurse crossed the mill floor, boots grinding on grit and old chaff, and stopped in the shaft of light beneath the broken hoist, and looked around the ruin with an expression Hargreave could not immediately classify — until he realized it was the expression of a person taking attendance. Counting heads. The habit of triage applied to architecture.

Almeida saw Fenwick in his turned-up collar by the far wall and dismissed him in a glance, the way one dismisses furniture. Then the satchel came off the shoulder, and Hargreave's breath went thin.

"Now?" Quill breathed beside him.

"No." His own voice surprised him with its steadiness. "Wait."

Footsteps outside. A whistle — low, three notes, answered from the trees. And then Captain Ivo Reyes came through the eastern door with his hands visibly empty and his coat open, the way a man comes when he wants to be searched and found clean, and Hargreave watched the two of them look at each other across forty feet of ruined floor and understood, with a clarity that felt like a slap, that he had misjudged the entire geometry of the morning.

Because they were glad to see each other. It was in the way Almeida's chin lifted; in the way Reyes's stride broke for half a step, as if the sight of the nurse were a physical thing encountered at speed. Whatever else this was — treason, trade, desperation — there was something underneath it that no forged order had manufactured and no ambush could account for. Hargreave had planned for conspirators. He had not planned for whatever this was, and it unsettled him more than any mishap could have.

"The book," Reyes said. His voice carried flatly in the stone room.

"First tell me Odile is free."

"She walks out of Marrowfield tonight either way. That was the arrangement."

"That wasn't the arrangement. The arrangement was she walks out *today*, escorted, before I hand over anything."

"And I've honoured it. She left the chaplain's house at seven. There's a cart waiting at Tarn Bridge. You have my word."

"Your word." Something in Almeida's voice cracked — not with doubt, Hargreave thought, but with the effort of holding doubt and love in the same mouth. "You know what they'll do to you if this goes wrong. Both sides."

"Yes," Reyes said simply. "I've done the sums."

*Sums.* The word landed in Hargreave's chest like a dropped instrument. Everyone in this ruin, it seemed, was doing arithmetic. The whole war had narrowed to a book of numbers, and every soul chasing it was only trying to make the totals come out in favour of the people they loved.

Almeida opened the satchel.

Hargreave came up off his heel. "Now," he said, and stepped out of the shadow of the barley bin with his pistol drawn, and heard the world answer him.

It answered wrong.

Later — in the tribunal years, in the bad hours of the night — he would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and find they would never quite assemble. What he knew for certain was this: the six men inside the mill moved on his word, but the eight in the drainage cut moved early. Perhaps a nervous sergeant had mistaken the scrape of Hargreave's boot for a signal; perhaps the sacking had shifted and given away the line. What was certain was the sound — a shout from the south wall, then a shot, wild, cracking off the iron hoist-gear — and then the whole delicate machine of his design tore loose from his hand and began to grind forward under its own weight.

"Down!" Reyes had Almeida by the cloak, dragging the nurse behind the grain bin, and Hargreave's ordered shot — the one meant for the moment the ledger changed hands — went instead into the ceiling, into dust and pigeon-startle, and the mill became noise.

Men firing from cover at shadows of other men in cover. Fenwick, poor doomed Fenwick, standing up in his borrowed greatcoat and shouting words of surrender that nobody on either side was positioned to hear, and going down with a bullet through the throat from one of his own snipers in the kiln, who had seen a stranger's uniform rise armed from behind a rebel position and done precisely what he had been trained to do. The snipers' second volley stitched across the eastern door just as Reyes broke from cover toward it, and Hargreave saw the captain spin, shoulder hit, and go through the doorway into the light anyway, dragging Almeida with him.

"The satchel!" Hargreave roared. "The book! Nobody leaves with the—"

Then Tam Weller.

He had been at the edge of the mill floor all along, forgotten, a lame orderly with no weapon and no role in anyone's plan — Hargreave had genuinely forgotten him, had filed the man under *harmless* nine days ago in a cell and never re-filed him. Now the orderly came out of nowhere at a stumbling run, straight through the field of fire, arms wide, and Hargreave understood one half-second too late what the man was running *at*.

At the nurse. At the satchel. Between them and the doorway, between them and the bullets chewing splinters off the doorframe.

Weller took the first ball high in the chest and it spun him, and he took — God knew from where, from whom, the mill was all muzzle-flash and screaming now — a second lower, in the belly, and he did not fall so much as fold, catching Almeida around the legs with both arms like a man clinging to a mast in heavy weather, and the two of them went down together behind the doorpost as Reyes returned fire blind through the doorway and the world outside filled with hoofbeats — hoofbeats, *whose* hoofbeats —

A cart. A farm cart bursting out of the mist on the marsh road at a pace no farm cart had any business achieving, driven by a large woman in a shawl with a face like a hatchet, and behind her riders — irregulars, marsh men, the cook woman's network revealed all at once as something far more organized than bread loaves — and the arithmetic of the morning inverted itself with terrible speed.

"Fall back!" Quill was shouting. "Colonel, we are outnumbered on the south side, fall back to the copse!"

Hargreave did not move. He was looking at the doorpost, at the heap of grey cloak and bloodied wool, at the orderly's hand still knotted in the nurse's sleeve. Tam Weller had lain in a cell for nine days and said nothing. He had walked free for four and said nothing still. And now he lay in the chaff with two balls in him, having purchased with his body the three seconds his family needed to reach the cart.

*This,* Hargreave thought, with a lucidity that felt like drowning, *is what the book is. This is why it must burn.* Not paper. People. People who would do this, for each other, for names — and against such a thing what was a colonel with twenty men and a falsified conscience?

"Sir!"

He ran. He hated himself for it and he ran — out the western door, through the weir-spray, into the copse, while behind him the cart wheeled in the yard and hands pulled the wounded aboard, and above the ringing in his ears he heard the driver woman's voice, huge and furious and unmistakable:

"Leave him, leave him, we've got him — *drive*, girl, *drive!*"

The cart lurched onto the marsh road and was gone into the mist, and Hargreave stood among the birches with his pistol smoking in his hand and the ledger — the ledger — gone with it.

"Casualties?" he said. His voice came out of him like someone else's.

"Fenwick dead. Two wounded. The orderly — " Quill hesitated. "We don't know, sir. They took him."

They took him. Or he was already dead, and they took the body; either way the man was beyond questioning and beyond blame, and Hargreave found he could not decide which possibility he dreaded more.

Somewhere across the marsh, a church bell began to ring — the wrong pattern, urgent, arrhythmic, the alarm of a countryside waking. The mist was burning off. The morning was turning ordinary and bright, and somewhere under that bright sky a farm cart was jolting toward the deep water with five years of his crimes in its bed and his son's name sewn somewhere into its cargo like a bullet with his own hallmark on it.

"Colonel," Quill said quietly, "the armistice delegation will have heard the shooting. Every bird from here to the river heard it. By noon they'll know Reyes met an agent at this mill and that our troops were waiting."

Let them know. Let them choke on it. But they would also know the ledger survived, and that it was now in flight, in the open, carried by people with nothing left to lose — and a document in flight, Hargreave understood, is worth ten times a document in hiding, because it can arrive anywhere.

He looked at his pistol, and holstered it, and heard himself give the order that would finish him, though he did not yet know it:

"Ride ahead of them. Seal the marsh road at Tarn Bridge. And send word to my wife that I'll be home late."

The last was habit, nothing more — a sentence he had spoken a thousand times into a hundred couriers. But it came out of him strangely, in that smoking wood, over that abandoned mill, and Major Quill looked at him for a moment with something almost like fear before turning to obey.
