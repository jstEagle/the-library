## 32. Burning the Mill Road

Bess had driven carts through worse weather than this. She told herself that as she wrapped the reins twice around her fists and looked out at the rain coming down like the sky meant it personally.

She had driven through the sleet of '09 with a broken axle and a lame horse. Through fog so thick on the coast road she'd navigated by smell — salt, then pine, then the tar yards. Through the night her husband died, when she'd hauled his body home from the quarry in a cart bed lined with flour sacks because there was nothing else and no one else and the road was eleven miles long.

So she could do this. She was fifty-three years old, she had hands like tree roots, and she had spent thirty years feeding people who were frightened, which was its own kind of warfare, only quieter, and she was done being quiet.

The trouble was not the weather or the road. The trouble was what sat in the back of the cart under a tarpaulin, beneath a false floor of kindling, beneath a blanket that smelled of the infirmary's carbolic soap: a book weighing less than a loaf of bread, holding more dead men than her whole village held living ones. And beside it, hunched under the same tarpaulin as if hiding from God's own eye, Wendell Almeida, who had never in five years let Bess see them cry and whose face right now was doing something very close to it.

"You don't have to come," Bess said for the fourth time. "I can drive this alone."

"I know." Wendell's voice came out scraped raw. "That's why I'm coming. You shouldn't be alone either."

Bess snorted. Sentiment. She'd have to beat it out of them before it got someone killed. "Then listen to me, and don't argue, because I've buried one husband arguing about whether the road was safe and I won't bury you the same way. When I say get down, you get down. When I say run, you run. Don't look back at me. Whatever you hear behind you — gunfire, shouting, my voice calling your name — you keep running until you reach the willows, and then you go into the marsh and you don't come out till morning. Swear it."

"Bess—"

"Swear it or stay home."

A long silence, filled only by rain drumming the canvas over the cart bed. Then, quietly: "I swear."

"Good." Bess flicked the reins and old Duchess leaned into her collar, and the cart rolled out of the mill yard onto the road that ran south toward the marsh, and Bess began the work of getting herself killed properly.

* * *

She had planned it the way she planned a feast: backward, from the moment that mattered most, working out every dish and every hour. The moment that mattered was dawn, when Hargreave's riders would come down the mill road expecting to find a cart, and they must find a cart — but the wrong one, going the wrong way, carrying the wrong cargo, driven by the wrong woman.

The plan had three parts, and she trusted none of them past the first mile, but plans were like pastry: the recipe got you started and after that it was all feel.

Part one was the decoy itself. Tam, bandaged shoulder and all, had insisted on driving the second cart — the real-looking one, loaded with grain sacks shaped to suggest hidden weight, taking the north fork toward the river crossing where any fool would expect fugitives to run. Tam, who could barely lift his arm above his ribs since the ambush, who had taken a bullet meant for Wendell and hadn't once complained, had looked at Bess with those young-old eyes and said, "They'll chase me faster if they think I'm scared. I'm good at scared now. I've had practice."

She had wanted to slap him and hold him and she did neither, because both would take time they didn't have. She'd packed him bread and laudanum and told him that if he got caught he was to remember he knew nothing, he carried nothing, he was a conscripted orderly fleeing service, and that story would hold because it was half true, and the best lies were always half true. Then she kissed his forehead like he was six years old, and he let her, and that was how she knew how badly he was hurt.

Part two was the marsh. Old Marta's fen, everyone called it, though nobody remembered any Marta; four miles of black water and reed islands where the army maps showed solid ground because the surveyors had come in summer drought and the water had been lying to them even then. Bess's father had cut peat there. She knew the channels the way she knew the burn scars on her forearms — by memory deeper than sight. There was a path, if you could call it that, a drowned causeway from the old turf-cutting days, wide enough for a cart at low water, and tonight the tide was with her if the rain didn't drown the moon entirely.

Part three was Reyes. He would be waiting at the far edge, at the ruined turf-shed where the causeway met firm ground, with horses and whatever papers he'd forged to move two fugitives through the picket lines. If he wasn't waiting — well. Bess had learned in the last weeks that hope was a muscle. You used it or you lost it, and she intended to die with hers strong.

The rain thickened. The horse plodded. Behind her, Wendell shifted against the kindling, and Bess said, without turning, "Sit still. You'll rock the axle loose on the ford."

"I'm sorry," Wendell said. "About Tam. About all of—"

"Don't apologize to me while I'm driving. It makes me sentimental and I crash carts when I'm sentimental."

A wet, cracked sound from under the tarpaulin that might have been a laugh. Good. Bess gripped the reins and watched the dark road unspool ahead of her, and thought about names.

That was the thing nobody understood about her ledger-keeping friends, she thought — the officers least of all. They thought the book was treason. It wasn't. Treason was easy; she could commit treason between courses. What Wendell did was harder. Every night, after the lamps went low, writing down the names of boys the reports erased — that wasn't politics, that was mothering. That was saying *you existed, you were somebody's baby, somebody fed you porridge and worried about your cold feet, and no colonel with a stamp gets to make you into arithmetic.* Bess had fed most of those boys their last hot meal. She knew their favorite dishes. Private Aldous Vane liked his onions burnt. Corporal Merrick couldn't abide fat on his meat and blushed when he asked her to trim it. She could have written her own ledger of suppers.

So yes. She would drive through fire for that book, and she would not apologize for it, and if God objected He could come down to the marsh and say so to her face.

* * *

The first trouble came at the crossroads above Hollow Bridge, where the mill road bent along the ridge and you could see, on a clear night, the whole valley lit by the army's watchfires.

Tonight there should have been nothing to see but rain. Instead, orange light bloomed in the north — not watchfires, but one fire, sudden and wrong, off toward the river fork.

Tam's route.

Bess pulled the cart up under a leaning elm and stood in the stirrups, rain streaming off her hat brim, and watched the glow pulse. Too far to see detail. A burning cart, maybe. Or a barn. Or a signal fire, and Tam walking between soldiers with his hands up and that terrible practiced fearlessness on his face.

"They might have gotten clear," Wendell said beside her ear, having risen without permission. "It might be nothing. It might be—"

"It might be my boy burning alive, is what it might be." Her voice came out flat and strange, and she heard Wendell go still. "And there is nothing — *nothing* — I can do about it but keep driving, and if you tell me to turn around I will put you out of this cart in the mud and you can walk to the marsh yourself. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," Wendell whispered.

"Good." She sat down. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady on the reins, steady as stones, and some cold practical part of her mind observed that this was what her body did instead of weeping, and had done since the quarry, and would do until it couldn't anymore.

She clicked her tongue at Duchess and drove on.

But she made herself a promise in the dark, quiet as kneading dough: whoever had lit that fire, whoever had touched her boy, she would feed them last. When the war ended and the hungry came to her door — and they always came, victors and vanquished alike, because hunger didn't check uniforms — she would serve them cold. Let them taste what they'd earned.

It was a small vengeance. It was the only kind she had. She clutched it like a coal against her chest and drove into the rain.

* * *

The sentry post at the marsh road was supposed to be unmanned. It was not.

Two figures stood in the lamplight of the tollhouse, and Bess saw them a full minute before they saw her, which was the entire advantage she had and she meant to spend it well.

"Down," she breathed, and felt Wendell fold flat beneath the tarpaulin among the kindling. Then she did the hardest thing she had done all night, harder than the driving, harder than leaving Tam: she stopped looking afraid.

She let the cart roll up slow and easy, humming — actually humming, an old song about a girl and a goose girl, her mother's tune — and when the lantern swung toward her she lifted one hand in cheerful, weary salute.

"Well, thank the saints. Thought I'd be out here till Michaelmas."

The nearer soldier was young, rain-soaked, miserable. The older one kept his hand near his rifle and his voice bored. "Cart's to be searched. All traffic south of the bridge."

"Search away, love. Mind the eggs." She climbed down with the groaning deliberation of a woman whose knees had opinions, and began, unbidden, to unpack the basket she'd set on the seat beside her — the third part of the plan, the one she'd argued with herself about at three in the morning and finally included because her mother had taught her that a soldier's rifle was aimed at the enemy and his stomach was aimed at home. "You'll be the garrison lot, then? You poor creatures, I brought double rations up from the mill thinking I'd sell to the work crews, and the crews never came, and now I've a basket going soft in this wet."

The young one's eyes had already gone to the basket. The older one's hand stayed near his rifle, but his nostrils moved.

"What's in the cart?" he said.

"Kindling and a tarpaulin and a woman's whole fortune, and if you poke about you'll find my spare petticoats and I'll never forgive you." She thrust a wrapped parcel at the young one. "Here. Cheese and onion pasty, still warm under the cloth. Go on, before your sergeant eats your share."

He looked at the older one. The older one looked at the pasty the way men look at things they're trying not to want.

"The cart," he repeated.

Bess sighed, set down the basket, and hauled open the rear flap herself, revealing kindling stacked neat as church pews, the tarpaulin, the blanket. "Pull it apart if you must. You'll owe me a cord of wood and an apology." And while the older soldier stepped forward, she turned to the younger and lowered her voice to a confiding rasp: "Don't mind him, dear. He's just doing his job. Here—" she pressed the parcel into the boy's hands, folding his fingers over the warm cloth, holding them a heartbeat longer than necessary, looking up into his face the way she'd looked into ten thousand faces across a serving counter. "Your mum know where you are?"

Something happened in the boy's face. Something small and catastrophic. His eyes shone in the lamplight.

"She thinks I'm stationed at Corven," he said, barely audible.

"And where does she think you eat?"

A wet laugh. "She sends parcels. Half of them arrive crushed."

"Soldiers steal them," Bess said. "I know. Eat that one quick and no one can steal it." She released his hands and stepped back, and the older soldier was already poking the kindling apart with his bayonet, and Bess stood in the rain with her heart going like a hammer-mill and her face arranged into mild offended patience, and waited.

The bayonet slid between sticks of kindling. Above it, invisible, the false floor. Below the false floor, the blanket. Below the blanket, the book. Below the book, curled among the fuel, Wendell Almeida breathing as softly as a sleeping child, trusting her with their life the way she had trusted her father with the marsh channels.

The soldier grunted, satisfied, and shoved the kindling roughly back into place. "Clear," he said.

"Eggs too?" Bess demanded. "You didn't even check the eggs."

"Madam, I am not checking your eggs."

"Then take two for your trouble, and one for him, and don't tell me you can't, I've bribed sergeants with less." She loaded them up — the older man refusing, then accepting, then almost smiling despite himself — and climbed back onto the bench, and gathered the reins, and gave them a piece of her mind about the state of the roads, the price of salt, and the shame of a nation that left its boys standing in the rain without so much as a hot meal, and drove through the tollhouse light and out the other side into the great dark breathing dark of the marsh country.

Only when the lamp was a smudge behind them did she allow her shoulders to drop half an inch.

"Wendell," she said quietly. "You can breathe now. Slowly. Like you're smelling soup."

From under the tarpaulin, muffled, shaking: "You gave them our eggs."

"I gave them *some* eggs. There's a difference between a bribe and a robbery." And then, because the darkness hid her face and some things needed saying: "You did well. Staying flat. Most folk would've bolted."

Silence. Then, very small: "I thought of the names. I thought — if I move, the names drown. So I stayed."

"That's it exactly," Bess said. "That's the whole trick of courage, love. You just decide what you're more afraid of losing."

* * *

The causeway took them at midnight.

She found it by counting — twenty paces past the split willow, thirty from the leaning stone — because the water had swallowed every landmark she'd known as a girl and given the fen a new geography each season. The cart wheels sank to the hubs in the flooded track, and Duchess strained, and Bess walked at the mare's head through knee-deep black water, one hand on the cheek strap, talking nonsense to her the whole way. *Come on, old girl. Nearly there. Best oats you ever ate waiting at the end of this, I swear it on my mother's grave.*

Twice the cart lurched as a wheel found a hole, and twice Bess's heart stopped and restarted. Once something long and sinuous slid away from her leg through the water and she elected, firmly, not to think about it.

The rain slackened near the end. The clouds tore, and a ragged slice of moon came through, and Bess saw the turf-shed roofline against the sky — and beneath it, a shape. A horse. Two horses. A figure standing in the ruin's doorway with a shuttered lantern, and even at that distance, even after everything, she knew the set of his shoulders. She'd fed that boy supper every Sunday for two years before the war took him. She'd known him at nine years old, thieving apples, before anyone called him Captain anything.

Ivo Reyes.

Beside her, Wendell sat up under the tarpaulin, and made a sound Bess had never heard from them — half sob, half prayer — and Bess reached back without looking and found their hand and squeezed it hard, once, the way she'd held her children's hands crossing the market square, and let go.

"Not yet," she said. "First the firm ground. First we're safe. Then you can fall apart all you like, and I'll put the kettle on, and we'll do our crying proper, with tea."

The cart ground up onto the turf bank, solid at last, and Reyes came down through the reeds at a run, lantern swinging, and his face when the light found it was not a captain's face at all, just a young man's face, wrecked with relief.

"You made it," he said. "We saw the fire on the north road, we thought—"

"So did I." Bess's voice cracked on it and she hated it and let it crack anyway. "Tam's route. We don't know. Nobody knows. And I'll tell you what I told Wendell: we don't stop, we don't turn back, we finish the job he bled for, and *then* we go find out." She fixed Reyes with the look she used on officers who tried to skip the queue. "You have horses, I assume, and papers, and some notion of where we're going that isn't a hole in a marsh. Because I've driven forty miles tonight, Captain, and I have a book of dead men to deliver and a living boy to rescue, and I am *not*, under any circumstances, going home without either."

Reyes looked at her. Then at Wendell, climbing stiffly from the cart bed, clutching the ledger against their chest with both arms like a child rescued from a flood. Then back at Bess.

"No," he said slowly. "I don't suppose you are."

"Good lad. Help me unload the mare."

Behind them, far off across the fen, the rain closed over the north road again, and somewhere out in that dark, Bess knew, her boy was moving or captured or dead, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. But the ledger was out of the cart and into Reyes's oilskin wrap, and Wendell was on a horse, and the east was not yet grey, and there was still road enough to fight with.

Bess swung up onto the second horse, joints screaming, heart iron, and pointed the way south toward the armistice town.

"Hold on, Tam," she said to the wind. "Mum's coming."
