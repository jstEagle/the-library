## 33. The Reading

The marsh house had been a fowler's hut once, before the war made the coast a place of gun emplacements and drowned paths. Now it was only walls — turf and driftwood, a stove that smoked more than it warmed, a single window glazed with glass so old it rippled the grey light into something like water. Bess had left them there at first light with a sack of bread, a jug of milk gone warm in transit, and an instruction delivered in her kitchen-sergeant's voice: *You don't move until I come for you, and if anyone comes who isn't me, you go out the back and into the reeds and you don't stop walking until the water's over your boots.*

Then she had turned the cart around and driven back toward Marrowfield with Tam bleeding in the bed of it, and Wendell had stood in the doorway of a stranger's hut and watched her disappear down the mill road, and had understood that they were trusting their lives to a woman whose weapons were loaves and lies and love, and that this trust was the only sane act left available to them.

Now it was evening. The tide had come in across the flats with its long whispering, filling the channels, erasing the paths, making the whole world out beyond the window into a single moving sheet of pewter. Reyes sat on the fowler's stool by the stove with his arm bound where the ambush had torn it, his uniform jacket hanging from a nail by the door like a shed skin. He wore one of the hut owner's ancient fisherman's sweaters, wool gone soft as moss, and without the jacket he looked — younger. Unfinished. A man not yet decided.

Wendell sat on the bunk with the ledger on their knees.

They had carried it wrapped in oilcloth inside Bess's flour sack through the marsh, through the reeds that cut their hands like small knives, while somewhere behind them shots cracked over the flats and Tam fell and Bess swore and drove. The ledger had weighed nothing and everything. Five years of ink. It had ridden against Wendell's ribs like a second heart.

Reyes had not asked to see it. That was the thing Wendell kept turning over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. He had bled for it — or at least bled near it — and he had not once said *show me*. He sat warming his hands at the stove and watching the fire with the patience of a man who has learned that some doors must be opened from the inside.

"You should eat," Wendell said.

"You should sleep."

"We're both liars tonight." But they took the bread and broke it, and handed him half, and for a while there was only the sound of eating and the tide and the wind working at the turf roof the way a slow reader works at a difficult page.

Then Reyes said, quietly, "I heard the names. At the mill. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "When Hargreave's men came over the wall, you were shouting them. I thought at first it was a signal, some code. Then I realized what it was." His hands curled around the mug Bess had left, though it was empty. "You were reading them. Out loud. Like—"

"A litany," Wendell said.

"Like a prayer."

"It isn't one. It's less than one. A prayer asks for something." Wendell looked down at the oilcloth, at the string knotted the way Odile had taught them, double and reversed, so it could be untied blind in the dark. "This just refuses to ask. That's all it does. Refuses."

"Wendell." His voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice he'd used years ago across the infirmary supper table when someone else's grief had come too close to his own. "You don't have to show me. I want you to know that. Whatever Hargreave told you about why I need it, whatever my own side wants it for — you don't owe me a page of it. You owe the names. That's all."

Wendell untied the knot anyway.

"I promised you once," they said, "that if they ever called you enemy, we'd hear you out before we believed the report. I've been trying to work out how to keep that promise, and I think—" their fingers were steady, which surprised them; everything else trembled — "I think this is how. You hear them. All of them. Because if you're going to carry this book anywhere, Captain Reyes, you're going to carry every name in it, and you're going to know what each one cost, and then no handler, no tribunal, no colonel with a signature will ever be able to make you set it down."

* * *

The ledger opened to the first page the way old books do — reluctantly, then all at once, the spine cracking softly like a knuckle. The lamp Bess had left burned low and yellow, and Wendell tilted it close, and began.

"Almeida, Wendell, nurse, this book begun the fourth of March, five years past, because Private Emeric Voss died on my ward at two in the morning and the morning report listed him *wounded, evacuated*. Nobody evacuated him. I buried him myself behind the chapel with Odile saying the words. So that no one else goes unrecorded, I write:"

And then the names.

Wendell read them the way they had always written them — slowly, fully, rank and regiment and wound and hour of death and the small human detail that the reports never held. *Emeric Voss, private, 2nd Rifles, abdominal wound, died 02:14. Liked to whistle when the shelling got bad, said it annoyed the guns.* *Doretta Quill, laundress attached to the garrison, fever, died 19:40. Kept a canary she'd taught to sing the retreat call; the bird outlived her by a week.* *Sergeant Ambrose Hale, mortar fragment, died 11:03. Asked three times whether his letter to his mother had been sent. It had.*

The tide breathed in and out. Reyes did not move. Somewhere in the first hour his empty mug slipped from his fingers and rolled against the stove leg and neither of them reached for it.

Wendell had read these pages silently ten hundred times. Reading them aloud was a different country. Each name had to be shaped by the mouth, given breath, given duration, and in giving it Wendell discovered they had been hoarding grief for five years the way a miser hoards coin — touching it nightly, counting it, spending none of it. Now it poured out into the smoky air of a dead fowler's hut, and it did not diminish. That was the terrible discovery. Grief spent aloud does not lessen. It multiplies its holders.

By the third hour Reyes's eyes were wet and he let them be wet and made no apology for it, and Wendell loved him a little for that, and hated themselves a little too, because love was exactly the thing Odile had warned could not be trusted as a witness, and yet here it was, sitting across a stove in a borrowed sweater, listening.

There were forty-one names before the middle of the book. Then sixty. Wendell's voice went rough and they drank the warm milk and went on. Some entries were long — the soldiers who had lingered days, whom Wendell had known, whose last sentences deserved recording. Some were a single line for a man brought in already dead and unidentified, marked only by what he carried: *Unknown, private's coat, 4th Regiment buttons, photograph of two girls in his breast pocket, died en route, 16:20. May whoever finds his family be kinder than the army was to him.*

Once, near midnight, Reyes said, "Stop. Please." And Wendell stopped, alarmed, thinking he would tell them it was enough, that he couldn't, that the deal, the exchange, the whole architecture of risk they'd built was too high a price for an hour of names.

But he said: "The one before. Corporal Ennis. Say the last part again."

"*Corporal Peter Ennis, sappers, died 04:55 of wounds to both legs. Spent his final hours teaching the boy orderly Tam how to play draughts, and let Tam win, and everyone on the ward knew he let him win, and Tam knew too, and it was the kindest lie anyone told that winter.*"

Reyes pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Tam won't remember it that way now. After today. After taking that bullet." His voice came out mangled. "He'll remember the blood instead. Someone should have written down the kinder thing somewhere it can survive him."

"That's what this is," Wendell said. "That's the entire point of it. Everything else — the counts, the figures, the proof, the politics — that's just what the book *does*. This is what it *is*."

They read on.

* * *

It was deep in the small hours, the candle down to its last thumb's width, when Wendell reached the entry dated the previous autumn — the page they had read alone a dozen times and never once aloud, the page that had rearranged the entire war in their hands like a card turned over too late.

Their voice caught on the date. Reyes looked up.

"Whose?" he asked. "Whose name?"

Wendell turned the ledger around, briefly, so the lamplight lay across the ink, and then took it back, because it seemed suddenly obscene to hand it over even for a moment — this was a reading, not a showing.

"Elias Hargreave," they said. "Lieutenant Elias Hargreave. The colonel's son."

The wind found some gap in the turf and moaned through it, thin as a flute.

"He was wounded at the crossing action in October — you'd have still been with your delegation's forward post then, maybe you heard the guns. They brought him in at dusk under a false name, which I didn't know at the time, none of us did. Staff said he was to be recorded as a common soldier. *Private Wren*, the intake paper said." Wendell's finger rested beneath the entry but they didn't need it; they had the words by heart, carved now into them. "Abdominal wound. The same wound that killed Emeric Voss in year one. We worked on him nine hours. Odile sat with him through the worst of it. Near the end he stopped pretending to be Private Wren. He said his father's name. He said — " Wendell swallowed. The hut was very cold and very quiet. "He said, *tell him I held*. As if holding the line were the thing. As if a father would rather hear that than *I was frightened, I wanted to come home*."

"And the report?"

"The report says Private Wren, died of wounds, next of kin unknown." Wendell closed their eyes. "Hargreave signed the falsification himself. I've seen the morning reports since — I know his hand. He struck his own son from the record to keep the casualty count low enough for the war ministry, because the count was the thing keeping the war alive, and the war was the thing keeping *him* alive, and somewhere in that arithmetic he erased Elias." Their eyes opened. "Or perhaps he doesn't know. Perhaps someone below him did it to protect *him*, and he has spent a year believing his son is alive in some hospital far away, writing letters that no one answers, hating the army for its slowness." Wendell shook their head slowly. "I don't know which is worse. I've tried to decide for three days and I can't. A father who sold his son for a signature, or a father who doesn't know his son is in the ground behind my chapel with thirty-nine others, waiting five years for somebody to say his real name out loud."

"So say it," Reyes whispered.

Wendell read the entry. All of it. Rank, wound, hour, the false name, the true name, the dying words, the burial behind the chapel between Doretta Quill and Sergeant Hale, the depth of the grave, the marker — a river stone, unmarked, because marked stones invite questions and questions invite officers.

When it was done, the silence was total. Even the tide seemed to pause.

Reyes rose, unsteadily, and stood at the ripple-glassed window looking out at the black flats where nothing was visible at all, and Wendell watched his back and saw his shoulders shaking and understood that this man — this man in the enemy's uniform, this declared collaborator, this presumed-dead brother — was crying for the son of the man who had ordered him shot six hours ago.

"That's why he can't let it surface," Reyes said at last, without turning. "Not the leak. Not the negotiators. Not the falsified counts, though God knows those are crime enough. It's the boy. If the delegation reads that name into the record tomorrow — if the armistice commission hears that Colonel Hargreave's own son died unnamed in a garrison infirmary while he signed reports omitting him — " He turned. In the guttering light his face was stripped to its foundations. "He doesn't fear exposure, Wendell. He fears *mourning*. He has built his entire life out of not mourning. And your book is going to make him do it in front of the whole world."

"Then it will do to him," Wendell said quietly, "what it has done to everyone in its pages. Make him real."

* * *

The candle died a little before dawn. Neither of them lit another. Grey light seeped up out of the marsh like something rising through water, and the tide turned, and far off a curlew started its rising call, and Wendell sat with the closed ledger in their lap feeling the strange emptiness of a vessel finally poured out.

Reyes crossed the room and knelt — actually knelt, awkwardly, his bandaged arm braced against the bunk frame — so that his face was level with theirs, and the ledger lay between them on the blanket like a sleeping animal.

"I was going to give it to my handler," he said. "That was the plan. Trade it for Odile, trade it for the talks, let our people verify the figures and bury the rest in an annex nobody reads. That was the plan right up until an hour ago." He laid his good hand flat on the oilcloth, lightly, the way you'd touch a door you weren't sure you had the right to open. "But you've ruined me, Wendell. You've made me listen to every name. I can't put this in a handler's briefcase. It'll die in a filing cabinet in the capital and Elias Hargreave will be Private Wren forever, and Tam's draughts game will be a blood-soaked corridor, and every one of these people will have been read aloud for the last time in a marsh hut by two exhausted traitors and then forgotten by breakfast."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I'm done carrying orders." His voice was steady now, scraped clean. "The talks resume day after tomorrow. Both delegations. Same hall, same table, same stalemate over casualty figures both sides know are lies. I'm going to walk into that hall carrying this book, and I'm going to put it on the armistice table itself, in front of both commissions and every clerk and every observer, and I'm going to open it to the first page and start where you started tonight. *Emeric Voss, private, 2nd Rifles, died 02:14.* And I won't stop until they either sign an honest peace or shoot me between one name and the next."

"They'll call it treason. Against *both* sides."

"They already call me that." He almost smiled. "At least now I'd be treasonous for something true." His hand turned palm-up on the ledger, offered. "But I won't take it from you. It was never mine to carry alone. It's yours — yours and Bess's and Tam's and Odile's. You built this out of floorboards and defiance for five years while I was playing soldier in the wrong uniform. So I'm asking, Wendell Almeida. Come with me. Bring the book to the table yourself. Read the first name. Let the man who wrote it begin the reading, and I'll stand beside you and swear to every word."

Out on the flats the tide was withdrawing, uncovering channel by channel the drowned paths Bess would come back by. Wendell looked at the ledger, at five years of nights compressed into a block of sewn paper, at the last blank page waiting at the back like an open mouth.

"My name isn't Almeida," Wendell said. "Not really. Not the one I was born with. There's a line at the end of this book I've been saving for five years, and when this is over — if we live — I'm going to write it." They lifted the ledger and held it against their chest, heart over heart. "So yes. We carry it together. But I read the names, Ivo. Every one. Even his son. Especially his son. And Hargreave can stand in that hall and listen or he can order us shot, but either way the book gets read into the record, because that's the only mercy it knows how to give."

Reyes nodded once, solemn as a vow. Then his head came up, sharp, toward the window — toward the causeway, where through the lifting mist a cart had appeared, moving fast, one figure on the bench, and the way the horse was driven told Wendell before anything else did that Bess had come back alone.

She was waving her hat. Both arms. Over and over, the way you wave when the news cannot wait.

Wendell was on their feet with the ledger strapped beneath their coat before the cart had covered half the causeway, and Reyes was reaching for the uniform jacket on its nail, and neither of them needed to hear the news yet to know that whatever it was, the time for hiding had just run out.
