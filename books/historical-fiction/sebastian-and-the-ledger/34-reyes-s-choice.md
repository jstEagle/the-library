## 34. Reyes's Choice

The marsh house smelled of peat smoke and lamp oil and, faintly, of Bess's bread, which she had insisted on baking even here, in hiding, because a household without bread was a household that had given up. Ivo Reyes sat at the scarred plank table with the ledger open before him, and he did not touch it.

Wendell had been reading aloud for the better part of two hours. They had begun after supper and gone past midnight, Wendell's voice low and steady as a mason laying stones, each name set down with its dates, its wound, its regiment, its small human annotation — *loved whistling; carried his mother's letter sewn into his coat; was seventeen and lied about it* — until the room itself seemed to fill with the dead the way a church fills with candlelight, one flame at a time, until you could not see the walls.

Tam lay propped on pallets by the fire, bandaged from the mill ambush, half-dreaming. Bess knitted and listened and occasionally wept without noise, her hands never stopping, as though grief were wool to be worked through. Odile was still in Hargreave's custody; her absence sat in the room like an empty chair at a full table.

And near the end, Wendell had read the name.

*Alastair Hargreave. Lieutenant. Died of wounds improperly treated at the second Marrowfield engagement, 11th October. Left untreated four days. Reported as missing, later struck entirely.*

Reyes had watched Wendell's mouth shape it. He had felt the name go through the room like a cold draft under a door. And he had understood, in that moment, something he had been circling for days without landing: that the ledger was no longer evidence. It had become testimony, and testimony demanded a congregation.

"You've gone somewhere," Wendell said now, closing the book but keeping their hand flat upon it, the way one keeps a hand on a frightened animal.

Reyes looked up. The lamplight made Wendell's face half gold, half shadow, and he thought — not for the first time since the mill, since the shots, since Tam's blood on the cobbles — how strange it was that he had crossed a war to arrive at this kitchen-table country, this lamplight, these people who were not his people anymore and yet were more his than any uniform he had ever worn.

"I've been thinking about my handler," he said.

Bess's needles paused. Resumed.

"Vasquez wants the ledger delivered to him by Friday," Reyes went on. "He'll want it verified, copied, folded into our delegation's position papers. He'll use the corrected casualty figures to argue their side was bleeding worse than ours, press harder terms, take more territory off the table. That's what handlers do with truth. They make it ammunition."

"And?" Tam said from his pallet, his voice thin but wry. "You'd rather they make it furniture?"

Reyes almost smiled. "I'd rather they make it a mirror."

He stood and walked to the window. The marsh stretched black to the horizon, stitched here and there with the small fires of the picket lines around the armistice city — both armies camped within shouting distance of the negotiating hall, waiting, growing angrier and colder by the day while the talks stalled over numbers. Over numbers. As if the dead were a dispute about grain prices.

"Both delegations are lying," he said. "Ours inflates enemy losses and hides our own. Theirs does the same in reverse. That's why the talks are stuck — each side's arithmetic proves the other is either losing or lying, and neither will sign anything built on the other's ledger." He turned back. "But there exists one ledger that lies to no one. One count that names the dead on both sides, including the ones the officers wanted forgotten. Including—"

"Including Hargreave's son," Wendell said quietly.

"Including Hargreave's son."

Bess set down her knitting. "If you put that name in front of those men," she said, "in front of the whole armistice table, with clerks writing it all down — Hargreave doesn't just fall. He burns. And everything attached to him burns with him. Including a chaplain sitting in his cells right now, whose only crime is loving us."

"That," said Reyes, "is the risk. And also the lever."

*

He spent the next morning composing and destroying letters.

It was a discipline he had learned in the years of double work: write everything, send nothing, let the act of writing show you which truths you actually believed. He wrote first to Vasquez, the handler who had ordered him to find the ledger to prove his loyalty, and who now believed — because Reyes had fed him exactly enough — that the ledger would be in Reyes's hands within the week.

*Sir: I have secured the document. Before delivery, I must flag a complication. The final entries concern personnel connected to Colonel Hargreave's command chain. If this material passes through liaison channels, it will reach Hargreave's allies before it reaches the table. I recommend direct presentation to both delegations simultaneously.*

He read it back and burned it. Too clever. Vasquez would smell the maneuvering and wonder why Reyes cared who saw what.

He wrote again. *Sir: The ledger is larger than expected. Its value is not in the figures alone but in the named witnesses behind them.* Burned that too. Sentiment would finish him faster than treason.

The third attempt he kept.

*Sir. I have the ledger. I am not delivering it through liaison channels. At the session on Saturday, when the casualty question resumes, I will present it openly to both delegations, with your permission or without it. I understand what this costs me. I ask only that you consider what it buys: a signed peace instead of another winter of stalled arithmetic. If you move to stop me, you confirm to both sides that our delegation feared the true count. Decide accordingly.*

There it was. Not a request. A corner, drawn in ink, with himself inside it.

He sealed it and gave it to Bess, who had become, over the past weeks, the most reliable courier in the occupied territories, a distinction she wore with the modest pride of a woman who had always known her kitchen was the center of the world.

"He'll have you shot," she said, taking the letter.

"He'll try to have me shot. There's a difference, and the difference is whether the record defends me." He met her eyes. "That's why it has to be the table, Bess. A tribunal can be managed. A negotiation can be steered. But a public reading, entered into the record, witnessed by clerks of both nations — that's the one thing in this war nobody can unpick afterward. I'm not carrying the ledger to safety. I'm nailing it shut where everyone can see."

Bess studied him for a long moment. Then she tucked the letter into her bodice beside the flour-dusted fold where she hid everything that mattered.

"You sound like Odile," she said. "Preaching."

"I had a good teacher. Three of them." He glanced at Wendell, bent over the ledger across the room, copying entries by daylight onto loose sheets — the duplicates they had agreed to prepare, so that no single capture could destroy the whole. "Four."

*

Vasquez's reply came back inside a day, which told Reyes everything: the handler had been expecting something like this, had perhaps hoped for it, had certainly prepared for it.

*Your insubordination is noted. Your logic is reluctantly accepted. You will present the document at Saturday's session. Be advised: if the document damages our position more than theirs, or if it appears at any point that you have acted as an agent of the other delegation, the protection you believe the record affords you will be examined very carefully. Come alone. Do not inform the enemy delegation in advance. And Reyes — if this is some final loyalty to the people you left behind, remember what leaving them cost you the first time.*

Reyes read it twice and laughed, once, without much humor.

*Come alone.* As if he had ever been alone. As if the last three months hadn't been a long lesson in precisely the opposite.

He went to find Wendell, who was washing bandages at the basin because Tam's wound needed changing and because Wendell could not bear idle hands the way other people could not bear hunger. Outside, rain had begun, soft and persistent, turning the marsh to hammered pewter.

"They've agreed," Reyes said. "Saturday. Both delegations, the full session, the casualty question on the agenda anyway. I walk in with the book and read it into the record."

Wendell's hands stilled in the water. "They agreed too easily."

"Yes."

"What's the trap?"

Reyes leaned against the doorframe. He admired that about Wendell — the refusal to receive good news at face value, as if hope unexamined were a form of negligence. Five years of recording the dead had taught them that relief was usually the prelude to something worse.

"The trap is that I don't know yet," he admitted. "Possibly Vasquez believes the figures help our side. Possibly he intends to seize the book mid-session and call it procedure. Possibly he simply wants me standing in that room holding evidence of my own treason when he decides to spend me. But none of those traps change the arithmetic. Once the names are read aloud — once the clerk writes them down, once the other delegation's clerks copy them — the book exists in the record. They can shoot me and the words stay read."

"And Hargreave?"

"Hargreave will be in that room. He has a seat at the table — he engineered it, everyone says, to keep control of the figures." Reyes paused. "Which means when we get to the final page, he will hear his son's name read aloud in front of the men whose reports erased him. And whatever he does in that moment — rage, collapse, denial — every man at that table will see it. Power's weakness is its grief, Wendell. We talked about this. We're going to stand in front of the wound and open the book."

Wendell dried their hands slowly. When they spoke, their voice was very quiet. "I want to be the one who reads it."

"Wendell—"

"Not the names of our dead — those are yours to carry into their hall, and I know what it costs. But the last page. Alastair Hargreave. I recorded that entry. I held the boy's hand while he died, or someone from my infirmary did, and the colonel signed the paper that erased him. It should come from the person who wrote the truth down first." They looked up, and Reyes saw in their face the same expression he had seen the night they refused Hargreave's bargain — fear met with spine, fear simply refusing the chair it was offered. "Besides. You'll be arrested before you finish. Someone has to finish."

Reyes wanted to argue. He catalogued the arguments — the danger to Wendell specifically, the legal standing a commissioned officer carried that a nurse did not, the simple protective instinct that had survived everything else the war had stripped from him. And then he discarded them, one by one, because Wendell was right, and because he had promised, three years ago over bad wine in a peacetime kitchen, that he would trust this person's judgment above his report, above his orders, above his own fear.

"You finish the book," he said. "I start it."

Wendell nodded once. Then, unexpectedly, they crossed the room and took his hand — briefly, hard, like a soldier gripping a shoulder before a wall goes up. "Whatever happens in that room," they said, "you hear me out first. That was the promise. This is me hearing you out: thank you. For choosing the table. For choosing the truth over the safe version of yourself."

"It isn't a choice anymore," Reyes said. "It hasn't been since the mill. Treason's just testimony that got there early."

*

Friday night he could not sleep, and so he sat up with the ledger a final time, alone by the banked fire, reading not the names this time but the margins — Wendell's marginalia, five years of small annotations in a careful hand. *Treated by candlelight, out of laudanum.* *His brother came looking; sent home with bread.* *Asked me to write to no one; said no one was left.* Page after page of the war's underside, the accounting no staff officer would ever sign.

This was what he would carry into the armistice hall tomorrow. Not a weapon, though it would end careers. Not ammunition, though both delegations would try to fire it. A litany. Five years of the overlooked, bound in oiled cloth, smelling faintly of floorboard dust and chapel incense and Bess's kitchen.

He heard movement and looked up. Tam, pale but upright, had lowered himself onto the bench across from him.

"Couldn't sleep either," Tam said. "Leg hurts when it rains. Also I've been thinking about being shot at the armistice table and it keeps me up." He grinned, ghost of his old grin. "Bess says if either of you dies in that hall she'll never forgive you, and she means it in the way she means everything, which is absolutely and forever."

"Tell her I intend to be unforgivable in the surviving sense."

"She'll hold you to that." Tam's grin faded. He looked at the ledger. "When you read them tomorrow — the names — read them slow. Some of those men waited years to be said out loud. Let them take their time arriving."

Reyes closed the book. "I will."

Outside, the rain had stopped. In the east, over the armistice city, the sky had begun to turn the color of old tin — dawn coming, and with it Saturday, and with it the hall where two delegations sat stalled over arithmetic, and Colonel Hargreave sat among them, guarding his falsified figures, not yet knowing that his son's name had been waiting in a hidden ledger for five years, and that tomorrow, at last, it would be read aloud.

Reyes wrapped the ledger in its oilcloth and held it against his chest like something born rather than written.

Tomorrow they would learn what a book could do that an army could not.
