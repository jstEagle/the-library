## 39. The Tribunal

The room they gave him for the hearing had once been a ballroom.

Reyes understood the choice immediately — it was a courtesy with teeth in it. The high ceiling, the parquet gone grey under a decade of boots, the tall windows through which winter light fell in long pale bars: all of it said *we are treating you as an officer still*, and everything else in the room said *we could unmake you before luncheon*. Seven men sat at a table that had been carried up from some colonel's quarters and now looked stranded in all that space, like furniture left behind after a retreat. Behind them, on the wall where mirrors would once have hung, someone had nailed a map of the front, its lines already obsolete, the ink of the new borders not yet dry anywhere in the world.

He stood where they told him to stand, in the middle of the floor, alone. No counsel. He had asked for none. There was no lawyer alive who could argue this better than the ledger already had.

"Captain Ivo Reyes," said the president of the board, a major general named Corvane whose face Reyes knew from dispatches and whose voice he knew from nothing, flat as a ledger line. "You stand accused of collaboration with enemy forces, of transmitting false intelligence to your handlers over a period of eleven months, and of unauthorized disclosure of classified casualty records at the armistice negotiations." A pause, the rustle of paper. "You will note that the third charge is the one that concerns us."

*Concerns us.* Not *condemns you*. Reyes kept his eyes on the middle distance, on a water stain on the far wall shaped like a country that no longer existed.

"You will also note," Corvane continued, "that the armistice was signed at dawn on the fourteenth. You will further note that the corrected casualty figures entered into the record at those negotiations were sourced, by your own admission, from a document compiled outside any authorized chain of command." He looked up then, and his eyes were tired in the particular way of men who have signed too many things they did not read. "Captain, this board has spent nine days reading that record. We have found nothing in it we can refute. That is not the same as saying we approve of it."

"I wouldn't expect approval, sir."

"No," Corvane said dryly. "I imagine approval is the one thing you never expected from anyone again."

* * *

They took him through it hour by hour, though the testimony itself had been given weeks ago and they were only walking the ground again, checking the fences. The handler who had ordered him to find the ledger — a thin, careful man named Aldous Vey — testified from behind a screen, his voice distorted into anonymity, and Reyes listened to the distortion and thought: *so even his own side will not look at him.* Vey admitted the order. Vey admitted the authorization of the exchange at the mill. Vey did not admit, and was not pressed on, the fact that he had intended the mill meeting to end with Reyes dead and the ledger in official hands, a version of events Reyes knew only because Wendell had guessed it first and because Tam's blood on the marsh road had confirmed the guess. Some truths survived only because people bled on them.

Then came the part he had not prepared for, because he had not known it was coming: a clerk, young, hollow-eyed, was brought in to read aloud from the armistice record itself. The corrected figures. The names. Page after page of names, read in a voice that cracked twice and steadied itself both times, and the seven men at the table sat very still while a boy recited their war back to them in the only language that could not be court-martialed.

Reyes watched their faces. That was his whole occupation during those hours — watching faces, the old liaison's habit, the spy's incurable craft. Four of the seven showed nothing; they had been sanded smooth by rank. One closed his eyes at certain names, and Reyes marked him. And one — the youngest, a colonel with a bandaged hand — wrote steadily throughout, filling page after page, and when the clerk reached the entry dated the autumn before last, the one naming the son of Colonel Edmund Hargreave among the omitted dead at Marrowfield, the writing stopped altogether, and the pen hovered, and did not come down for a long time.

Hargreave himself was not present. Reyes had learned that much in the corridor: retired, effective that morning, his resignation accepted "with regret" by men who had spent a week discovering what regret actually weighed. He had not been arrested. Nothing so clean. He had simply been allowed to become a private citizen carrying a name that appeared, in the permanent record of the war, beside a lie he had authored. Reyes had imagined that punishment often enough during the dark stretches of the last year — imagined it as fire, as a cell, as a wall at dawn. Now that it had arrived in its actual form, he found it was worse than all of those, and colder, and would last longer.

The session broke at midday. As the board rose, Corvane paused beside him on his way out, close enough that only Reyes could hear.

"The record defends you," the general said quietly. "That is the problem. Everyone in this army can now see exactly how a man is made into a traitor, and every step of the making was ours. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Captain?"

"I think so, sir."

"I don't think you do." Corvane's eyes went briefly to the map on the wall, to the bleeding edges of the new world. "We cannot call the record false. We cannot call it treason, because it ended the war. But if we promote you, we tell every officer in this army that the road you walked leads upward. So we must do something with you that looks like neither punishment nor reward." His mouth did something that was almost, but not quite, a smile. "Tomorrow. Wear your uniform. All of it."

* * *

That night they quartered him in a room with a real bed and a locked door, and the lock, he understood, was pointed inward. He slept badly, which was usual now, and woke before dawn out of habit rather than fear, and lay listening to the garrison wake around him: the clatter of the kitchens, the distant trumpet, a horse complaining in the stable yard. Sounds that had structured his entire adult life. He tried to feel what he supposed he ought to feel — the soldier's dread of discharge, the identity crisis the chaplains always preached about — and discovered instead a strange, level quiet, like the air after the first hard frost.

He thought about Marrowfield. He thought about Bess Arden's kitchen, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke, the loaf with the message baked into its heart. He thought about Odile standing in her pulpit preaching on names while officers sat in her pews not knowing they were being warned. He thought about Tam, who had taken a bullet meant for a nurse and lived, changed, quieter, and who had looked at Reyes across the infirmary table afterward without hatred and without warmth either, just assessment, the way one man measures whether another can be trusted with the people he loves. And he thought about Wendell Almeida, who had kept a promise made by lamplight three years ago at a cost that would be paid out for years yet, and who had written a name on the last page of that terrible book that Reyes still had not seen, because some things belonged to the writer alone.

He got up and dressed in full uniform, as instructed. The tunic fit differently than he remembered. Or perhaps it was the same and he was different; the tailors of the army had never accounted for what eleven months of double life did to the set of a man's shoulders.

* * *

The second day of the tribunal was short.

Corvane read the findings in a voice pitched to carry to the back of the empty ballroom, though there was no one in it but guards and stenographers and the seven men who had decided him. The charges of collaboration: dismissed. The evidence of his double role, corroborated by the armistice record and by testimony of the delegation itself, established that his transmissions had been false intelligence fed deliberately to the enemy, and that the final disclosure had been material to the signing of the armistice. The word *material* landed in the room like a coin dropped on stone.

"In recognition of service rendered to the state under conditions of exceptional hazard," Corvane read, "the board recommends Captain Reyes for promotion to the rank of Major, with assignment to—" here the pause, deliberate, a knife offered handle-first— "the Directorate of Records and Reconciliation. The Directorate, Captain, will oversee the correction of wartime documentation across all commands. Your familiarity with falsified reports is considered a qualification."

Reyes heard it clearly then, what Corvane had tried to tell him in the corridor. They were not offering him a post. They were offering him a tomb with honours — a career spent inside the machine that had made him, correcting on paper the lies the machine would go on telling in practice, promoted precisely so that his silence would have a salary attached. Every officer who watched him accept would learn the lesson: betray the truth publicly, serve it privately, and rise. The ledger would become a department. Wendell's five years of devotion would become a filing system with his name on the door.

And God help him, some tired surviving animal part of him wanted it. Wanted the rank, the rooms with real beds, the restoration of a name that had been dragged through both armies' mud. He let himself want it for the length of one breath, honestly, the way Wendell had taught him — *name it before you refuse it, or the refusal isn't yours.*

Then he spoke.

"With respect, General, I decline the promotion."

The stenographer's hands stopped. Somewhere behind the screen where witnesses waited, a chair creaked.

Corvane regarded him for a long moment. "You understand the board is not obliged to offer alternatives."

"I understand, sir."

"You would leave the army with your record cleared but your rank unchanged, your commission surrendered, and your future—" he glanced down at the page, and Reyes saw that the next words had been pre-written, waiting, a second door held ready in case the first was refused— "your future subject to the ordinary fortunes of a civilian. Is that your intention?"

"It is my intention to resign my commission, sir. Effective upon your acceptance."

"May I ask why? For the record." And there it was, the flicker in the flat voice, the thing Corvane had perhaps hoped for and perhaps dreaded: a reason spoken aloud, on the record, in front of the stenographer and the guards and the boy with the bandaged hand who had stopped writing when Hargreave's son's name was read.

Reyes turned so that he faced not the board but the stenographer, so the answer would go down clean.

"Because I have seen what this army does with names," he said. "It strikes them from reports. It files them. It promotes the men who strike them and buries the men who write them down. If I take this promotion, I become one more officer standing between the truth and the families who own it. I have been that officer. I declined the work once already, at cost to everyone I love. I will not accept wages for it now." He paused, and heard Wendell's voice under his own, steady as a pulse. "There is a ledger being copied, right now, by hand, and delivered door to door across four counties, so that mothers can stop waiting for sons the reports say never died. That work needs doing for years. It pays nothing. I intend to do it."

Silence. The kind that has weight, that presses on the ears. Then Corvane nodded slowly, once, like a man closing a ledger of his own.

"Noted for the record," he said. "The board accepts your resignation with regret." And then, off the record, in a voice suddenly human and very tired: "Captain — Major Reyes — whoever you are now. The board read every name. All of them. We read them aloud to each other on the third night, after hours, when the stenographers had gone. I want you to know that." He gathered his papers. "Go and copy your book."

* * *

Outside, the winter light had thickened toward afternoon, and the streets of the capital were loud with a noise he had not heard in years: bells, rung not for a funeral or a manoeuvre but for the peace, clumsy with joy, badly coordinated, parish answering parish across the rooftops. Men were chalking the date on walls. A woman had pinned paper flowers to the grille of a government window. Nobody looked at him twice, and he realized with a vertigo close to laughter that he had walked out of the tribunal into the first day of his life in which no one — no handler, no board, no colonel — owned the question of what he would do next.

He walked to the edge of the garrison district before he let himself stop, and there, at a corner where a baker's cart stood selling bread to a crowd that still behaved as if hunger might return tomorrow, he saw the cart.

Bess Arden's cart. Marsh mud still dried on the wheels, and the mare — the same impossible mare that had outrun a cavalry pursuit — dozing in the traces with the profound indifference of a veteran. And beside the cart, arguing cheerfully with a customer about the price of a loaf, arms flour-white to the elbow, stood Bess herself, in the capital, eighty miles from Marrowfield, delivering copies of the ledger to families who had lost men in the city regiments. Of course she was. She had organized a courier network out of a kitchen under occupation; a journey of eighty miles was nothing to her. Probably she had fed half the tribunal's clerks on the way and knew their mothers' names.

She saw him. Her argument stopped mid-sentence. She looked at his uniform — the full uniform, the last time he would ever wear it — and something moved across her broad, kind, unsentimental face that was not quite tears and not quite triumph.

"Well," she called out, loud enough for the whole street. "Look what the war's finished chewing. Are you going to stand there, or are you going to help me carry these up three flights to a widow on Cordwell Street who's been waiting six years to hear her boy's name?"

Reyes looked at the loaves stacked in the cart, each one holding its folded slip of paper, each slip holding a name, each name a small resurrection travelling under flour and crust. He pulled off his cap — his officer's cap, worn for the last hour of its existence — and tucked it under his arm like a man setting down a weight.

"Bess Arden," he said, "I have just resigned a major's commission to do exactly that."

Her laugh rang off the shopfronts. And as he lifted the first warm loaf from the cart, she leaned close, and the joy in her face folded into something urgent and low:

"Good. Because there's news from Marrowfield you'd best hear before you see them — about Wendell, and about the name on the last page. Get up on the cart. I'll tell you on the way."
