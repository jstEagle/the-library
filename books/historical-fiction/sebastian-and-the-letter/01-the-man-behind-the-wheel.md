## 1. The Man Behind the Wheel

The rain had been falling on London since Tuesday, and by Friday it had worked itself into the bones of the city, into the mortar of the terraces, the gravel of the mews, the shoulders of every man who shuffled past with his collar up and his eyes down. Quentin Fairweather drove through it the way she did everything: competently, quietly, and without leaving a mark.

She sat behind the wheel of the Daimler in the covered yard off Great Ormond Street, engine ticking as it cooled, gloved hands resting at ten and two out of habit even though the motor was off. Through the windshield the lamplight came down in ropes of gold, and the rain moved through those ropes like something alive. Somewhere beyond the wall a church bell counted seven, and beneath the bell, fainter, the sound of the city clearing its throat — trams, a newsboy crying the evening editions, a woman laughing in a doorway somewhere near the hospital.

*Quentin.* The name fit her now the way an old coat fits a body that has worn it long enough. It had been five years since anyone had called her anything else, and there were whole days — good days, busy days — when she did not feel the seams of it at all. She was Mr. Fairweather, chauffeur to Mrs. Aldous of Hampstead, late of the Women's Social and Political Union, still of the movement in every way that mattered. Mr. Fairweather was twenty-eight years old, clean-shaven by nature rather than effort, narrow through the hips, flat-voiced, forgettable. Mr. Fairweather tipped his cap to policemen and they tipped theirs back. Mr. Fairweather could stand in a crowd, could queue for bread, could walk home at midnight through streets full of demobbed soldiers, and no one's eyes would snag on him. Invisibility, Quentin had learned, was not the absence of being seen. It was the art of being seen *through*.

She had gotten very good at it. That was the trouble, some nights. That was exactly the trouble.

The kitchen door opened and Mrs. Aldous came down the steps with her coat half on, one arm hunting for its sleeve, her grey hair escaping its pins in the wind the way it always did within four minutes of her leaving any house. She was sixty-one and built like a sparrow, all sharp bone and quick movement, and she talked before she was fully through the door, as was also her habit.

"Fairweather. The Henderson letter has to go tonight — no, don't start the engine yet, listen first. There's been another arrest in Manchester. They took three women from a meeting at a chapel, if you can credit it, a *chapel*, and the Henderson woman is next on the list unless we move her correspondence out from under the floorboards before Sunday." She found her sleeve at last and shrugged herself into it. "You'll take it to the Holloway address. And you'll take the long way."

"The long way," Quentin repeated.

"The very long way. Constable Prewitt has developed an interest in our street. I'm told he's been asking after 'the lady with the motor car.' I told him the lady was my sister visiting from York." Mrs. Aldous's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "He seemed satisfied. Men usually are, when you give them a simple answer about a woman. It's the complicated ones that frighten them."

Quentin almost smiled too. Almost. "And the letters themselves?"

"In the case under the seat. Sealed, mostly. One or two not." Mrs. Aldous looked at her then — a look that lasted half a second longer than it needed to, that held something Quentin could never quite name, whether trust or warning or simply the particular weariness of a woman who had spent thirty years trusting people and burying the ones who'd failed the test. "Drive carefully, Mr. Fairweather. It's filthy out."

"It always is, ma'am."

"Yes," said Mrs. Aldous, turning back toward the house. "Isn't it lucky, then, that neither of us melts."

The door shut behind her, and Quentin was alone with the rain and the ticking engine and the leather case under the seat.

* * *

She should have driven straight to Holloway. She knew the route; she could have done it in forty minutes and been back by nine, dry, unremarkable, invisible. Instead she turned south along Gray's Inn Road and stopped in the lee of a closed ironmonger's, pulled the handbrake, and sat for a moment with her hands flat on her thighs, listening to the rain drum the hood like fingers on a table.

It was not stealing. That was what she told herself, and after five years the telling had gone smooth as river stone. The letters were going to be read anyway — by the Henderson woman, by whoever the Henderson woman trusted, by the police if things went badly. Reading them first changed nothing except who knew what, and knowledge, Quentin had come to believe, was the only property a person like her would ever hold free and clear. Her father's house had been taken from her when he died because the law said a daughter could not inherit where a nephew existed. Her name had been taken because a girl alone on the road from Shropshire to London in 1913 was prey, and a young man in a borrowed driving coat was merely poor. Everything else could be seized. But what she knew — what she carried folded into the lining of herself — no bailiff could touch.

She lifted the case onto her knees and worked the brass clasps.

Inside: perhaps fifteen envelopes, most sealed with wax or gum, addressed in hands ranging from schoolroom-round to furious-slanted. The topmost was from Manchester, dated the ninth, and it was not sealed at all — Mrs. Aldous had marked it with a pencil cross, meaning *read this yourself before you move it*. Quentin slid the page out and held it into the spill of the dashboard lamp.

*Dear Edith,* it began, in the furious-slanted hand. *They have closed the mill fund. You will have heard. What you will not have heard is how it was done — not by the owners but by the vicar, who stood up at the harvest supper and said the money would be better spent sending comforts to the boys at the front, and every man in that room nodded, and every woman looked down at her plate. I could have screamed. I did scream, afterwards, in the scullery, into the dishcloth, which is where we keep our screaming these days.*

Quentin read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the third time was not for the words but for the voice underneath them, the voice of a woman she had never met screaming into a dishcloth in a scullery in Manchester, and something in Quentin's chest turned over and lay down again, heavy.

Below it were the others. A note about railway timetables and which conductors could be trusted. An account, written in code so thin it was barely code — numbers standing in for names — of money collected and money owed. And near the bottom of the pile, wrapped in brown paper though it bore no address at all, a single sheet in a hand she recognized without knowing why: small, upright, controlled, the writing of someone who had learned penmanship from a governess and discipline from something harsher.

*E — The situation at H— is deteriorating. He is failing faster than the doctors allow him to know. The heir has already begun. Yesterday he dismissed the housemaid — you know which one — and had her trunk carried out while she was at market. No reference. No character. She has a child, E. He says the child must be "dealt with" and I confess I no longer understand what he means by the word. If anything can be done, it must be done soon. After the funeral it will be too late, and everyone will agree it was always necessary.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Quentin sat with the paper trembling slightly in her gloved hands and felt the rain close around the motor like a held breath.

*Everyone will agree it was always necessary.*

She knew that sentence. Not the words — the architecture of it. She had heard its twin spoken across her aunt's dinner table in 1913, in voices of perfect reasonableness, about a girl who had asked one question too many: *it's for the best, really; she was always a difficult creature; in a year no one will remember why we made such a fuss.* And then the girl had cut her hair with sewing scissors by candlelight and walked eleven miles to the station with a carpet bag and become, somewhere between Shropshire and Euston, a young man named Quentin, and no one had ever made a fuss about her again.

The letter went back into its brown paper. The brown paper went back into the case. Quentin latched the clasps and sat with her palms pressed flat against the cold leather, and outside the ironmonger's window the rain came down and down, and a policeman passed the motor without a glance, seeing through her as easily as light through glass, and she thought: *I am the safest woman in England, and I am nowhere at all.*

She started the engine. She drove the long way to Holloway, and delivered the case, and tipped her cap to the woman who answered the door, and drove home through the wet gold dark. And all the while, in the back of her mind where she filed the secrets she could not put down, the unsigned letter sat glowing like a coal.

*A child. He says the child must be dealt with.*

She did not yet know whose child. She did not yet know that in six days' time the answer would be left on her doorstep in a storm, wrapped in a blanket, with a note in that same small upright hand. She only knew that somewhere in England a maid was walking away from a great house with no reference and no character, carrying whatever she carried, and that somewhere behind her a boy of perhaps two years old was already being erased — smoothly, reasonably, with everyone agreeing it was always necessary — and that no one, no one at all, was keeping the record.

Quentin parked the Daimler in the yard. She checked the lamp, checked the tyres, wiped the rain from the windscreen with the chamois, and performed each small ritual of her small safe life.

Then she stood a moment longer in the dark, looking up at the water running down the wall, and thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than any constable ever had:

*I could keep it. If it were ever given to me to keep — I could keep the record.*

The kitchen light went out above her. The bell of St. George's struck ten.
