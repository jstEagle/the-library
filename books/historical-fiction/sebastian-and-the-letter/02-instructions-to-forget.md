## 2. Instructions to Forget

The storm came up the river like something with intent.

Quentin felt it before she saw it—that pressure behind the ears, the way the gas lamps along the Embankment guttered in their glass cages, as though London itself were drawing breath. She had been driving Mrs. Aldous home from a committee meeting in Marylebone, the Daimler's tyres hissing through standing water, when the first thunder rolled across the rooftops and Mrs. Aldous had said, without looking up from her papers, "It will be a bad night. Take care going back, Fairweather."

Fairweather. The name fit her now like the gloves she wore—supple, broken in, no longer pinching. Three years since she had cut her hair with sewing scissors before a cracked mirror in a Whitby boarding house, three years since she had practised saying *yes sir, no sir* in a voice pitched low and flat until it stopped feeling like a costume. Quentin Fairweather, chauffeur. A man of no particular consequence, reliable, forgettable. She had built herself out of exactly those materials: reliability, unremarkableness, the grey wool of being overlooked.

She was good at her work. That was the trouble with becoming invisible—you got asked to carry things.

Not just parcels. Letters. Mrs. Aldous's correspondence moved through Quentin's hands the way blood moves through a heart, and once or twice a week, on nights when the office was empty and the rain made a curtain of the windows, she read what she carried. She told herself it was prudence—a driver ought to know which houses were safe, which streets watched. She told herself it was loyalty. But alone in her lodgings over the garage in Pimlico, lamplight trimmed low, she spread the letters across her counterpane like a fortune-teller's cards and read them for the pleasure of knowing things, because knowledge was the one possession her old life had never let her keep.

That night, the last delivery was to a house in Belgravia.

* * *

The instructions had come by telephone that afternoon, filtered through the office girl: a package to be collected from a solicitor's clerk at the Cadogan Hotel side entrance, at nine o'clock precisely, and conveyed to an address in Chelsea that Quentin did not recognise. No name given. Payment in advance. It was not unusual—during the war half of London had learned to move its secrets after dark—and Quentin thought nothing of it beyond noting that the clerk who handed over the wrapped parcel would not meet her eye, and that his fingers shook, though whether from cold or conscience she could not say.

The parcel was small. Heavy for its size. Wrapped in brown paper and tied with butcher's string, and it sat on the seat beside her all the way down Sloane Street making no sound at all, which should have told her something, because packages do not usually breathe.

The address in Chelsea was a tall narrow house wedged between two taller ones, black with rain, its windows blind except for one on the second floor where a lamp burned behind yellow curtains. Quentin parked in the shadow of a plane tree and sat for a moment with the engine ticking, watching the rain come down in ropes, and told herself the unease crawling up her spine was only tiredness.

She took the parcel under her coat to keep it dry—which meant she felt it shift against her ribs, a small warm weight turning over—and stood very still on the pavement with the water running off her cap brim while the truth assembled itself, piece by terrible piece, in her chest.

She climbed the steps and knocked.

The door opened before the second knock landed. A woman stood there, middle-aged, plainly dressed, holding a candle in a dish that threw her face into planes of shadow. Behind Quentin the thunder broke directly overhead, close enough to feel in the teeth, and in the lightning's white flare the woman's expression was naked terror.

"You're the driver." Not a question. Her voice was low and urgent. "You came from—she said you'd come. You're to take him tonight. Now."

"Him," Quentin said.

The woman stepped back from the doorway—an invitation, or a surrender—and Quentin, God help her, went in.

* * *

The hall smelled of beeswax and boiled mutton and something else underneath, something medicinal, laudanum perhaps, or the sweetish rot of illness long tended. The woman—Mrs. Prewitt, she said, though whether it was her own name Quentin doubted—led her up two flights of stairs, the candle guttering with every draught, and talked in a rapid whisper without once looking back.

"The master's dying, you understand. Weeks, they say, perhaps days. And the family—" she stopped at a landing, pressed her ear to a door, listened to the rain. "The family has plans for the child. *Plans.* I've served this house eleven years and I'll not stand by while they— he's innocent, sir, whatever the circumstances of him."

"Mrs. Prewitt," Quentin said carefully, "I think there's been a mistake. I'm here to deliver a parcel. I don't know anything about a child."

The woman turned then, and in the candlelight her face was wet—not with rain, Quentin saw, but tears, shed silently sometime in the last hours and never wiped away. "You're the only name I was given," she whispered. "A driver. Fairweather, they said. Trustworthy. Oh, God forgive me, but I've no one else."

And she opened the door.

The nursery—for it had been a nursery once, decades ago, its wallpaper faded to ghosts of circus animals—was dim and warm. A fire had been laid but was burning low. In a cot against the wall, a child slept: a boy, perhaps eighteen months old, with dark hair curling damply at his temples and one fist curled under his chin, breathing the deep untroubled breaths of the very young who do not yet know what is being decided about them.

On the cot's rail lay a folded note, weighted down with a hairbrush.

Mrs. Prewitt took it up and pressed it into Quentin's hand with both of hers, as if transferring a sacrament. "He was left on our step six months ago," she whispered. "A maid's child. His lordship knows—he knows, and there's been a letter, a private letter, and now that he's dying the heir has sent men round asking questions, and tomorrow—" her voice cracked. "Tomorrow a doctor comes. For the child. I heard them arranging it. There's a place in Yorkshire. An institution. Children go into those places and do not come out again, sir. They go in and the paper says one thing and the truth says another, and nobody ever asks after them because officially they never were."

Quentin looked at the sleeping boy. Something moved in her chest, vast and slow, like ice shifting on a river.

"I can't," she heard herself say. "I'm a chauffeur. I have lodgings the size of a coffin. I know nothing of children."

"You knew enough to come," Mrs. Prewitt said. "That's more than anyone else in London knows enough to do tonight."

* * *

Later—much later, though the clock in the hall claimed it was barely midnight—Quentin stood in her rooms above the garage with the boy asleep in a drawer lined with her spare shirts, and read the note by the light of the lamp she had learned to shade with her body, out of habit, out of three years of reading other people's secrets.

The paper was good stock. Cream, heavy, the kind that cost more per sheet than Quentin earned in a day. The handwriting was slanted, assured, done quickly but by a hand accustomed to being obeyed:

*Forget the face.*

Nothing else. No signature. Two words and a full stop, and beneath the words the ghost of a crest where a seal had been broken away, leaving only the impression of a boar's head in wax dust.

Quentin sat down slowly on the edge of the bed.

She knew that handwriting.

She knew it the way you know a voice heard once through a wall—instantly, completely, with your whole skin. Eight months ago, a Wednesday in March, she had sat in this same room with a stolen hour and a bundle of Mrs. Aldous's intercepted correspondence, and one letter among them had been different from the rest—colder, better paper, a man's hand, all economy and threat. A letter concerning a certain family's arrangements for certain inconvenient persons, and the sums paid to ensure silence, and the phrase *the matter must be concluded discreetly*, which Quentin had copied into her notebook because some animal part of her had understood, even then, that such phrases were the hinges on which people's lives swung shut.

The heir. The handwriting belonged to the heir—the son of the dying lord whose house Mrs. Prewitt had fled, whose money bought doctors for institutions and silence for servants, whose seal was a boar's head and whose pen wrote *forget the face* as easily as another man might write *yours faithfully*.

The child was not a burden dropped on her by accident. The child was evidence. A living, breathing, dark-haired piece of evidence that someone powerful wished erased, and the note in her hand was not instructions—it was an alibi, pre-written, ready to be produced later if anyone ever asked: *see, we told her to forget him; whatever became of the boy, she disobeyed, she is to blame.*

Her hands were shaking. She set the note on the table and pinned it there under the lamp base, and looked at it for a long time while the storm worried at the roof tiles overhead.

She should burn it. Every instinct honed by three years of survival said so: destroy the evidence, return the child to the church steps of some distant parish, be a chauffeur again, be nobody, be safe.

Instead she found herself crossing to the drawer.

The boy had kicked free of his blanket. She drew it up over him, careful, the way you'd handle something rescued from a fire, and he stirred and opened his eyes—dark eyes, absurdly solemn—and regarded her for a long moment with the frank appraisal of the very young. Then he reached up one small hand and closed it around her finger, and held on.

Quentin stood perfectly still in her shabby room above the garage, in her dead brother's name, in her borrowed life, while a child she had known for four hours held onto her finger as though he had already decided the matter and required no further discussion.

"They want you forgotten," she whispered. The rain filled the pause. "I know how that feels."

He did not answer, of course. He simply held on, and somewhere in the walls the pipes knocked and settled, and Quentin understood that her quiet life had ended tonight, at half past midnight, in a Chelsea nursery, and that whatever came next would begin with a single question she could not yet answer: why did the handwriting on that note belong to a man whose letters Mrs. Aldous had been intercepting since spring?

She tucked the note into the tin box where she kept everything she had ever sworn to remember, and slept, fitfully, with one hand resting on the edge of the drawer.

In the morning, she would take it to the letters.
