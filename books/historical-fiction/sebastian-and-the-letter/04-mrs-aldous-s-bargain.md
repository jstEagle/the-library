## 4. Mrs. Aldous's Bargain

The boy had a name. I learned it the way one learns anything in wartime—accidentally, at three in the morning, from a scrap of linen sewn into his collar: *S——, b. 1916.* The rest of the thread had been picked out, but not thoroughly enough. Someone had been in a hurry, or someone had hesitated, and either way the needlework told me what the note in my pocket would not: that he had been named once, by someone who loved him, and that the naming was meant to be undone.

I called him "little man" and he called me nothing at all, because he could not yet manage the shape of *Quentin* and I dared not teach him *Miss Fairweather* or any feminine thing. He said *Ten-Tin* instead, which was its own kind of secret, sealed between us like wax.

For six days we lived as carefully as smugglers. I fed him bread softened in milk, taught him to be quiet when boots sounded on the stairs, and kept the curtains of my lodgings drawn against the November light, which seemed too honest for what we were doing. At night he slept curled against my back like a coal against a dying fire, taking warmth without apology, and I lay awake listening to his breath and to the city beyond the glass—the trams grinding their last runs, the sirens that no longer meant Zeppelins but still made my heart stumble—and I understood with terrible clarity that I had crossed some border in myself. There was a before-him and an after-him now. I had a face worth forgetting only so long as no one loved it.

Mrs. Aldous came on the seventh day.

She did not knock. She never knocked; knocking, she said, was a courtesy extended to people with nothing to hide. I heard her cane strike the landing—*tak, tak, tak*, metronome of my conscience—and had exactly four seconds to decide whether to lie to her, which was always a losing proposition, or to open the door.

I opened the door. Behind me, in the narrow room, the boy sat on the floor pushing a wooden spoon through the bars of a chair, making engine noises, because everything in his world was an engine just then.

Mrs. Aldous looked past me. Her eyes were grey and quick and missed nothing, having spent forty years missing nothing in a country determined to overlook her. They went from my face to the child's to the milk pan drying on the sill to the small wool sock steaming by the grate, and I watched her assemble the whole arithmetic of the scene in the time it took me to say, "Madam."

"Well," she said. "You've been busy."

"Madam, I can explain—"

"I don't pay you to explain. I pay you to drive." She stepped inside, uninvited, because doors were things that happened to other people. "But since you've apparently taken up fostering, you may explain while I sit down. That chair is damp. The other one."

I gave her the dry chair. She settled into it with her cane across her knees like a rifle, and the boy looked up at her and offered her the spoon, handle first, with the grave generosity of the very young.

Mrs. Aldous took it. She examined it as though it were a document requiring authentication, then handed it back. "How old?"

"Nearer two than one, I'd guess. He doesn't say much. There's stitching in his collar where a name was cut out."

"And the note."

I gave it to her. *Forget the face.* She read it twice, tilting it toward the window's thin light, and something moved behind her eyes—not surprise. Recognition. "This hand," she said slowly, "I've seen this hand."

"So have I." My mouth had gone dry. "Among the letters. Last spring. A correspondence about—settlements. Payments made quiet. I filed it under Ashworth, the solicitor's name, because the writing signed itself with initials only. R.A."

"Ashworth." She set the note down on her knee and pressed it flat with two fingers, as though pinning a moth. "Ashworth is the man who keeps Lord Vane's accounts."

The room went very still. Even the boy seemed to stop his spoon mid-engine.

"You know," Mrs. Aldous said, watching me, "for a woman pretending to be a man, you are a remarkably poor liar. You already knew whose child this is."

"I suspected."

"Suspecting is knowing wearing a coat. Tell me."

So I told her—all of it, the cross-referenced letters, the maid called Ellen Marsh who had written three desperate notes before her correspondence stopped mid-sentence, the heir Mr. Cyril Vane whose signature appeared on receipts for silences, the dying lord in his house in Kensington with his lungs filling like a tide coming in. I told her about the collar, the picked thread, the note in a hand I knew. And when I finished she was silent long enough that I could hear the boy breathing and the fog pressing against the glass and my own heart conducting its foolish, frightened business.

"You understand what they'll do," she said at last. Not a question. "If they find him. There'll be no inquest. No inquiry. He'll go to an institution under another name, and the paper trail will burn, and in twenty years there will be a man walking around England who doesn't know he exists. That's not murder. It's tidier than murder. It's *editing*."

"Yes, madam."

"Don't call me madam when we're conspiring. It makes me feel like a bishop." She leaned forward. "Here is what will happen, Quentin. You cannot keep him here. This building leaks tenants the way a colander leaks water; by Friday the whole street will know you've a child and no wife, and questions follow gossip like gulls follow trawlers. He must be moved somewhere safe. Safe costs money, and safe requires cover, and cover—" she tapped the note on her knee "—requires leverage. Which brings me to my offer."

My stomach dropped ahead of my understanding, the way it does stepping off a curb in the dark. "Offer, madam."

"*Constance,*" she said irritably, "when we're conspiring." Then, briskly: "Armistice week. Whenever it comes—it may come tomorrow, it may come at Christmas, but the whole country is holding its breath like a kettle about to sing. When it sings, there will be chaos. Crowds, bells, drunkenness, and every policeman in London pulled toward Whitehall and the parks. Chaos is a door, Quentin, and we are going to walk through it. I need documents moved. Papers. Testimony. Things that cannot travel by post because the post is read, and cannot travel by train because the trains are watched, and cannot stay where they are because the rooms they're in are going to be searched."

"How many checkpoints?"

"That depends on how drunk the celebrants are and how nervous their officers." She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a general reviewing terrain. "Three that I know of between here and Limehouse. Possibly five. There's a printer there—a widow named Okafor—who owes the movement more than she can easily repay, and she has a cellar. The papers go in the cellar. You go back overland before dawn."

"And if I'm stopped."

"If you're stopped, you're a driver running petrol errands during the celebrations. Men aren't stopped during armistice week, Quentin; men are *waved through*. That disguise of yours, which costs you so much—" she gestured at me with the cane, not unkindly, but not gently either "—is worth more than you know. It is a passport. I'm asking you to spend it."

There it was. Laid on the table between the milk pan and the cold grate like a card turned face-up. Spend it. Every day for seven years I had paid out small coins of myself to keep the larger hoard hidden—a lowered voice here, a bound chest there, a name spoken flatly until it stopped stinging—and I had believed the account inexhaustible because I had never been asked for a withdrawal all at once. Now she wanted the whole balance. One run, one search, one pair of hands too thorough, and Quentin Fairweather the man would come apart in a police station under a gas lamp, and everything after that would be wreckage: the charge sheets, the newspapers, the boy taken by strangers with clean gloves and good intentions.

And yet.

I looked at the boy. He had lost interest in our war council and resumed his spoon-engine against the chair leg, *brm-brm-brm*, soft and relentless, a small percussion section accompanying our negotiations. He had a bruise on his forehead shaped like a thumbprint, fading yellow, from whatever life he'd led before mine. He had a name cut out of his collar. He had, as far as the world's paperwork was concerned, no existence at all—which meant the world could do anything to him and call it nothing.

I had been that person. I had been edited once already; I knew the operation intimately, every stitch of it. And I knew what it cost to survive as an omission.

"The papers," I said. "Whose testimony?"

"Women who worked in Vane's houses. Ellen Marsh's sister. A nurse from the Kensington household who wants to speak before she loses her nerve or her lord dies, whichever comes first." Mrs. Aldous rose, using the cane, her joints cracking like green kindling. "The heir is buying up witnesses, Quentin. Paying them off, scaring them off, and when money fails, other methods. Every day that passes, the record gets thinner. We are in a race against a shredder. So: the papers move armistice week, and you move them, and in exchange the movement hides your boy so well that even God will need a subpoena to find him."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll help you anyway, because I'm not a monster, merely a strategist." She pulled on her gloves, finger by finger. "But you should know that refusing won't save you. They've sent men already, haven't they? I saw one across the street when I arrived. Grey hat, reading the same page of the same newspaper for ten minutes. They know where you live. Refusing only means you'll be caught standing still."

She was right, and we both knew she was right, and the knowledge sat in my chest like swallowed river water.

"I'll do it," I said.

"Of course you will." She allowed herself one moment of something softer—her hand, briefly, on the boy's head, ruffling the fine dark hair with surprising tenderness before retreating behind wool and purpose. "He's a pretty little thing. Stubborn jaw. Vane jaw, worse luck. Get him a new one before the photographs start."

At the door she paused, back to me, cane poised.

"Quentin. Armistice week will feel like the world being born. Bells, flags, strangers kissing in the streets. Remember, underneath all that noise, that some histories get written in ink and some get written in blood, and the difference is usually who held the pen first." She rapped the doorframe twice—*tak, tak*—and descended.

The boy banged his spoon. Outside, the grey-hatted man folded his newspaper at last and walked away, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world and all of it mine to fear.

Two days later, at eleven minutes past eleven on the eleventh day of the eleventh month, the guns along the Western Front fell silent, and London began to scream with joy, and I put the satchel of testimony beneath the seat of the car and buckled it down, and drove into the crowds toward Limehouse with my heart hammering like the church bells, past the first police checkpoint where a young constable waved me through with tears on his cheeks—

—and at the second checkpoint, near the Tower, the officer who stepped into the road was not crying, was not celebrating, was wearing a grey hat, and was holding up one gloved hand for me to stop.
