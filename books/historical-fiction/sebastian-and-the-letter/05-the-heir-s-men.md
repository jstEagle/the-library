## 5. The Heir's Men

The boarding house on Rivington Street kept its secrets the way old women kept teeth—fewer each year, and none of them reliable. Quentin had chosen it precisely for that decay: a landlady half-blind with cataract, a register written in three different inks and no consistent spelling of her own assumed name. Fairweather, Q., gentleman driver. She had signed it in her best masculine hand, the one she'd practiced against the mirror until her wrist forgot its own sex.

She took the back room on the third floor, the one that smelled of coal smoke and mouse, and told the landlady she was convalescing from gas at Passchendaele—a lie that purchased privacy better than money could. Soldiers were not questioned. Soldiers were pitied, fed, and left alone.

Sebastian had learned to sleep through anything by then. The boy could doze through the rattle of carts, the shriek of the milk train, the midnight arguments below about ration cards and absent sons. But he woke when the knock came at eleven o'clock on Tuesday night—three slow knocks, deliberate as a metronome—and sat up in his cot with his eyes wide open, listening the way animals listen.

Quentin was already awake. She was always awake now.

"Hush," she breathed, and gathered him against her chest before he could make a sound. He weighed almost nothing still. Under the thin blanket his heartbeat fluttered like a bird held too tightly.

Below, footsteps on the stairs. Two sets. The heavy tread paused at the second floor long enough to ask something of the widow Crane, whose answer came back muffled and obliging. Then they climbed again.

Quentin pressed her lips to the boy's hair and thought, with terrible clarity: *they are reading the register.*

The third-floor corridor was short. There was nowhere to go except the window, and beyond the window four storeys of soot-slick brick dropping to the yard. She had looked at that window every night since she arrived, measuring it the way she'd once measured the garden wall behind her father's house, the night she'd climbed out of her girlhood with nothing but a valise and a pair of stolen trousers. Some escapes you rehearse without meaning to. The body keeps its own archives.

The knocks came at her door.

"Mr. Fairweather." A voice like wet gravel. "Mr. Quentin Fairweather? We're looking for a man of that name."

She said nothing. Sebastian's breath warmed her collarbone.

"We understand you drive motorcars. We've questions regarding employment. There's a position. Good wages."

*Good wages.* The oldest lure in London, older than the war, older than lies. Quentin eased toward the window with the boy bundled tight and worked the sash up an inch at a time, praying to whatever god governed chauffeurs and thieves that the frame would not cry out.

"We know you're in there," said the gravel voice, softer now. "We know you have something that isn't yours."

The sash gave. Cold November air poured over her hands.

What happened next she would remember always in fragments: the drop onto the coal shed roof that jarred her teeth; the slide down its slope into the frozen mud of the yard; Sebastian's fist knotted into her collar, silent, trusting, which frightened her more than any scream would have. She went over the back fence where the boards were rotten, ran two streets east through rain that came down like judgment, and did not stop until she stood beneath the gas lamp outside the pie shop on Cheshire Street, where Ada Brill was stacking chairs for the night.

Ada took one look at her—mud to the knees, a child under her coat, terror written plainly across a face that was supposed to pass for a man's—and did not ask a single question.

"Through the back," she said. "There's a kettle on."

* * *

Ada Brill sold pies and eels and, unofficially, silence. Her shop smelled of vinegar and pepper and the sweet burnt sugar of apples going soft, and in the eleven days since Quentin had first stumbled in half-frozen with a hungry toddler, it had become the only place in London where her shoulders dropped from around her ears.

"You'll stay in my back room tonight," Ada said now, setting a mug of tea strong enough to strip paint beside Quentin's elbow. "And tomorrow we'll find you somewhere better. That boarding house is finished. They'll be back."

"They followed me from the house on Cheyne Row." Quentin's voice was low. Sebastian sat on her knee, gnawing solemnly on a heel of bread, watching everything with those grave grey eyes. "They know I took the letters. They know what I am."

"Do they?" Ada asked, wiping down the counter with unhurried strokes. "Or do they only know what they think they're looking for?"

Quentin stared at her.

"I've served gentlemen in this shop for thirty years," Ada said. "You learn to see what a man is carrying before he says a word. You—" she nodded at Quentin, at the mud, at the boy—"are carrying more than most. And hiding worse than most. Neither of which is my business." She set the cloth down. "But whoever sent those men thinks the child is theirs to dispose of. That *is* my business, because no one disposes of children in my parish."

Something loosened in Quentin's chest—not safety, nothing so generous as safety, but the beginning of it. The feeling of a rope thrown across water.

"The heir's name is Vane," she said. "Julian Vane. His uncle is dying in Bedford Square, and the old man left something behind that Mr. Vane cannot permit to exist. A son."

Ada's hands stilled on the counter.

"And the mother?"

"A maid. Sent away. I believe she's dead—the letters stopped, and the payments stopped, and men like Vane don't stop paying unless there's no one left to pay." Quentin smoothed Sebastian's hair. "The boy is the proof, Ada. His face is the proof. That note they pinned to his blanket said forget the face. They meant it literally. If the child vanishes quietly, the whole story dies with him."

"And if he doesn't vanish?"

"Then there has to be paper. Names, dates, testimony—something in an archive somewhere that a lawyer can't burn." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "I spent six years filing other people's secrets, thinking paper was power. Now I'm learning how easily paper burns."

"Not all of it," Ada said. "Not if enough people hold copies." She untied her apron. "My sister works at the inquiry office for war orphans. There's a registry. Official. They can't burn official paper without questions being asked—questions even a Vane can't buy his way past." She looked at Sebastian a long moment. "But that's a bridge for another day. Tonight you sleep. Tomorrow, Mrs. Aldous. You'll need more than a pie seller between you and that family."

* * *

They moved at two in the morning, because Ada insisted that anything worth moving was worth moving while honest people slept. Sebastian rode in a market basket lined with Ada's good shawl, drowsing, one hand curled around a wooden horse that Ada produced from somewhere as if by magic. Quentin walked beside them in her driver's cap and greatcoat, and Ada walked on her other side, broad and unbothered, a woman carrying eels to Billingsgate, nothing unusual, move along.

The city was beautiful at that hour in the way only exhausted cities are—the fog lying along the rooftops like a sleeping animal, the lamps haloed, the river smell threading through every street. Armistice bunting already hung in shop windows further west; here in the East End the celebration would be quieter, a drink raised, a name spoken. Quentin found herself memorizing it anyway: the fog, the basket, the weight of the boy's sleeping breath. In case this was the last quiet hour. In case the history being written elsewhere had no room for nights like this one.

At the corner of Whitechapel High Street a motorcar idled with its lamps hooded. Quentin saw it and pulled them both into a doorway, heart hammering. Two figures in the front seats. One of them lit a cigarette, and the match flared long enough to show a face she had studied across intercepted correspondence for weeks: lean, precise, a scar through one eyebrow.

Mr. Merrick. The heir's right hand.

He wasn't looking for them. He was waiting for someone else—a woman came hurrying out of the fog, handed him an envelope, and was gone again. He read it by matchlight, and even at that distance Quentin saw his expression change. Satisfaction, cold and complete, like a man closing a ledger.

"What did he get?" Ada whispered.

Quentin watched the car pull away, watched its hooded lamps shrink into the grey. Her mind, that tireless clerk's mind, was already turning pages: *who knew about the boarding house, who could write such a note, who had seen the boy.*

"I don't know," she said. "But he knows something. And whatever it is, he got it tonight."

She looked down into the basket. Sebastian slept on, his thumb in his mouth, the wooden horse loose in his grip, entirely unaware that half of London's machinery was slowly turning itself toward finding him.

Ada put a steadying hand on her shoulder. "Come on," she said. "Mrs. Aldous will want to hear this before dawn."

And Quentin, lifting the basket, understood that the hunt had changed shape. They were no longer following her trail.
