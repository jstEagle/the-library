## 1. The Weight of Sorts

There are forty-one ways to say nothing in a print shop, and I had learned every one of them by the time I was nine.

The first is the silence of the hand: when my father pauses over a letter he is cutting and his thumb covers the face of it, you do not ask what letter it was. The second is the silence of the floorboards: there is one plank by the paper store that speaks if you step on it wrong, so you learn to walk like a thought passing through a room. The third belongs to customers — when a man in a gray coat asks what we print for whom, Father answers "almanacs," and you nod as though almanacs were the only thing ever set in type in the history of ink.

I could go on. I have, in fact, gone on, in my head, on slow afternoons, cataloguing them the way other girls catalogue suitors or ribbons. Silence number fourteen: never hum while the committee's criers pass in the street, because humming means contentment and contentment is a thing they like to tax. Silence number twenty-two: when Mother's name comes up — which it rarely does, twelve years being long enough to wear even grief smooth — you look busy. Silence number thirty, the hardest one: you do not ask about the river.

That morning in late autumn, the year everything changed, silences one through thirty were all in use at once.

* * *

The shop smelled of tallow and metal shavings and the particular cold sweetness of old paper, which is the smell of things waiting to be said. My father, Aldering Nakamura — half the city assumed Aldering was our surname and Nakamura some exotic given name, and neither he nor I ever corrected them; that was silence number six — sat at the graver's bench with his shoulders curved around a piece of steel like a man sheltering a candle flame from wind. He had been cutting the same lowercase *g* for the better part of an hour, which meant either that the *g* was very important or that he did not want to be interrupted, and with Father these were usually the same thing.

I sat across from him at the second bench, working through a tray of sorts that needed their faces dressed. It is patient work, minding type. You hold each little column of lead up to the light and look for the flaws — the nicked shoulder, the clogged counter, the hairline crack that will spit ink into a white space and ruin a page and possibly a reputation. People think printing is about words. It isn't. Printing is about the spaces between words, and about knowing which pieces of metal are quietly broken.

"You're holding that *e* too tight," my father said without looking up. "You'll warm the face."

"The face can't feel anything, Papa."

"Everything feels how it's held." He said it mildly, the way he said most true things, as though truth were a household chore rather than an event.

I loosened my grip. Outside, Vellamare was doing its November impression: gulls arguing over the fish market leavings, cart wheels complaining on the wet cobbles, the river running brown and fast beneath the bridge with all the urgency of something that intended to keep going whether anyone approved or not. Our shop sits on Tanner's Row, three doors up from the water stairs, close enough to hear the ferry bells. I had grown up believing those bells were the sound of safety — proof, rung twice hourly, that the world still connected us to itself.

"Father." I turned a sort in my fingers. "Marta Voss says her cousin's regiment has orders for the bridge gates. Chains, this time, not just sentries. For the winter."

The graver stopped.

It stopped for perhaps two seconds — no longer than a man takes between heartbeats when he decides to take another one — but in our shop, two seconds of stopped work was a paragraph. Then the graver resumed, shaving a curl of steel off the *g*'s ear as thin as onion skin, and my father said, in a voice of perfect, practiced blandness:

"Marta Voss says a great many things. Last spring she told me the Corvane estates were selling off their horses. Did the Corvanes sell their horses?"

"I don't know."

"There you are, then." Shave. Curl. "Almanacs," he added, apropos of nothing, which was our code for *this conversation is over*, and also for *I love you*, depending on the decade.

But his thumb had come up to cover the face of the letter again, and I pretended not to see, because pretending not to see was silence number one, and I was fluent.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about growing up inside a trade: you learn to read people the way you read type, flaw by flaw, holding each one up to the light.

My father's flaws were beautiful ones, mostly. He cut letters so fine that compositors in three districts asked for Aldering Nakamura's sorts by name, because a well-cut letter sets clean and prints honest — his phrase, printed honest, though he had never once in his life printed anything more dangerous than a wedding announcement. His hands were enormous and precise and slightly stained blue-black at the knuckles, permanently, as though ink had decided to claim him the way tide claims a seawall. He whistled off-key when he thought no one could hear, always the same four bars of a song Mother used to sing, and he stopped mid-phrase whenever the door opened, so that our whole childhood was scored to interrupted music.

And he was afraid. Not loudly. Fear in our house was not a fire but a dampness, invisible, everywhere, getting into everything. I had been breathing it since before I knew there was any other air.

The cause of the dampness had a shape, though the shape was never named. It lived across the river, where the orchards started — the Nakamura orchard, my mother had called it once, exactly once, in a voice like a door opening onto a lit room and then closing very quickly. I was seven. I asked what she meant. My mother smiled at me with her whole face and said, "Hand me the burnishing cloth, little bird," and I did, and that was the end of the Nakamura orchard for eleven years, until it wasn't, but I'm getting ahead of myself, and a good compositor never gets ahead of himself. You set the line you're given, in order, and you trust the story to hold its own weight.

That morning, the story arrived in barrels.

* * *

At half past ten, the knock came — three raps, then two, the freight signal — and Ferro the ink-man's boy staggered in under the autumn shipment, cheeks red as sealing wax. Four barrels of lampblack stock, a keg of varnish, and, wedged between them like contraband, a parcel of Dutch paper that Father had been waiting on since summer.

"Ferro says the quay's crowded," the boy announced, dumping news the way he dumped goods, without sorting. "Troop barges at the arsenal stairs. And down at Bridge Square there's men measuring the gateposts. Measuring them, miss! With chains laid out ready, coiled up like snakes. Old Ferro says last time they measured like that was the hard winter, and you remember what happened then."

Nobody remembered what happened then out loud, because what happened then was that the bridge closed for five months and the east bank ate its seed grain and the west bank pretended not to notice, and both banks learned things about themselves that they preferred to leave unlearned.

"Thank you, Piet," my father said. "Mind the threshold plank."

The boy stepped over silence-number-two's plank with the unconscious grace of the young, took his coin, and clattered out into the noise of the street, carrying his news to the next pair of ears. Father stood among his barrels with his hands flat on the lid of the nearest one, and I watched him do arithmetic I couldn't see. Ink was money. Paper was money. A closed bridge meant no shipments, no customers from the far bank, no commissions from the Corvane printing office — not that we'd taken Corvane commissions in years, another silence, number nineteen, *we don't discuss why the biggest patron on the west bank stopped sending work* — and winter coming on like a debt collector.

"Four barrels will see us to spring," he said finally, to the barrel, or to me, or possibly to the river. "If we're careful."

"When have we ever not been careful?"

He almost smiled. "That is the one thing we have never once been, little bird. Careful is a performance. We are something else entirely."

"What's the word, then?"

He considered the question with the seriousness he gave all questions, even the ones he wouldn't answer. "Practiced," he said at last. "We are practiced. Now fetch the varnish key before I misplace my entire personality into this barrel."

* * *

We worked through the noon bells. That was the rhythm of the shop, and rhythm, I have come to believe, is just silence made useful. Father cut new faces for a commission — a bookseller wanted a title page in a style "modern but respectful," which is the typographic equivalent of asking for a portrait that flatters — and I sorted and dressed and distributed, moving trays of type with the ease of long habit, my fingers reading the nicks in the shanks the way a blind woman reads a page.

Somewhere past the second bell, the street noise changed.

You'd know it anywhere, that change — the way market murmur goes suddenly glassy, the way everyone starts walking purposefully in the same direction, which in Vellamare means toward whatever they've been told not to look at. I went to the window. Father didn't tell me not to. That was its own kind of permission, and I noted it, filed it under the growing category of things my father permitted lately that he would have forbidden a year ago.

Down Tanner's Row, people were drifting toward the water stairs, and beyond them, visible in gaps between rooftops, Bridge Square was filling. I could see the gateposts — twin stone pillars older than anyone's grandmother, carved with river gods whose faces had been worn to suggestions — and I could see the soldiers, and I could see the chains, coiled and gleaming and patient as Piet had said, like snakes indeed, or like punctuation. Like the beginning of a very long sentence that no one would be allowed to finish.

And on the far bank, at the head of the far stairs, stood an officer in a gray-blue coat.

Even at that distance — the river is wide, the light was flat — something about him held still in the middle of all that movement. Everyone else on the far bank was hurrying or crowding or gawking. He simply stood, facing our side, and though it is absurd — I know it is absurd; I have had twelve years to know it — I felt looked at. Picked out of the crowd of the entire west bank by a pair of eyes I could not possibly have seen, let alone met.

"Isandro." My father's voice, quiet, from behind me. Not sharp. Quiet, which was worse.

"He's watching the bridge," I said.

"They always watch the bridge. It's what officers are for." He came and stood beside me, not touching me, our shoulders a careful inch apart, and we watched together for a moment — the chains, the crowd, the still figure on the far stairs. "Away from the window now. There's work."

"Papa. If they close it—"

"If they close it, the river becomes a wall, and walls are only terrible if you were planning to cross." He said it lightly. He turned back toward his bench. But I saw his hand, halfway there, hesitate over the tray of finished sorts — hover, actually hover, above one particular compartment, the small deep one at the end where he kept the private punches and matrices that no apprentice was ever allowed to touch — before continuing on to the varnish shelf as though nothing had happened.

I filed that away too. Silences, I've said, come in dozens. But hesitations — hesitations are rarer, and worth more.

* * *

That evening, after the shutters were latched and the stove had been fed and the shop below us had gone dark except for the pilot light Father kept burning over the stone sink, I sat in our rooms above the shop and listened to the river.

You can hear it at night when the wind is right — a low continuous voice, patient, saying the same thing over and over the way rivers do. All my life that voice had sounded like comfort. Tonight, with Piet's snakes coiled around the gateposts and a gray-coated figure standing motionless on the far bank, it sounded like a countdown.

Mother had crossed that river. This much I knew, the way you know a fact you've never been permitted to turn over and examine — she had family stories from the east bank, a way of pronouncing certain words, a locket with a pressed blossom in it that she wore on festival days and never explained. Blossoms grew in orchards. Orchards grew on the east bank. And somewhere between those plain facts and my father's hovering hand lay a country I had never been permitted to enter, gated and chained, my whole childhood, by silences.

From below, faintly, came the sound of my father moving in the dark shop. Checking the stove damper, probably. Checking the door bolt. Or standing in front of the type case with its one untouchable compartment, doing whatever he did down there on nights when the river got loud.

I blew out my candle.

Forty-one ways to say nothing, and I was about to need a forty-second, because tomorrow Marta Voss would bring fresh rumors, and Piet would bring fresh news, and the measuring men would bring their chains, and somewhere across all that brown running water a young officer in a gray-blue coat was standing very still, as if he were waiting — had maybe always been waiting — for someone on our side of the river to finally speak.

I fell asleep composing the sentence I would never dare set in type, and dreamed, for the first time in eleven years, of blossoms.
