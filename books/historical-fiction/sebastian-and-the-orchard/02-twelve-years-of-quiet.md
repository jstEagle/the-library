## 2. Twelve Years of Quiet

There is a particular sound a type case makes when you slide it shut properly — a soft wooden sigh, like a secret being put to bed. I have heard that sound ten thousand times. My father taught me to listen for it before he taught me my letters, which tells you most of what you need to know about the Aldering household: we learned containment before we learned expression.

It was October again, and the ink shipment had come up from the coast smelling of tar and salt and, faintly, of other cities where people were saying things out loud that we only said with our eyebrows. Father stood at the mixing table with his sleeves rolled, working lampblack into linseed oil the way a priest works through a liturgy, and I was cutting sorts beside him, and neither of us mentioned the bridge.

We never mentioned the bridge. That was rule one.

I should explain the rules. Not the official ones — those were posted on the customs house in letters tall enough to read from a galloping horse, and they changed with the weather. I mean ours. The household rules, unwritten, unspoken, maintained for twelve years with the discipline of men who set type for a living and understood exactly how much damage one misplaced character could do.

Rule one: we do not speak of the orchard.
Rule two: we do not speak of Mother's family name in connection with anything east of the river.
Rule three: if anyone asks about Mother, we say she was from Vellamare, born and bred, and let them assume whatever side of the river bred her.

There were smaller rules too, sub-rules, footnotes. We did not buy pears, because pears invited questions about orchards. We did not walk past the Corvane shipping office on Quay Street, though it was the shortest route to the fish market. And every autumn, when the ink shipment arrived with its smell of far places, Father would work the press until midnight as if exhaustion were a form of forgetting.

He was doing it now. I watched him from across the shop, this man who could cut a letter so fine you'd need a lens to find its flaws but who could not say the word *river* without his hands going still.

"Isandro." He didn't look up. "You're pressing too hard on the counter. You'll split the shoulder."

"I'm pressing exactly hard enough."

"You're pressing like a girl with something on her mind." Now he looked up, and there it was — that flicker of alarm fathers develop, the one that says *not here, not now, not in front of the windows.* The shop had four windows facing the street, and in Vellamare that autumn, windows were mouths.

* * *

Twelve years ago, the night my mother died, it rained the way it only rains in river cities — sideways, vindictive, as though the sky itself had a grudge against the architecture. I was nine. I remember being nine the way you remember a debt: precisely, unwillingly.

She had been ill since midsummer, some slow failure the doctors argued about in Latin over her body while she lay listening, translating it all in her head, because Mother understood six languages and found doctors' Latin unintentionally hilarious. On the last night she sent me out of the room. That was the part I hated longest — not the dying, which I barely saw, but the sending away, the door closing, the murmur of adult voices through wet wood.

I pressed my ear to the door anyway. Children always do. It's practically a civic duty.

Through the rain and the door I heard fragments. My mother's voice, thinner than I'd ever heard it, and my father's, low and cracking. And certain words that meant nothing to me then and everything to me now: *orchard*, *deed*, *the boy*, *never*, *promise me*. I remember *promise me* especially, repeated twice, with a pause between the repetitions that even a nine-year-old could hear was load-bearing.

Then the door opened and my father stood there with lamplight behind him and my mother's death already sitting on his shoulders like a second coat, and he knelt down on the wet floorboards — he ruined a good pair of trousers doing it, and I know because Mother had bought him those trousers — and he took both my hands and said:

"Your mother loved you more than the whole world, and she asked me for one thing, and I need you to be brave enough to help me keep it."

"What thing?"

"We will never speak of the orchard. Not the word. Not the place. Not the name Nakamura in connection with anything across the water. If anyone asks, your mother was a Vellamare woman, and her whole history is this city, and there is no other page."

"But why—"

"Because love sometimes looks like silence," he said, "and you'll hate me for teaching you that, and you'll be right to, and I'll do it anyway, because she asked."

He was right on both counts. I did hate it. I do it anyway.

They buried her in the west cemetery under a stone that said *MIREILLE NAKAMURA ALDERING, BELOVED OF VELLAMARE* — note the ordering of names, the way *Nakamura* was wedged in the middle like a hostage between two respectable citizens — and my father went home and washed the press and began twelve years of not-saying.

* * *

Presently, in the shop, the present-day version of my father wiped his hands and came to inspect my work. He picked up one of my sorts, held it to the window light, and turned it with the exaggerated slowness of a man buying time.

"The shipment came with news," he said finally. "Talk in the low town. Soldiers at the bridge gates come first frost."

"They say that every year."

"They're buying chains this year. I saw the smithy taking orders." He set my sort down with unnecessary gentleness. "If the bridge closes—"

"Then it closes. It closes every winter."

"It won't open again in spring. Not this time. Not after what happened at the granary." He said *granary* the way other men said names of the dead.

I thought about pointing out that rule one technically covered this conversation, that we were skirting territory adjacent to the orchard, and decided against it. Some silences are walls; ours was more of a roof — you don't notice it holding until you imagine it gone.

"Father," I said, instead. "Why does the ink shipment make you sad?"

That surprised him. I could tell because he became very interested in the grain of the table. Twelve years of quiet teaches you to read a man by what he refuses to look at.

"Ink comes from outside," he said. "Ink remembers there's an outside." Then he laughed, short and unhappy. "Listen to me. Your mother would have made something better of that sentence."

"She'd have made a joke."

"She'd have made a joke with a knife in it." His voice went soft in the middle, the way it always did when she entered the conversation sideways, which was the only way we ever allowed her in. "Go home, Isandro. It's late. Take the lantern."

I took the lantern. At the door I paused, because some instinct older than the rules stirred in me — the same instinct, though I didn't know it yet, that would later send my fingers prying up floorboards.

"Father. What was the boy? In the room. That night. She said *the boy*."

The lantern flame leaned in the draft from the door, and my father's face did something complicated and brief, a whole library burning down in miniature, and then he was just a tired man with ink to his elbows.

"Bed," he said. "We open early tomorrow."

* * *

Here is what grief becomes after twelve years, if you feed it nothing and water it with routine: it becomes furniture. You stop seeing it. You navigate around it in the dark without thinking, the way you navigate around the press, and guests who visit never understand why you take that strange wide turn near the corner of the room, and you can't explain, because explaining would require looking at it.

Our grief-furniture was the orchard. A pear orchard, east bank, that I had never seen and could not describe and nevertheless knew — the way you know a story someone told you before you could talk — had belonged to my mother's family for longer than either city had had its current name. I knew it in the negative space of everything we didn't say. I knew it every time Father bought apples instead of pears. I knew it in the way the word *east* sat in sentences, heavy, handled carefully, like a sort with a cracked shoulder that might split if you pressed wrong.

Tomas, the apprentice, knew none of this. Tomas was sixteen and believed the Aldering print shop was simply a print shop, run by a serious master and a serious daughter, in a city where serious people did serious work and history happened elsewhere, to other families, in books — which, given our trade, was almost funny enough to be forgivable.

"Tamsin says the committee's going to seal the bridge," he told me the next morning, hauling paper with the cheerful doom of the young. "Proper chains, iron ones. My cousin saw the wagon."

"Your cousin sees a great deal."

"He sees true, though." He stacked reams with a thump. "Doesn't it worry you? Being cut off half the winter?"

"We've always been cut off half the winter."

"Not like this. This isn't ice closing the river. This is people deciding the river shouldn't connect anybody anymore." He frowned at a sheet, squaring it against the stack. "My grandmother says when a city cuts itself in half, the halves start telling different stories about each other. She says that's how wars actually start. Not with armies. With stories drifting apart."

Tomas's grandmother, whom I had met once at the winter feast, was a small ferocious woman who frightened my father pleasantly. She was also, I would later learn, entirely correct, which is the worst quality in a prophet.

"That's very grim for a Tuesday," I said.

"It's very grim for a century, probably." He grinned suddenly, that total grin that made everyone forgive him everything. "Master Aldering wants the jobbing forms ready by noon. Something official-ish for the customs house."

Official-ish for the customs house meant safe work, sanctioned work, the kind of printing that kept shops unburned. Father had built our survival out of such jobs the way a wren builds a nest — twig by dull twig, nothing pretty, everything load-bearing.

But when I carried the finished forms to him that noon, I found him standing at the shop's back window, the one that faced the river, which he otherwise avoided looking at the way you avoid looking at an old scar. Across the water the far bank sat grey under a low sky, chimneys smoking, and somewhere in that smoke-smear of roofs was a thing shaped like an absence, an orchard-shaped hole in our family's map that we had kept polished and empty for twelve years.

"Father?" I said.

He didn't turn around. "When the bridge closes," he said quietly, "it won't just close the road. It'll close the possibility. Do you understand? While the river runs, there's always a boat, always a crossing, always a chance someone goes and — " He stopped himself with visible effort, jaw tightening around the end of the sentence like a man biting through a wire. "Never mind. Old man's superstition. Bring the forms."

I brought the forms. But I stood there a moment longer, watching him not look at the river, and felt for the first time in years the full weight of the roof over our heads — that great silent structure my mother had ordered built with her last breaths, *promise me, promise me,* and my father holding it up alone ever since, and me beneath it, trained from childhood to move carefully, speak softly, and never, ever ask how much longer the beams could hold.

Outside, far off toward the bridge square, a hammer rang against iron. Once. Twice. Steady as typesetting.

Someone, it seemed, had begun measuring for chains.
