## 3. The Bridge Closes

My father always said the river was Vellamare's best employee: it showed up every morning, did its work without complaint, and never once asked for a raise. He said this while wiping ink from his hands with a rag that had been gray since before I was born, and he said it the way men say things they've decided to believe so they don't have to think anything harder.

On the last morning of autumn, the river stopped working.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the trade I was raised in. The river didn't freeze — not yet, not fully. What happened was smaller and worse. The ice began to form at the banks in long white teeth, creeping toward the center like two arguments that hadn't yet met, and the ferry pilots looked at those teeth and made the arithmetic every pilot makes: *how much is my boat worth against how much am I worth*, and the answer came back cold.

By dawn the ferries were chained to their moorings. By midmorning the city criers were out. And by noon, when I stood in the bridge square with a basket of type specimens I didn't actually need to deliver, I watched soldiers chain the gates of the Corvane Bridge for the season, and I understood that the world had just gotten very, very small.

* * *

You should understand something about the bridge square before I tell you what I saw there, because the square has a personality, and personalities matter in a city where everyone knows everyone's business except their own.

The square sits on our bank like a hand cupped to catch whatever falls off the bridge. There's the customs house with its green shutters, which have needed paint for eleven years and will need it for eleven more because no administration has ever been able to decide whose fault the shutters are. There's Madame Ostric's coffee stand, which dispenses bitterness in two forms, liquid and verbal, at identical prices. And there's the bridge itself — three arches of pale stone, wide enough for two carts and one argument, connecting us to the far bank where the Corvane lands begin.

For twelve years I have crossed that bridge or watched people cross it, and in all that time I have never once thought of it as a door that could be closed. That's the trick about doors. You only think about them when they stop being open.

The crowd gathered the way crowds do — slowly, then all at once, drawn by the smell of officialdom. Soldiers in revolutionary blue-gray, looking younger than their rifles. A sergeant with a voice built for parade grounds and wasted on weather. Two clerks from the Committee of Public Order carrying chains coiled over their shoulders like festive garlands, if your festival happens to celebrate captivity.

"They can't close it," said a man near me, a fishmonger I knew slightly, in the way you know someone whose haddock you've smelled for a decade. "The treaty says—"

"The treaty says nothing," said his neighbor, an old woman with a market basket and the flat certainty of someone who has survived four governments. "Treaties are paper. Paper burns. Ask anyone who prints it."

I kept my mouth shut, which is a skill, and looked at my basket, which is a habit.

The chaining itself took less time than the crowd took to form. That's the thing about endings — they're administratively efficient. The soldiers wrapped the great iron links around the gateposts, threaded a padlock the size of a fist through them, and stepped back. The sergeant read a proclamation. I caught perhaps a third of it, because the wind was coming off the river and taking his consonants with it, but the gist arrived intact: the crossing would remain closed until the thaw, for reasons of public safety, in light of the current situation, signed by authority of the Committee, may order prevail.

"Public safety," muttered the fishmonger. "Whose?"

"Ours," said the old woman. "That's the joke."

Then, because crowds are crowds, someone laughed, and then several someones laughed, and the laughter had that particular Vellamare flavor — half defiance, half despair, whipped together and served cold. The sergeant glared. Nobody threw anything. It was that kind of morning: everyone too cold and too frightened for theater.

I turned to go, and that's when I saw him.

* * *

He was standing on the far bank.

Not among the soldiers there — apart from them, up on the low rise past the tollhouse, a single figure in an officer's coat the color of wet slate, hands clasped behind his back, watching our side of the river the way you watch a fire you've been told is contained.

The distance between the banks is maybe sixty yards of dark water and beginning-ice. At that distance faces dissolve into suggestion. But there was something about the way he stood — still, deliberate, as though he'd been placed there rather than having walked — that pulled my eyes across the water and held them.

And then he looked at me.

Now, I'm going to be honest with you, because honesty is another thing I'm told I should practice: I don't believe in being seen at a distance. I don't believe in fate or omens or the meaningful glance of strangers. My mother died teaching me that the universe doesn't send messages; people do, usually bills. So when I say that across sixty yards of freezing river, through a crowd and a wind and the whole accumulated silence of twelve years, that officer looked at me and I felt *seen* — I want you to understand I know exactly how it sounds.

But it wasn't mystical. It was worse than mystical. It was specific.

His eyes found me the way a compositor's eye finds a wrong sort in a locked galley — not searching, exactly, but unable to pass over. The way you look at a page and know, before you've read a word, that something in it doesn't belong to you but concerns you anyway.

I did what I was raised to do. I became furniture. I adjusted my basket, turned my shoulder, and joined the thinning crowd flowing away from the square like water finding the path of least attention.

Behind me, faintly, I heard the padlock click shut.

* * *

Here is what the bridge closing meant, practically speaking, for those of you who enjoy practicality:

No goods across. Our bank got its grain from the Corvane farms; the far bank got its ink, its paper, and most of its manufactured goods from us. The winter ink shipment — due any week now, already delayed twice — would now arrive never, or arrive by some absurd northern route at triple the cost, which amounts to the same thing for a shop our size.

No letters across. Whatever correspondence existed between families divided by the river — and there were more such families than anyone admitted politely — was now frozen along with everything else.

No leaving. This was the part nobody said aloud in the square, but everyone carried home under their coats like contraband. A closed bridge in wartime isn't a border. It's a lid.

I carried it home too. I set the basket down in the shop, hung up my coat, and went to stand beside my father at the composing stone, because that is what we do in our family when the world contracts: we make ourselves smaller to fit inside it.

"Papa," I said. "The bridge is chained."

"I heard." His hands kept moving, setting sorts with the blind fluency of thirty years. "It'll open at thaw."

"And if there's a war by thaw?"

His hands paused. Just a fraction — the length of one breath, the width of one em. If you hadn't spent your childhood learning to read him the way he reads proofs, you'd have missed it entirely.

"Then," he said, resuming, "we print."

* * *

Tomas arrived twenty minutes later, breathless and pink, with the particular energy of a boy who has run somewhere important and needs you to understand the importance.

"You missed it," he said. "At the square. After the chaining. Someone — I don't know who, I couldn't see — nailed a paper to the customs house door."

"What kind of paper?" my father asked, not looking up.

"A list." Tomas caught his breath. "Names. Of people on the far bank. Collaborators, it said. People the committee on *that* side wants handed over if the city ever reunifies. Or — no, wait, it's people on *our* side the far-bank committee wants. I read it twice and I'm still not sure. That might be the point."

My father did look up then. "Who printed it?"

"That's the strange part." Tomas lowered his voice, though we were alone with the type cases and the smell of oil and the presses sleeping under their canvas covers. "It's good work. Really good. Even spacing, clean impression. Whoever set it learned somewhere serious."

My father and I exchanged the kind of glance that contains an entire conversation conducted in a dead language only the two of us speak.

"Someone with a press," my father said quietly.

"Someone with a *good* press," said Tomas, and then had the grace to blush, because there are perhaps four good presses in Vellamare and he was standing next to one of them.

"We haven't printed anything," I said.

"No," my father agreed. "We haven't."

But he went back to his composing stick, and for the rest of the afternoon his spacing was a hair tighter than usual, and I noticed, because noticing is my inheritance.

* * *

That night I dreamed of the orchard.

This will require context I don't yet intend to give you, so let me offer only the shape of it: trees in rows, snow in the furrows, a promise spoken in a voice I can no longer fully reconstruct, and a deed — paper, always paper — passing between hands. In the dream I could read every word of the deed. I woke with the certainty of it still warm in my chest, and within four steps of the bed it had evaporated, leaving only the feeling, which is how dreams collect their debts.

I lit the lamp and sat at the kitchen table until the feeling faded and the ordinary night reassembled itself around me: the tick of the stove, the creak of the house, my father's footsteps overhead as he checked the window latches, a ritual he performed every night of his life and twice on nights when the news was bad.

The bridge was closed. The river was teeth. Somewhere on the far bank, an officer in a slate coat had looked across sixty yards of ice-water and found me in a crowd, and I had no idea why, and every instinct I owned told me I would find out.

Twelve years of quiet, and the quiet had developed a crack down the middle, fine as a hairline flaw in a cast sort — invisible until the pressure comes, and then, under pressure, the whole piece shears clean in two.

I blew out the lamp. Outside, the wind moved off the river and rattled the green-shuttered world, and beneath the floorboards of the room where my mother used to sleep, something waited — though I didn't know that yet, and wouldn't, for eleven more days.

Eleven days. I count them now the way pilots count the ice: how much is the truth worth against how much am I.

The answer, it turns out, comes back cold.
