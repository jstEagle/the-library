## 6. The Conscription Order

The knock came at seven in the morning, which was suspicious in itself. Nobody knocked at the Aldering shop at seven in the morning unless they owed us money, and nobody who owed us money had ever once knocked with good news.

I knew this because I kept the ledger. Twelve years of keeping ledgers teaches you a kind of arithmetic the schoolmasters never cover: that debt arrives early, that grief arrives late, and that authority — real authority, the kind that doesn't have to say please — arrives exactly when it chooses, dressed however it likes.

Father opened the door before I could reach it. He always did that. Some old instinct from the years before me, when being slow to answer a door in Vellamare could cost you more than a commission. I heard his voice change as he spoke to whoever stood on the step — not afraid, exactly, but flattened. The voice of a man making himself small in a doorway he owned.

"Isandro," he said over his shoulder, too casually. "Fetch the good ink."

There is no "good ink." There is only ink, and we were out of the lampblack batch until Thursday. Which meant Father wanted my hands busy and my ears elsewhere, and I loved him for it and resented it in the same breath, the way you love and resent most things in a house built on silences.

I went to the ink shelf anyway. And I listened.

Three men on the step. Two in the grey coats of the River Committee's wardens, one in black — black wool, black gloves, the particular black of a man who wanted you to notice his clothes and understand they were the least of him. His voice carried the polished boredom of someone reading from a document he'd read aloud forty times that week.

"—by authority of the Provisional Committee of the Left Bank, the premises known as Aldering & Nakamura, typefounders and printers, are hereby conscripted to the service of the Revolution, for the production of such materials as the Committee shall direct—"

I stopped listening to the words after *conscripted*. The word itself did the work. It has a shape, that word. It fits into a sentence the way a wrong-faced sort fits into a galley: everything around it keeps its composure while something essential goes crooked underneath.

I came back to the door with the ink pot held like an offering, because that is what it was. An offering. You give the god at your doorstep whatever is on the nearest shelf and hope he takes it and goes.

The man in black took the document from under the warden's arm and presented it to Father with two hands, almost courteously, the way you hand a coffin across a grave. "Master Aldering. Your reputation precedes you. The finest cutting house on the left bank, they tell me."

"They're generous," said Father.

"They're accurate. Which is why you'll understand that refusal—" and here he smiled, a small dry thing, "—is not among the options I've been asked to present."

*Asked.* As if somewhere a committee had deliberated. As if some room full of earnest men had weighed the question *shall we leave the Alderings alone?* and found it wanting.

"I'll want to see the press run before we begin," the man in black continued. "Your compositors. Your cutters. Names, ages, loyalties."

"Loyalties," Father repeated. Not a question. A stone dropped down a well, listening for water.

"The Revolution asks little," said the man in black. "Legible type, honest labor, and no surprises. You'll find we're easy masters compared to hunger." He glanced past Father then — past him, deliberately, into the shop, into the dim rows of cases and the smell of lead and linseed — and his eyes passed over me the way a man counts coins without taking them out. "Your daughter?"

"My cutter," said Father. Flat. Final. A wall laid brick by brick over twelve years.

"A woman of trade." The man in black inclined his head, and I could not tell if there was respect in it or inventory. "Good. Hands that know precision are wasted on sentiment."

They left the document nailed to the door frame — actually nailed, four strokes of a hammer, so that removing it would be defacement and leaving it would be obedience — and went down the lane toward the bridge square, their boots keeping time like men who had never once walked out of step with anyone.

Father closed the door. Stood with his forehead against it. The wood creaked under the pressure, very slightly, like ice on the river at dusk.

"Well," I said.

"Don't," he said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something with your whole face." He turned. He looked tired in a way I hadn't seen since the winter Mother died — not worn out but worn *through*, as though something had finally rubbed a hole in him. "We comply. We set what they ask. We print what they ask. We keep our heads down and our names off every list they bring us, and when this is over — if it ends, when it ends — we still have a trade."

"And if what they ask is a lie?"

He looked at me then, and I watched him decide not to hear the question properly. I had watched him do this my entire life. It was practically a family craft, second only to typecutting: the careful casting of a face that says *I did not quite catch that.*

"We open at the usual hour," he said. "Tomas will need to be told. Hessler too. And Isandro—" He hesitated, and for one unguarded moment he looked like a man standing on the far bank of something, calculating whether the ice would hold. "Whatever they bring us. Whatever we set. It stays between these walls. No talk in the taverns, no talk to the neighbors, no talk at all. Do you understand me?"

Silence, I thought. You're asking me for silence again. It's the family currency. We mint it ourselves, right here, between the walls.

"Yes," I said. Because I did understand him. That was the terrible part. I understood him completely, and understanding him felt less like comfort and more like complicity, and I carried both feelings to my case and began cutting a capital S that came out with a barb on it I hadn't intended.

* * *

Hessler arrived at half past and read the nailed document without touching it, the way you read a diagnosis.

"So," he said.

"So," said Father.

"They'll want denunciations first. They always start with lists. Names are cheap to set and expensive to read." Hessler had been foreman since before I was born, and he had survived three governments in Vellamare by treating all of them as weather. "You'll want the apprentices on the simpler faces. Keep the girl on the display sorts where I can see her."

"I'm standing right here," I said.

"I know where you're standing," said Hessler. "That's rather the point of me."

There was something almost tender in it, buried under thirty years of sawdust and gruffness, and I let it pass without naming it, because naming things in our shop had always been how you broke them.

Tomas came last, cheeks red from the cold, carrying the morning bread and entirely ignorant of the document above his head. He was sixteen and had been with us two years, and he had the particular brightness of boys who believe institutions are made of what they claim to be made of. He read the conscription order twice. Then he laughed — an actual laugh, short and startled.

"They can't just—"

"They can," said Father.

"But the charter — the guild charter, Master Aldering, the shop is chartered, it's protected—"

"Tomas." Father's voice was gentle in the way that breaking things gently is gentle. "Charters are printed on paper. Paper burns. Ask anyone in this city what paper is worth this month."

Tomas looked at me. I shrugged, or tried to; it came out as something smaller. He looked back at the document, at the committee seal stamped fat and black at the bottom, and I watched the laugh drain out of him and leave behind something older. Sixteen is young to learn that the world reads its own rules selectively. But Vellamare had never been patient with childhood.

"When do we start?" he said finally.

"Tomorrow," said Father. "Tonight we clean the galleys. Tonight we make sure—" and here he paused, and I felt the pause like a hand on my shoulder, "—we make sure nothing is lying about that shouldn't be."

Nothing lying about that shouldn't be. I heard it the way Tomas didn't. There were things in this shop that had lain quiet for twelve years — under floorboards, inside old type cases, in the locked drawer of my father's desk where he kept the accounts no auditor ever saw. And now the authorities were coming, and my father's first instinct, before compliance, before survival, was to make certain the past stayed hidden where he had put it.

I helped him clear the galleys. I handed him rags and stacked the furniture and said nothing, and the whole time I thought: *What are you hiding, Papa? What is it worth more than charter and law and truth?*

But asking was not in our grammar. So I swept, and he counted, and the shop grew clean and innocent around us like a stage set between acts.

* * *

They came the next afternoon with the copy.

Not soldiers — worse. A clerk. A thin, damp man named Pellin who smelled of vinegar and carried the Committee's copy in a leather satchel he handled like scripture. He spread the manuscript on the composing stone and smoothed it flat with both palms, twice, as if the paper might disagree with being read.

"Two thousand copies," he said. "By Friday."

Father read the first line. I read it over his shoulder, because I was his cutter and cutters stand at shoulders; it was in my contract, or would have been, if contracts still meant anything.

It was a proclamation. Of course it was. But beneath the grand phrases about liberty and the river and the will of the people ran a second current, thinner and colder: a warning. *Citizens harboring enemies of the Revolution, or concealing documents pertaining to the estates of traitors, will be considered accomplices to treason.*

*Documents pertaining to the estates of traitors.*

I felt the words go through me like a burr through cloth. Somewhere in this city — maybe in this very shop — there were documents pertaining to estates. Estates across the river. Estates belonging to men the Committee had decided were traitors, which in Vellamare that autumn meant any man whose name began with Corvane.

Father's face did nothing at all. It was magnificent, really. Thirty years of negotiating with customers who haggled over every character had given him a face like a locked case.

"Two thousand by Friday is possible," he said. "Twenty-five hundred if you don't require the ornamental border."

"The Committee requires dignity," said Pellin.

"Dignity costs a day," said Father. "You may choose which dignity: the type's or the deadline's."

Something flickered across Pellin's face — the clerk's eternal anguish, caught between instructions and competence. "Friday," he said. "With the border. And Master Aldering —" he gathered his satchel, paused at the door, and delivered the rest without turning around, to the room in general, as though reciting a footnote: "The Committee is aware that yours was a mixed household. Nakamura, isn't it? The mother. Deceased, yes. Records show land interests on the far bank, lapsed." He adjusted his glove. "Lapsed interests attract attention, in times like these. Best they stay lapsed."

The door closed behind him. The shop was very quiet. Outside, the wind coming off the river carried the smell of ice beginning to think about forming.

Father stood with both hands flat on the composing stone, the way a man steadies himself against a deck. I watched his knuckles whiten.

"Papa," I said. Quietly. Just the two of us, with Hessler gone to buy metal and Tomas sent home early. "What land interests?"

"There are no land interests."

"You heard him. Records show—"

"Records show what clerks write." He straightened up. His voice had the smooth, rehearsed quality of a man laying type he'd set long ago and kept in a form all these years, waiting. "Clerks write errors. Errors get corrected. Or," he added, softer, "they get forgotten. That's all this is, Isandro. A clerk misreading an old registry. Nothing more."

Twelve years I had lived inside this man's silences, and I knew all their textures the way I knew the textures of lead — the soft silence, the hard silence, the silence that means *I love you* and the silence that means *you cannot afford this question.* This one was new. This one had a weight to it, a specific gravity, like a sort cut too deep, ready to punch through the paper.

"Nothing more," I repeated.

"Nothing more." He picked up his apron. "Now come. Two thousand copies by Friday won't set themselves, and the Committee requires dignity."

He went to the stone and began locking the chase, and I stood a moment longer looking at the proclamation copy lying there, at that cold thin sentence about concealed documents, and I thought about my mother — twelve years dead, her handwriting surviving nowhere in this house that I had ever been permitted to find — and about the word *lapsed*, and about how a thing that is truly nothing does not usually need to be defended three times before breakfast.

That night I stayed late in the shop. Later than Father knew. I told myself I was checking the Friday imposition, and partly I was. But partly — the larger part, the part I only admitted to myself somewhere around midnight, by lamplight, alone among the cases — I was thinking about floors.

Old houses have them. Old shops especially. And my mother had spent twenty years in this building, and she was not a woman, by every account of her that survives in other people's memories, who trusted important things to drawers.

I put out the lamp. In the dark, the type cases seemed to breathe, thousands of small leaden letters waiting in their compartments, each one capable of building any sentence in the language — including, someday soon, I suspected, the one sentence my father had spent twelve years making sure no one ever set.

I put my hand flat against the floorboards of the paper store and felt the cold come up through the wood from the cellar below.

And somewhere beneath my palm — faint, patient, older than the revolution, older than the war, older than my grief — the house kept its secret, the way it always had.

Until, I promised the dark, I made it speak.
