## 7. A Name in the Galley

There are two kinds of apprentices in a print shop: the kind who dream of setting great books someday, and the kind who learn quickly that they will spend their lives setting other people's garbage. I am, by every honest measure, the second kind. My father wanted me to be a cooper. Barrels, he said. Honest work. You put a thing in, you take it out, nobody shoots you over it.

Instead I stand at the galley of Aldering & Sons — which is Aldering and one daughter, but nobody corrects the sign because correcting signs is dangerous now — and I set type for a list of names that the Committee of Public Order would like the whole city to hate.

The conscription order went up three weeks ago, pasted to our own door like a taunt. *The press of the house Aldering is hereby placed in service of the Revolution.* Master Aldering read it twice, folded it along its creases with the tenderness most men reserve for love letters or death notices, and put on his apron. Isandro watched him from the stairs. Neither of them said anything. In this shop, silence is not an absence; it's a material, like ink or lead, something you handle carefully because it stains.

I had been there eleven months when the soldiers came. Long enough to know where everything was kept. Not long enough to be trusted, which, as it turns out, saved me in ways I didn't understand at the time. Old men trust boys who are useless. Useless boys get to keep their heads down.

* * *

The morning it happened was cold enough that the ink needed warming before it would flow properly, and Master Aldering stood at the stove turning the ink slab with a wooden paddle like a man making porridge for an army he despised. The denunciation list came in under seal — a courier with red armband, breath steaming, who set the manuscript on the stone counter the way you'd set down something dead.

"Two hundred copies," he said. "By tomorrow's light."

Master Aldering didn't answer right away. He never answers right away anymore. He looks at things — the manuscript, the window, his hands — as if the answer might be hiding among them and he'd rather not startle it.

"Two hundred," he said finally.

"You have a problem with the number?"

"I have a problem with nothing." And he began to read the copy aloud, because reading it aloud was the fastest way to measure it — how much type, what faces, how long the forme would take — and also because saying terrible things in a flat voice is the only way to say them without choking.

I won't repeat the preamble. Preambles are all the same. *Enemies of the people, traitors within the walls,* etcetera, etcetera, the revolution has a vocabulary of about forty words and uses them in rotation like a mill wheel. What matters is the list. Forty-one names, each with a street and a crime. Most of the crimes were invented with real craftsmanship — I've developed an eye for forged prose lately, God help me — hoarding, correspondence with the enemy, harboring officers. One man was denounced for "excessive wealth disguised as poverty," which is the most honest sentence the committee has ever produced, since it describes nearly everyone in Vellamare including, presumably, the committee.

I set names for a living now. That's the joke of my trade. A name is just letters — seven, nine, twelve pieces of lead in a row — and lead doesn't care whose name it makes. Lead has no politics. I told myself that around the tenth name and believed it around the twentieth, and then I reached the twenty-third line and stopped believing anything at all.

**MIREILLE DANTON, WIDOW — RUE DES FONTAINES — HARBORING ENEMY CORRESPONDENCE.**

My composing stick went heavy in my hand. That sounds like poetry but it isn't; it's just what happens when your fingers forget their work. Eleven months I'd been in this shop, and Mireille Danton had been in it longer than I'd been alive. She brought honey cakes at Midwinter. She'd taught Isandro her letters, so the story went — sat her on a paper bale when she was four years old and traced capitals in flour dust. Every soul in this building loved her, and love in this city had become, without anyone announcing it, contraband.

I looked up. Master Aldering was at the far end setting his section, back turned, shoulders curved around his work like a wall around a garden. Isandro was at the type case nearest the window, sorting, her hands moving in that way she has — fast, exact, no wasted motion, the hands of someone who learned early that precision is a kind of safety. Twelve years she's worked beside her father, they tell me, and in twelve years she has perfected the art of being present and invisible simultaneously. I don't know how she does it. When I try to be invisible I knock something over.

Here is what I want to be clear about, since history may read this someday, if any of us survive to print it: I did not decide to do what I did next. There was no decision. There was a name in a galley and a girl at a type case and my feet carried me across the floor before my mind was consulted, and when my mind caught up it found my mouth already open, already lowered, already whispering.

"Isandro."

She didn't look up. Of course she didn't look up. Looking up is how people get noticed, and getting noticed is how people die, and if Isandro Nakamura has a religion it is the faith of the unremarked. But her hands stopped. Just for half a second. Her hands heard me even if the rest of her declined to.

"Twenty-three," I said. "Come see twenty-three."

She looked up then. Which frightened me more than anything else that morning, because it meant she understood that whatever I had was worth breaking her oldest rule for.

She crossed the room without hurry, took the composing stick from me with a glance of professional criticism — I'd spaced the line loose, and even at the edge of catastrophe she noticed, and honestly I respect her for it — and read.

I watched her face while she read it. I've spent eleven months learning to read faces in this shop, because words here are rationed and faces carry the surplus. Master Aldering's face says *we endure.* The foreman Hessler's face says *I am owed something.* Isandro's face usually says nothing at all, beautifully, completely nothing, a closed book with the spine facing out.

But for one breath, the book opened. And what was in it wasn't surprise.

That's the part I can't stop thinking about since. She wasn't surprised. Grieved — yes, her jaw went tight and her eyes went bright and wet in a way she corrected immediately, blinking it back down into nothing. Frightened — yes, a quick dart of her gaze toward her father's back. But underneath both, older than both, there was something that looked like recognition. Like she'd known this name might appear somewhere terrible someday, and here it was, in her own shop, in her own type, in my incompetent spacing.

"Mireille," she said. Very quietly. Testing the word the way you test ice.

"She taught you your letters," I said, stupidly, because I had to say something. "Everyone says —"

"I know what everyone says." She set the stick down on the stone with a click that sounded, in the quiet of the shop, like a lock turning. Then, louder, flat, in her working voice: "The line's too loose, Tomas. Reset it."

And she walked back to her case.

* * *

I reset the line. I want that on the record too. I stood there and I put Mireille Danton's name into lead, piece by piece — M, I, R, E, I, L, L, E — and each letter clicked into place with the little kiss of metal on metal that I used to find soothing and will never find soothing again as long as I live. Two hundred copies. By tomorrow's light. Somewhere out in the city, two hundred strangers would read her name over their breakfast and feel the particular pleasure of righteous anger, that cheapest of all emotions, served hot by people who never have to pay for it.

You want to know what complicity smells like? Ink and lamp oil and cold sweat. That's the smell. I know because I wore it all day.

At midday we broke for bread and Master Aldering went to argue with the courier about paper stock — an argument he was guaranteed to lose, conducted purely, I think, so that he could feel like a man who argued. That left the two of us, and the presses, and the galley full of names.

Isandro came and stood beside me. Not close. Never close. Close enough to speak under the sound of Hessler scraping the press bed in the back room.

"He doesn't know yet," she said.

"Hessler? No. He's been in the—" I stopped. Because I realized what she was actually asking, and the question rearranged itself in my head into its true shape: *does anyone else know what I'm about to show them, and can we control who finds out.*

"No one knows," I said. "It's just been me and the stick all morning."

She nodded slowly. "When he comes back, he'll check the galley. He checks everything now." A pause. "Tomas. Why did you bring it to me?"

I had prepared exactly zero answers for this question, mostly because I hadn't known I was going to be asked it. So I gave her the true one, unpolished, the way you hand someone a chipped cup because it's the only cup you have.

"Because if I brought it to Hessler, it becomes a fact. Facts go upstairs. Facts get reported. And if I brought it to your father—" I glanced at the door through which Master Aldering had gone. "—he'd have to decide whether to warn her. And deciding things is costing him pieces of himself, one per week. I've watched. Whereas if I bring it to you..."

"Yes?" she said, when I didn't finish.

"Then maybe it stays a person instead of becoming a fact. For another day, at least."

She looked at me for a long moment. Outside the window, the river ran gray between its banks, and beyond it the far side of the city sat in its winter haze with its enemy flag, and none of it moved except a gull hanging on the wind like a dropped letter.

"Tomas," she said, "do you know what Mireille was to my mother?"

The question hit sideways, the way her questions always do. I shook my head.

"Neither do I," she said. "And that is beginning to look like the only question that matters." She rubbed her thumb across her ink-stained fingertips, an unconscious gesture, a printer's rosary. "If they take her — if the committee takes her and she talks about this family, about the shop, about—" she stopped herself, jaw working, some word swallowed whole. "We need to reach her before they do. Tonight. After the formes are locked and Hessler goes home."

"Reach her how? Rue des Fontaines is half a mile inside the—"

"I know where she lives, Tomas. I've been delivering her flour since I was six years old." Something moved behind her eyes then, quick and private, a thought she wasn't sharing — the second time that day I'd caught her knowing something I didn't. It was starting to become a habit between us, her knowledge and my ignorance, and I couldn't decide whether I resented it or was grateful to be standing adjacent to it.

"And if she's already gone?" I asked. "If the committee got there first?"

"Then we'll know," she said simply. "Knowing is worth something now. It's practically currency." She almost smiled. It wasn't a happy almost. "Finish the list, Tomas. Set it clean. If we're going to print a lie, we'll print it well — sloppy lies insult everyone, the liar included."

That's the closest thing to a philosophy I've heard in this city since the bridge closed, and it came from a nineteen-year-old girl sorting type, and I have decided to live by it.

* * *

We finished at dusk. Two hundred copies of forty-one ruined lives, pulled off the press while the lamps guttered, stacked and tied in bundles for the courier. Master Aldering signed the delivery docket without looking at it. His signature has changed since the conscription — smaller, faster, as though he's trying to spend less of himself on each one.

Hessler left at dark. Master Aldering went up to the apartment above, and I heard him bolt the door, which he never used to do, and then I heard nothing, which is its own kind of news.

Isandro was waiting by the paper store with two cloaks and a shuttered lantern, and she handed me one cloak and said, "You don't have to come," in the tone of someone who has already calculated that I will.

We went out through the yard, past the woodpile, into streets that belong after curfew to nobody and therefore to everybody, which means you walk fast and you don't look at windows. Snow had started — fine, dry snow, the kind that squeaks. Isandro navigated by routes I'd never seen, cutting through courtyards and along the backs of tanneries, and I understood that she had walked these paths her whole life, mapping the city the way her father maps a page, margins and gutters and safe white space.

At the corner of Rue des Fontaines she stopped, put a hand on my arm, and held us both in the doorway of a shuttered chandler's shop.

And that's when we saw it. Down the street, in the blue-dark, lanterns. Four of them, moving in formation, and the low murmur of men's voices doing official business at a doorstep, and — faint, thin, unmistakable — a woman's voice raised not in screaming but in the worse thing, in dignified protest, the voice of someone insisting on her rights to men who have abolished rights.

Mireille Danton's door stood open. Light spilled out onto the snow.

Beside me, Isandro made a small sound, and I felt her arm go rigid under my hand, and I heard her breathe one word that I don't think she meant me to hear, a word that had nothing to do with Mireille Danton at all:

"*Corvane.*"

One of the men at the doorway had turned toward the light, and the lantern caught the collar of his coat — not committee gray, but army blue, an officer's blue, the blue of the far bank — standing in a knot of revolutionary officials like a wolf invited to the hunt.

Isandro stepped backward into the shadow of the chandler's doorway, pulling me with her, and I swear to God her heart was hammering so hard I could feel it through the cloak, through my own coat, like a second pulse that wasn't mine.

"We have to follow them," she whispered. "They're taking her to the cellar under the bridge gate. I've seen prisoners go. Tomas — she knows something. About my mother. And I think she's the only one left alive who does."
