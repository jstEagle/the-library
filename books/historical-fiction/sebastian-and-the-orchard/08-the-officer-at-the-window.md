## 8. The Officer at the Window

The summons came the way all official things came to our shop that winter: on paper, carried by someone who didn't knock.

Tomas brought it to me where I sat cutting a batch of long s's for the committee's next broadside, and he held it out flat on both palms, as though it might go off. "Gate parley," he said. "Thursday, last light. They want a printer's witness. Someone who can read and write, to record terms."

"Record terms," I repeated. The phrase had the flavor of something rehearsed by men who enjoyed saying *record*.

"The bridge committee wants their own scribe. The Corvane side sends theirs. Ours is sick — fever, they say." He lowered his voice. "They say. Hessler thinks it's the drink."

"And so they've chosen me."

"They've chosen the shop," Tomas corrected, which was worse. In Vellamare that winter, the two were the same thing.

My father read the order twice, set down his burnisher, and looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't file under any of his usual faces. Not fear exactly. Something adjacent to fear. The look of a man watching weather come toward a field he has already planted.

"You'll speak to no one but the scribes," he said finally. "You'll write what is said, nothing more. You'll wear your hood up and stand behind the committee man."

"Yes, Father."

"If anyone asks your name—"

"I'm the Aldering hand. I know."

He nodded, satisfied or pretending to be, and went back to his type case with the particular care of a man putting small metal letters in small wooden boxes because the large uncontrollable things were beyond him. I understood. It was how I'd learned to live too: sort the sortable, box the unboxable, keep your hands moving.

What I did not tell him — what I could not have told him without producing a conversation neither of us would survive — was that when I read the word *parley*, my first thought was not of the committee, nor of terms, nor of my duty as witness.

It was of the officer on the far bank. The one who had watched me through the frost-furred window of the gatehouse three weeks ago, the day the chains went on, and whose gaze had landed on me with the specific, terrible weight of recognition. Not recognition of my face. He couldn't have known my face; we had never met. But there had been something else in it — the look of a man reading a title page and finding, printed there plain as day, a name he'd been promised.

I had spent three weeks telling myself I imagined it. Three weeks was apparently my limit.

* * *

Thursday came down gray and windless, the kind of day that makes the river look less like water and more like a decision nobody can finish making. The ice had thickened past the point where the ferryman's old chart called it doubtful and into the territory where it called it merely suicidal, which in Vellamare counted as optimism.

The bridge square filled early. Revolutionary committees understand theater the way printers understand ink — it is the medium, not the message — and Commandant Ferreol, who led ours, had clearly ordered the staging himself. A table. Two chairs. A brazier that smoked more than it warmed. The chain-gates at the bridge's center, locked since autumn, gleaming wetly in the last light like the teeth of something patient.

I stood where I'd been told to stand, one step behind and left of Ferreol's shoulder, ledger open, pencil ready. My hood was up. My breath made small clouds that rose and dissolved, rose and dissolved, and I fixed my eyes on them because looking anywhere else felt dangerous.

Across the table, on the far side of the gates, the Corvane delegation arranged itself with the economy of people who had done this before. Two clerks. A colonel whose mustache had its own gravity. And behind them, slightly apart, slightly forward — the way a blade sits apart from a scabbard — Lieutenant Elias Corvane.

He was younger than I expected. That was the first obscenity of the evening. I had built him, over three weeks, into something monumental: the son, the heir, the keeper of whatever promise my mother had stitched into those hidden letters. I had made him forty and iron-eyed. The man across the river was perhaps twenty-five, with his father's jaw and none of the general's weight yet, dark-haired, standing in the cold like a man who had decided the cold owed him nothing and intended to collect anyway.

He was not looking at the commandant. He was not looking at the colonel or the table or the smoking brazier.

He was looking at me.

Not staring — he was too well-trained for staring — but holding me in the periphery of his attention the way you hold a line of type you haven't yet justified: aware of its exact position, waiting for the moment it matters.

*Don't be ridiculous,* I told myself. *You are a hooded shape behind a fat man. You are furniture.*

And then the parley began, and the furniture turned out to have opinions.

The terms themselves were tedious and grim, the usual winter arithmetic: coal shipments, prisoner exchanges, the question of whether the mill district's grain allotment would pass through neutral hands. Ferreol spoke in the rounded, generous tones of a man offering you the privilege of being robbed. The colonel answered in clipped counters. The clerks wrote. I wrote. The brazier smoked. Somewhere beyond the gatehouse gulls argued over something dead on the ice.

It was during the fourth item — prisoner exchanges, names read aloud from lists — that Elias Corvane finally spoke, and when he did, he did not address the commandant.

"My father kept accounts," he said, in a voice pitched low enough to be almost private and carrying far enough to be unmistakably public. "Before all this. He believed debts should be honored even when governments change. Especially then." A pause, measured to the syllable. "There is one account outstanding. Concerning an orchard."

The square went very quiet. Quiet in the way Vellamare did quiet: not silence but a gathering-in, every ear tilting.

Ferreol's smile stayed exactly where it was while everything behind it reorganized. "An orchard," he said pleasantly. "Lieutenant, we are discussing prisoners."

"We are discussing what survives wars," Elias said. His eyes never left the middle distance — never quite mine, never quite not mine. "Orchards survive. Deeds survive. Promises—" and here he paused again, and I felt the pause land somewhere beneath my ribs like a fist striking a press bed, "—promises are harder to verify. Ink fades. Paper burns. Men forget what they signed, especially when signing was inconvenient." He turned, at last, fully toward Ferreol, and gave a small courteous bow. "But I'm sure the commandant knows nothing of any orchard. Forgive the digression."

"Not at all," said Ferreol, whose voice had acquired the smoothness of poured lead. "Though if the lieutenant has documents, my office welcomes documents. We are great respecters of paper."

"I don't doubt it," said Elias, and there was something in the way he said it — dry, precise, aimed — that made me certain, with the cold absolute certainty of the truly doomed, that he knew exactly what kind of respecter of paper Commandant Ferreol was. What kind of *collector*.

The parley concluded on schedule. Terms initialed. Lists exchanged. Clerks bowing to clerks. And through all of it, as the delegations folded themselves back toward their respective banks, Elias Corvane found one final half-second to look directly at me — hood, ledger, pencil, all of it — and give the smallest inclination of his head. Not a greeting. An acknowledgment. The nod a man gives a co-conspirator across a crowded room when the conspiracy exists only in his own mind.

Or only in mine.

* * *

Ferreol walked me back along the quay himself, which no one wanted, least of all me.

"Your father," he said conversationally, "is Aldering. The typeface cutter."

"He is."

"Old family. Old trade." He strolled with his hands clasped behind him, a man admiring the river he had chained. "Tell me, does the Aldering shop hold much old paper? Correspondence, deeds, that manner of thing? Printers accumulate. I've noticed it."

"We print," I said. "We don't archive."

"Mm." He smiled at the water. "The lieutenant mentioned an orchard. Curious word. There are no orchards in Vellamare worth a lieutenant's breath." He stopped walking and turned to me, and up close his face had the pleasant blankness of well-kept machinery. "If such a document existed — a deed, say, crossing the river, crossing families — it would be a thing of value. To many parties. Value enough that careless people get hurt around it. Do you follow me?"

"Paper burns easily," I said. "That's why printers keep presses."

Something flickered behind his eyes — amusement, possibly, possibly appetite. "Yes," he said. "Keep your presses. And Isandro—" he let my name fall out of nowhere, casually, the way a man drops a key on a table to show you he already owns the door, "—if anything of that nature passes through your hands, you'll bring it to me. Before anyone else. That's not a request dressed as advice. It's advice dressed as nothing at all."

He resumed walking. I matched his pace and discovered my legs were doing it without consulting me.

How did he know my name. How did he know my name. I ran the evening backward like bad type through a reader's fingers — the order, the summons, Hessler's arrangements, Tomas — searching for the loose letter, the one character that had slipped the composing stick and given the whole line away. I couldn't find it. That was the worst part. Somewhere in the machine of my careful, silences-stitched life, a piece had moved without my hearing it click.

At the corner where the quay met Printer's Row, Ferreol touched his hat brim with two fingers and left me, whistling something tuneless, a man entirely satisfied with his afternoon.

* * *

My father listened to the whole account standing at the window, his back to me, which was how he listened to anything that frightened him. When I finished — the orchard spoken aloud, the promise tested in public, the commandant's soft inquiry about old paper — he was quiet for so long that I checked, absurdly, whether he was still breathing.

"He said *orchard*," my father said at last. "By name."

"In front of everyone. As a test. He was testing whether anyone flinched."

"Did you?"

I thought about lying to him. It would have been the household custom, the family craft, practiced across twelve years of boxed griefs and unsaid names. But the parley had cracked something, or maybe the letters had cracked it weeks ago and the parley had simply widened the gap until I could see through it.

"I didn't flinch," I said. "I wrote. But Father — he looked at me. Before he ever said the word. He looked at me like he knew."

"Knew what?" My father turned. In the lamplight his face was older than it had been at breakfast, and I understood with a lurch that some part of him had known this day was coming for twelve years and had simply hoped, the way cowards hope, that hoping might substitute for preparing. "Isandro. Knew *what*?"

"That the promise survived. That there's a daughter of the other family on this bank. That she cuts type and keeps her head down and holds a piece of paper her mother died hiding." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "He wasn't asking the commandant about an orchard. He was asking *me*. Whether I still have it. Whether I remember. Whether the Nakamura side of the deed means anything to anyone anymore."

The name landed in the room like a dropped tray. Nakamura. My mother's name, the name we did not say, the name that had lived under the floorboards with the letters for twelve years, and now it was out in the open air between us and neither of us bent to pick it up.

My father crossed to the type cases — his sanctuary, his litany — and stood with his hand resting on the drawer of em quads, the empty sorts, the letters that print nothing. "Elias Corvane," he said slowly. "The son. Your mother corresponded with him when he was a boy. She taught him his letters, did you know that? Summers, before—" He stopped. Started again. "She believed promises outlive politics. She was wrong about most things that mattered and right about that one, God help us."

"Then he'll come for the deed."

"He'll come for the deed," my father agreed. "And Ferreol will come for the deed. And every man in this city who has learned that paper is power will come for it, and they will all come through this shop, because this shop is where paper lives." He drew out the drawer of empties and looked at it as though it might advise him. "Twelve years I kept you safe by keeping you silent. One afternoon at a bridge gate and the whole architecture comes down."

"It was already coming down," I said. "The bridge closed. The revolution happened. Mother's letters happened. The only thing silence ever bought us was time, and the time is spent."

He looked at me then — really looked, the way he looked at a newly cut letter, turning it in the light, checking the serif against the soul of it — and whatever he saw made him close the drawer gently instead of slamming it.

"There's a copy of the chart," he said quietly. "In the case with the Greek sorts. The ferryman drew it for your mother, years ago. If it comes to crossing—"

"It will come to crossing."

"—then we do it properly. No heroics. Ice is honest, at least. It either holds or it doesn't, and it tells you which in advance, if you're patient enough to listen." He almost smiled. Almost. "Your mother used to say the river was the only honest man in Vellamare."

Outside, the last light failed over the frozen water, and somewhere on the far bank a young officer who had once learned his letters from my mother stood in a window looking west, I was nearly sure of it, toward the print shop, toward the deed, toward the daughter who now had four days at most before Commandant Ferreol came calling with softer questions and harder instruments.

And beneath the floor, wrapped in oilcloth beside her mother's letters, the true deed waited — naming heirs of both families jointly — and it occurred to me, with a terror so clean it was almost calm, that the next person to read it aloud might decide everything.
