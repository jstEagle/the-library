## 9. Mireille in the Cellar

**Isandro**

The knock came at half past eleven, three soft raps spaced like a man trying to remember a tune he'd only heard once.

I knew the rhythm. Mireille Duval had knocked on our door that way for as long as I could remember — always slightly off, always apologetic about it, as if even her knuckles didn't want to presume. She was the one who brought candied ginger when my mother was ill, who taught me to read music badly, who once talked a customs officer out of searching Father's ink shipment by praising his wife's embroidery for forty minutes until he forgot what he was doing.

She was also, as of four days ago, a name in a galley of type that Tomas and I were supposed to be setting for the Committee of Public Order.

I opened the door with the chain still hooked, which is the sort of caution you learn when your family's survival depends on not being interesting. Mireille stood in the lamplight looking like a woman who had walked out of her own funeral. Her gray hair had escaped its pin. Her coat was buttoned wrong — top to bottom, one hole off, so the whole garment sat crooked on her like an accusation.

"Isandro." Her voice was a whisper with gravel in it. "I need the cellar."

Not *hello*. Not *child, how you've grown*. The cellar.

"The paper store," I said.

"The paper store," she agreed, and then her eyes rolled back and she folded toward me like a press sheet slipping its rollers, and caution went out the window along with any hope of a quiet night.

* * *

**Tomas**

Here is what nobody tells you about apprenticeship: you learn to sleep light. Not because of the work — though the foreman's bell is no lullaby — but because in Vellamare this winter, everything worth knowing happens between midnight and dawn, and everyone worth trusting is awake for it.

So when Isandro shook my shoulder in the dark and said, "Tomas. I need your arms and your silence, in that order," I was up before my feet found my boots.

We carried Mireille down through the shop, past the standing presses with their great iron jaws hanging open like patient beasts, past the galley where the denunciation list sat locked under Hessler's key — her name still in it, I'd checked, small and black and damning in nine-point Caslon — and down the stairs to the paper store.

A print shop's cellar is a cathedral of waiting. Reams of stock stacked floor to ceiling, wrapped in brown paper, each one a book that hasn't happened yet. Isandro pulled three reams from the corner behind the water cistern, revealing a hollow barely big enough for a cot made of felt blankets and a straw pallet we used for drying proofs.

"She can't stay here," I said, because somebody had to say it. "The committee audits the stock. Hessler counts every ream."

"Hessler counts what Hessler sees." Isandro was tucking a blanket around Mireille's shoulders, her hands quick and certain in a way that made me feel sixteen, which I am, but don't enjoy being reminded of. "Help me move the cistern forward."

We shifted it together. It took both of us, and left a scuff mark I immediately hated myself for noticing, because noticing things is how people like us stay alive and also how we go mad.

Mireille stirred. Her eyes opened, unfocused, fixed somewhere around my collarbone. "Is the child safe?" she asked.

"There's no child," Isandro said gently.

"There's always a child," Mireille said, and closed her eyes again, and that was the moment I understood this was going to be worse than a hiding place. This was going to be a vigil.

* * *

**Isandro**

Fever is a strange houseguest. It talks constantly and says nothing true, and you find yourself listening anyway, because buried in the nonsense there is sometimes a coin of real gold.

For two days, Tomas and I took shifts. He had mornings; I had nights, since Father slept early now, worn hollow by the propaganda work. We fed Mireille broth through her half-sleep, sponged the fever from her wrists, and listened.

She talked about orchards. About a table with a green cloth. About a man signing something while another man watched from the doorway "with the look of a ledger balancing itself." She talked about my mother — called her *Lien*, which stopped my heart the first time, because I hadn't heard my mother's given name spoken aloud in twelve years. In our house she had been *your mother*, full stop, a subject closed like a drawer.

And once, lucid as morning, gripping my wrist hard enough to leave marks, she said:

"They know about the deed, Isandro. The committee. They've known since autumn."

My mouth went dry. "What deed?"

"Don't play the fool with me, child, I changed your swaddling." She coughed, and pressed on. "Verrone knows. He was there. At the signing. He stood in the doorway and watched Corvane and your mother put their names to paper, and he smiled at them both, and then he went home and told the Committee exactly nothing — until now. Until leverage was worth more than friendship." Her grip tightened. "They don't want justice. They want the deed. A joint holding across both banks — do you understand? While the city tears itself in half, they'd hold the one document that says the river belongs to nobody. That's power. That's ransom."

I sat very still. Somewhere above us, the night press crew stamped through their shift, and the whole building trembled faintly with rhythm, like a heartbeat we lived inside.

"How do you know what Verrone knows?"

"Because he came to my house on Tuesday with two men and a warrant, and I went out the back door while they were reading it aloud to impress each other." Something that might have been a smile crossed her cracked lips. "Forty years in this city. I know which floorboards announce visitors."

"And they'll come here next."

"They'll come everywhere eventually. They're revolutionaries, dear, not magicians. But yes. They'll come here." She looked at me then, fully, fever-bright, and I saw that beneath the illness there was still the woman who had out-talked the customs officer, sharp as a burin. "Your father helped hide that deed. Don't look shocked — Lien told me herself, God rest her, the night before the end. He cut away the truth the way he cuts type, clean and complete, and he's been living inside the white space ever since."

"He wouldn't—"

"He would and did, and he'd do it again, because love makes cowards of careful men and your father is the most careful man in Vellamare." She sank back against the blankets. "The question, Isandro, is not whether he lied. It's whose side the lie was on."

I didn't answer. I climbed the stairs and stood in the dark shop for a long time among the sleeping presses, listening to the building breathe.

Three directions, I thought. The committee wanted the deed as a weapon. Elias Corvane — the officer on the far bank, the boy from the letters — wanted it as a promise kept. And Father wanted it never to have existed at all.

And I was the only person alive who could carry it to any of them.

* * *

**Tomas**

On the third day, Hessler started counting.

I saw him doing it from the composing stone — moving through the paper store with his tally book, lips moving, tapping each ream like a man taking attendance. When he reached the corner by the cistern he stopped. Stood there a long moment. Looked at the scuff mark on the floor.

Then he wrote something in the book and moved on, and I aged approximately nine years.

I went straight to Isandro. She was cutting type, hands steady, face doing that thing hers does where it becomes a locked room with excellent furniture. "Hessler counted the reams," I whispered.

"I know. I watched him."

"He stopped at the corner."

"I know that too."

"Do we move her tonight? There are boats on the canal if — no, the bridges are chained, the canal's got pickets, maybe the drainage tunnel by the tannery, except Mireille can't walk to the end of the cellar—"

"Tomas." She set down her graver and looked at me, and I shut up, because Isandro mid-thought has the same authority as a closing press. "If Hessler wanted to report the discrepancy, he'd have done it already. What he wants is to decide what he is. Give him a day."

"A day for what?"

"For becoming the kind of man who doesn't look in corners." She picked the graver back up. Then, quieter: "Or the kind who does. Either way, we'll know soon."

That evening I brought Mireille's broth down, and she was having one of her clear spells, propped on her elbows like a duchess in a hay wagon.

"You're the apprentice," she said. "Tomas. The one who caught my name in the list and brought it to Isandro instead of the foreman."

News travels fast in a cellar, apparently. "Yes, ma'am."

"You could have stayed quiet. Kept setting the type. Nine points, wasn't it? Caslon?" She almost smiled. "You could have printed me into history and slept soundly after."

"It didn't feel sound."

"No," she said softly. "It wouldn't. You have your grandmother's trouble, then — the kind that hears a wrong note in the middle of a march and can't unhear it." She waved off my surprise. "I knew your grandmother, boy. Everyone in this city knew everyone, back when the river connected more than it divided. Now sit, and listen, because I'm going to tell you what the committee wants and why, and you're going to have to help this girl choose, because she can't carry it alone and her father will drown himself in silence first."

So I sat on a ream of uncut folio, in a cellar full of books that hadn't happened yet, and listened to a fevered old woman explain that the war over the bridge was, at its rotten center, a war over a piece of paper about fruit trees.

It should have sounded absurd. It didn't. Nothing sounds absurd anymore. Absurdity packed up and left Vellamare sometime around the chaining of the gates.

* * *

**Isandro**

That night I brought the letters down. All of them, bundled in oilcloth against my chest like contraband, which is precisely what they were.

Mireille read them by candle stub, slowly, her finger under each line, and I watched her face do arithmetic. When she reached the last page — the one with the clause I'd spent three nights deciphering, the joint-heir wording, *the heirs of Nakamura and Corvane jointly, neither to be dispossessed while the other holds* — she let out a breath that seemed to empty her entirely.

"So it survived her," she murmured. "Lien, you impossible, magnificent woman. You hid the promise inside the promise."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean your mother was cleverer than either government in this city. The deed names heirs jointly — so the committee can't use it to dispossess the Corvanes, and the Corvanes can't use it to dispossess anyone. It's not a weapon for any side. It's a lock." She looked up at me, and the fever-light in her eyes had gone steady. "Which makes it worthless to Verrone as leverage and priceless to everyone else. And that, child, is why you must never let him hold it."

Footsteps overhead. Heavy ones, unhurried, crossing the shop toward the cellar stairs.

Tomas appeared at the top, pale as fresh stock. "Hessler," he breathed. "He's coming down. He's bringing the lamp."

Mireille's hand found mine and squeezed. "Decide quickly, Isandro," she whispered. "Whose side is your silence on?"

The stairs creaked under the foreman's weight, one step, then another — and I stood in the dark between the paper stacks with a dead woman's letters against my heart, understanding that whatever I said in the next ten seconds would say everything about the next ten years.

The lamp swung into view.

Hessler stopped at the bottom step, raised the light, and looked directly at the corner behind the cistern.

"Well," he said.
