## 10. Ink and Ash

There is a particular sound a press makes when it prints a lie. Most people wouldn't know it. The untrained ear hears only the usual rhythm — the platen falling, the ink singing off the forme, the paper taking its impression with that soft, satisfied kiss. But I have stood at these machines for thirty years, and I am here to tell you: a lie has a different weight in the type. It sits looser in the stick. It demands less of the compositor's hand and more of his soul, and the press knows, the way animals know weather.

Or perhaps I invented all of that on the third morning of the conscription, because inventing things was easier than admitting what we were doing.

The order had come down from the Committee of Public Instruction — a title so bureaucratic it could only mean blood — delivered by a boy of perhaps seventeen wearing a sash too big for him and an expression borrowed from a much harder man. *The Aldering press will render service to the Revolution. Weekly output: two thousand broadsheets. Content to be supplied. Failure to comply will be interpreted as counter-revolutionary sympathy.* The word "sympathy" had been underlined twice, in ink the color of old wounds.

I signed the acknowledgment because there was nothing else to do. Isandro watched me sign it. She said nothing, which is her inheritance from me, God help her, though lately I've begun to suspect she learned silence from her mother instead, and her mother's silences were always worth more than mine.

* * *

The first supplied content was innocuous enough — a proclamation about grain requisitions, turgid but honest in its dishonesty, the way official language always is. I set it myself rather than trust it to Hessler, who has never met a sentence he couldn't make worse, and by evening we had our two thousand sheets bundled and gone.

The second delivery came on a Thursday, wrapped in oilcloth, carried by the same boy with the same borrowed face. Inside: a manuscript in a clerk's careful hand, describing the massacre at Sainte-Aurelle.

I should explain what I knew of Sainte-Aurelle before that moment, which was this: nothing. A market town upriver, famous for its plums. My wife once bought brandied plums from a Sainte-Aurelle merchant at the autumn fair and pronounced them superior to anything Vellamare could produce, which caused a small domestic scandal I still smile at when I allow myself to remember my wife smiling.

The manuscript described how government troops — the Corvane loyalists' garrison, retreating — had fallen upon the town and put four hundred civilians to the sword. It named streets. It named victims. It gave ages. A child of six, it said, cut down beneath the plum trees while carrying water for her grandmother.

I set type for a living. I can read a page the way a physician reads a body. And every line of that manuscript was wrong — not wrong in its details, which were plausible enough, but wrong in its grain. Real accounts of real horror have gaps. They trail off. They contradict themselves, because witnesses contradict themselves. This document had no gaps. Four hundred dead, each one accounted for with the tidy precision of a man counting coins he had never held. No one who had seen a massacre wrote like this. Only someone who needed one to have happened.

I took the manuscript to the back room where Isandro was sorting leads, and I laid it on the bench between us like something dead.

"Read it," I said.

She read it. She read faster than anyone I've ever known, and she reads with her whole body — shoulders forward, lips moving slightly, one finger tracing the margin as if holding the words in place. When she finished, her finger stopped on the account of the child.

"The grandmother isn't mentioned again," she said quietly. "After the child dies. You'd think—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. My daughter has spent twelve years learning what people leave out of stories; she was raised in a shop where the truth lived under floorboards.

"It's fabricated," I said. "Wholly or partly. There was fighting at Sainte-Aurelle, the reports are confused, but this—"

"They want us to print it anyway."

"They want us to print it anyway."

She looked at me then, and I want to be honest, since honesty is the currency this whole account is supposedly denominated in: I looked away first. Twelve years I had taught her that looking away was safety, and now I taught her that it was also something else, and I could see her understanding arrive, the way frost arrives on glass — slowly, and then all at once, covering everything.

"If we refuse," I said, "they close the shop. They mark us. And they find someone else to print it, and we starve, and it gets printed anyway."

"Yes," Isandro said.

"That's the arithmetic."

"I know, Papa." And then, because she is her mother's daughter after all: "Do the arithmetic out loud next time. I'd like to hear you say it."

* * *

We printed it.

I could dress it up — tell you about the technical difficulties, the shortage of good display type, the way Hessler complained about the woodcuts they sent along (a lurid engraving of soldiers among burning houses, recycled, I'd swear, from some older war, some older lie). I could describe the mechanics until your eyes glaze, because mechanics are my refuge and always have been. A compositor under spiritual assault can lose himself in kerning. There were three days in which I adjusted the leading on that accursed broadsheet eleven times, chasing a perfection that would change nothing about what the page said, because if my hands were going to do this thing, they were going to do it beautifully, and if that isn't a definition of damnation I don't know what is.

But the plain fact stands: we printed it. Two thousand copies, then two thousand more the following week, with corrections — the committee had "refined" the casualty figure upward, and someone in their office had added a detail about a priest being forced to baptize children in blood before the killing started. That last one even made Hessler pause. Hessler, who once set a funeral notice for a man he'd personally seen drunk and alive the day before, on the grounds that the family paid extra for promptness. Hessler read the baptism detail, set down his composing stick, went into the yard, and was sick against the wall. Then he came back and finished the forme without a word, and neither of us ever spoke of it, and that silence felt different from all our other silences. Heavier. Like lead, actually, which is fitting, since it sat in the shop with us from then on, occupying space, weighing on every case.

Isandro refused to touch the massacre broadsheet. She found reasons — the galley needed reorganizing, an order of quoins hadn't arrived, her father required assistance with the ledger. Transparent, all of it. The committee's overseer, a narrow man called Perrin who smelled of vinegar and ambition, noticed her absence from the press floor and asked me about it directly.

"My daughter handles the accounts," I told him. "A press that can't keep books is a press that can't keep promises."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I understood that he was deciding whether I was clever or stupid, and that either answer might be fatal, and that the only escape was to be both at once. So I held his gaze with the mild, slightly bewildered expression I have cultivated over decades of dealing with customers who want their wedding invitations yesterday, and eventually he sniffed and moved on.

My father taught me that craft is neutral — that type serves whatever hand sets it, the way a knife serves cook or cutthroat. I believed him for fifty years. I am here to report that he was wrong. Craft is not neutral. Craft remembers. Every night since the broadsheet, my hands have remembered what they set, and they wake me at odd hours flexing and unflexing, trying, I think, to shake something off that cannot be shaken off, because ink doesn't wash out of the skin no matter what the apprentices claim, and somewhere in this city there are two thousand sheets with my spacing, my justification, my care, telling mothers in the market square that their sons died at Sainte-Aurelle who may be alive, or telling the living that they must now grieve people who never existed, or — worst thought of all, the one that comes at three in the morning — telling the truth badly, in place of the real dead, whose actual names nobody will ever set now, because we filled the column inches first.

* * *

Here is what I did, though. Here is the thing I did, which is either the smallest virtue or the most elaborate vanity of my life, and I genuinely do not know which.

On the morning the second printing ran, before the overseer arrived, I set one additional copy. A single sheet, on the proof press, small and quick, the way I pull proofs for checking. But this was not a proof of their lie. It was the true record — or as true as I could assemble it, which wasn't fully true, and I want that recorded too. I wrote it myself, in the voice of a printer's note, the kind of thing that used to appear in colophons in the old days, when printers still signed their work like men:

*Printed at Vellamare, in the winter of the bridge. The undersigned compositor was ordered to produce the foregoing account of Sainte-Aurelle. He did so under compulsion, believing the account false in whole or part. No independent testimony was available to him. He records here that the document was produced by persons unknown, without witness names, without dates verifiable, in the manner of men writing what they wish had happened. If any reader ever learns the truth of Sainte-Aurelle, let them weigh this sheet against it, and forgive the hands that set the other.*

Then I set my name. Not Aldering — the shop name, the shield we'd hidden behind for twelve years. My own name. Sterling. A name from the far bank, from before, from the life my wife and I built and buried. Setting those letters was the hardest work my hands have done since her funeral. S-t-e-r-l-i-n-g. Seven sorts. I picked each one out of the case individually, and my hands shook, and I let them.

I pulled exactly one proof. Then I broke up the type — distributed every letter back to its compartment, because leaving it standing was a death sentence for everyone in the building — and I dried the single sheet over the stove lamp, folded it once, and burned it.

Not all of it. I burned it halfway. Held the corner to the flame until the fire had eaten across a third of the page, curling the edges of my confession into black lace, and then I pinched it out. What remained was half a relic: my statement of compulsion legible, my signature half-consumed, the ash-fractured edge like something recovered from a fire that had tried to destroy the evidence and been interrupted.

Why? I have asked myself this every night since, lying awake beside a wife who is twelve years dead, in a house above a shop that prints lies by day. Why burn it at all, if you wanted it kept? Why keep it at all, if you were willing to burn it?

The honest answer — and I promised myself honesty, though you may have noticed how expensive it's become — the honest answer is that I wanted to have done it. I wanted, someday, when this war ends and someone asks me, as someone surely will, *what did you do during the bridge winter*, to be able to take this scorched fragment from its hiding place and say: I complied, and I confessed, and I kept the confession where the fire could almost reach it. Look. Half a page. Proof that somewhere inside the coward there was a man trying to get out.

And I already know what Isandro would say to that, if she ever saw it. She'd look at me with her mother's level gray eyes and she'd say: *Papa, a confession nobody reads is just a nicer way of hiding.* Because she found the letters under the floor, my daughter. She found what her mother hid from me — or what I agreed, God forgive us both, to hide with her mother — and she has not yet asked me the full question, but it is coming. It is coming down the corridor of our quiet house with slow deliberate steps, and I can hear it getting closer every day.

Her mother made me promise. That's the shape of it, though the shape keeps shifting whenever I try to look at it straight. *Never speak of the Nakamura orchard.* Never speak of what we owned across the river, of what we owed and were owed, of the deed that sat behind its brick like a heart behind ribs. She made me promise on the last night, when speaking was nearly all she had left, and I promised because you promise the dying everything, and because keeping the secret had kept us alive through the purges, and because grief makes cowards of us and calls it loyalty. Twelve years I have kept it. I told myself I kept it for the children. Some nights I still believe that. Other nights I understand that I kept it because the truth would have required things of me — journeys, admissions, confrontations with men named Corvane — and I am a man who has always preferred the trouble he knows to the trouble he deserves.

Now the revolution wants my press. The committee wants my compliance. Somewhere across the ice a Corvane officer stands at a window, and my daughter has seen him, and come home with a look on her face I haven't seen since her mother died — that look of a person who has discovered the floorboards lift.

And I sit here at midnight with half a burned page in my breast pocket, over my actual heart, like the hypocrite's amulet it is, knowing that the time of my silences is running out. Knowing that my daughter is going to come to me soon — tomorrow, next week, the day the auditor arrives, some hour I cannot choose — and ask me what I cut away, and why, and whether the promise she found in her mother's hand means what it says.

And I will have to decide, in that hour, which father she gets: the one who kept us safe, or the one who tells the truth.

I have been the first one for twelve years.

I burned a page tonight to prove there's another one in here somewhere.

Tomorrow the committee sends new copy. Tomorrow we set more lies, and my hands will do it beautifully, and I will carry my scorched half-truth against my chest like a coin for a ferryman who hasn't arrived yet. And downstairs, past midnight, I heard Isandro's lamp burning in the workroom, and the small patient sounds of her reading — reading something old, something hidden, something with her mother's handwriting on it — and I lay in the dark and listened to my daughter excavate our buried life one page at a time, and I understood that the question I dreaded was no longer coming.

It had already begun.
