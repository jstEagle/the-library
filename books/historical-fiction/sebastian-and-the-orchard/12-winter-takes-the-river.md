## 12. Winter Takes the River

### Isandro

The river froze on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. If a thing as vast as the Vellamare is going to die, I thought it might at least have the courtesy to do it on a day of consequence — a Sunday, a festival, the anniversary of some atrocity. Instead it chose a gray workday, and by morning the ferries were chained to their moorings and the ice had gone white from bank to bank like a page someone had blotted before the ink could dry.

Father stood at the shop window with his hands behind his back, watching the ice boats — the foolhardy few who walked out to test the crossing — and said nothing. That was its own kind of sentence.

"You're thinking about the chart," I said.

"I'm thinking about bread," he said. "The chart can wait until we've eaten."

We had not eaten well in nine days. The granaries on the north bank had been seized by the committee in the autumn, ostensibly for equal distribution, and distribution had turned out to be a word with flexible joints. What reached us was flour cut with chalk — I knew chalk; I could taste the difference between filler and mineral the way other people tasted salt — and dried beans that required an optimism about soaking time I no longer possessed.

Downstairs, Mireille coughed.

It had started as a rattle in her chest the week the bridge closed, the kind of damp sound a cellar makes when the damp gets into things. She was still sleeping beneath the paper store, between the reams of stock and the tins of lamp oil, because moving her was impossible and admitting she existed was worse. Every cough traveled up through the floorboards and into the composing room, where we all pretended to be very interested in our quoins.

"She needs a doctor," I told Father.

"Doctor Ferro was denounced in October."

"Then a different doctor."

"The remaining ones are committee men, Isandro. You'd be asking them to certify the health of a woman the state believes doesn't exist." He rubbed his eyes with ink-stained fingers, leaving faint gray crescents under them, so that he looked permanently exhausted even on the mornings he wasn't. "I'm trying to think of a way to buy willow bark without anyone asking why a print shop needs medicine."

"Say it's for your joints."

"My joints are famous," he agreed. "Everyone knows my joints. It's practically the shop's second product line."

It should be noted that my father jokes now. He did not used to. The joking began around the time he burned the true account of the massacre at Harrow Field, one page out of a run of four hundred, and kept the ash of it in a tin on his shelf like a saint's finger bone. I have decided not to decide what that means. There isn't time, and besides, deciding things has become dangerous. In Vellamare this winter, opinions were held the way you hold a full cup while walking — carefully, close to the body, aware that any sudden movement would spill you into ruin.

The city itself had learned to whisper. You could hear it if you stood in the bridge square at dusk: hundreds of people breathing quietly on both banks, looking across the ice at each other, doing arithmetic. Who had food. Who had fuel. Who had been seen talking to whom. The revolution had promised us a new world and delivered instead a very old one — the kind our grandparents fled — where every neighbor was a ledger entry and every silence was either loyalty or evidence.

Mireille coughed again. I went downstairs with water I'd boiled twice, and sat with her in the dark among the paper, and listened to her fever talk.

"It was snowing that day too," she murmured. "At the orchard. Old Corvane wore gloves the whole signing, did I tell you? Never took them off. A man who signs a promise in gloves—" she smiled at something only she could see, "—is a man planning to wash his hands of it."

"Mireille. Drink this."

She drank. Then her eyes found me in the lamplight, suddenly clear and terribly awake. "Girl. When they come for the deed — and they will come — remember that paper burns faster than people forgive. Move first. Paper always moves first."

I did not know what she meant. I wrote it down anyway, later, in the margin of a proof sheet, where marginalia goes unnoticed by everyone except future readers, who are exactly the people I was beginning to hope for.

### Tomas

Here is what nobody tells you about being questioned by the Committee of Public Vigilance: there is a great deal of waiting, and the waiting chairs are deliberately uncomfortable. I know they are deliberate. I am an apprentice compositor; I notice craftsmanship, even malicious craftsmanship. Someone built those chairs to punish sitting, and someone else approved the design, and both of them probably sleep fine at night, which tells you most of what you need to know about the new government.

They questioned me about the type case.

"Inventory discrepancies," said the thin one, whose name was Audin and whose voice sounded like a file drawn slowly across soft metal. "Your shop reports eleven cases of brevier lost since September. Explain."

"Broken sorts," I said. "We melt down damaged type. It's standard practice."

"Eleven cases' worth?"

"Winter is hard on lead." This was technically true and entirely false, depending on which eleven cases you meant — the ones genuinely cracked by cold, or the ones Isandro and I had been quietly redistributing to hide the ferryman's chart, folded small, between layers of tissue paper. I had never lied to an authority figure before. It turns out it is exactly like setting a line of type: you must commit fully to each character or the whole justification fails.

Audin leaned forward. Behind him, a stove burned peat, and the smell of it made me homesick for a home that hadn't been mine in two years, which is the sort of trick grief plays when you're tired.

"You were seen at the riverbank on Thursday night," he said. "Near the old ferry stairs. With the Sterling girl."

"We were walking."

"At midnight."

"We're young," I said. "Midnight is when we're at our best."

He didn't laugh. Nobody on the committee laughed; I suspect it was in their charter. But something flickered behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, of a boy he might once have been, before whatever had happened to him happened — and he waved me toward the door with a listlessness that I understood, with a lurch, to be mercy.

"One more thing." He said it as I reached the door, and my heart performed a maneuver I would describe as a press jam if presses could feel terror. "The woman your shop took in. From the east quarter."

I turned around very slowly, the way you turn when turning fast would confirm something.

"What woman?"

"The one the Sterlings feed." He looked at me for a long moment, and then, quietly, almost inaudibly, he added: "Fever cases are being reported to the medical bureau now. All of them. It's policy." And then, louder, bored again: "Go home, apprentice. Watch your justification."

I walked back through streets where snow had filled the gutters and frozen into gray ridges, past shuttered bakeries and a wall where yesterday's denunciations had already been pasted over by newer denunciations, the paper layering like sediment, like geology, like the city growing a shell. And I thought: *he knows.* Of course he knows. Everyone on the committee probably knows everything; knowing is their industry. The question is not whether they see us. The question is what they're waiting for.

When I told Isandro, she was silent for a long moment, and then she said the worst thing she could possibly have said:

"They're not searching yet. Which means they don't need to search. Which means they're waiting for us to come to *them*."

### Aldering Sterling

I have cut letters for thirty-one years, and I am here to tell you that a letter is a small machine for holding a breath. Every character in every case in this shop is a breath somebody caught and made permanent. *B* is a breath held against bad news. *P* against prayer. *S*, God help us all, against screaming.

This winter the whole shop holds its breath.

I keep inventory because inventory keeps me sane. Flour: three days, four if we water the soup, which we do, which is why the soup is now essentially warm chalk with ambitions. Lamp oil: a week. Willow bark: none — the apothecary wanted payment in coal, and coal is the one currency colder than coin this season. Mireille's fever has climbed from *damp cellar* to *worrying* to a register I measure by how much she talks. At *low* she sleeps. At *middle* she talks about orchards and gloves and a man named Perrin who I gather was a ferryman. Tonight she reached *high*: she talked to people who weren't in the room, and answered them, and one of her answers was *"No — not the girl — take the type instead,"* which frightened me more than anything the committee has done all winter.

My daughter thinks I don't hear her at night, going down the stairs with boiled water. She thinks her father's hearing stopped at the same age as his courage. Perhaps she's right. Perhaps courage and hearing are the same organ, and mine atrophied years ago, the night I helped bury a deed and a promise under the floorboards of a dead woman's choosing.

But here is what Isandro does not know, because she was a child and I let her remain one: I did not hide that deed only out of cowardice. I hid it because her mother asked me to, with a fever of her own in her lungs, and her last coherent instruction to me was *"not yet"* — not *never*, mark you, but *not yet*. A printer learns the difference. *Never* is a locked case. *Not yet* is type set and tied, waiting in the galley for its page.

Some nights I go down to the stone where the letters sleep and I put my palm flat on the floorboard and I think: *soon, Anouk. Soon the page will make up.* I do not say it aloud. Even the mice inform on people now.

Tonight, though — tonight something changed. A knock at the street door at half past ten, soft, three beats, pause, one beat. Not the committee's knock; theirs is a fist with paperwork. I opened it to find a boy of perhaps twelve, half-frozen, holding out a scrap of paper with both hands like an offering.

"For the print shop," he said. "A man paid me a copper. He was wrapped up. He talked funny, through cloth." The boy ran off before I could ask anything else, sensible child.

The note, in a hand I did not know:

**THE COMMITTEE AUDITS YOUR SHOP WITHIN THE WEEK. THEY SEEK PAPER OF THE OLD PROMISE. WHAT YOU DO BEFORE THEY KNOCK DECIDES WHO BURNS.**

No signature. But the phrasing — *what you do decides who burns* — had the cadence of a man who spends his life weighing consequences, and there is precisely one family in this city that weighs nothing else.

Corvane. The son, presumably. Reaching across the ice by proxy, the way you reach across a table too wide for arms: with a note, a coin, a child.

I read it three times by the banked stove. Then I carried it upstairs to my daughter, who was mending a broken *Q* by lamplight, her tongue between her teeth, her mother's stubbornness sitting on her shoulders like a shawl.

"Isandro," I said. "Put down the type. We need to talk about burning."

### Mireille

The fever has made the cellar honest. That's the one gift of it. When you're hot enough, you stop curating.

I am lying among the reams of paper — good rag stock, the kind that lasts centuries, which strikes me as hilarious, since the words printed on it lately deserve pulp — and I am having conversations with the dead, who turn out to be excellent listeners. Old Corvane is here, gloved to the elbow. My brother is here, though I don't discuss him with the others. And Anouk Nakamura is here most nights, standing at the foot of the imaginary ladder, patient as a proof sheet, waiting for me to get the story right.

Because that's the trouble, you see. I have the story. I have had it for twenty years, folded up inside me like the chart in the type case, and a story you keep is a story that curdles. I watched the promise signed. I watched Perrin ferry the lawyer across at dawn with the deed in an oilcloth wrap. I watched the committee's man — young then, ambitious, careful — stand in the orchard doorway with his hat in his hands and swear he'd witness it faithfully, and then I watched him, three weeks later, carry the news of that witnessing straight to the men who needed the promise to fail.

I told no one. Telling felt like spending the last coin of a dead woman. So the coin stayed in my pocket through two governments and one marriage and one long widowhood, and now I lie in a cellar spending it at last, feverishly, in fragments, to a girl who mostly thinks I'm raving.

Maybe I am. But raving and testifying use the same mouth.

The girl came down again tonight. She does that — comes down with water, sits in the dark, asks small questions designed to look like kindness and function as reconnaissance. Good instincts. Wrong century for them, but good.

"How high is it today?" she asked, meaning the fever, pressing her cool wrist to my forehead like a printer testing ink coverage.

"High enough to see the whole page," I told her.

"What page?"

"The one they're going to ask you about." I gripped her hand, harder than I meant to; the fever does my gripping for me now. "Listen. When the auditor comes — and he's already come to others, I've heard it through the floor, the whole city is one floor now — he'll offer you a trade. They always dress it as mercy. Safety for silence. Your father's name cleared for the deed surrendered. And it will sound reasonable, child, that's the horror of it, it will sound like the only sane thing in an insane winter—"

"Mireille, slow down—"

"—and you must ask him," I said, "for time. Three days. Ask for three days, and spend every hour of them. Because a bargain offered in panic is a bargain priced for the seller's ruin, and you—" the cough took me then, doubled me over among the clean white reams, and I finished it into her shoulder, "—you are not for ruining."

She held me a while. She has her mother's stillness, that girl, the stillness of someone who learned early that movement attracts attention. But underneath it, when I pressed my fevered ear to her collarbone, I heard a heart going like a treadle-press, fast and steady, printing something.

Good, I thought. Let it print. Let it print all winter if it must.

Let it set the page.

### Isandro

On the last morning of the chapter of our lives I would later call *the waiting*, I woke to a sound I had not heard in weeks: the bell above the street door.

Nobody used the door anymore. The committee knocked; clients had vanished with the bridge; the bell had hung silent so long that its voice had gone rusty, and it rang now with a cracked, complaining note, like a man announcing terrible news against his will.

I came down the stairs to find Father already in the shop, standing very straight, wearing his company face. In the doorway, stamped shut against the cold, stood a woman in the gray coat of the Bureau of Records, with a leather folio under her arm and snow melting on her shoulders, and beside her — because the universe is not subtle — the thin man from the questioning rooms. Audin. His breath smoked around him like incense at a funeral.

"Sterling," the woman said, unrolling her authority like a bolt of cloth. "Aldering Sterling, proprietor, and the daughter, Isandro, listed as apprentice. Good. I am Auditor Verel, Committee of Public Accounts. We're conducting reconciliation of conscripted properties." Her eyes moved across the shop the way a compositor's eye moves across a galley — quick, total, missing nothing. "You'll open your stores. All of them."

"All of them," Father repeated pleasantly.

"Paper, type, ink. And any private correspondence retained on premises." She set the folio on the imposing stone with a click that sounded, to my ringing ears, exactly like a typecase sliding shut. "Effective immediately."

Behind me on the stairs, barely visible in the dark of the stairwell, I heard Mireille's door ease closed — softly, softly — and below the fear in my chest, steady as a treadle, something began to print.

Three days, she'd said.

I counted what we had left on my fingers, and came up with fewer than one.

"We'll need a moment to fetch the keys," my father said, and his voice never shook, and I loved him for it, and I hated that love was the only thing left in the shop we couldn't inventory.

Auditor Verel smiled, took off her gloves finger by finger, and settled onto the stool by the imposing stone as though she intended to stay until spring.
