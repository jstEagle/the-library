## 13. The Committee Comes Calling

The auditor arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were ink days — the one morning of the week when the shop smelled so thoroughly of lampblack and linseed that no visitor could leave without carrying proof of us on his coat. If the Committee of Public Correspondence wanted to catch the Aldering shop in a lie, they had picked the one day we could not help but look guilty.

"His name is Auditor Vess," Tomas said, sliding through the back door with snow in his hair and panic in his voice, both melting at once. "He's coming here. Today. Before noon."

My father did not stop setting type. That was his way — fear went into his hands and came out as industry. "Did they say why?"

"They never say why." Tomas pulled off his gloves finger by finger, as if even undressing were now a suspicious act. "Mireille's fever broke last night, that's something. But Hessler was seen talking to a committee man by the tannery, and now this. It's the timing, Isandro. You don't send an auditor because everything's fine."

I looked at the floor.

Not idly — I want to be clear about that. I looked at it the way a gambler looks at cards she has already counted. Beneath the paper store, beneath three layers of plank and forty years of spilled ink, my mother's letters lay wrapped in oilcloth beside the true deed: the document that named heirs of *both* families, Nakamura and Corvane jointly, in language that made the revolution itself illegal where our orchard stood. The committee wanted that paper. Mireille had told us so through her fever, and the auditor was coming to collect.

Three days. That was what I'd bargained for when Vess's superior had first made the offer — the deed in exchange for my family's safety and Mireille delivered to prison. Three days to find another road. I had spent two of them learning ice charts and testing the river's dark places with Tomas by lantern light, and I had one left, and the river did not care about my arithmetic.

"Papa," I said. "The letters."

"I know where the letters are." He still didn't look up. His composing stick clicked, clicked, clicked — a small patient metronome that had governed my whole childhood. "I have known since the night you found them, when you thought you were being quiet."

"You knew?"

"I raised you in a print shop, Isandro. I know what your quiet sounds like. Your mother's quiet was different." He set a line of type down with exaggerated gentleness. "Hers had grief in it."

* * *

We prepared the way printers prepare for anything: obsessively, and mostly too late.

The letters stayed where they were. Moving them was riskier than keeping them — Vess would know every hiding place worth knowing, because auditors are trained by other auditors, and hiding places are a tradition passed down like trade secrets. Instead we built a decoy. My father pulled a case of damaged sorts from the scrap bin, the ones too worn or chipped to sell, and we buried them under the loose boards near the furnace where any searcher would be sure to dig, finding exactly the kind of pathetic cache a frightened printer would keep: old correspondence from the Guild, two years stale, nothing but invoices and a complaint about paper prices.

"It has to look found," my father said, arranging the fake cache with the tenderness he usually reserved for his best matrices. "Not hidden well, not hidden badly. Hidden the way an honest man hides things — which is to say, stupidly."

"And if he doesn't stop there?"

"Then we're past preparation and into luck." He straightened, wiped his hands on his apron, and for the first time all morning allowed himself to meet my eyes. He looked older than he had in autumn. Revolution ages people faster than grief does; grief at least lets you sit down. "Isandro. Whatever he asks me, whatever he says — let me answer. You were raised on silences. I need you to trust mine one more time."

I wanted to argue. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of my own narration: I wanted to argue, and I didn't, because the bridge was closed and the ice was thin and there was no version of arguing that got the letters across the river by nightfall.

"All right," I said.

Mireille, upstairs in the loft bed we'd moved her to, insisted on being carried down and propped in the chair by the cold stove like an invalid aunt — which, in the way of these things, she had somehow become. She was gray-faced and sharp-tongued, which I had learned to read as recovering. When I told her who was coming, she laughed until it turned into coughing.

"Vess," she said. "Little Vess. He used to carry my mother's marketing basket for a copper. Now he carries the Committee's warrants." Her eyes, fever-bright, fixed on me. "He'll search the girl's room first. They always do. It flatters their theory that secrets live with women and children."

"Where should I be?"

"In plain sight, doing something tedious. Mend some bindings. Auditors distrust anyone caught idle and ignore anyone caught boring." She patted my hand with fingers that still trembled. "Boredom, child, is the cheapest armor there is. Wear it well."

* * *

Auditor Vess arrived at half past eleven, four minutes early, with two men whose function was to stand in doorways and be furniture with opinions.

He was smaller than I expected. That sounds like a foolish observation, but I've thought about it since, and I believe it mattered. Men who are small learn early how to make rooms feel small instead, and Vess stepped into our shop and shrank it around himself without raising his voice. He wore the Committee's gray sash over a coat that had been good fifteen years ago, and he removed his hat, and he thanked my father for the warmth as though the warmth had been extended voluntarily.

"Aldering," he said, reading the name off the lintel sign though everyone in Vellamare knew it. "Typefounder. Conscription order seventeen-C. You've been productive for us."

"As ordered," my father said.

"Yes. As ordered." Vess smiled the smile of a man who has been issued smiles by the same authority that issues his paper. "The pamphlet on the Harrow Street action was very well received. Very convincing work."

I was mending a binding at the bench by the window, and I kept my eyes on my thread and my face on nothing at all, because *convincing* was doing terrible work in that sentence. The Harrow Street pamphlet described a massacre that had not happened, printed from plates my father had cut with his own hands, and every letter of it had cost him something I could see leaving him like heat off iron.

"The Committee appreciates diligence," Vess continued. "Diligence, and discretion. Which brings me to why I'm here." He drifted along the type wall, trailing one gloved finger an inch from the cases, touching nothing, cataloguing everything. "There is a rumor. Rumors are my profession, Aldering — the Committee collects them the way you collect lead. This one concerns a certain document. A deed. Perhaps you've heard it mentioned?"

"Heard of deeds," my father said. "Cut plenty of them. Land transfers, dowry settlements, the usual paperwork of property." He said it the way he set a line of type: level, unhurried, each word seated firmly against its neighbor. "People pay good money for certainty on paper."

"Mm. And have you printed such a document recently? For a client?"

"No."

"For anyone?"

"No."

Vess stopped at the furnace corner. Of course he did. Mireille had called the search pattern before breakfast, and there it was unfolding like a proof sheet: he crouched, ran his glove along the loose board we'd prepared, and lifted it with the delicacy of a man lifting a stone he fully expected to find a beetle under. The Guild papers sat in their shallow hollow, dusty, unremarkable, damningly mundane.

He read every page standing there. Twice, I think. His men watched the doors. I stitched my binding and bored myself so thoroughly I nearly yawned, which I suppose meant the armor was working.

"Invoices," Vess said at last, sounding almost disappointed. "You hide invoices?"

"A printer hides everything," my father said mildly. "It's habit. Paper costs money; sentiment costs more. Better to bury both than have them lying about."

Something flickered across Vess's face — not suspicion exactly, but its cousin, the one that lives next door and borrows tools. He refolded the invoices along their original creases, which I noted with professional admiration was exactly correct, replaced them, and reset the board so precisely that I alone could have told it had been moved.

And then he turned toward the paper store.

* * *

I will describe the next four minutes as accurately as I can, because accuracy is the whole religion of my trade and I have never needed it more.

Vess walked into the paper store slowly. The room is narrow, shelved floor to ceiling, reeking of rag stock and sizing. The trapdoor to the underfloor space sits beneath the third shelf, covered by a rug my father bought specifically because it was ugly enough that no thief would imagine treasure under it. The letters and the deed were under that rug, under that door, under that floor, wrapped in oilcloth, twelve inches from a man sent to take them.

Vess stood in the doorway. He looked at the shelves. He looked at the rug.

"Stock levels," he said. "The Committee requires an accounting of conscripted materials. What remains of the requisitioned paper?"

My father gave him numbers. I watched him do it and understood, in a way I hadn't before, that my father had been lying to someone his entire life — to the Guild, to the tax assessors, to my mother about how bad the debts were — but always in increments, always in the currency of approximation. What he did then was different. Vess asked whether any stock had gone missing since the conscription, whether any sheets had left the premises unrecorded, whether anyone unconnected to the shop had access to the stores.

"No," my father said.

"No to which?"

"All of them." And then — and this is the part I return to, the part I keep like a relic of my own — my father smiled. Not Vess's issued smile. A warm one, rueful, the smile of a tired craftsman sharing a joke with a fellow professional. "You'll think less of me, Auditor, but I'll say it: I'm glad the Committee took the paper. My daughter hoards it. Every scrap, every misprint. She says we'll need it when the war ends, to print the truth." He shook his head fondly. "Sentiment, like I said. Costs more than paper ever did."

Every eye in the room came to me. I looked up from my binding with the expression of a young woman interrupted mid-stitch, faintly annoyed, entirely harmless.

"It's not hoarding," I said. "It's inventory."

Vess studied me for a long moment. Then he laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of him, the laugh of the boy who had once carried Mireille's mother's basket. "Inventory," he repeated. "God help the peace, when it comes. Everyone will have an inventory."

He stepped into the paper store after all. He walked the length of it. He stood directly over the rug.

I have since decided that time is a typesetter like any other, and in those seconds it was setting a line I couldn't read yet. Vess looked down. The stove ticked in the next room. Somewhere above us Mireille shifted in her chair, one floorboard speaking under her weight — a single soft syllable, *there*, and Vess glanced up at the ceiling, and my heart performed a maneuver I did not authorize.

"Your lodger?" he asked.

"My wife's sister," my father said. "Convalescent. Fever took her hard in the new year. She's no interest in politics; her interests are broth and complaining about my housekeeping." He said *my wife's sister* the way he'd seat a difficult ligature — smoothly, with no visible effort, and I knew it for the second fluent lie of his life inside ten minutes, because Mireille was no kin of ours at all except in the ways that had come to matter more.

"Fever," Vess said. "Half the city has it. The other half will." He stepped back out of the paper store, and the rug lay undisturbed behind him, and the line of type held. He settled his hat on his head. "Thank you for the warmth, Aldering. Thank your daughter for the lesson in inventory." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "One thing more. A formality. The Committee has learned that a woman named Corvane may have correspondence in circulation — old correspondence, pre-revolution. Family letters. If such papers surfaced in a respectable shop like this one, the finder would be remembered favorably. Favorable memory is a kind of currency these days. Scarce as paper."

"We print what comes through the door, Auditor," my father said. "Nothing else has ever come through it."

"No," Vess agreed softly. "Only what you let in."

He left. The furniture-with-opinions left. The door closed, and the bell over it gave its small idiot cheer, and none of us moved for the length of ten breaths.

* * *

Upstairs, Mireille was laughing again, or something adjacent to laughing, wheezing into her shawl. "Broth and complaining," she gasped. "Oh, Aldering, you've ruined me. I'll never complain about your housekeeping again without wondering if you mean it as cover."

My father stood in the middle of the room with his cap in his hands, looking at it as though he'd never seen it before. "Twice," he said quietly. "In thirty years of this shop, I have told the truth for a living — cut it, cast it, sold it by the pound. And today I lied twice in ten minutes and it was the easiest work I've done all winter." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "Do you understand what that means, Isandro? It means I could have done it anytime. All those years of silence — I told myself I wasn't capable of anything else. That silence was just who I was." His voice cracked on the last of it. "It was a choice. It was always a choice. I chose wrong for twelve years, and today I chose right, and I don't know yet which choice puts us in the ground."

I put my arms around him. He smelled of lampblack and linseed, the smell of my entire childhood, the smell of Tuesdays.

"Papa," I said into his shoulder. "Tonight I cross the river. Tomorrow Vess comes back for the exchange, and he finds either the forged deed or nothing at all, and either way it won't be me standing here when he does."

He held me at arm's length then, and I saw him weigh it — the protector's arithmetic, the risk of letting me go weighed against the larger risk of keeping me. Twelve years of silence had taught him nothing if not how to calculate a loss.

Below us, in the paper store, the ugly rug lay over everything we had. Outside, the light was already going the color of weak tea, and the river waited, frozen, indifferent, holding its breath the way the whole city held its breath — and somewhere on the far bank a Corvane was watching the sky for me, with hours, not days, left to be found.
