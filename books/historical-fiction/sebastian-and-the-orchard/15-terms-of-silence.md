## 15. Terms of Silence

The auditor arrived on a Thursday, which felt like an insult. Thursdays were ink-delivery days. Thursdays were the one morning my father allowed himself the small ceremony of testing each new barrel against his thumb and pronouncing it *serviceable* or *an insult to the craft*, as though the fate of nations hung on viscosity. It was the closest thing our household had to church.

So when Citizen Auditor Veyre came up the alley with two militiamen and a leather satchel that looked expensive in the way confiscated things look expensive, I was elbow-deep in a keg of revolutionary broadsheets, and my first thought was not *we are undone*. My first thought was *not today, of all days, he'll get ink on his boots*.

I have since learned that this is how terror works in people like me. Not as thunder. As housekeeping. You meet catastrophe with your sleeves rolled up and your mind fixed on the wrong detail entirely, because the right detail would stop your hands from moving, and stopped hands are dead hands in a print shop during a war.

"Citizen Nakamura." Veyre's voice was mild. That was the worst thing about him. The committee's other men shouted; Veyre conversed, as though we were two merchants haggling over the price of rag paper. "Your father is at home?"

"He's at the press."

"Then we'll disturb him there."

He did not wait to be led. He knew where the press was. Of course he did — he'd sat through the inventory three weeks ago, running his gloved finger along our type cases like a man reading braille, asking questions about output quotas with the pleasant persistence of a dentist probing for the soft tooth. Hessler had answered most of them. I remembered that now, watching Veyre's back cross our threshold without invitation, and I felt something cold settle behind my sternum. Hessler had been tired lately. Tired men answer too much.

* * *

My father stood at the hand-press with a proof sheet in his hands, and I watched him do the thing he always did when frightened: become very good at his work. His shoulders squared. His chin lifted a degree. He examined that proof sheet as if it were the last copy of scripture on earth.

"Auditor," he said. "We're behind on the committee's order. If you've come about the delay—"

"I haven't." Veyre set his satchel on our stone floor — *our* stone floor, laid by my grandfather — and folded his hands. "I've come about an orchard."

The word dropped into the room like a piece of type into a galley: small, hard, exactly shaped to fit the space waiting for it.

My father set down the proof sheet with exaggerated care. I saw him decide, in real time, what face to wear. Twelve years of practice went into that decision. He chose polite bafflement. "There's no orchard in Vellamare, citizen."

"There's one across the river." Veyre smiled faintly. "Nakamura land, deeded jointly to your late wife's family and to the Corvanes, by some act of peacetime sentimentality that both families appear to have spent the intervening years pretending never happened. The committee has known of its existence since autumn. What the committee lacks" — he turned, and looked directly at me for the first time — "is the deed itself. The original. In her mother's hand."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs Mireille coughed — a small, dry sound, quickly smothered, but in that silence it might as well have been a bell tolling. I saw Veyre's head tilt a fraction, a hound marking wind, and then let it go. Or seeming to let it go.

"Citizen," I said, and was surprised to hear my own voice come out level, "if you believe such a document existed, you must also believe we'd have burned it months ago."

"Oh, certainly." He said it warmly, approvingly, as one craftsman complimenting another's technique. "That's precisely what a careful family would do. But your family, Citizen Nakamura, is not careful in the way you imagine. Your father hid debts from the Corvanes for six years. Your mother corresponded with their heir under a false name. And you—" he stepped closer, and I caught the smell of him, cedar and lamp oil, "—you have spent the entire winter looking at me across that parley table like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want anyone else to see."

At the bridge gate. Three weeks ago, Elias Corvane on the far bank saying *the orchard* in that low voice, and me standing in the snow feeling the ground tilt under my boots. Apparently I had worn all of that on my face like a headline.

"You're mistaken," my father began.

"I'm rarely mistaken, Master Aldering. It's the one talent they pay me for." Veyre opened his satchel and drew out papers with unhurried precision, laying them on the composing stone one by one like a dealer laying out a losing hand. "Let us dispense with the theater. Here is your shop's conscription status — compliant, currently. Here is your daughter's name, entered twice in the gate logs this winter, once on a day no errand could account for. And here—" he tapped the third sheet, "—is the disposition of one Mireille Duval, formerly of the Riverside choir, currently wanted for sedition, and currently, according to my information, residing somewhere within forty paces of where I am standing."

Mireille's cough had been thirty seconds ago. Forty paces was generous. Veyre was telling us, with perfect courtesy, that he knew exactly which cellar wall she crouched behind, and that he was choosing not to knock on it. Yet.

My father moved then — a small motion, half a step, placing his body between Veyre and the cellar door. He didn't seem to know he'd done it. I don't think Veyre missed it either.

"The terms," Veyre said, folding his hands again. "You will find them reasonable. The committee finds everything reasonable; it's how we sleep."

* * *

Here is what reasonable looked like, laid out on the composing stone among the loose sorts and the wax:

One. The original deed, delivered into committee hands, intact and unaltered, within four days.

Two. In exchange: the Aldering shop struck from the requisition lists. My father's name cleared of the *irregularities* noted in the autumn audit — irregularities being, as far as I could tell, the crime of having once printed pamphlets for a faction that lost. Our family walked away whole.

Three. Mireille Duval surrendered to committee custody. Not executed — Veyre was explicit on this, almost gentle, the way a butcher is gentle explaining the humane stunner. *Detained pending review.* Which everyone in the room understood to mean held until the war found a use for her or forgot her, whichever came first, in a cell whose dimensions I preferred not to imagine.

"And if we decline?" I asked.

Veyre gathered nothing up. That was the answer. He simply stood there amid our tools and our livelihood and our hidden fugitive, letting the silence do arithmetic of its own. Then, mildly: "If you decline, I return tomorrow with fewer courtesies and more men, and we conduct this conversation in the manner the times prefer. I am offering you the version of events where everyone keeps their fingers." He glanced toward the cellar door. "Mostly."

"Why do you want the deed?" It wasn't strategy; it was genuine curiosity, the kind that gets people killed. But I needed to understand the shape of the trap before I stepped in it. "A scrap of old paper. Half the city thinks the Corvane name is finished. Why does the committee care?"

For the first time, something flickered across Veyre's face that was not mild. It was brief — a door opening onto a lit room and shutting again — but I caught it, because reading faces is the second trade they teach a printer's daughter, right after reading letters.

"Because paper outlives regimes, citizen," he said. "Ask your father; he makes his living on the principle. A deed naming heirs of both banks, signed in peacetime, witnessed — it is a document that says the war is a temporary condition. The committee cannot permit documents that say that. Unless, of course, the committee holds them." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was worse. "Leverage is only leverage if you're the one holding it. Your side of the river understands this. Ours merely says it aloud."

* * *

He gave us the room while we discussed it. This was courtesy, or theater — with Veyre the categories had collapsed into each other entirely. He withdrew to the front of the shop and stood examining our specimen books while my father and I conferred in the back in the low rapid dialect of people who have shared a trade and a fear for twelve years.

"We give him a forgery," my father breathed. "Three days is time enough. We cut it clean, age the paper—"

"He'll know." I kept my voice under the press's ambient hum. "Veyre isn't a soldier playing at paperwork, Papa. He read our gate logs. He counts things. And if he catches a forged deed, we haven't bought safety, we've handed him proof we deceived the committee — which turns a negotiation into a conviction."

"Then we give him the true one." His hands were shaking; he pressed them flat against the composing stone to still them. "Isandro. Listen to me. It is paper. Your mother is dead, the orchard is on the far bank, and the man who might have honored that promise is an enemy officer. We give him the deed, we take Mireille's—"

"Mireille goes to prison. That's the price. That's the whole architecture of the bargain — the deed buys *our* safety, and she's the currency."

"She is one woman against four."

"One woman who hid in our cellar for two months. Who told us what she saw at that signing — who told us that *Veyre's own committee leader* stood in that orchard and heard the promise made and buried it anyway." I gripped his wrist. "Papa. She is not currency. She is the witness. And the moment the committee holds both the deed *and* the witness, there is no one left alive who can say what that paper actually means. They can burn it, rewrite it, claim it never existed. We won't be selling a document. We'll be selling the truth's last copy."

My father looked at me for a long moment. There was something in his face I hadn't seen since the night my mother died — not grief exactly, but grief's older sibling, the thing grief grows from. Recognition. He had made this exact trade once before, I realized. Twelve years ago, some smaller version of this bargain, some quieter cellar, some promise traded for safety. And here it was again, grown to full size, come back around like debt comes around.

"You want the three days," he said quietly.

"I want the three days."

"What will you do with them?"

And because lying fluently was still, as of that morning, exclusively his gift and not mine, I told him a partial truth. "Something better than surrendering it and better than forging it. I don't know what yet. But I know that every choice he's offering us costs someone everything, and I want to see if there's a fourth option hiding in the margins. That's how you find them, Papa. In the margins."

From the front of the shop, pleasantly: "Citizens? I do have other appointments. Several of them regrettable."

* * *

I went out to deliver the answer alone. My father wanted to come; I told him someone should stay with Mireille, which was true, and also that his face at that moment could not be trusted near a negotiator, which was truer.

Veyre was standing at the window over the press, looking out at the river. The frozen river, white and locked, the bridge gates chained, the far bank smoke-streaked and distant. From this window you could see the whole shape of the problem: a city sawn in half, a promise sawn in half, a girl standing in a print shop holding half of everything in her head.

"Well?" he said, without turning.

"Three days."

He turned. "Two was my figure."

"Two days to locate and verify a document of that age? Citizen, even your own auditors would call that unreasonable, and you people pride yourselves on reasonableness. Three days, and the deed comes to the committee intact, verified, in my hands — not left in a drawer for some junior clerk to lose or copy. And I want assurance in writing. Your signature. That the shop's exemption stands regardless of what the document contains, and that Mireille Duval's detention is *pending review* — those words, in writing, not 'pending disposal.'"

He considered me for a long moment. I had the distinct sensation of being typeset — measured, fitted, assessed for kerning. I kept my face at body temperature.

"You negotiate like your mother wrote," he said at last. "Precisely, and always with a second meaning." He took out a card, wrote three lines in a small clerk's hand, signed, and set it on the composing stone. "Three days. Friday evening. Bring the deed yourself, Citizen Nakamura. And Isandro—" he paused at the door, and for one unguarded instant looked less like an auditor than like a tired man in a bad winter, "—whatever you're planning in those margins, plan it carefully. I would genuinely prefer not to come back here with the other kind of men. This shop smells like my childhood."

Then he was gone, and the militiamen with him, and the alley was just an alley, and Thursday resumed trying to be a normal day and failing at it completely.

* * *

I stood at the window after he left, holding the signed card, looking at the river.

Three days. On the far bank, somewhere past the smoke, a Corvane lieutenant was presumably still alive and still, impossibly, the other half of my mother's promise. In our cellar, a fevered witness slept with a knife under her blanket and woke calling names of people who'd betrayed her. On the composing stone lay a signed exemption worth more than the building it sat in — and beneath the floor, wrapped in oilcloth under the paper store, the true deed, joint-heired, war-proof, worth everything and safe as long as no one moved it and doomed as soon as anyone did.

Every road out cost somebody everything. Surrender the deed, lose the truth. Forge it, lose the family. Refuse outright, lose all of it at once. Veyre had built his bargain well; every exit was priced.

Unless.

I looked down at the card in my hand, at his neat clerk's script, and I noticed what I'd been too rattled to notice before: he had written *pending review of the Duval matter*, not *pending review of the prisoner Duval*. Review of the *matter*. A lawyer's distinction, or a printer's. It meant the detention terms were attached to the case, not the person — which meant that if the case changed, the terms changed. If the committee's interest in the deed collapsed — if the document became worthless, or dangerous, or publicly known — then Mireille's custody clause collapsed with it.

Paper outlives regimes, Veyre had said. He had meant it as a threat.

He had also, without knowing it, handed me the fourth option.

I tucked the card into my bodice, next to my heart, where we keep the things we intend to betray or be betrayed by, and I went downstairs to find Tomas, because if I was going to learn the ice in three nights, I would need to start tonight — and because there was a ferryman's chart folded inside the type case in the corner, and I had a sudden, urgent need to know whether the river between the banks had any honest crossings left in it at all.

Mireille met me at the bottom of the cellar stairs, fever-bright, gripping the rail.

"They came," she whispered. Not a question. She'd heard everything through the floorboards; the cellar had excellent acoustics for other people's bargains.

"They came."

"And you asked for three days." Her eyes were burning and clear at once, the way they got on her good hours. "Child. Whatever you're thinking of doing — the ice, the boy, the officer, all of it — you should know one thing first." She pulled me close, and her whisper smelled of fever and camphor. "Veyre wasn't bluffing about knowing I'm here. But he *was* bluffing about why he wants the deed. It isn't leverage he's after. His committee leader was in that orchard the day of the signing — and Veyre knows it, and Veyre wants the deed for the same reason I do."

"To destroy it?"

"No." Her grip tightened. "To prove the bastard lied. To hang him with his own broken promise."

Outside, far off, the river groaned under its new ice — a long, deep sound, like the city clearing its throat to say something.

I had three days.
