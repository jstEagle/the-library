## 16. Learning the Ice

**Isandro**

The chart came out of the type case the way all our secrets did: sideways, in the dark, when nobody official was looking.

It was Tomas who found it, which surprised me less than it should have. My father's cases were organized with a monkish precision — every sort in its compartment, every compartment labeled in his small, upright hand — and there was exactly one case he never let anyone touch, the old one with the cracked brass corners that sat at the back of the store like a retired uncle nobody wanted to talk about. I'd assumed it held his grief. Apparently it held a ferryman's map.

"Look at this," Tomas whispered, and pressed a folded square of oilskin into my hands. It smelled of linseed and river.

I opened it by candle stub in the cellar, under Mireille's sleeping shelf, because we had learned by then that secrets kept in daylight grew teeth. The paper inside was soft as cloth from years of handling. Someone had drawn the river in brown ink — not the tidy river of the almanac-makers but a real one, wide-shouldered and opinionated, with little annotations marching along its banks like a gossip's commentary.

*Shallows under the mill race — drowns dogs.* *Ice holds here first, two fingers thick by Saint Ardenna's week.* *NEVER trust the ice past the second pylon; the current keeps a door open all winter.*

The handwriting wasn't my father's. It was looser, more confident, the letters leaning like men walking into wind. At the bottom, signed with a flourish: *Old Bramwen, ferryman of Vellamare, who has drowned nothing yet and means to keep it so.*

"Bramwen," I said. "He ferried Mother across once, didn't he? Before I was born."

"He ferried everyone," Tomas said. "Before the bridge, before all of it." He hesitated. "Your father gave me the case to clean. He said the compartments stuck."

"My father," I said, "gave you the case."

"I think," Tomas said carefully, "that your father wanted us to find it. He just couldn't bring himself to say so."

That was the thing about my father that winter: he could no longer say anything directly. The committee's auditor had made sure of that. Every honest sentence he owned now had to be smuggled out disguised as something else — an errand, an instruction, a case with sticky compartments. He had spent twelve years teaching me silence, and now he was fluent in it too, and fluency, I was learning, is its own kind of confession.

I folded the chart along its worn creases. Somewhere above us, the press stood loaded with tomorrow's lies, and somewhere beyond the north wall, three days of the auditor's grace were already bleeding away. And here in the cellar, a dead ferryman was telling us, in his gossiping brown ink, exactly where the river would hold a person's weight.

"Well," I said. "Saint Ardenna's week is now."

* * *

**Tomas**

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: it gets bored. Ours had sat in the shop for weeks, chewing on us slow — the auditor's visits, Hessler's gray silences, Mireille coughing on her pallet below the paper store — and somewhere in all that chewing, fear got tired of being fear and turned into something with more spine. Call it purpose. Call it desperation wearing purpose's coat. Either way, it was what Isandro and I carried down to the riverbank that night, wrapped in sacking against the cold, with a ferryman's chart in her coat and an iron poker in mine.

We went out through the yard where the coal bins stand, past the wall with the loose stones, moving the way you learn to move in a city full of ears: heel first, no talking, breath held at the corners. The moon was a fingernail paring. Good. We didn't want light. We wanted the dark that knows things.

The river had been freezing for a fortnight, and the city had been pretending otherwise. On the near bank, the revolutionary pickets stamped and smoked at the bridge gate, their brazier throwing orange shadows that made them look taller than they were. On the far bank — invisible tonight, just a black suggestion of rooftops — Lieutenant Corvane's men did whatever enemy officers do at night. Probably also smoked. Probably also looked taller than they were. War is mostly standing around, if you believe the songs, and the songs are liars about everything except that.

"The chart says the mill race shallows freeze first," Isandro breathed. We crouched behind the ice-houses, close enough that I could see her eyes go bright and terrified and determined, all three at once. She'd gotten good at wearing all three. "Two hundred yards downstream. Then the deep channel past the second pylon — we don't touch that. Then the gravel bar opposite the willow bend."

"And how does a dead ferryman know his chart's still true?"

"Ferrymen are like typesetters," she said. "The river doesn't change. Only the letters get rearranged."

I liked that. I decided to believe it, which is most of believing anything.

We reached the mill race with our hearts going like a press run. The ice there was black glass over shallow water, and when I knelt and put my palm flat against it, the cold bit straight through my glove like the river testing whether I was serious. I was. I tapped the poker. Solid knock — not the hollow drum-sound Old Bramwen's margin note warned about (*if it rings, it sings, and if it sings, it's lying to you*).

"Holds," I whispered.

"It'll hold a fox," Isandro said. "Will it hold a woman carrying a deed and a coward's worth of courage?"

"You're not a coward."

"Not yet. Give me until the middle of the river."

She went out onto it anyway. That was Isandro — she'd catalog every risk twice, write the inventory in her head in perfect little columns, and then step onto the ice regardless, because the alternative was staying on the bank, and staying had stopped being safe sometime around the day the letters came up through the floorboards. I watched her walk twenty feet out, weight low, arms out like a girl playing at balance, and come back. When she reached solid ground she exhaled a long white flag of breath and laughed, shaky.

"One crossing tested," she said. "Ninety-nine to go."

"That's not how charts work."

"That's how *our* chart works. We test everything ourselves. Bramwen drowned nothing in forty years, and I intend to be able to say the same."

* * *

**Isandro**

Three nights, we worked. I want to set it down properly, because people afterward imagined something romantic, and it was not romantic. It was cold in the way that makes thought simple. It was kneeling on river ice by moonlight with a poker and a length of rope tied between our waists — Tomas's idea, and the single wisest object either of us brought. If one of us went through, the other was supposed to haul. We agreed solemnly that this was the plan. Privately I suspected we'd both just be dragged in together, a matched pair, but hope is a rope too, and you tie yourself to what's offered.

Night one: the mill race, confirmed. Night two: the willow bend bar, where the ice lay whiter, snow-dusted, treacherous-looking and honest — it knocked solid, held our crawling weight, held our standing weight. Night three was the hard one. The stretch past the second pylon, where the current kept its open door. We didn't cross it. You can't cross a door the river leaves open. But we paced its edge, marked its true width against landmarks on both banks — the broken chimney, the leaning crane, the third arch of the bridge itself — and wrote the measurements into memory, because we couldn't write them anywhere else. Paper gets confiscated. Heads, so far, only get read by God.

On the way back the third night, Tomas stopped in the shadow of the ice-house and said, "Your father knows."

I'd been waiting for it. "Hessler told him?"

"No. Nobody told him. He looked at my boots when we came in. River mud, frozen stiff, different from yard mud. And yesterday he left the poker by the yard door — cleaned, oiled, sharpened. He didn't say a word. He just moved it from the pressroom to where we'd find it."

I stood there in the dark and felt something in my chest turn over, heavy and warm, like a press taking its first pull of the morning. Twelve years of silence between us, and here he was, speaking volumes through the placement of a fire iron. The Nakamura way. The Aldering way too, maybe. All the families built on quiet learn the same language eventually.

"He's telling us to go," I said.

"He's telling us he knows," Tomas corrected. "Telling us to go is Mireille."

And it was. When we climbed down to the cellar after, she was awake — she slept less these nights, the fever leaving her thin and clear-eyed in a way that frightened me worse than the fever had — and she took one look at our frost-brightened faces and knew everything, because Mireille had always known everything, it was practically her trade.

"So," she rasped. "The ice."

"We've found the crossings."

"Good girl." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Then listen, because I won't say this twice and my voice is leaving. The committee doesn't want the deed for leverage. Leverage they'd trade. They want it *burned*. A joint heirship is a weapon pointed at them — it says the war can't legally dispossess Corvane's son, and if the war can't dispossess him, half their confiscations on the far bank are thefts. They need the paper gone and everyone who's read it gone quiet. Do you understand? When you cross, you aren't running from them. You're carrying the one document in Vellamare they cannot allow to exist."

The cellar was very still. Above us, faintly, the press frame ticked as the building cooled.

"Then why haven't they taken it?" Tomas asked. "They've searched twice."

"Because the auditor thinks your father will crack," Mireille said. "Because Hessler bought you days without knowing it. Because committees are slow, and rivers freeze faster." She smiled, and it was a terrible, gallant smile. "You have perhaps one night of grace left. Use the ice while it's honest."

* * *

**Tomas**

We consolidated that last hour the way armies do — badly, and with feeling. Isandro laid out the whole scheme on her father's composing stone, using actual type to mark the positions: a capital Q for the pickets, an ampersand for the pylon, her mother's letters for the rest, because she said the route should be spelled in family characters. It was ridiculous and it was perfect. I memorized it the way I memorize galleys, which is the only reliable memory I own.

I'm the apprentice. I know my place in this story. I carry things, I ask questions, I spot names in denunciation lists that shouldn't be there. So when Isandro finished the plan and said, "You stay. If I don't come back by matins, take Mireille and the true deed to Father and let him decide," I did the bravest thing available to me.

I said yes. Out loud. Convincingly.

And then I went home and sharpened the poker again, and packed the rope, and put on two coats, because I had already decided — the way you decide these things, quietly, days earlier, without letting yourself notice — that nobody crosses that river alone. Not because I'm brave. Because Isandro taught me the inventory of risks, and I ran it, and the largest entry on the list wasn't the ice or the pickets or the committee. It was the phrase *if I don't come back*. I'd lived in that shop long enough to know what those words cost a household. I refused to let her spend them on us.

So when she slipped out the yard door at midnight, I was already at the coal bins, breath steaming, holding the rope.

She stared at me a long moment. The moon had fattened slightly, enough to show her expression: fury, relief, and resignation arriving simultaneously, like three sorts dropped in one compartment.

"You lied to me," she said.

"You taught me silence. I'm applying my lessons."

For a heartbeat I thought she'd send me back. Then the corner of her mouth moved — the smallest cut of a smile, half a point's width — and she took the other end of the rope and tied it around her own waist herself.

"Keep three arm-lengths," she said. "If the ice sings, drop flat. If I go through, you haul and you don't argue with the hauling." She glanced once back at the shop, at the dark windows where her father and Mireille were surely watching, saying nothing, saying everything. "Come on, then. Bramwen drowned nothing in forty years."

"Forty-one," I said, "counting us."

The river lay ahead of us, black and silver and holding its breath, and the far bank was just a rumor with lights in it — and somewhere on that rumor, an officer named Corvane was waiting for a promise twelve winters old. We stepped off the shore together, heel first, no talking, and the ice knocked solid beneath us like a press pulling its first clean proof.

Behind us, faint on the wind, came a sound neither of us placed until much later: the bridge gate, opening early.

The committee wasn't waiting three days after all.

They were coming tonight.
