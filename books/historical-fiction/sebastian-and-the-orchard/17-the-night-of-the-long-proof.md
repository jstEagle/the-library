## 17. The Night of the Long Proof

**Isandro**

There is a particular silence a print shop makes at midnight, and I had spent twelve years learning every dialect of it. The settling creak of the type cases as the stove's heat left them. The tick of the press cooling in its frame like an animal folding its legs. My father's breathing, which even in sleep kept the measured rhythm of a man counting em quads.

That night the shop made none of its usual silences, because no one was sleeping.

We had lit two lamps instead of one — a extravagance of oil that would have made my father wince on any ordinary evening — and spread across the composing stone everything we knew about a document none of us had ever seen.

"Again," my father said, "from the top. How does she date her letters?"

I held my mother's correspondence in my hands so often now that the paper had gone soft at the folds, gone the color of weak tea. "She writes the month out long. *The ninth of October.* Never numerals. And she crosses nothing out — if she errs, she begins the page again."

"She'd have made a fine compositor," Father murmured, and there was grief in it, and pride, and something else I chose not to name because we were about to spend the whole night forging her hand and I could not afford to weep before the work began.

Tomas sat cross-legged on the floor with the ferryman's chart unrolled beside him, though the chart had nothing to do with tonight's labor. He simply needed something true near him. I understood that. By dawn I would need it too.

The plan was Hessler's, which surprised everyone including Hessler. When Isandro had laid out the auditor's bargain — three days, then the deed or the family's safety, and Mireille delivered to prison either way — it was the foreman who had said, in his gravel-and-ash way, "*Then give them a deed. Just not the right one.*"

"They'll know," I'd said.

"They'll check," he agreed. "But they'll check tomorrow, after they've already marched to the bridge to make their exchange. A forgery doesn't need to last forever. It needs to last eight hours."

Eight hours. We were four people and a handpress against eight hours, and behind those hours stood a committee of men who could read, and worse, who could *count* — count the watermark fibers, the age of the paper, the wear on a signature's ink.

My father looked up from the stone where he'd been arranging blank sheets of old stock, paper bought years ago from the same mill that supplied the notaries on the far bank. Even that had taken doing; he had traded three fonts' worth of unused Greek sorts for it back in autumn, when the bridge still opened, when he had perhaps already begun preparing for a night like this without admitting it to himself.

"Isandro." His voice was very quiet. "You understand what we're doing."

"We're lying," I said. "With excellent kerning."

He almost smiled. Almost. "We are committing a crime that, if discovered, will be called treason by whichever side loses and cleverness by whichever side wins. Your mother—" He stopped. Started again. "Your mother believed documents should tell the truth. She married into ink because of it."

"And she hid a deed under our floor for twelve years," I said gently. "I think she understood that paper can protect as well as testify. Tonight we're asking it to do both."

He looked at me for a long moment, lamplight catching the grey in his beard, and whatever he saw in my face decided him. He rolled his sleeves to the elbow — a gesture I had only ever seen before the day's first proof pull, the gesture of a man beginning honest work — and said:

"Then let's perjure ourselves properly."

* * *

**Tomas**

Here is what nobody tells you about forgery: it is the hardest work in printing, harder than any masterpiece edition, because a lie must survive scrutiny that the truth never faces.

Master Aldering set me to cutting. Not new sorts — there was no time to cast — but to altering. The deed, as Isandro read it out from her mother's letters, named certain legal phrases that belonged to the far bank's notarial house, and those houses used ligatures and abbreviations we didn't keep in our cases. So I sat with a graver and a jeweler's loupe and shaved the tail off a long-s until it passed as an f, and cut a tiny notch into a capital T to mimic the Corvane notary's habit, learned secondhand from Mireille's fevered descriptions, of splitting his serifs.

Mireille herself lay upstairs, too weak to come down but refusing to be absent. Every quarter hour Isandro carried questions up to her and carried answers back down, and each answer arrived more precise than the last, as though the fever had burned away everything in her memory except that single afternoon thirty years ago when she'd stood in General Corvane's study and watched two men sign a promise neither intended to break.

"The wax," she had said, through the floorboards, in a voice like paper tearing slowly. "They used green wax. Unusual. The general brought his own from the estate — orchard wax, he joked. Apple-blossom oil in it. You could smell the orchard in the sealing."

So we made green wax. Isandro melted candle stubs and tinted them with the dregs of a bottle of verdigris pigment Master Aldering kept for festival posters, and when she pressed the seal — carved from a cork stopper over two hours by lamplight — the smell that rose off it was hot pigment and tallow, nothing like apples.

"It won't fool anyone who smelled the real thing," Isandro said.

"It has to fool a man reading by candlelight in a hurry," I said, "with a gun at his own back. That's a lower bar. Aim for it."

She gave me a look that would have curdled ink, but she aimed for it.

What I remember most about that night is not the fear. Fear was there, certainly, sitting in the corner like an unpaid apprentice, but it had become almost comfortable, the way cold water becomes comfortable if you stay in it. What I remember is the moment I understood that I had stopped being a boy who worked in a print shop and become a person who would go to the wall for the people in it.

It happened somewhere around the third hour, when I mis-cut a serif and Master Aldering, instead of scolding me, took the ruined sort from my hand, examined it, and said, "Good error. Now I know exactly how thin this stroke can go before it fails. Cut five more like it."

Five more deliberate failures. Weaknesses built into the forgery on purpose — a slightly crooked rule line, an ink blot where a tired notary's pen would have tarried, the green seal struck a half-degree off center. Perfection, the master explained, was the surest signature of fraud. Real documents were made by real hands on real bad days.

"So we are forging," he said, "not a document, but a *day*. Someone's worst careful hour. Do it well and no auditor alive will question it, because no one wants to believe their own paperwork was born ordinary."

I cut five imperfect sorts. They were the best work of my life.

* * *

**Father**

I have spent my life teaching apprentices that the compositor's first duty is fidelity — to the copy, to the author, to the reader who will hold your work in his hands and trust it. For thirty-one years I have signed proofs with a clear conscience.

Tonight I taught my daughter to forge.

Let no one mistake what happened in that room for anything cleaner than it was. We were not resisting tyranny with artistry. We were deceiving dangerous men with craft, and if it went wrong, every person in that shop would hang for it, and the ones who loved us would hang beside us for knowing. I did the arithmetic myself, twice, while Isandro slept an hour against the stone with her mother's letters under her cheek like a pillow of thorns. Two presses' worth of guilt. Four lives. One sick woman upstairs whose name was already on a denunciation list once.

And still I set the type.

Because here is the truth I burned one page to avoid writing down, the truth that has lived in my chest since the night I helped bury that deed: silence is also a forgery. All these years I printed other men's words faithfully and withheld my own, and I told myself the withholding was protection, was prudence, was love. It was counterfeiting. I minted a false version of our family — loyal, quiet, unconnected to the Corvanes or the orchard or the promise — and circulated it daily, and the city accepted it at face value, and I called the acceptance safety.

Tonight we forged a piece of paper. It was the more honest work.

At some small hour Isandro woke and began setting the body text, and I stood behind her watching her hands move among the cases, and I saw her mother there. Not in her face — in her *hands*, in the way she lifted each sort and turned it once before seating it, checking its face against the light, a habit no one taught her, a habit she must have absorbed standing beside me at this same stone since she was six years old. Her mother had done the same. I remembered it suddenly, completely, the way grief returns things all at once with interest accrued.

"You'll want to space it looser than feels right," I told her. "Notaries are paid by the line. They pad."

"That's cynical, Papa."

"That's the entire profession."

She laughed — a short, exhausted, real laugh, the first in days — and loosened the word-spacing, and the forged deed came out looking less like a monument and more like a Tuesday, which was exactly what we needed it to be.

* * *

**Isandro**

The press broke at dawn.

Of course it did. The frisket's hinge — a hinge older than I am, older than the shop's lease — cracked clean through on the fourteenth impression, and the platen came down crooked and smudged the sheet with a black stripe like a scar across the seal line.

For one long moment nobody spoke. Then my father knelt, examined the break, and said, in the tone of a man discussing weather, "Hessler. The spare hinge from the jobbing press. And someone wake Tomas — no, don't, let him sleep another quarter hour, he's been cutting since moonrise—"

"I'm awake," said Tomas, from the floor, eyes shut.

"—and Isandro, pull the spoiled sheets. All fourteen. We burn them."

We burned them in the stove while Hessler fought the hinge, and I fed the flames one forged deed at a time and thought: *this is what it costs.* Fourteen impressions of a lie, each one a small sin, each one ash. If the committee ever found the stove's leavings they'd find nothing but paper ghosts, and maybe that was the point of the whole trade — everything we made eventually burned, and what survived was only what we could carry in our hands and our heads.

By full daylight the press ran true again, and the fifteenth impression came off clean, and the sixteenth, and by the time the morning bells rang across the frozen river — both banks' bells, slightly out of tune with each other, as they always were — we had twenty-five copies of a document that never existed, aged with tea, creased along plausible fold lines, blotted, sealed in apple-less green wax, and folded the way a man folds a thing he has read many times and hidden often.

My father held the final copy up to the window light, turning it, squinting at his own crime the way he'd squint at a proof.

"Well?" I asked.

He was quiet a moment. Then: "It's good work. It's the best lie I've ever helped make." He set it down carefully, face-down, as though it might overhear us. "And I find I hate it less than I expected, and that frightens me more than the committee does."

"Hessler says a forgery only needs to last eight hours."

"Hessler," my father said, "is correct, provided nothing goes wrong. Something always goes wrong. Which is why—" he touched the loose floorboard with his toe, the one over the cavity where the true deed and my mother's letters lay wrapped in oilcloth, "—the original stays where it is. No one carries it tonight but you, and only if the exchange goes truly wrong. Do we understand each other?"

"Yes, Papa."

"Say it back."

"If the handoff happens cleanly, the committee gets the forgery and believes they've won. If it goes wrong—" my mouth went dry saying it, "—I cross the ice with the real deed and put it in Elias Corvane's hands before they can spring their trap."

"Before midday," he said. "After midday the ice softens on the eastern channel. Mireille confirmed it. The river gives us until noon and not a minute past, and rivers," he added, with the flat certainty of a man who had grown up hauling paper shipments off barges, "do not negotiate."

* * *

**Isandro**

We should have slept. We tried. But guilt, it turns out, keeps worse hours than printers, and around nine o'clock we all drifted back down to the shop one by one, drawn like moths to the composing stone, until all four of us stood around the finished forgery in a silence that finally broke the way silences in our family had broken for twelve years: badly, honestly, all at once.

"I keep thinking about the man who checks it," Tomas said. He was turning a blank sort over and over in his fingers. "Some clerk. He's got a family probably. He'll look at our lie and believe it, and believing it might get him—" He stopped.

"Might get him promoted," Hessler said grimly. "Or dead. Same coin, different sides."

"My mother lied too," I heard myself say. Everyone turned. "All those years. She lied by not-telling, and it nearly destroyed Papa, and it nearly destroyed me, and it saved no one — Mireille still ended up on a list, the orchard still burned in the end, the war came anyway. Silence didn't protect anyone. It just delayed the bill." I put my hand flat on the forgery. "So yes. This is a lie. But it's a lie with a destination. It's buying hours to deliver a truth. That's the difference. That has to be the difference."

My father came around the stone and put his hand over mine, over the false deed, and his hand was shaking, and he let me see it shake, which was its own kind of honesty, the first new kind our family had practiced in twelve years.

"When this is done," he said, "whatever happens — we print it. All of it. The promise, the burial, the fire, tonight. Our names at the foot of the colophon, both families', the whole record, no silence left in it anywhere."

"Even the parts that shame us?"

"*Especially* the parts that shame us. Shame kept secret is a debt. Shame printed is a payment." He looked around at all of us — the apprentice, the foreman, the daughter, himself. "Agreed? All in? Because after tonight there's no version of this story where we're bystanders again."

Tomas put his hand on the stack of forged deeds. Then Hessler, grumbling something about sentimental fools and doing it anyway. Then mine.

Four hands on a lie, agreeing to spend it on the truth.

Outside, the bells finished their mismatched ringing, and the light on the ice went hard and white, and somewhere downriver a committee courier's boots were already crunching toward our door to collect what we'd promised them.

Two hours early.

The knock came before we'd moved apart.
