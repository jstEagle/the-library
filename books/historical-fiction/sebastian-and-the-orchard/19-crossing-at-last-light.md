## 19. Crossing at Last Light

There is a particular quality to a lie you tell with your whole body. My father had learned it three days ago in front of the auditor, his hands steady on the composing stone while he said *I have never held anything belonging to the Corvanes* as though it were a line of type he'd cut himself, kerned and justified and true. I understood him now. Standing in the paper store with the true deed sewn into my coat lining, telling Tomas I was going upriver to check the ice at the mill race — that lie lived in my shoulders, in my even breathing, in the way I let my gaze slide past Mireille's cellar cot without lingering.

Tomas looked at me the way an apprentice looks at a locked case. He knew there was something behind the glass.

"You'll want company," he said.

"I'll want quiet."

"Those are different things, Isandro. You've been confusing them for twelve years."

I almost laughed. That would have ruined everything. "If I'm not back by the second bell after dark—"

"Then I'll know exactly where you went, because I've known for two days." He said it flatly, without triumph, which was somehow worse. "You sleep with your boots laced now. You stopped eating the salt fish because it's heavy. And you asked me last week what the ferryman's chart said about the channel under the middle arch, when you already knew, because I'd told you twice."

The chart lay between us in its hiding place inside the broken type case, folded small enough to fit in a fist. The river drawn in a dead ferryman's careful hand: *sound ice here, death here, death here also.*

"Tomas—"

"Don't say thank you," he said. "Say where."

I looked at him — sixteen years old, ink to the elbow, a boy who had carried Mireille's name out of a galley of the condemned like a coal cupped in both hands — and I thought about what my mother would have done with him. What she did with everyone. She collected people the way Father collected matrices, one at a time, each chosen for a purpose no one else could see yet.

"The willows past the tannery sluice," I said. "Where the current bends slow. But not until full dark, and if I'm caught—"

"If you're caught, you were checking the ice at the mill race." He turned back to his stone and began setting type that would never be used, his fingers moving with terrible calm. "Go. Before I talk myself into coming anyway."

* * *

The city at dusk in deep winter is a city pretending to be asleep. Smoke stood straight up from every chimney because there was no wind, which meant the committee's patrols could hear footsteps three streets off. I took the back way past the tannery, where the reek covered everything, and came down to the river through the willow stand, gray whips against a sky the color of cooling iron.

The last light was doing something obscene to the far bank. It caught the windows of the orchard house — Elias Corvane's house now, or his father's once, depending on whose account of the fall from grace you believed — and set them burning gold, as if the fire that was coming tonight had sent ahead a rehearsal.

I made myself look away from it. Look at the ice instead. The ice you can read, if you were raised by a man who taught you that every surface tells you what it has survived.

The ferryman's chart said the bend below the sluice froze soundest, because the current slowed there and laid its ice in layers, patient as a compositor building a page. I walked out ten feet, twenty, listening. Good river ice talks. It creaks and sighs and mutters to itself, a long low conversation, and you can walk on a talking river all night. It's the silent ice that kills you — the black glass over fast water, holding its breath.

This ice talked. I kept walking.

Halfway across, the wind found me. There is no shelter on a frozen river; you are the most exposed thing for a mile in any direction, and I felt like a character standing on an open page waiting for someone to strike her through. Behind me Vellamare's west bank hunched under its smoke. Ahead, the east bank — Corvane country, enemy country, *orchard* country — showed lamplit windows and, at the bridge gate, the small moving lights of the garrison that still held it.

They would be watching the bridge. Everyone watches bridges; it's the only drama the winter offers. Nobody watches the empty bend below the sluice, because nobody crosses a frozen river alone at last light carrying a document that three governments' worth of men would kill for.

Nobody, I corrected myself, except a type cutter's daughter who had spent twelve years learning that the safest thing in any room is the thing no one thinks to look at.

My mother had hidden letters under the floor for eleven years. She had hidden a promise, an heir, a debt of honor — she had hidden an entire orchard beneath our ordinary floorboards and died without telling me it was there. And I had spent a week furious at her for it before understanding, somewhere around the third night of deciphering her cramped, beautiful hand, that she hadn't been keeping the secret *from* me. She'd been keeping it *for* me, the way you set type and store it, waiting for the print run no one knows is coming.

The deed pressed against my ribs with every step. Joint heirs, both families, named in full: *Nakamura and Corvane, their children and their children's children, in equal and indivisible share.* Legalese, dry as autumn paper. And legally bulletproof, which meant the revolutionaries couldn't seize the orchard without making themselves liars before the whole city, and General Corvane's faction couldn't claim it alone without breaking the one promise that might have saved them. A piece of paper that forced enemies into being family. My mother had written it that way on purpose. I was sure of it now. She had always believed typesetting was a moral act.

Two-thirds across, the ice spoke differently — a higher note under my boot, thin as a cracked matrix — and I stopped breathing, which every sensible part of me objected to. Don't stop, don't gather, don't hesitate; hesitation puts all your weight on one opinion of the ice. I kept walking, faster, counting strides because counting was something my hands knew how to do when the rest of me wanted to scream.

Forty strides later my boot hit shingle and the river was behind me, and I discovered I had been so rigid for so long that my knees simply declined to function for a moment. I knelt on the east bank gravel like a woman praying, and perhaps I was. To whom, I couldn't have said. My mother, possibly. She seemed the likeliest candidate for patron saint of impossible crossings.

* * *

The orchard road ran uphill through trees that in another season would have been the whole argument for the deed — apple and pear, plum and quince, rows planted by Nakamura and Corvane hands together, according to the letters, back when such a sentence wasn't madness. Now they stood bare and black, and between their trunks I could see the pickets of the garrison's outer posts, and beyond them, on the rise, the house.

Getting past the pickets was easier than it should have been, and that frightened me more than the ice had. An unguarded road means either safety or a trap, and I had learned this week that the committee preferred traps. But the sentry I finally flagged down — an older man with a frost-reddened face and the eyes of someone who had stopped expecting the war to make sense — looked at me, looked at the river behind me, and said only:

"You're the printer's girl."

Not a question. Which meant Elias Corvane had described me to his sentries, which meant he had been expecting someone, which meant—

"He said you'd come by the gate or you wouldn't come at all," the sentry said. "He's been wrong about a lot of things lately. Glad to be wrong again. Walk straight up, miss. Don't mind the dogs; they're all bark and supper-obsessed."

The house smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and, faintly, apples — stores put by in the cellar, the ghosts of the orchard keeping their own counsel. In the long room a fire burned low and a map of the river lay on the table weighted with bayonets at the corners, and Elias Corvane stood over it in a uniform that fit him worse than it had at the parley, as though the weeks since the bridge closed had shrunk him inside it.

He looked up, and whatever he saw in my face made him dismiss the two soldiers in the room with a jerk of his chin before he said a word.

"You crossed the ice." His voice was careful. Men who command learn careful voices; men who lose commands learn them better. "In daylight. Alone."

"In daylight, yes. It's nearly dark. And I'm not alone." I unpicked the stitches in my coat lining — my fingers, thank every god of the trade, steady now that the work had begun; give a Nakamura a seam and she becomes calm — and drew out the deed, oilcloth-wrapped, warm from my body. "I'm accompanied by my mother, my father, and about forty years of other people's lies. Sit down, Lieutenant. This requires a compositor's patience."

He sat. I unwrapped the paper and spread it on the map table, weighting the corners with his own bayonets, and watched him read his family's name beside mine in my mother's hand.

"They told me it was destroyed," he said at last. "The original. After the general fell. They told me the committee burned it in the square."

"They burned a page," I said. "My father saw which one. He's been carrying that page like a relic ever since — guilt makes excellent kindling, apparently, but it doesn't burn the truth." I tapped the joint-heir clause. "Do you understand what this wording does? Not what it promises. What it *does*."

He read it again, lips moving slightly, an officer parsing orders. Then he looked up and I saw the moment the legal machinery engaged behind his eyes — the same look my father got when a client realized too late what the contract actually said.

"It can't be dispossessed," he said slowly. "Either side. By anyone. The war doesn't touch it. The committee can't confiscate it without—" He stopped. "Without proving to the whole city that their own rules don't apply to them."

"And your father's faction can't hold it alone without breaking a signed promise, witnessed, that half the old guard still remembers. Mireille remembers. She was in the room." I heard my own voice go strange and fierce. "Elias. Your father promised my mother an orchard for both families when we were children, and then everyone involved spent twenty years pretending otherwise, and now everyone wants to pretend the pretense was the truth. I am *tired*. I have been tired since the bridge closed. So here is what happens next, and you're going to hate some of it."

"Almost certainly," he said, and there — briefly — was the ghost of the man who had tested me at the gate with one word, *the orchard*, and watched my face like a proof sheet.

"The exchange is set for tomorrow noon. The committee offers you this deed—" I didn't say the forgery; we had all agreed the word *forgery* was not to be spoken aloud anywhere, as if the type itself might inform on us. "—in return for Mireille, and for our shop's silence, and for a public statement that will hang my father's name next to a massacre he printed because refusing would have killed him. Except the exchange is a trap. Hessler talked. I don't know how much, but enough. They mean to take you and the deed both, stage it as your faction's treachery, and use it to justify taking the east bank warehouses before the thaw. Mireille goes to a cell either way. My family goes to a wall eventually, because witnesses are only useful once."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged and resettled.

"So," Elias said quietly. "Instead you brought me the real one, hours early, across a frozen river, and now you propose... what? That we refuse the trap and produce the truth?"

"I propose we do what my family does best." I smoothed the deed flat between us. "We set the record. Tonight, before witnesses from both banks, before the committee arrives to spring their trap and finds the ground pulled out from under it. We read this aloud — jointly, both names, both banks — and we let it be witnessed and sworn and, if necessary, printed by morning. You cannot trap two families who have just publicly become one. It's bad theater. Even the committee knows bad theater when it sees it."

"And your father? Does he consent to having his name bound to mine in public? To everything that follows?"

"My father spent twenty years cutting away the parts of himself that made noise." I thought of him burning the single true page, keeping the ash. "He's finished cutting. We all are."

Elias stood, and for a moment he was only a tired man in a badly fitting uniform looking at a document his father should have honored a generation ago. Then he reached across the table and put his hand flat on the deed — deliberately, formally, the way a man swears on a book — and said, "Then we do it now. While the light lasts."

Which was when the dog began to bark.

Not the idle, supper-minded barking the sentry had promised. This was the flat, continuous, businesslike voice of an animal announcing strangers on the orchard road, and under it, faint but unmistakable across the frozen fields, came the crunch of many boots moving fast on frozen ground — coming not from the bridge gate, but from the north road, the one the committee had no reason to know led anywhere at all.

Except Hessler had known. Hessler had known everything.

Elias was already moving for the door, snuffing lamps as he passed, his careful officer's voice gone hard and low: "They came early. God help us — they came *early*."

And I stood over the true deed on the map table, my mother's promise glowing pale in the last of the light, and understood that we were out of time — that the reading, the witnesses, the record, all of it would have to happen now, tonight, in a house that was about to burn.
