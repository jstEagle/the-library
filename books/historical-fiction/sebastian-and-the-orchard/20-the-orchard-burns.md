## 20. The Orchard Burns

**Isandro**

The ice held.

I want to write that first, because everything after it is ash and shouting and the smell of a house I never saw until it was dying — but the ice held, all nine hundred paces of it, groaning like a press pulling its first impression of the night. I crossed at last light with the true deed sewn into my coat lining, over my heart, where Mother had hidden it under the floor for twelve years and where it had beaten against my ribs like something alive that had waited long enough to be let out.

Elias Corvane met me at the far bank with a lantern and a face I will not forget: not surprised, exactly, but braced, the way a man braces when he has been expecting a letter for years and has stopped believing in letters.

"You're the daughter," he said.

"I'm the typeface cutter's daughter," I said. "It matters which."

He looked at me for a long moment, snow gathering on his officer's shoulders, and then he did something no soldier on that bank had done in weeks. He smiled.

"Then you'll know how to read it properly," he said. "Come. The house is cold, but there are witnesses in it."

* * *

**Elias**

I had rehearsed this meeting so many times that when it finally came, standing in the orchard gate while the light died behind her, every rehearsal abandoned me at once. She was smaller than I'd imagined. Sharper, too. Ink-stained fingers, a cutter's calluses, eyes that took the measure of my uniform and found it wanting without saying so.

My father had spoken of the Nakamuras the way other men speak of saints or weather — something reliable, something gone. He had told me of an orchard deed signed in peacetime by men who believed rivers were bridges waiting to happen, and he had made me swear that if ever the paper surfaced, I would honor it before I honored his name. He fell from grace before he could explain why the second promise needed the first.

And here she was, crossing a frozen river at last light because a piece of paper said we belonged to each other.

The house was full of what remained of my father's household staff — four souls, plus Corporal Vey, who pretended not to be moved by anything and failed. Isandro unsewed the deed from her coat at my father's old writing desk, and I watched her hands work the needle out of the cloth with the same economy she must use setting type, no wasted motion, and I thought: *my father would have liked her immediately and been insufferable about it.*

She spread the paper flat. The candle guttered.

"Read it aloud," she said. "Both our names are in it. It should be both our voices."

So I read. *To the heirs of Nakamura and Corvane jointly, and neither singly, the orchard and its ground, in perpetuity, that neither war nor debt nor grief may dispossess either house while one heir remains to speak for both.*

Outside, somewhere down the dark river, the ice spoke in a long cracking syllable, and none of us knew yet that it was not the ice at all.

* * *

**Isandro**

They came at moonrise, and I will always believe they came early — that Hessler's betrayal bought them a faster crossing than any chart promised, or that the committee had boats stashed upriver all along and simply lacked a reason to use them. A reason arrived tonight, riding in my coat lining.

There were perhaps thirty of them, torches first, then muskets, then the smell. The orchard's bare trees stood black against the firelight like type locked in a chase, rows and rows of characters waiting for a sentence someone had decided should end in flame.

Elias put himself between the door and the yard. "There are civilians in this house," he said, in the parade-ground voice, and I confess it worked on me even knowing what was coming.

"There are traitors in this house," said the man at the front — Auditor Renholt, whom I knew by reputation and now by the particular pleasure he took in pronouncing the word *traitors*, as though tasting it. " harboring enemy property. Enemy paper." His eyes found mine through the doorway, and I understood that Mireille had told them more than the crossing. She had told them about the deed. Of course she had. Under interrogation, everyone tells everything; the only question is whether they tell it true.

"The paper belongs to both banks," Elias said. "By law older than your committee."

"Laws burn beautifully," Renholt said. "Watch."

* * *

**Elias**

I have stood in engagements. I have heard the whistle of things meant to kill me and felt the strange insult of surviving them. None of it prepared me for watching the house of my childhood catch fire because a committee of idealists decided that a signature was a weapon.

They fired the east wing first, where the granary was. Then the wind turned and took the rest with an appetite I can only describe as bureaucratic — thorough, indifferent, complete. We got the staff out through the kitchen passage, Vey hauling old Marta bodily, and we stood in the orchard rows and watched twelve generations of Corvane timber go up like a page fed to a stove.

Isandro stood beside me. She did not cry. She took off her coat — the coat, with nothing left sewn in it — folded it over her arm, and said, very quietly, so that only I could hear:

"They think they're burning the deed."

And I understood, in the way one understands a line of type suddenly locking into place, what she had done. What we had done, on the walk out from the house, when she had pressed the true deed back into my hands and kept only the folded copy — the forgery we'd bled three nights over in the shop — sewn against her own heart, walking toward the men who wanted it.

"You gave them the forged one," I said.

"I gave them a forged one," she said. "Yours is real. That distinction is going to matter in about four minutes, so pay attention."

* * *

**Isandro**

Here is what I learned in the Aldering print shop that I could never have learned anywhere else: paper is only powerful when someone reads it aloud. A deed in a drawer is a rumor. A deed in a voice is a fact.

Renholt came down the orchard rows with the burning house lighting his face orange, holding the seized document aloft like a relic, and behind him his committee men, and behind them, drawn by firelight the way moths are drawn and villagers too — dozens of them, from the far bank and near, faces I knew from the bridge square, faces that had watched the gates chained all winter.

"Auditor," I said, stepping forward before Elias could stop me, because someone had to and because I had spent twelve years learning silences and I was, at last, finished with them. "You're holding a forgery."

The word landed the way a dropped galley lands. Thirty muskets shifted uncertainly.

"It was taken from the girl," Renholt said.

"It was printed three nights ago in the Aldering shop, by our own hands, as bait," I said. "Because we knew what you wanted, and we knew you'd stop looking once you thought you had it. The true deed has been on this side of the river since sundown." I turned to Elias. "Read it. Read it loud enough for the far bank to hear."

And he did. My God, he did. Elias Corvane stood in his father's burning orchard and read the joint-heir clause at full parade volume, twice, while the roof beams went and the sparks climbed, and I watched the words cross the ice — *neither war nor debt nor grief may dispossess either house while one heir remains to speak for both* — and land among the crowd on the far shore, where people began, hesitantly, to repeat them. Voice to voice. The way news traveled before anyone trusted print.

"That paper means nothing," Renholt said. "Your committee voids—"

"It means everything," said a voice from the far bank, carried thin across the frozen river, and I knew it — old Ferren, the ferryman whose chart had taught Tomas and me the ice. "It means the Corvanes hold this ground by right, and half of us witnessed it, and if your men fire on witnesses from both banks, Auditor, you'll have a city on your necks by morning, not just a family."

Muskets wavered. Fire makes brave crowds and cowardly committees in equal measure, and Renholt's men could count. Thirty rifles against a burning orchard, two named heirs, and a hundred witnesses who had just discovered they had a stake in the answer.

Renholt looked at the forged deed in his hand. Looked at me. And I watched him arrive at the arithmetic — that arresting us now proved the forgery, that the forgery proved his own gullibility, that gullibility before a crowd was a career ending — and I saw him choose, with visible distaste, the smaller loss.

"This matter," he announced, "will be adjudicated by the committee in session," and he turned and marched his men back toward the river, and the stand-down rippled out behind him like a wave flattening.

* * *

**Elias**

We stood in the orchard until the fire burned itself down to a red breathing glow, because there was nowhere else to go and because leaving felt like a second dispossession. The house was lost. The trees, miraculously — bare, wet with snow, stubborn — mostly were not. Scorched on the windward side. Alive.

Isandro sat down in the snow between two apple rows, quite suddenly, the way a person does when the adrenaline leaves and the body presents its bill. I sat beside her. For a while neither of us said anything, which is a kind of conversation I am told the quiet people practice professionally.

"We won," she said at last, in the tone of someone reading a suspect proof.

"The house burned."

"The house burned," she agreed, "and we won. Both of those are going in the record, or I'm no printer's daughter." She pulled the true deed from inside my coat, where I'd carried it against the cold, and looked at it — really looked, the way she must look at fresh-cut type. "Twelve years my mother hid this under a floor to keep two families safe. One night of fire and it's safer than it's ever been. Do you understand what that means?"

"Tell me."

"It means silence was never protecting anyone. It was just delaying the reading." She looked across the black river toward Vellamare, toward the shop, toward her father who had cut away the truth to keep her small enough to hide. "Tomorrow we set it. All of it. The promise, the betrayal, the fire, the names. Both families' names at the foot, together."

"On whose press?"

"Mine," she said, and there was no wryness in it at all, only iron. "Ours. If your corporal can carry a case of type across the ice."

Vey, twenty feet away, pretending to inspect a scorched tree, straightened like a man called to colors.

Behind us the embers settled. Ahead, across the river, the city slept unaware that by tomorrow evening every wall in it would carry the one sentence the committee feared above all others: *the whole truth, printed, signed, and read aloud.*
