## 21. Setting the Record

The morning after the fire, my father opened the print shop at the hour he always did — half past six, before the frost on the windows had decided whether it intended to become ice or merely sulk — and I understood, watching him turn the key in a door that no longer needed to hide anything, that we had both been waiting our entire lives to be this tired.

Not the tiredness of a bad night. We'd had bad nights by the dozen; twelve years of them, in fact, stacked like spoiled sheets in the back of my mind. This was another animal entirely. This was the tiredness of a man who had carried a barrel of water across a desert and finally set it down, only to discover his arms had forgotten any other occupation.

"You're staring," he said without turning around.

"You're opening the shop," I said. "We're not conscripted anymore. The committee is ash along with the orchard house, and you're opening the shop at half past six as though the revolution kept office hours."

"The revolution didn't keep office hours," he said, lighting the lamps one by one with the unhurried economy of forty years' habit. "That was always its most honest quality." He hung the striker back on its hook and looked at me then, properly, for the first time since we'd stood on the scorched ground while Elias Corvane read the deed aloud to a crowd of soldiers from both banks who had come expecting an execution and received, instead, paperwork. "Isandro. Do you know what I thought about, all those years? When I was cutting type and keeping quiet?"

"Whether Mother would have approved."

He laughed — a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody. "No. Well. Yes. But underneath that. I thought about the letter *k*." He crossed to the case and drew out a sort, holding it up to the lamplight: a small brass letter, worn at the shoulders from decades of use. "The *k*. It's the worst letter in the alphabet for a cutter. All those angles, and every one of them wants to be slightly wrong. And I would sit there cutting *k* after *k* for some merchant's invoice, and I would think: if I can make this letter true, then something in the world is still true. Even if everything I said out loud was a lie."

"And now?"

"Now—" He set the sort down on the stone with a small definitive click, like a gavel. "Now we are going to tell the truth so loudly that no one in Vellamare will be able to pretend they didn't hear it."

* * *

It took us four days to compose what should have taken two.

Not because the setting was difficult — though it was, God knows it was; I have never in my life set a more unforgiving text — but because every sentence had to survive three readers before it earned its place in the galley. My father read for accuracy. Tomas read for clarity, having appointed himself the voice of the common reader on the grounds that he was, in fact, a common reader. And Mireille, propped up in the chair by the stove with a blanket over her knees and her fever finally broken but her hands still unsteady, read for memory. She had been there, thirty-one years ago, in General Corvane's study when the promise was signed. If a detail was wrong, she knew.

"Start again," she said on the second morning, when my father had drafted the account of the signing. "You've made the general too kind."

"He *was* kind," my father said. "That's rather the point, isn't it? That kindness wasn't enough."

"It's not the point," Mireille said, with the mild immovability of a woman who has survived fever, prison, and betrayal and therefore considers herself entitled to editorial authority. "If you write him kind, people will say the whole story is sentimental invention. Write him as he was: proud, distracted, generous in large gestures and careless in small ones. He signed the promise and then forgot to tell his son about it for twenty years. That carelessness is what let Aldous Verenne betray it. Kindness doesn't betray. Carelessness does."

My father looked at her for a long moment. Then he pulled the sheet off the stone, crumpled it, and began again.

I will admit — privately, here, in the privacy of this page which I am writing for no one but whoever finds it after me — that I enjoyed it. Not the fear; there was plenty of that still, flaring up at odd hours, whenever boots sounded on the cobbles outside or a stranger paused too long at the window. But the work itself. Setting truth in metal. For twelve years my craft had been a hiding place: type cut fine enough that no one noticed the girl doing it, silences maintained so smoothly that even I sometimes forgot they were silences rather than simply the shape of my life. Now every character I set was a small act of publication. The word *promise*, in fourteen-point Caslon. The word *betrayal*, set twice, once for Verenne's signature on the forged order and once for the night Hessler sold our crossing plan for a guarantee of his own safety — because Mireille insisted we name that too, gently, without hatred, since Hessler had confessed it himself, weeping, in front of the whole household, and exhaustion is a betrayer we could all become.

"Read me the fire again," she said on the third evening, and I set down my burnisher and recited it:

*"On the night of the ninth of Deepwinter, the Committee of Public Order, having failed by fraud and force to obtain the joint-heir deed to the Nakamura-Corvane orchard, raided the property with armed men. The dwelling was fired in the course of the raid. No life was lost, through the courage of Lieutenant Elias Corvane, who sheltered neighbors of both banks in the press-house cellar. The deed was read aloud before witnesses of both banks at dawn. Its terms hold: neither family may be dispossessed by war, decree, or committee."*

Mireille closed her eyes. "The general's boy read it well?"

"He read it like a man reading his own eulogy and finding he approved of the deceased," I said. "Yes. He read it well."

"Good." She opened her eyes and fixed them on me, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before — not the fever, not the old grief. Something almost like envy, of all things. "Your mother wrote that promise in a hand so careful it looked printed. Did I ever tell you that? She wanted it to be legible forever. I used to think it was vanity. It wasn't. It was faith — in exactly this. That someday someone would set it in actual type and make it undeniable."

* * *

My father's account of these days, as he later told it — and he told it often, in his old age, to anyone trapped within earshot, usually twice:

I had imagined, all my life, that confession would feel like dying. Instead it felt like typesetting. You select the words. You examine each one for flaws — the nicks in the shoulder, the hairline cracks that will fail under pressure. You reject the flattering word and the evasive word the way you reject a damaged sort. And then you lock the line, and there it is: fixed, irreversible, capable of a thousand copies. A spoken lie evaporates. A printed truth cannot be unsaid.

The hardest passage was not the betrayal. I had expected the betrayal to be hardest — Verenne's name, the committee's theft, the years of my own silence — but no. The hardest passage was four lines long and concerned my wife.

*Sana Nakamura came to this shop as a bride and left it, eleven years later, in a coffin paid for by the family she had been forced to renounce. In that time she wrote forty-one letters to her brother across the river, every one of them hidden beneath the floor of her own home, because her husband asked her to hide them and she loved him enough to agree. Her silence was not cowardice. It was a gift given to a frightened man. I am that man. I am printing this so that the gift is returned.*

Isandro set those lines herself. I watched her do it — watched her pick each character with the terrible competence she inherited from me and the courage she inherited entirely from her mother — and when she had locked the forme she pressed both palms flat against the type, the way a physician presses a wound, testing whether it will hold.

"It holds," she said.

And we ran the press.

* * *

We printed five hundred copies on the fourth day, which was reckless, since paper was rationed and the good stock was gone, but Mireille solved that with the serene ruthlessness of a woman who had spent a week listening to revolutionary rhetoric through the slats of a cellar floor. "Verenne requisitioned three reams of fine paper for his next denunciation list," she said. "It's still in his office. Denounce him with his own paper. There's a justice in it that even a lawyer could love."

Tomas stole the reams himself — volunteered, actually, with an eagerness that alarmed me until I realized what it was. The boy who had once brought me Mireille's name in a galley instead of carrying it to the foreman, because his conscience had outranked his survival for the first time in his life, had discovered that conscience is addictive. He came back flushed and triumphant, paper under his coat, and announced that Verenne's own inkwell had been left behind and he had liberated that too, as a souvenir, and could he please be the one to ink the forme.

"No," my father and I said together.

He got to pull the first proof anyway. Fair is fair.

The broadsheet ran under a single headline, in letters tall enough to read across a street:

**THE TRUTH OF THE ORCHARD: SET AND PRINTED BY BOTH FAMILIES**

Beneath it, two lines of attribution, side by side, in equal size — and this detail mattered more than any other in the whole composition, Mireille insisted on it:

*Published by Isandro Nakamura and Kenji Nakamura, of the Aldering Press, and by Elias Corvane, of the far bank, heirs jointly named.*

Both names. Both banks. Neither family above the other in the order of the type. I had argued for alphabetical — Nakamura before Corvane, a small victory I confess I wanted badly — and Mireille had looked at me with such gentle disappointment that I withdrew it before I finished proposing it. "The whole point, child," she said, "is that there is no *before*."

* * *

Distribution was Tomas's department, and he organized it with the tactical brilliance of a boy who had spent months memorizing which streets the patrols walked and at what hours, knowledge acquired under duress and now converted, at last, into its proper use. Copies went into bakeries, into the fish market, into the pews of three temples whose clerics owed Mireille old favors. Copies were slipped under the doors of every alderman. One copy was nailed — nailed, with a proper nail, by Tomas, standing on a crate in full view of anyone who cared to stop him, and no one did — to the door of the Committee of Public Order itself.

By noon, people stood in the bridge square reading it aloud to those who couldn't read. By afternoon, according to the fishwife who told Mireille, a woman had begun reading it to a crowd near the fountain and had gotten as far as Verenne's forged order before the crowd started walking toward the committee offices, and the woman had simply stopped reading and joined them, because the document was now redundant; the crowd had absorbed it and become the argument themselves.

By evening, Verenne's own clerks had stopped coming to work. Not struck. Not arrested. They simply did not come — because a clerk's entire trade is credibility, and no clerk can take dictation from a man whose forgery is being read aloud in bakeries. The committee did not collapse in flames or in gunfire. It collapsed the way a bad forme collapses: quietly, all at once, when someone finally lifted the quoins. Verenne was found the next morning attempting to leave the city in a fish cart, wearing a hat that fooled no one, and the militia — the same militia he had commanded — escorted him to the cells with what witnesses described as perfect courtesy and visible enjoyment.

My father heard the news at the case, mid-sort, and did not celebrate. He finished the *k* he was cutting — angles checked twice against the gauge — set it in its row, and only then sat down heavily on his stool and put his face in his hands.

"Papa?" I said.

"I'm all right." His voice was muffled and unsteady. "I'm just — twelve years, Isandro. Twelve years I thought silence was the wall that kept us safe. And it turns out the wall was the thing we needed rescuing from." He lowered his hands. His eyes were red and he made no attempt whatsoever to disguise it. "Your mother hid her letters under our floor and I let her, and called it protection. She must have lain awake every night listening to me sleep, knowing what was under the boards."

"She knew," I said. "Mireille told me. Mother said hiding them was easy. What was hard was waiting for you to be brave enough to bring them out." I hesitated, then gave him the rest, because he had earned the truth in full and I was done rationing it in this family. "She also said — and Mireille swore this was verbatim — 'Kenji will know when to speak. He always cuts the letter perfectly. He just needs a text worth printing.'"

My father laughed then, really laughed, wetly and helplessly, and pulled me into a hug that smelled of lamp oil and ink and thirty years of this shop, and over his shoulder I saw Tomas in the doorway pretending very badly not to watch, and Mireille behind him pretending nothing at all, smiling like a woman who has waited a long time for a scene she predicted.

* * *

We printed a second edition that night. Not because the first was insufficient — the first had done its work in a single day — but because my father insisted, and when my father insists on a printing matter, rivers freeze and committees fall.

"The second edition corrects an error," he explained, deadpan, holding up the corrected page.

I read it. The attribution line was unchanged. The text was unchanged, except for one addition, appended at the bottom, in small italics, in a hand of prose I recognized as his because it was stiff and formal and trying very hard not to tremble:

*The publishers acknowledge with gratitude the forty-one letters of Sana Nakamura, without which this record could not exist. Her handwriting was better than ours. So was her courage. This press will bear her name on its colophon henceforth, alongside our own.*

Below it, the new colophon itself, set in a frame of ornaments:

**NAKAMURA-ALDERING PRESS — TRUTH IN TYPE, BOTH BANKS, EST. THIS WINTER OF THE FIRE**

"You're giving the press her name," I said. "Our name. The Aldering name."

"I'm correcting the historical record," my father said. "It seems to be going around."

* * *

There was one copy left on the stone at the end of that last night — the final sheet off the press, still warm, ink smelling sharp and alive — and we stood around it in a strange little ceremony nobody had planned: my father, Tomas, Mireille wrapped in her blanket, and me. Outside, for the first time in a season, the city sounded like a city rather than a held breath. Somewhere a fiddle had started up, badly, in the bridge square, and someone was laughing at it, and the laughter had no fear in it.

"What happens now?" Tomas asked. "To the shop, I mean. To — everything."

"Now," my father said, "we print invoices. Merciful, boring, honest invoices, for merchants who pay on time. And school primers. And whatever else this city needs said plainly." He glanced at me sideways. "And in spring, when the bridge opens properly and the roads clear — your cousin in Harrowgate writes that their printer died last autumn and his widow can't run the shop alone. I thought perhaps—"

"You want to send me away?" My stomach dropped before I caught the look on his face and understood.

"I want to ask if you'd like to go *see*," he said. "For a month, maybe two. To learn how another shop runs. And to come home after. Home being—" he gestured at the cases, the press, Mireille's chair by the stove, the whole improbable architecture of us "—home."

I looked at the last sheet drying on the stone. Forty-one letters, a burned orchard, a forged deed, a betrayal, a crossing on black ice at last light, and a promise kept mid-stride — all of it locked up tight in five hundred copies scattered through a city that had finally, at long last, chosen to listen.

"Yes," I said. "But not yet. First—" and I picked up the last sheet and turned it over, and reached for a pencil, because there was one more line the record needed, and it belonged to the person who had carried it across a frozen river, and everyone in the room already knew I was going to write it, "—first we finish the story."

Under the colophon, in a cutter's daughter's careful hand, I added the final line of the true history of the Nakamura-Corvane orchard:

*The orchard burned. The promise did not.*
