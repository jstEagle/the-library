## 22. Spring Thaw

The bridge opened on a Tuesday, which struck me as fitting. Tuesdays were delivery days in the old life — the day the ink carts came rattling up Aldering Lane, the day my father would stand in the doorway counting crates like a man counting his reasons to keep going. If the city had to remember itself, it might as well start on a Tuesday.

I went down to watch the chains come off.

Not because I expected ceremony. Vellamare had spent a winter learning that ceremonies are what committees do instead of things, and the committee had collapsed in March under the weight of its own printed lies — lies we had printed, I should say, and then unprinted, in the only way ink can be unprinted: by printing over it. But still, half the east bank turned out to watch four soldiers with cold-reddened hands unwind the links from the gate posts, and when the last chain hit the cobbles with a sound like a dropped bell, somebody cheered, and then everybody did, and an old woman near me wept into her shawl as though the bridge were a son coming home from the wars.

In a way it was. It was everyone's son. It had been taken from all of us at once, and now it was given back, and nobody quite trusted it.

I stayed until the first cart crossed. A flour cart, of all things — the driver whipping up his mule like a man late for his own wedding, as if he feared the river would change its mind about being crossable. The planks rang under the wheels. The whole span hummed like a type case being sorted.

I put my hand on the cold stone of the gatepost and thought: *Mother, you would have laughed at us.* All winter I'd imagined crossing that ice alone, and here was the river opening like a book, and here was I, still on my own bank, with nothing left to smuggle but saplings.

* * *

The orchard smelled of char and thaw.

You cannot imagine the two together until you have stood in it. Burnt ground has a smell like a struck match that never finishes; spring ground smells green and patient and slightly rotten, in the good way, the way of things deciding to live. The Nakamura orchard in April was both at once — black skeletons of the Corvane house still standing at the top of the slope like punctuation after a sentence no one wanted to finish reading, and below them, row after row of apple trees budding as if nothing whatsoever had happened, because trees are the only honest witnesses and they refuse to dramatize.

Elias met me at the gate. There wasn't a gate anymore — the fire had taken it along with the roof and the east wall and eleven years of his father's correspondence, which Elias claimed not to mourn — but there was a gap in a hedge where a gate used to be, and he stood in it, and that's how gates work if you're honest about it.

"You're late," he said.

"The ferryman wanted to argue about the fare."

"Did you pay him?"

"I paid him double to stop talking." I handed down the basket. "Forty trees. Father counted them three times, which for Father is a religious act."

We had spent the winter arguing about trees, in the way that people who have survived something together argue about small things because the large ones are settled or unbearable. Elias had wanted to replant immediately, in February, in frozen ground, out of sheer stubbornness — a Corvane trait, apparently, along with good cheekbones and catastrophic loyalty to lost causes. I had insisted on waiting for the thaw, because my mother's letters said the nursery stock should go in "when the river forgets its temper," and I had decided sometime around midwinter that my mother's letters were the closest thing to scripture I intended to follow.

The thaw agreed with me, which Elias pretended not to mind.

By eight o'clock the slope was full of people, and this is the part I would print if I could print everything, the part I will print, actually, because we did print it, in June, two hundred copies on the Aldering press with the good roller: hands from both banks, planting trees in each other's holes.

That's the detail. That's the detail that made Father cry, though he blamed the pollen, in April, from burnt ground, which fooled no one. We didn't divide into Isandro's side and Elias's side. We mixed. A fishwife from the east bank dug the hole; a Corvane cousin — there were more surviving cousins than Elias had led anyone to believe; the family bred like lawyers — set the root; someone's child tamped the earth with a enthusiasm that bordered on assault. Forty trees, forty mixtures, and by noon the orchard looked less like a restoration than like a quilt, stitched by people who mostly couldn't stitch.

Mireille sat on a bench we'd dragged up from the ruined kitchen garden and supervised. Supervising was her form of participation now. The fever had taken something from her — some portion of her former speed, her certainty that she could outrun anything by never stopping. What remained was slower and, oddly, funnier. She watched Tomas wrestle a sapling into a hole he'd clearly dug at an angle known only to himself and announced, to the entire hillside:

"That boy plants trees the way he sets type. With confidence and no regard for the reader."

"I regard the reader fine," Tomas called back, not looking up.

"The reader is the tree, love."

He replanted it. Straighter. She let him think it was his own idea.

* * *

Tomas came to us officially in May, though unofficially he had been ours since the night he brought Mireille's name to me instead of to Hessler — since the first crack in the silence, which, it turns out, is how every good thing enters a sealed room. Hessler had gone west in February with the retreating committee clerks, carrying his exhaustion with him like luggage. I don't hate him. I've tried. Hatred requires believing a person chose their worst hour freely, and I watched Hessler through enough winters to know his worst hour chose him. Father says forgiveness is like pulling a proof: you can't do it well while your hands are shaking. I'm letting my hands steady.

So the shop needed an apprentice, and Tomas needed — well, Tomas needed what I had needed at twelve: somewhere the silences had walls thick enough to hold.

Father taught him the quoin key first. This surprised me. I'd have started him on distribution, the humble work of putting letters back in their cases, which is where I started and which teaches you that every word ever printed is borrowed, not owned. Father started him on locking up the forme — making it tight, making it hold — and when I asked why, Father said:

"Because he already knows how to keep a secret. Now he learns why we keep one."

Which was very nearly wise, and I told him so, and he went pink behind the ears and cut type for an hour without speaking, which is how my father says everything.

The conscription order had never formally been rescinded. Committees collapse messily; their paperwork outlives them like hair on a corpse. But in April a tired clerk from the provisional council came by, stamped something, and said the shop could resume "private contracts," and Father waited until the man was down the stairs before he laughed — actually laughed, a sound I could count on one hand per decade — and pulled out the paper we'd hidden in the type case since winter.

*The True Account of the Orchard Promise,* set in my mother's favorite face, the one she'd cut herself, the one with the slight lean to the lowercase e that Father could never bring himself to correct. Both families' names at the foot. Mine and Elias's, joint heirs, exactly as she had written it twenty years before anyone thought to make those words dangerous.

Two hundred copies. We posted them in the bridge square, sent them to the councils on both banks, sold them at the cost of paper to anyone who asked. Some people called it history. Hessler, if he ever saw one, would call it a betrayal. Mireille called it "the longest apology I have ever read, and I once read a Corvane legal document."

But here is what I learned, setting that account, letter by letter, in my mother's leaning type: memory doesn't survive in blood. Blood forgets. Blood dilutes. My mother knew it — why else hide letters under the floor of a print shop? Why else marry her promise to paper? She kept the truth the only place truth keeps: in type, where it can be reprinted whenever the world mislays it.

We are not an orchard family or a print family anymore. We are both. And the orchard, like the shop, is a press — every tree a sort, every season a new edition.

* * *

Summer came early that year, the way summers do after you've earned them.

In June I crossed the bridge without thinking about it — just walked across, mid-morning, with a parcel of proofs under my arm, and got halfway over before I realized what I'd done. Twelve years of treating that span like the edge of the world, and now my feet simply carried me over it the way they carried me to the bakery. Freedom, it turns out, is mostly a matter of forgetting to be afraid on schedule.

Mireille had moved into the rebuilt east room of the Corvane house — rebuilt smaller, Elias insisted, "because the original was sized for my father's ego" — and kept the orchard accounts, having discovered in her convalescence a genius for arithmetic that she deployed with the ruthlessness of a customs official. She and Father argued constantly about paper costs across the whole width of the river, loudly, by letter, with copies to me, as though the bridge existed specifically to facilitate their dispute over rag content. I have never seen either of them happier.

Elias and I walked the rows in the long evenings. The young trees were taking — most of them; seven failed, and we grieved them individually, because after a winter like ours you grieve whatever grows. He talked about his father sometimes now, in the flat careful voice of a man handling broken glass: General Corvane, who had signed a promise in peacetime and spent the war pretending it was worthless, because admitting its worth meant admitting what he'd surrendered it to protect. Cowardice, Elias called it. Father, who knew the species intimately, called it fear with good posture. Between the two diagnoses I have built something like understanding, which is more than I expected to carry out of that winter.

"Do you ever miss the silence?" Elias asked me once, in July, watching the light go amber down the rows.

I thought honestly about it. "It fit better," I said. "Silence fits everyone. Truth has to be tailored."

"And yours fits?"

"It chafes," I admitted. "Every day. But it's mine."

He nodded like that answered something, and maybe it did, and maybe what it answered was why we were walking the rows together at dusk, which neither of us had yet said aloud, and which the whole valley had apparently discussed without our permission. Mireille had begun leaving the east room's shutters pointedly open on my evenings. Tomas had taken to needing urgent deliveries to the far bank at precisely the wrong hours. Even the fishwife who'd dug the first hole had opinions, delivered at volume across the market.

I am a printer's daughter. I know a forme when it's locked and waiting. I simply hadn't yet pulled the lever.

* * *

On the last morning of summer, I found the sapling.

Not in the orchard — in the yard behind the shop, in a barrel of rainwater where Tomas had stuck it weeks ago and forgotten, a stray from the delivery, roots balled in sacking, somehow alive out of pure spite. An apple tree, doing its level best to grow in a courtyard the size of a tablecloth, between the paper store and the drainpipe.

"Tomas," I called up the stairwell. "Your tree's dying of ambition."

He came down, looked at it, looked at me. "It won't fruit in a yard that size."

"No."

"It'll crowd the paper store in ten years."

"Yes."

He grinned, that grin that had cracked our silence open a year and a lifetime ago. "Good," he said. "Let it."

We planted it together in the corner where the morning light came off the river, and Father came out and said nothing, which from Father was a benediction, and Mireille — visiting, ostensibly to argue about rag content — stood on the step with her arms folded and pronounced it "crooked," and we let it be crooked, because crooked things that insist on growing have earned the right.

That evening I sat at my case, sorting type, the lowercases whispering back into their boxes, and I caught myself composing — the way you do, hands moving on their own — a line for the autumn edition of the account, the anniversary printing we'd all quietly agreed to without agreeing.

*Mother,* I thought, *you hid your truth under the floorboards and I carried it across the ice, and now it grows in a courtyard.*

And beneath the floor where her letters had slept for twelve years, the boards lay quiet, empty, done with their work. I'd had Tomas plane them smooth and leave them plain — no hiding place there anymore. Above them, in the case, forty pounds of type waited in the dark of their boxes, each character a small hard seed, patient as anything planted in scorched ground, asking only for a hand willing to set it in order and a press strong enough to say it out loud.

Tomorrow we'd begin the autumn pages. Tonight I banked the lamp, and the shop held its breath around me the way it always had — not a silence of fear now, but the held breath of a thing about to speak.
