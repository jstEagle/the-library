## 1. The Year of Great Storms

The year began with the sea in a temper and never recovered its manners.

Casper Ashgrove had kept the light at Wrackstone Point for eleven winters, first as a keeper's wife and then — after the sea took Thomas in October of '43 — as keeper entire. The Board had grumbled about it, she knew. A woman alone on a rock with nothing but a lamp and her own two hands was not, in their estimation, a proper arrangement. But they could not find another man willing to live where the wind came off the Atlantic like something with teeth, and so they had signed her name into the ledger between two dead men's names and let her be.

She did not mind being left alone to be. There was a shape to her days that suited her, small and exact as stitching.

At dusk she climbed the ninety-one steps to the lamp room, trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork that turned the great lens, and set the flame to its work. At midnight she climbed again to check the oil feed. At dawn she doused the light and wrote the night into the logbook: *Wind SW, moderate. Clear. No shipping.* The logbook was the spine of her life — every hour accounted for, every weather noted, every vessel seen or not seen. Nothing in it ever happened. That was the point of it. A lighthouse logbook was a record of the ordinary, and Casper had come to love the ordinary the way other people loved God: faithfully, without expectation of anything in return.

History, she had decided long ago, belonged to other people. It belonged to men in London who argued in stone rooms about things done in countries whose names changed when the wind shifted. It belonged to admirals and colonels and the editors of newspapers. It did not belong to a widow on a rock, whose whole war with the world consisted of keeping one flame lit against the dark so that strangers' ships would not break their backs on the reef below.

Thomas had thought differently once. He had been a signalman before he was a keeper — had served his years ashore at the coast stations, tapping out messages in flags and shutters, and he used to say that every message was a little piece of history flying through the air, if only anyone bothered to read it. She remembered him teaching her the code on their honeymoon winter, laughing because she was quicker at it than he'd expected. *A woman who can read signals,* he said, *is never entirely at anyone else's mercy.* He had drowned within sight of this tower, in the shallows he had crossed ten thousand times, and no signal in any code had warned him. After that, Casper had put the code away like a dress for a warmer climate.

The storms of '47 tested her devotion daily. In January the wind blew for nine days without pause, and the spray went over the lamp room itself, freezing in white rime along the glass so that she had to go out on the gallery with a scraper in a gale, harnessed to the rail, chipping ice while the sea tried to pluck her off like a barnacle. In February the supply boat did not come at all, and she ate salted cod and the last of her flour and told herself hunger sharpened the mind. In March it hailed stones the size of gull eggs. By April, when the weather eased just enough for the boat to risk the crossing, the island looked like something scoured — grass flattened to the direction of the prevailing wind, gulls blown inland, the very soil seeming tired of holding on.

She stood at the landing stage that grey morning and watched the boat come round the headland, pitching, and felt the particular relief she allowed herself only when no one could see it.

* * *

The boat was the *Kittiwake*, forty feet of oak and stubbornness, worked by old Enoch Pellow and his two grandsons. Enoch had run supplies to Wrackstone since before Thomas died, and he had never once asked whether it was proper for a woman to sign for her own barrels. He simply handed them over and took her money and complained about the price of tar, and Casper considered this one of the more decent relationships of her life.

"You look thinner," he said, clumping up the stage with a crate of oil tins on his shoulder.

"You look older."

"I am older." He set the crate down and straightened with a wince. "We all are. Except you. You're preserved in brine, like a herring." His grandsons — Jowan, fourteen and all elbows, and little Tam — ferried boxes up the path to the store shed, arguing about who carried the heavier end. Casper counted the cargo against her list: lamp oil, coal, flour, tea, salt pork, a bolt of flannel, lamp glass — two panes wrapped in straw, thank God, because the last storm had cracked one and the cold came through the fissure like a needle.

Enoch lingered after the work was done, which was unlike him. He stood looking out at the water with his hands in his pockets, working up to something, and Casper waited with the patience of someone who had learned that news arrived on its own schedule, like weather.

"There's talk," he said finally.

"There usually is."

"Talk from the mainland. From Falmouth way, and further." He turned to face her, and she saw that under the weathering there was something genuinely troubled in his old face. "They're saying the 12th Regiment's in disgrace. Officers cashiered. A court of inquiry, maybe worse. And nobody will say what for, which is how you know it's bad."

Casper folded the empty sacking slowly. "The 12th. That's the foot regiment? They were posted abroad, weren't they?"

"Some colony or other. Hot place. The papers here only print what the London ones tell them, and London prints what the War Office lets them." Enoch spat thoughtfully over the edge of the stage. "But sailors hear things. We brought out a passenger last month, some clerk going to Penzance, and he talked my grandson's ear off. Said there were letters coming home that officers' wives were burning in the grate rather than be caught with them. Said a captain shot himself in his club. Said—" He stopped, embarrassed.

"Said what?"

"That villagers were killed. Over there. Women among them. And that it wasn't the enemy that killed them, if you follow me." He said it quietly, the way a man says a blasphemy in church — testing whether the roof falls in.

Casper looked out at the sea. It lay flat and pewter-coloured, innocent as milk. Somewhere beyond the horizon were places she would never see, where things had happened that she would never have cause to think about again after tonight. That was the arrangement she had made with history: it stayed on its side of the water, and she stayed on hers.

"War is ugly," she said. "It always has been. That's why sensible people stay home."

"Aye." But Enoch did not sound convinced, and neither, quite, did she. "Well. Your flour's in the shed. Jowan! Tam! We've a tide to catch." He hesitated at the top of the stage, then dug in his coat and produced a newspaper, folded soft with handling, weeks old by its date. "Brought you this. Thought you might like to know what the world's up to. Even if the world's not up to much good."

He pressed it into her hands before she could refuse, which was wise, because she would have refused. News was a door she preferred to keep painted shut.

* * *

That evening she read it anyway.

Not all of it — she skipped the parliamentary drivel and the society columns full of names that meant nothing to her — but the war reports drew her eye the way a sore draws the tongue. There was fighting somewhere new every month; the empire seemed to itch all over. And buried at the bottom of page four, in three lines of print so small they might have been an apology, was a paragraph about the 12th Regiment:

*It is understood that certain irregularities attending the recent operations of H.M.'s 12th Regiment of Foot are the subject of official inquiry. Further particulars are withheld.*

*Irregularities.* Casper set the paper down on the kitchen table and looked at it for a long time. The fire ticked. Rain began against the north windows, soft at first, then with intent. She had lived beside the sea long enough to know that a smooth surface meant nothing; the danger was never what showed, but what moved underneath.

Three lines of type. Somebody in London knew exactly what those irregularities were — knew the names and the dates and the orders — and somebody had decided that three vague lines were all the public deserved. She found herself wondering who made such decisions, and what kind of man decided what a country got to remember about itself. Then she caught herself at it and stopped, almost annoyed. This was precisely the sort of thinking that led nowhere. The world's accounts were kept by the world's accountants, and it was not her business. Her business was oil, wicks, glass, weather, the logbook. Small things, kept exactly.

She banked the fire, checked the barometer — falling, and falling fast — and climbed the ninety-one steps.

The wind had swung round to the southwest and was rising in long, deep surges, like breathing in sleep that was turning into nightmare. Out past the reef the swell was already stacking up, green-black, with white manes torn off it sideways. Casper trimmed the wick, wound the mechanism, and stood a moment at the glass watching the dark come down over the water. Somewhere out there, tonight, there would be ships. There always were, in all weathers, carrying grain and coal and soldiers and letters, carrying the world's business back and forth across a surface that forgave nothing.

She went down to the kitchen and opened the logbook to the day's page. *Wind SW freshening, heavy sea. Rain beginning. Barometer falling rapidly.* Her pen hovered. For no reason she could name, she added a line she did not usually bother with: *Expect a hard blow tonight.*

Then she sat by the fire with Thomas's old coat across her knees, mending a tear in the elbow seam that she had already mended twice, because the coat fit no one now and she mended it anyway, the way she wound the clockwork anyway, the way she did everything anyway — because a thing kept in order was a thing that could not surprise you. The rain thickened. The wind found its voice. And far out in the dark, though she could not yet know it, the sea was arranging matters so that by morning, everything she believed about the size of her life would be washed up on her doorstep, small and breathing and impossible to ignore.
