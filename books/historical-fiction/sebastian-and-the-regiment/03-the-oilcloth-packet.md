## 3. The Oilcloth Packet

The boy slept through the whole of the next day.

Casper had half expected him to wake ravenous, or weeping, or calling for someone — children in stories always did one of those things — but he did none of them. He lay in the narrow bed that had been hers as a girl, before she'd married and moved the forty steps to the keeper's cottage, and he breathed shallowly and steadily, like the sea after a storm has spent itself, and when she touched his forehead his eyes opened a slit, took her measure, and closed again. He ate what she put beside the bed — bread softened in milk, a spoon of honey — but only when she turned her back, only when he believed himself unwatched. By evening the plate was empty and he had not once looked at her while eating it.

She knew something about that kind of hunger. It was the hunger of an animal deciding whether the hand that fed it might also be the hand that trapped it.

She had set the oilcloth packet on the dresser, beneath the lamp with its smoked glass chimney, and all day it had sat there like a thing washed up from a grave. Which, she thought, watching the lamplight pool on its wet grey surface, was very nearly what it was. The note had said *forget the face*, and the face belonged to this child, and whoever had left both on her doorstep in the worst gale of the year had trusted her with them for reasons she could not yet see. Trust, or desperation. On a night like that night, they were probably the same thing.

Near midnight, when the light above her head swung through its patient arc and the wind had dropped to a long low moan out of the northwest, the boy woke properly for the first time.

She heard the bed creak, and then nothing — the particular nothing of a child holding himself absolutely still, listening. She let the silence stand a while. Then she carried the lamp into the little room and set it on the chair and said, "There's water in the jug if you're dry, and there's a privy down the stair and along, and I won't ask you your name until you've a mind to give it."

He looked at her. He had grey eyes, she saw now, the grey of slate before rain, and a small white scar through one eyebrow, old enough to have healed smooth. He was perhaps seven. Perhaps eight. Thin the way children go thin when fear eats faster than food can fill.

"Sebastian," he said. His voice was rusted from disuse.

"Sebastian." She nodded, as though he'd told her the weather. "I'm Casper Ashgrove. This is my light you're sleeping under." She poured water into the chipped blue cup and held it out. He took it without touching her fingers — she noticed that; she noticed everything about how he declined to touch — and drank it down in three swallows, and held the cup out for more.

"There's bread and cheese by the stove," she said. "You can eat it here or at the table, whichever suits."

At the table, then. He sat across from her with the table between them like a fortification, and ate slowly, watching the window, watching the door, watching the dark shape of the packet still lying on the dresser. When his gaze caught on it the third time, she decided the hour had come.

"You know what that is," she said.

He stopped chewing.

"I'm not going to open it tonight unless you say I may," she went on, keeping her voice level and unhurried, the voice she used with frightened things — gulls with broken wings, once, a seal pup tangled in netting. "But it came with you, and I think it belongs with you, and I think somebody meant me to read it. So tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever you're ready, you'll tell me yes or no. That's all I ask tonight. Yes or no, when you're ready."

Sebastian swallowed. He looked at the packet a long time, and then at her, and something moved behind his eyes like a fish turning under ice.

"He said you'd keep it safe," the boy said. "He said the light-keeper's wife keeps things safe. He said—" and there his voice failed him, and he pressed his lips together, and finished instead: "He smelled of lamp oil. Like you."

*He.* She did not ask who. The word hung in the kitchen between them, warm and terrible, and Casper understood that somewhere beyond the reach of this island there was a man who had known her husband's habits, known the signal codes Tam kept in his head, known that a widow alone would take in a stranger's child because no one else remained to do it. And understanding that, she felt the first cold weight of dread settle into her chest, where curiosity had been sitting comfortable all day.

"Then he shall have it kept safe," she said. "Go to sleep now, Sebastian. Nothing gets up that stair past my hearing."

He went. She heard him lie down, heard the breathing lengthen, and she sat alone at her table with the lamp burning and the packet waiting, and she thought: *I could burn it. I could bury it under the third stone of the causeway and forget the face and the name and the smell of lamp oil, and go back to being small.*

She did not believe she would. But she noted, with a kind of clinical sadness, that the thought had come at all. That was what the packet had already cost her: the comfort of not having choices.

* * *

In the morning the sea had gone flat and gleaming, that post-gale calm like a held breath, and Sebastian stood at the window in yesterday's clothes watching the tide withdraw from the rocks with the fixed attention of a creature memorizing escape routes. Casper fried herrings and set the table and waited until he had eaten half of one before she brought the packet over and laid it between his plate and hers.

"Yes or no?" she asked.

His jaw worked. He stared at the oilcloth. Then he reached out and put two fingers on it, lightly, the way one touches something hot to confirm it is real, and pushed it an inch toward her.

"Yes," he whispered.

She cut the tarred twine with her sewing scissors. The oilcloth was folded in thirds and stiff with salt, and inside were two bundles: papers wrapped in a second layer of waxed linen, and a small canvas bag that clinked faintly — coins, a dozen or more, gold, sovereigns by the weight of them. She set the money aside untouched. Papers first.

The top sheet was written in a soldier's hand, square and careful, the letters of a man who had learned penmanship late and practiced hard. The ink was brown where it had dried slow. It began without salutation, without address, as though the writer had not dared to imagine who might read it.

*I, Thomas Ellery Vane's most unwilling subordinate, Sergeant William Pruen, 12th Regiment of Foot, being in sound mind and failing body, set down what was done at Kandari village on the fourteenth of October last, so that it may be known by someone, somewhere, after I am dead.*

Casper read the sentence twice. The fire ticked in the grate. Outside, a curlew called its thin rising note over the flats, indifferent as scripture.

She read on.

It was worse than war. She had lost Tam to war's ordinary cruelty — a boat swamped, a court-martial that blamed the drowned man for the drowning — and she had thought herself acquainted with what men in uniform did to other men. But this was a different country of cruelty altogether. Sergeant Pruen wrote plainly, without ornament, which somehow made it worse, the way a plain room makes a coffin look longer. He wrote of orders given by Colonel Vane to make an example of a village that had withheld grain. He wrote of the firing line and the huts burned with people in them, of the count taken afterward — one hundred and six, of whom thirty-one were children — and of the order given that same evening, before the smoke had cleared, that no report would mention the village at all. The official account, Pruen wrote, had been drafted *in advance*. He had seen the pages. They described a skirmish. They named no villagers. They were signed and dated as though the dead had already agreed to be forgotten.

And then: *I have copied the true record into cipher, keyed to a system I will not name here, for names are dangerous. The key is a book, and the book is in the keeping of a man who tended lights. If God is good, the woman who reads this will understand.*

Casper lowered the page. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake. Sebastian watched her across the table, and she made herself fold the confession along its creases before speaking.

"This sergeant," she said. "The man who left you here. Was it him?"

"No." Sebastian's voice was very small. "Sergeant Pruen was Papa's friend. Papa was—" He stopped. Started again. "Papa was an officer. The bad colonel hated him because Papa wouldn't sign the paper. Papa said the paper was a lie. Then Papa was sent away, and Mama cried, and then soldiers came at night and Mama gave me to a man in a fish cart and told me to be quiet until morning, and I was quiet until morning, and then there wasn't any Mama anymore." He recited it flatly, the way children recite lessons they have gone over too many times alone in the dark. Only at the end did the flatness crack: "She said forget my face. So they couldn't find me. But I don't want to be forgotten."

"No," Casper said. Her throat ached around the words. "No, you won't be. That instruction was never meant for me, d'you hear? Forgetting is their trade, not mine. You eat your herring."

He bent to his plate. Presently his shoulders began to shake, silently, in the manner of a child who has learned that noise brings soldiers, and Casper rose and came around the table and put her hand on his cropped hair, and he did not pull away, and that was the whole of the embrace and it was enough.

* * *

The ciphered pages numbered eleven, written close on both sides in the same square hand, dense with figures and letters in arrangements that meant nothing she could parse. She spread them on the dresser after supper, when Sebastian had fallen asleep sitting up and been steered to bed, and she worked at them the way she worked at stubborn knots, patiently, without hope of genius, trusting to persistence.

They defeated her. Not entirely — she found patterns, repetitions, the telltale clustering that suggested groups of five, a substitution of some kind — but every system she tried, the simple ciphers she'd played at as a girl with Tam, the newspaper puzzles the mainland papers sometimes printed, produced nothing but gravel. Whatever key unlocked these pages, it was not arithmetic and it was not a child's game.

*Keyed to a book,* Pruen had written. *A book in the keeping of a man who tended lights.*

Tam had owned perhaps thirty books. The Bible, the almanac, a battered Shakespeare bought off a wreck, a navigation manual, a treatise on tides she'd never finished. She fetched them one by one and tested passages against the opening group of figures — first word, first chapter, counting letter by letter — and got gravel, gravel, gravel. Somewhere past midnight she sat back and rubbed her eyes and admitted the shape of the problem, which was not really the problem of the book at all.

The problem was that Pruen had written *a man who tended lights*, present tense, deliberate. Not *the* man. A man. As though there were several. As though tending lights were a trade with members, a brotherhood, and any one of them might hold the key — and the sergeant, writing in haste and terror, had chosen not to say which. Because names were dangerous. Because the wrong reader finding the right name would destroy everything.

Which meant the key was not merely hidden. It was *distributed*. Hidden the way a lighthouse scatters its beam — everywhere at once, and nowhere you could put your hand on.

Casper looked at the ciphered pages glowing pale in the lamplight, and at the sleeping boy visible through the doorway, one fist curled under his chin, and she felt the dread in her chest change its weight and temperature. Curiosity was a cat's emotion, cheap and pleasant. This was different. This was the feeling of standing on a ledge in the dark and realizing the drop goes further than you thought.

Because she had recognized one thing in the confession, one small detail that had snagged her like a nail in wool and that she had not yet said aloud even to herself: the date. *The fourteenth of October.* Tam had drowned in September, seven years ago, and the court-martial that followed — the court-martial that had blamed her drowned husband for a signal error, when she had known, known in her bones and in her husband's own careful logs, that the error was not his — had convened in October. The same autumn. The same season of storms.

And Pruen had written, in his plain soldier's hand, a single line she had passed over twice and returned to a third time:

*The colonel has friends who arrange such inquiries. Ask the widow of the northern light how her husband was judged, and she will know my meaning.*

The fire settled. The lamp overhead swung through its arc, and the beam swept the ceiling, and Casper Ashgrove sat very still in the circle of her own small light and understood that the packet had not come to her by accident, and that the past had not finished with her husband, and that whoever had chosen her doorstep in the teeth of the great storm had known precisely what they were doing.

She blew out the lamp, and lay awake beside the sleeping child, listening to the sea rehearse its one long sentence against the rocks — and near dawn, half-dreaming, she heard Tam's voice as clearly as if he stood at the foot of the bed, saying the phrase he used every time he taught her a new signal flag: *the code is nothing, Casper, the code is nothing — it's the keeper who remembers.*

She sat bolt upright in the dark.

Not a book. Not only a book. Tam had kept a logbook, all his working life, and in it, alongside wind and tide and ships sighted, he had recorded signals in a private shorthand of his own devising — a shorthand he had shown no one but her, laughing, saying a keeper needed one secret of his own. The logbook was upstairs in the lamp room still, in the drawer of the chart table, where she had never had the heart to move it.

The key to a murdered officer's testimony had been lying forty feet above her head for seven years.
