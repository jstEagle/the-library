## 4. The Cipher in the Logbook

Three nights the packet lay in the bread tin behind the flour crock, and three nights Casper lay awake listening to it as though paper could breathe.

It could not, of course. Paper was dead things — rags and rope-fiber and the sweat of some millhand's year — and yet she had come to think of that oilcloth-wrapped bundle as something warm-blooded, curled in its hiding place, patient as a tick. Sebastian slept in the little room that had been a stores cupboard before she'd widened the cot and hung a curtain of sailcloth for a door. He slept like a drowned man, silent and deep, and woke each morning with his eyes already open, already watching her, as if sleep were only a courtesy he paid her and never truly entered.

She had not opened the sealed letter again. The confession — if that was what it was — she had read once by lamplight with her heart going like a gaff against a deck, and once was enough to know she would need to be braver before she read it twice. But the loose sheets, the ones covered edge to edge in those small, disciplined figures — columns of numbers, letters paired off like dancers, dashes and dots in regimented ranks — those she could not stop thinking about. Not because they frightened her. Because they looked *familiar*.

On the fourth night, with the light turning overhead in its great slow wheel and the winter wind worrying at the glazing bars like a dog at a bone, she went down to the room where Thomas still lived in everything he had left behind.

* * *

Her husband's sea chest stood at the foot of the bed they had shared for eleven years, and she had not opened it since the day she'd packed it. That was the arithmetic of grief: eleven years of marriage, four years of widowhood, and one opening. She knelt now on the cold flags, and her hands knew the trick of the hasp even after so long, the way you press left then lift right, because the wood had swollen in the salt air and Thomas had never planed it, saying there was no hurry, they'd get to it, there was always tomorrow at a lighthouse.

The smell came up out of the chest like a voice. Tar and wool and lamp oil — no, not lamp oil, she corrected herself, *whale oil*, he'd insisted on whale for the chest linings though it cost more, because — because of course he did, Thomas Ashgrove had opinions about oil the way rectors had opinions about scripture —

And underneath her, quite suddenly, her knees gave, and she sat down hard on the floor with the lid open in front of her and wept without sound, the way she had learned to weep, the way a woman learns when there is a light to keep and no one to hear her anyway.

When it passed, as it always passed, she wiped her face with her sleeve and began to unpack a man's life.

Signal flags, folded into squares soft as linen. A brass rule gone green at the edges. His signal manual — the Admiralty issue, 1838, spine cracked white — and beneath that, a notebook bound in oiled canvas that was not Admiralty property at all. She knew it at once. She had watched him make it, winter after winter, hunched at this very table with the lamp turned low, muttering, crossing out, beginning again. He had called it his *private signals*. Every station keeper worth his salt kept them: a code between his own light and the boats that worked these waters, so a fisherman running home ahead of weather could speak to the tower without the whole coast reading over his shoulder.

Thomas had been prouder of that notebook than of any commendation. *Any fool can burn a lamp, Cass,* he'd said once, tapping the pages. *This is how you say something worth hearing.*

She set the ciphered sheets from the oilcloth packet beside it on the table, and held the lamp close, and looked from one to the other for a long time without letting herself understand what she was seeing.

Then she understood it anyway.

The same hand. Not the same handwriting exactly — but the same *architecture*. The same way of ruling columns with a hair-fine line. The same habit of grouping figures in fives. The same odd little flourish where a column broke, a small downward tick like the tail-feather of a bird, which she had teased Thomas about for years — *you sign your numbers like a man signing his name, love.*

Someone who had learned this system had written those pages in the packet. Someone who had learned it from Thomas, or beside Thomas, or — she felt the floor tilt gently under her chair — someone Thomas had taught it to, here, at this table, in the winters she could almost count backward if she let herself remember the visitors, the rare callers, the young officer who'd come ashore one spring with dispatches and stayed three days because the fog would not lift —

*Aldous.* That had been his name. Lieutenant Aldous Merrick. He had eaten their bread and admired Thomas's private signals and asked intelligent questions until midnight, and Thomas had been so starved for a mind to talk to that he'd shown him everything, and she remembered now — she remembered with a sick, swimming clarity — standing in the doorway with the coffee pot and hearing Thomas laugh and say, *You'll want to write this down, lad, I shan't say it twice,* and the lieutenant laughing too, and the lamp between them making two shadows on the wall that leaned together like friends.

Four years dead, Thomas Ashgrove had just spoken across his own grave.

Casper sat very still. The light wheeled above her. Somewhere below, in the sailcloth-hung cupboard, a boy breathed in his sleep, and she thought: *he sent his son to the one door in England where the key to this lock was already hanging.*

* * *

She did not sleep that night, or rather she slept the way a helm sleeps, one hand always on the wheel. In the grey hour before dawn she climbed the tower, trimmed the wicks, wound the clockwork that turned the lens, and watched the sea go from black to iron to a dull beaten silver, and all the while her mind worked the figures the way her fingers worked the machinery, without her quite ordering them to.

By full daylight she had cleared the kitchen table of everything but the notebook, the ciphered sheets, the lamp, and a pencil sharpened with her knife. She sent Sebastian out with the last of yesterday's bread crusts to throw to the gulls off the north wall — she needed him gone an hour, needed the room to herself, needed not to have those dark watchful eyes on her while she committed whatever she was about to commit.

Thomas's system, she recalled, was built on the signal manual itself. The key was a phrase; each word of the phrase pointed to a page and line in the manual; the letters there gave the substitution. It was elegant and it was tedious and it was, she realized with a cold drop in her stomach, unbreakable unless you knew both halves. And the packet's cipher used the same construction — she could see the skeleton of it now, the same joints, the same bones — but keyed to a different book. Something unfamiliar. The note in the first night's reading had said as much: *keyed as you know how.*

*As you know how.* Meaning: as Thomas knew how. Which meant Thomas had known the second book too.

She went back to the chest. This time she took out everything, laid it all on the bed in rows like a surgeon laying out instruments, and made herself look at each item as a keeper looks at a horizon — steadily, patiently, expecting nothing and missing nothing. Signal flags. The brass rule. A coil of tarred twine. Spare spectacles. Letters from his sister in Whitby, tied in a bundle. A prayer book with a pressed sprig of sea-pink fallen out when she touched it, and she put the sprig in her pocket and did not cry this time, because there wasn't time, because somewhere out past the horizon men were counting days until they found this house.

At the bottom of the chest, wrapped in an old shirt of his, was a small book she had forgotten entirely: a nautical almanac, three years out of date, worthless to any sailor alive.

Worthless to any sailor. Perfect to a signalman. A book every household might own, that no searcher would think to confiscate, that carried within its dull printed ephemerides a thousand pages and lines to point to.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake.

The work took her until the light failed. She will never afterward be able to describe those hours except as a kind of drowning in reverse — coming up and up through black water toward a surface that kept receding, breaking through for one gasp of meaning, going under again. Page forty-one, line six, cross-reference the marginal figure, invert, take every third letter. Thomas had loved his inversions. *A code should make a clever man feel stupid, Cass. That's half its value.*

The first fragment came clear at dusk, and she wrote it out in fair copy because her eyes were failing and because writing it made it real:

> *...the transports sailed from [—] on the ninth, contrary to the orders given. Vane countermanded at the water's edge. The village was to be spared pending inquiry. There was no inquiry. There was only the morning and the firing...*

She stopped. Read it again. The name seemed to stand up off the page — *Vane* — a name she had heard only weeks ago in the supply boat's gossip, attached to a decoration, a hero of the small colonial wars, a colonel whose photograph print was sold in shop windows on the mainland along with the Queen's and General Somebody's. Men she had assumed belonged to history, to newspapers, to the far bright elsewhere where other people's lives happened.

The second fragment took until nearly midnight, and it was worse, because it had dates in it, and ships' names, and the dates matched the ships' movements the way teeth match a jaw:

> *...H.M.S. —— anchored off the point on the eleventh; her log will show a calm sea and routine exercise. Her boats went armed at dusk. Ask what the log omits. Ask who signed the order of the tenth. The signature is genuine. The signature is the crime...*

And the third fragment, the last she could manage before her candle guttered, was the one that made her rise from the table and stand at the window with her arms wrapped around herself, looking down the black slope to where the village lights burned small and gold, Marta's house among them, all those sleeping families, all that fragile ordinary life:

> *...if I do not survive to speak, the record must speak. They have bought the newspapers and they will buy the inquiry. What cannot be bought is a written thing in many hands. Copy it, madam, whoever you are, wherever he has sent you. Copy it and hide it and give it to the sea, give it to the wind, give it to anyone but trust no single vessel of it. I was taught my trade by a keeper who told me the light does not choose which ships to save. Neither must we choose which truths to carry...*

He had known. Whoever Aldous Merrick had been, whatever corner he'd been backed into, he had known he might not live to deliver this himself, and he had spent his last months — she was sure of it now, sure with the certainty of the sleepless — arranging for it to travel without him. To a lighthouse. To *this* lighthouse. Because a lighthouse was the one station on the coast that no government could plausibly search without scandal, tended by the one sort of person — a woman, a widow, nobody — whom no one would credit with holding an empire's secrets.

He had used her insignificance as armor. She found she could not decide whether to be furious or grateful, and suspected, as the candle died, that she would spend the rest of her life deciding.

* * *

Sebastian appeared in the doorway at some point — she heard the sailcloth whisper and turned, and there he stood in his nightshirt with his feet bare on cold stone and the gulls' crumbs still presumably on his breath, watching the papers strewn across the table the way he watched everything: as though the world had proven itself untrustworthy and required auditing.

She should have swept the pages away. She didn't.

"Did you know your father could read cipher?" she asked quietly.

Something moved behind his eyes. He shook his head — no — and then, with the terrible economy of children, amended it: "He wrote letters. Long ones. Mama said they took him *ages*." His voice was small and rough with disuse. "He smelled of lamp oil."

"So do I," Casper said.

"I know." He said it without accusation and without comfort, simply as fact, and crossed the room, and stood beside her chair, and looked down at the fair-copy page with its careful lines. He could not have read it — he was seven, perhaps eight, and shaken besides — but he looked at it a long time, and then he reached out one finger and touched the little downward tick where a column broke, the tail-feather flourish, the mark of a man signing his numbers.

"That's how Papa made his fives," Sebastian said.

The fire settled. The wind rose a note. Casper put her arm around the boy's sharp shoulders and drew him against her side, and together, keeper and child, they looked at the dead man's handwriting, and outside the window the true light turned and turned above a sea that kept all its secrets and gave back none.

It was two mornings later that the supply boat came early, unscheduled, riding a flat grey swell, and the boy who ran up the path from the landing slip with a basket and a strange expression called up to her that there were gentlemen on it — *two gentlemen, mistress, come asking after a lost woman and her child.*
