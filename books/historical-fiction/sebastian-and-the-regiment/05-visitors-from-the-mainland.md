## 5. Visitors from the Mainland

The supply boat came on a Thursday, which was wrong, because it always came on a Tuesday. Casper noticed the wrongness of it before she noticed anything else — the day itself arriving out of season, like a bird blown off its course. She was in the oil room decanting paraffin when she heard the hull scrape shingle and stood very still with her hand flat against the cold tin drum, listening to the sound of men's voices carrying across the water.

There were two of them. That was wrong too.

She came up the spiral stair into the grey light of a morning that could not decide whether to rain. Sebastian was at the window of the sitting room, his forehead nearly touching the glass, watching the boat below. He had learned to move quietly since he'd come to her; even his breathing seemed to have been trained by whatever had happened to him into something small and careful. When she entered, he turned, and she saw the question in him before he could shape it.

"Fish buyers," she said. "Probably. Come early for the herring."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he went back to the window, and she saw him arrange himself so that only half his face showed above the sill — the habit of a child who had learned that being seen was a thing that happened to other people first, and then happened to you.

Casper took down her oil-stained apron and tied it on as though it were armor. It was not armor. Nothing she owned was armor. But the apron said *this is all there is here*, and lies were best told wearing something true.

* * *

They climbed the path from the shingle with the careful steps of men who had not been raised on rock. The taller one wore a good coat gone soft at the elbows and carried himself like a man accustomed to being listened to. The shorter one was broader, ruddy, with hands like knotted rope and the patient face of a man who did the listening for a living. Neither wore red. She noted that first — noted it with a small cold satisfaction — and then noted that the absence of red told her nothing except that they wished her not to think of regiments.

"Lady Ashgrove," the tall one said from the base of the tower, touching his hat brim. He had her name right, or nearly — no lady, never a lady, but he gave the word a courteous weight, and she understood it was a tool being offered handle-first.

"Keeper Ashgrove," she corrected pleasantly. "This is a working light, gentlemen. What can I do for you?"

"Cranmer," said the tall one. "And this is Mr. Dole. We're inspecting coastal lights on behalf of Trinity House — a survey of condition, stores, readiness." His smile was well-made, like everything else about him. "Routine."

"In a storm year?" Casper let her eyebrows rise. "I'd have thought your surveyors would wait until the sea stopped trying to kill them."

"The sea doesn't stop, Mrs. Ashgrove. We've merely learned to work around its moods." Cranmer produced a leather notebook and opened it with a flourish, as if to prove the routine-ness of his business. "Might we see the lamp room? And while my colleague examines the mechanisms — Mr. Dole has an eye for machinery — I wonder if I might trouble you for a few words. Records. Stores inventories. The usual tedium."

It was all reasonable. That was what frightened her, later, when she lay awake turning the morning over: how entirely reasonable it had been. A boat on the wrong day, two men with plausible papers, questions shaped like courtesy. If she had still been the woman of last winter — the woman who believed history belonged to other people — she would have shown them everything and slept soundly after.

She showed them the lamp room.

* * *

Dole climbed the stairs behind her with surprising grace for a heavy man, saying little, touching little, looking much. Casper narrated as she went — the Fresnel lens, the clockwork rotation, the reserve lamps stacked in their crates — and heard her own voice settle into the flat competence of a woman reciting what any keeper might recite. It was a performance, and it was also simply true, and the overlap between the two made the lying easier and more shameful in equal measure.

At the top, Dole circled the lamp once, slowly, like a man walking around a claim he intended to stake. Then his eyes went past it — to the logbook shelf, to the desk with its drawer, to the narrow door of the oil store.

"You keep the records up here," he said. Not quite a question.

"Where else? The salt gets into paper down below. Ask any keeper."

"Mm." He opened the logbook where it lay, turned a page, another. His finger moved down columns of wind speeds and barometer readings without reading them. "You write a fair hand, Mrs. Ashgrove."

"My husband wrote plainer than I do. You'll see the change where his entries end."

Something crossed his face then — quick, unreadable, gone. He closed the book and squared it on the shelf with a neatness that irritated her more than any roughness could have done.

Below, she heard Cranmer's voice rising through the stairwell, pleasant as weather. He was asking about the fishing families. How many boats worked the water off the point. Whether they landed here often. Whether strangers ever came ashore.

* * *

She left Dole to his inventory and descended to find Cranmer standing in her sitting room doorway, hat in hand, taking in the room with the mild thoroughness of a man appraising a house he might buy. Sebastian was not in it. She had sent him to the coal store with instructions to count sacks and stay counted, and the boy had gone without a word, which was its own kind of heartbreak.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." Cranmer smiled. "May I ask — have you had visitors this autumn? Beyond your supply runs?"

"Visitors come for the light or the fish. Mostly neither." She began gathering cups onto a tray, giving her hands honest work. "You'll take tea? The kettle's on."

"That's very kind." He sat where she indicated and accepted the cup when it came, and for a moment they performed the ordinary theater of hospitality, steam rising between them, the rain finally committing itself against the windows. Then, gently, as though resuming rather than beginning: "We're looking for a woman and child. Traveling together. They may have passed through fishing villages along this coast — perhaps took shelter where they could. The woman is the boy's nurse, we believe. There's some concern for their welfare. The weather this season has been—" he gestured at the window, at the whole grey violence of the world— "unkind."

"Unkind is the word," Casper agreed. "I've buried a husband in it."

"I'm sorry."

"So am I. It was three years ago and I'm sorry every day." She sipped her tea. "What makes you think they'd come here? This isn't a place people stumble onto. You need a reason to come to a lighthouse, and reasons are usually written in charts."

"A child needs feeding wherever he lands." Cranmer's eyes stayed pleasant. "And lights are landmarks. People who are lost steer toward them. Surely you know that better than anyone."

The words settled over her like a net settling over fish. *People who are lost steer toward them.* She kept her face doing what faces do — the small sympathy, the professional blankness — and inside herself she watched, with something like horror, how steady she remained.

"No one's washed up here but wreckage," she said. "Planks, a ship's bell last month. If a woman and child had come, I'd have rowed them to the mainland myself. There's no doctor on this rock, gentlemen, and no food to spare beyond what I count carefully. A child wouldn't thrive here."

"No," Cranmer agreed softly. "A child wouldn't thrive here."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, Dole's boots moved through the oil room. Rain ticked against the glass. Casper drank her tea and let the silence belong to her, because she had learned in three years of widowhood that whoever owns the silence owns the room.

"There was a family on the mainland side," she added at last, idly, as if the thought had just surfaced. "Fisher folk said something about a woman asking after boats south. That would be—" she frowned, calculating— "three weeks back? Before the big blow. If they got a boat south in that weather, I'd pray for them."

It was a beautiful lie. She knew it was beautiful because she built it the way her husband had built signal flags: each element plain, each plain element pointing somewhere else. A woman. Southward. Three weeks ago. By now, if anyone checked, some fisherman half-remembering a stranger's face would confirm it, because memory wants to agree.

Cranmer wrote nothing down. That frightened her most of all. Men who intend to use information write it down; men who already have what they need only listen.

"You're very helpful," he said.

"I'm very isolated," she answered. "Isolation makes a person attentive. You notice everything when there's nothing to distract you from it."

"Indeed." He set down his cup and rose, and the visit was over as smoothly as it had begun. At the door he paused. "One more thing, Mrs. Ashgrove. The boy."

Her heart, which had been beating like any ordinary heart all morning, stopped being ordinary.

"Boy?" she said.

"The fisher families sometimes leave sons here to help with the boats' maintenance. Nets, lines. We passed a small jacket drying on the fence line coming up." He smiled apologetically. "I only wondered if you took an apprentice. It seems lonely work otherwise."

For one clear moment she saw it all laid out — the jacket she had hung out that morning without thinking, because children's clothes dry fast and she had forgotten, *forgotten*, the simplest domestic fact of her new life. One jacket, pegged to a fence, saying *a child lives here* as loudly as any confession.

"Nephew," she said. "My late husband's sister's boy. Comes over when the crossings allow. Helps me haul stores. He's shy — sleeps through most visits, if you want the truth of it. Children sleep like cats in this weather."

"Of course." Cranmer replaced his hat. "Give him our regards. Or don't — shyness is best left undisturbed." He stepped out into the rain, then turned, courteous to the last. "We'll be making a circuit of the coast. If anything should occur to you — anything at all — messages can reach us through the harbormaster at Penlow. Any day, at any hour. We're easy to find."

"People usually are," Casper said, "when they want to be found."

His smile held a fraction too long. Then he was gone, down the wet path, Dole falling in beside him, both of them ducking into the boat like men who had done this many times on many coasts.

* * *

She waited until the boat was a smudge, then a rumor, then nothing, before she called Sebastian up from the coal store. He emerged dusted black, blinking, and read her face the way he read everything — completely and without comment.

"Were they looking for me?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Did you tell them?"

"No."

He considered this with the grave attention he gave all things, then asked the question that undid her: "Was it hard?"

And she wanted to say yes. She wanted to say *it was the hardest thing, child, my tongue fought me every word*. But it hadn't been. The lies had come out smooth and warm and well-shaped, better than her truths ever were, and some part of her — the part that had spent three years keeping a light for ships she'd never meet — had enjoyed the craft of it.

"No," she said honestly, because he deserved honesty even now. "That's what frightens me."

He nodded slowly, accepting this in the way of children who have seen adults do worse things for worse reasons. Then he went to the window, to his half-hidden post above the sill, and watched the empty sea.

* * *

That evening she pulled the logbook down and sat with it under the lamp, reading her husband's old ciphered pages until the letters swam. She checked the cipher key hidden in the spine. She counted her stores of oil and flour and candle-wick as though arithmetic could protect anyone. She took Sebastian's jacket off the fence line and burned it in the stove and hated herself for burning a child's jacket, and knew she'd burn ten more without hesitation.

Before she banked the fire, she wrote in the logbook the day's entry, plain and true: *Wind SW fresh. Rain. Two visitors by boat, claiming inspection. Left before dusk.*

Then, beneath it, in cipher keyed to her dead husband's system, she added a second entry that no inspector reading the book would ever understand:

*They asked for a woman and child. I sent them south. God forgive me, I sent them anywhere but here.*

She closed the book and set it on its shelf beside the lamp, and climbing into bed she understood, with the clarity of the fully wakeful, that something in her had changed permanently this morning. She had lied to powerful men and been glad of the skill. She had hidden a child and called it mercy and known it was also war. Whatever she had been before — the small quiet keeper, the woman who believed history happened elsewhere — that woman had stood on the shingle this morning and watched a boat carry away the last of her innocence, and had not waved it goodbye.

In the dark, the light swept its slow circle overhead, and far down the coast, in whatever room they had reached by nightfall, the men named Cranmer and Dole were writing their report.

Or not writing it.

She would not sleep well again for knowing the difference.
