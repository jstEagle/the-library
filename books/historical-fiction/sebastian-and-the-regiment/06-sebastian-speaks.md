## 6. Sebastian Speaks

The boy had a name for the silence. He called it the between, and he lived inside it the way a wren lives in a wall — small, pressed close, listening for the shape of the world through stone.

Before the lighthouse there had been a house with green shutters in a town that smelled of tar and bread. Before the shutters there had been other houses, other towns, because Papa moved when the Regiment moved, and Mama said a home was not a place but a length of time, and you must love it while it lasted.

Sebastian remembered her best in the evenings, when the light came low and gold through the shutters and lay across the floor in bars, like something escaped from a cage. She would sit with her sewing in her lap and not sew it, and she would sing very quietly, songs without words, just sounds that rose and fell like breathing. Her hands smelled of soap and of the lavender she kept in a drawer, crushed a little every time she opened it. When he was frightened at night — and he was frightened often, though he could not have said why, only that the grown-ups' voices had changed after Papa put on his red coat again — she would hold his hand against her cheek and say, *There. Now you know where you live.*

He lived in her cheek. That was what he understood. Not the green shutters, not the towns. Her cheek, and the sound under her skin when she hummed.

Papa came like weather. He would be gone for weeks and then be there all at once, filling a doorway, too big and too bright, smelling of horse and leather and, underneath it, faintly, sweetly, of burning oil. Lamp oil. Sebastian never knew why an officer of the 12th should smell of lamp oil until later, much later, when knowing was already a wound. But then it was only the smell of Papa, and he loved it the way he loved thunder from far away: dangerous and grand and not yet touching him.

Papa lifted him high. Papa called him *little clerk* and let him sit on his knee with a real pen and a paper of his own, and they made marks together, Papa's quick and slanting, Sebastian's round and solemn, and Papa said, *One day yours will matter more than mine,* and laughed, and Mama laughed too, but her laugh had a crack down the middle of it that Sebastian heard and did not understand.

The last time, Papa stayed three days. Sebastian remembered the number because Mama counted them aloud on her fingers afterward, standing at the window, as if the days were coins she needed to account for. On the third night Papa knelt down to him in the hall, and his face did a thing faces were not supposed to do — it smiled above and grieved below, both at once, split like a coin rubbed thin.

*Little clerk,* he said. *If ever a man in a red coat asks you questions, you answer nothing. You are a stone. Stones don't remember. Can you be a stone?*

Sebastian nodded, because Papa's eyes were wet and nodding made the wetness stop.

*Good lad.* And then Papa held him so hard his ribs ached, and whispered into his hair words Sebastian did not catch — or caught and buried so deep that for months he could not find them, and then one night at the lighthouse they rose up out of him whole, like drowned things coming to the surface: *forget my face, forget my face.*

But stones don't obey. Sebastian remembered everything. That was his curse and, though he didn't know it yet, his inheritance.

* * *

The night the soldiers came, the moon was a fingernail paring over the roofs, and the dogs began first.

Not barking — whining, that low hurtful sound dogs make when the world is wrong. Then boots. Many boots, out of rhythm, not marching, which was worse, because Papa's men marched even to breakfast. These boots scattered and stopped and started, and fists hit the green shutters like hail.

Mama woke him without lighting a lamp. In the dark she moved like water, fast and silent, and she dressed him wrong — his shirt backwards, one stocking missing — and pressed a bundle into his arms wrapped in oilcloth that crinkled and shone even in the dark. The bundle was heavy and it breathed oil-smell, Papa's smell, and Sebastian clutched it to his chest because holding it was like holding a piece of Papa's coat.

*Listen to me.* Mama's voice was the voice she used for storms. *You will go out the back and down the lane and you will keep walking and you will not look back. Not once. Whatever you hear behind you, whatever—* Her voice broke off its sentence like a snapped rope. She knelt and took his face in her cold hands. *You walk to the sea. Do you hear me? To the light. A keeper lives by the light. Keepers are good people. Go to the light and give them this.* She tapped the bundle against his chest. *And forget your face. Forget your name if you must. Be a stone.*

*I'm not a stone,* Sebastian said, and hated how small his voice was, how young. *I'm Sebastian.*

Something crossed her face then that he spent the whole winter afterward trying to name, and finally named at the lighthouse one grey morning watching Mrs. Ashgrove trim a wick: it was pride. Fierce, broken-backed pride, the kind that stands up straightest when it is being cut down.

*No,* Mama said. *You are not a stone. God forgive me, I hoped you'd be a stone.* She kissed his forehead, hard, like sealing a letter. *Remember, then. Remember everything. That is why we send you. Remember for all of us who can't.*

Then the front door gave way with a sound like a bone breaking, and she pushed him out the back into the freezing dark, and the last thing Sebastian ever saw of her was her shadow thrown huge on the kitchen wall by someone else's lantern, arms spread wide across the doorway, wings of a bird that chose to stay in the burning house so the fledgling could fly.

He ran. He did not look back. He heard things behind him that he would carry forever, sounds his mind refused to make into pictures, and he walked all the next day and hid in a hayrick and walked again, following the sea because Mama had said the sea, and on the second night, half-dead with cold, he saw it: a white tower with a star on top, sweeping its arm across the dark like a giant counting sheep.

He climbed the path. His legs shook. The bundle was warm against his chest now, as if the oilcloth had learned his heartbeat. He meant to knock. He meant to say the words Mama taught him — *a keeper lives by the light, I've come to the keeper* — but his body had spent its last coins, and instead he folded himself down on the stone doorstep like a dog returning to a familiar door, and slept.

* * *

He woke to warmth.

That was the miracle he never got over, the thing that undid him every time he thought of it: he woke to warmth. A blanket, heavier than any blanket he'd known, smelling of wool and smoke. A fire ticking in a little stove. And a woman moving around the room with a lamp, doing quiet things — banking coals, setting a kettle on, folding his wet stockings over a chair back — and every movement soft, unhurried, as if the room were a church and she its only congregation.

She turned and saw him awake, and Sebastian braced himself, because grown-ups seeing him awake meant questions, and questions meant red coats eventually, and stones—

But she only said, "There's bread on the table, and butter, if you can manage it. No rush."

No rush. As if he had all the winters of the world to eat bread in.

He ate. She watched him sidelong, pretending to mend a sock, and she asked no questions, which was itself a kind of question, and he answered it by not answering, and she seemed satisfied, and this strange silent contract became their whole first month.

But here is the thing Sebastian learned about her, the thing he told no one because it was his own private hoard, his single treasure: she smelled of lamp oil.

Not strongly. Not the way Papa did, soaked in it, wearing it like cologne. It was deeper in her, worked into the grain of her like salt into a sailor's skin — in her hair when she bent near, in the folds of her sleeves, rising off her hands after she trimmed the great lamps. Lamp oil and hot brass and a whisper of smoke. And whenever she leaned over him — tucking the blanket, setting down soup, lifting his chin to look at a bruise — the smell wrapped round him, and for one dizzy, unbearable moment it was Papa bending over him, Papa's shadow falling across him, Papa saying *little clerk,* and Sebastian's chest cracked open like ice on a spring river.

She was not Papa. She was a woman with grey threading her dark hair and lines around her mouth that deepened when she thought no one watched. But she kept a light. She fed a flame every night so that ships full of somebodies' sons did not die. And she smelled of the same fire Papa carried on his coat.

Trust, for Sebastian, could not be spoken. Words were what soldiers used to trick you — *we only want to ask the boy a few questions* — words were what the note said, *forget the face*, as if a face were a coat you could hang behind a door. Trust had to be given the way you feed a wild bird: hand flat, still, patient, ready to be empty for a long time.

So one evening in the second week, when the storm had worn itself out and the lamp above turned its slow wheel and the house hummed with the vast breathing of the sea, Sebastian did the bravest thing he had done since the green shutters broke open.

He took her hand.

He took it as she passed his chair, this woman whose name he did not yet know, and he laid it against his own cheek the way Mama had taught him, years ago, in the barred gold light: *there, now you know where you live.* Her hand was rough with work and warm from the stove, and it smelled of lamp oil, and Sebastian closed his eyes and let the memory come all the way up, let it hurt, let it be true.

The woman went very still.

She did not pull away. That was the second miracle. She stood there like a tree letting a bird land, and after a long moment her thumb moved — once, twice — across the back of his hand, gentle as a tide, and when he opened his eyes hers were shining in the lamplight and she said, quietly, as though the words might frighten him:

"You're safe here, Sebastian."

His name. His true name, the one Mama sealed into him, the one he was supposed to have buried.

He stared at her. Fear rose in him like floodwater — she knows, she'll tell them, they'll come — and then it receded, because her face held no trick in it, only a kind of fierce sorrow, and she said:

"The note asked me to forget your face. I expect whoever wrote it wanted you forgotten." Her jaw set, and Sebastian saw, with a child's unerring clarity, that this woman could be stubborn the way granite is stubborn. "I'm not going to do what the note says. So we had better both decide what we're going to do instead."

* * *

That night he dreamed of the green shutters, and woke crying without noise, the way he had learned, tears running silently into his ears. And the woman — Casper, Mrs. Ashgrove, though he thought of her as *the keeper* — appeared at his cot side with the lamp, and sat on the floor beside him like a girl, and did not say *there, there*, which was a lie-words thing to say, and did not say *hush*, which was worse.

She said: "Do you want to talk about them? Your mama and papa?"

And Sebastian, six years old and older than the sea, found his voice.

"She told me to remember," he whispered. "Everybody keeps telling me to forget. Forget my face, forget the soldiers, be a stone. But she said remember. She said somebody has to." His breath hitched. "I remember the boots. I remember the dogs whining. I remember—" and here the words failed him, the pictures pressing up that his mind had refused all this time, and he shook his head hard, hard, hard.

Mrs. Ashgrove took his hand again and held it against her cheek.

"Then remember," she said. "When you're ready. All of it. And till then, you don't have to be a stone. Stones are for graves." She paused, and something moved across her face like cloud-shadow over water. "I know something about graves."

He fell asleep to the smell of lamp oil and the sound of the great lamp turning overhead, its beam sweeping out across the black water like a hand smoothing a bedsheet, over and over, patient, endless, keeping watch so that nobody need be lost.

And in the morning, Sebastian did something else. He fetched the oilcloth bundle from where it had lain untouched beneath his cot all these weeks, and carried it to the table where Mrs. Ashgrove sat with her mending, and set it down before her with both hands.

"This is Papa's," he said. "Mama said give it to the keeper. You're the keeper." He swallowed. "I think it's why the bad men want me. I think it's why Mama—"

He couldn't finish. He didn't need to. Mrs. Ashgrove looked at the bundle a long moment, and then at him, and her eyes had gone the grey of the sea before weather.

"Sebastian," she said slowly, "what exactly did your papa do?"

And before he could answer — before either of them knew that the question would change everything, would bring polite strangers with mainland accents to their door and fire to the headland and a colonel with a voice like oiled steel — the boy heard, far off across the water, the thin cry of a gull, and beneath it, fainter, another sound that made his blood turn cold as ballast stone:

A boat's horn. Two short blasts. Someone was coming to the island.
