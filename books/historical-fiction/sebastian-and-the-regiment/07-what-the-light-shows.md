## 7. What the Light Shows

The storm came out of the north-east on the third night of November, and it came like a debt collector.

Casper had felt it building all day — a pressure behind her ears, a strange copper taste in the wind, the gulls gone silent and inland as if they knew better than to argue with what was coming. By dusk the sea had turned the colour of wet slate, and by full dark it had stopped being a sea at all and become something else entirely: a moving country of black hills and white screaming valleys, hurling itself at the headland as though it meant to take the rock down to its bones.

She kept the light burning. That was the whole of her religion, and she performed it faithfully.

The lamp room shook around her at the worst of it, the glass panes flexing in their leads like drum skins, the great lens turning its slow patient circle above her head. She trimmed the wicks with hands that had learned steadiness from repetition if not from nature, checked the mercury bath, wiped salt spray from the lower panes with a rag that was more brine than cloth. Sebastian slept below in the cupboard bed she had made for him, wrapped in her husband's old watch coat, and before she'd climbed the stairs she had knelt beside him for a long moment without touching him, the way you might kneel beside a fire you were afraid of waking.

He still flinched from red coats. He still woke sometimes crying names she did not know — his mother's, she supposed, and others. But he slept now, most nights, and he ate what she gave him, and twice this week he had laughed — small startled sounds, as though laughter were something he'd been told he wasn't allowed anymore. Each time, Casper had felt something shift in her chest that she refused to name, because naming it would mean admitting how much there was left to lose.

*Forget the face*, the note had said. As if a face could be forgotten. As if hers had not spent two years trying and failing to forget one.

At half past two the storm reached its full voice, and Casper, standing at the lantern rail with spray hammering the glass above her like thrown gravel, saw the light fall across something that should not have been there.

A ship's boat. Or what had been a ship's boat.

It was tumbling in the troughs maybe two hundred yards off the point, upside down more often than not, flashing white where the surf broke over it. No sail. No oars. No soul visible aboard — but at that distance, through that chaos, no soul could be seen even if there was one clinging on. She watched it for three turns of the lens, marking its drift, doing the arithmetic of tide and wind that Tam Ashgrove had drilled into her across fifteen years of marriage until it lived in her spine rather than her mind. The current would take it along the eastern shore, past the seal rocks, into the long shingle bay below the cliff path.

If anything was alive on it, it would come ashore there. And nothing alive could survive that landing unaided.

She went down the stairs two at a time.

* * *

The oil room held the spare lamps, the cans, the wicks in their oiled paper, and the long boathook that Tam had kept sharpened for exactly this work. Casper took the hook, a lantern, and a length of rope, and stepped out into a night that tried immediately to take her apart.

The wind hit her like a door flung open. Rain came sideways, mixed with spray so thick it was less weather than substance, and she bent into it and hauled herself along the rock line hand over hand, the way she had done in storms before, the way Tam had taught her — *never stand up straight in a gale, Casp, the wind respects only the humble*. The shingle bay, when she reached it, was a horror of white water, stones grinding against stones beneath the surf with a sound like enormous teeth.

The boat came ashore at ten minutes past three.

She saw it coming in the sweep of her own lantern — a dark shape rising on a wave, riding the wave's shoulder all the way in, and then the sea picked it up and simply threw it, and it struck the shingle with a crack she felt through her boots. The next wave took it again and dragged it back, grinding; the one after drove it higher. On the third surge Casper lunged forward with the boathook as the water sucked back, caught the gunwale, and held on while the undertow pulled at her like a drowning thing's grip.

"Let go," she said aloud, absurdly, to the ocean, to the night, to whoever was listening. "You've had enough."

There was someone in the boat.

She found him wedged under the stern sheets when she got the hull turned enough to see inside — a man, young, in a boat cloak that had been grey and was now the universal black of drowned things. His face when she turned him toward the light was bloodless and calm, the calm particular to the dead, and there was weed in his hair and a bruise along one temple that might have been made by the sea or might have been made before it. He wore no uniform, but the boots told her everything: heavy, well-made, government issue, the kind of boots the Army bought by the thousand. And stitched inside the collar of the cloak, in thread gone dark but legible, a number.

Twelve.

Her breath went out of her in a cloud and the wind took it. The 12th Regiment. The same number that stood in ciphered fragments in Tam's old signal book upstairs. The same number Sebastian flinched away from whenever Marta's boys came by in their father's old scarlet fishing smocks.

For a long moment she knelt there in the wash, holding the dead man's shoulder, rain streaming down the back of her collar, and felt the night rearrange itself around her. This was not an accident washing ashore. This was a message. The sea did not send things to her door by chance; Tam used to say the sea kept accounts, and here was another entry in them, delivered to *her* ledger now.

She searched him quickly, roughly, apologising under her breath to him the whole time — *forgive me, forgive me, I'm sorry* — because the dead cannot object and the living are running out of night. In the inner pocket of the cloak, dry as bone, protected by oilskin the way the packet at her doorstep had been protected, she found dispatches.

Three of them. Sealed, official, stamped. And a fourth thing: a leather wallet, and inside it papers in a fair military hand, and one page that stopped her heart — a copy of sailing orders annotated in pencil, and beneath them, in different ink, a note in the same hand she now knew as well as her own:

*Vane has seen the first drafts. Corrections requested before the mail leaves. Nothing is to reach London in its present form.*

Nothing is to reach London. Present form. As though the truth were a garment to be altered for fit.

The waves broke over her shoulders. She hardly felt them.

She put everything back except the papers. Those she stripped from the oilskin, rolled into a cylinder, and pushed down inside the front of her dress, against her skin, where her heartbeat seemed suddenly very loud. Then she closed the man's cloak over his chest with something like ceremony, said the few words she could manage — *God keep you, soldier, you're out of their hands now* — and marked the place with her lantern so she could find him at dawn and give him over to the parish and the coroner properly. There were rules for the dead. Rules were all that stood between people and animals, some days.

Then she climbed back up the cliff path with the wind trying to peel her off the rock at every step, and she did not let it.

* * *

Sebastian was awake when she came in, sitting up in the cupboard bed with his eyes huge in the lamplight, and she saw that he had heard the boat strike — or felt it, the way children feel things through floors and walls that adults have to be told.

"There was a shipwreck," she said. "A boat. One man. He didn't live."

Sebastian looked at her for a long moment. "Was he one of Papa's men?"

The question landed like a stone dropped down a well; she listened for it to hit bottom and never quite heard it. "I don't know," she said, which was true. "Maybe."

"They sent a man once," Sebastian said, in that flat careful voice he used for the worst memories, the voice of a child reciting something learned by heart against his will. "To our house. Papa burned papers in the stove and the man drank wine and smiled, and after Papa went away Mama cried and burned letters too." His hands worked in the blanket. "Papa said if ever anything came to me in oilskins, it was true, whatever it was. Even if it was terrible." He looked up at her. "Is what you found terrible?"

Casper sat down on the edge of the bed. The dispatches pressed cold and damp-feeling against her ribs, though they were perfectly dry.

"Yes," she said. "I think it is."

He nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that was somehow the worst of it — a child of eight nodding at the news that the world was terrible, as though he had completed that lesson long ago and was only waiting for the grown-ups to catch up.

"Will you tell it to somebody?" he asked. "So it counts?"

"So it counts," she repeated.

"Papa said things only count if somebody writes them down. Otherwise they just..." He searched for the word. "*Stop.* Like being asleep forever."

She tucked him back down. She sat until his breathing slowed. And then she carried the lantern down to the oil room, because it was the one room in the lighthouse no visitor entered — too dangerous, too foul-smelling, too full of the machinery of the light itself — and because, God forgive her, she already understood that she was becoming a keeper of a second kind of fuel.

* * *

The oil room at night was a warm dim cave smelling of rape oil and metal and the sweet burnt ghost of a thousand trimmed wicks. Casper lit the small shielded lamp she kept there, set the dispatches on the clean corner of the wick bench, and read them by its low flame with her sleeves rolled up as though preparing for work — which, she understood with a strange calm, she was.

Two were routine on their face: requisitions, a movement order, the kind of paper any regiment produced by the ream. The third was not routine. It was a draft — a draft of the official account of the action at a place whose name made her go very still, because the name appeared in the deciphered fragments upstairs, in Tam's cipher, in a dead officer's confession. The draft was dated *before* the date given in the cipher for the massacre itself.

Written before it happened. The history composed in advance, like a playbill printed before the performance. All it lacked were the casualties, and those, she saw, had been entered in a later hand, in different ink — tidied, rounded, reduced. Villages became "hostile encampments." Civilians became "irregulars." A massacre became "an engagement resulting in the restoration of order."

And at the foot of the draft, the signature she had seen once before in the officer's confession, rendered in cipher: Vane. Not signed as author. Signed as *approving authority*. A colonel approving an account of an event that, according to every honest clock, had not yet occurred.

Casper set the pages down on the bench. Her hands were steady. She noted that fact about herself with distant interest, the way one notes weather.

Tam had been ruined by a court-martial that had decided its conclusions before its first witness spoke. She had spent two years telling herself it was a private grief, a small injustice, the sort of thing that happened to small people and belonged to them alone. Now the same handwriting — not the same hand, but the same *habit*, the same imperial reflex, the same men writing history the way they wrote requisitions — lay drying on her wick bench, and it was not small, and it did not belong to her alone. It belonged to a boy asleep overhead who flinched at red coats. It belonged to villagers whose names she would never know, on a coast whose sand she would never walk. It belonged to everyone who would read the tidy version and believe it, because the tidy version would arrive in London on a fast ship with seals and signatures and the whole weight of the Empire behind it.

Unless something else arrived first.

She thought of the drowned courier on the shingle, his calm bloodless face. Someone had wanted these papers corrected before the mail sailed. Someone had sent a man to make sure. And the sea — the sea that keeps accounts — had sent the man instead to a widow's rock, with the evidence buttoned inside his coat like a letter addressed to no one, waiting two hundred yards off her light for three turns of a lens.

Evidence accumulating. She had felt it happening for weeks the way one feels a tide turn — the packet, the cipher, the fragments naming decorated heroes, and now this. Each piece alone deniable. Together, a weight. And weight, she thought, pulling out the little deal box where Tam had kept his spare spectacles and his signal code, lining the bottom with oilcloth, laying the dispatches flat — weight asks a question of whatever carries it. Either it presses you down into the ground, or it becomes ballast, and ballast is what lets a ship stand up straight in weather.

She was not going to be pressed into the ground.

She hid the box behind the spare lamp crates, in the shadow of the great red storage tank where no searching hand would bother to reach, and then sat for a moment longer with her palms flat on the bench, breathing the smell of the oil that fed the flame that kept ships off these rocks. Two kinds of keeping, she thought. The light warned people away from death. What she held now, if she was brave enough and clever enough and lucky enough, might warn them away from a lie.

Above her, faintly, she heard the floorboards creak — small feet, going to the window. Checking the light. Already learning the habit of it.

And below, far off toward the mainland road, carried thin on a lull in the gale, a sound she had been dreading without knowing it until this moment: hoofbeats. More than one horse. Moving fast, in this weather, at this hour, along a coast where no honest traveller rode by night.

Casper pinched out the little lamp and stood in the dark of the oil room, listening, with the evidence cold against the wall behind her and her heart going like a hammer on a drumhead — and understood that the tide of this business had turned, and it was bringing everything ashore now, all at once.
