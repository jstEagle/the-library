## 9. Neighbors and Nets

The winter had a way of teaching you who your neighbors were.

Casper had lived at the light for eleven years, first as the keeper's wife, then as the keeper, and in all that time she had learned the village below the headland the way one learns the surface of the sea — familiar, changeable, and not to be trusted too far from shore. The fishing families came up to the light on feast days and when their boats needed a bearing; otherwise they kept to their nets, their kelp-burning, their small economies of herring and debt. She traded eggs for mackerel with the widow Marta Quill and spoke three sentences to her a week. It was enough. It had been enough for years. Solitude was not loneliness, Casper told herself; solitude was a discipline, like winding the clockwork, like trimming the wick. You did it daily until it became the shape of your life.

Sebastian undid all of it in six weeks.

The boy could not be hidden the way papers were hidden, folded into oilcloth and pressed beneath the floor of the lamp room. He was seven years old and made of movement. He wanted to know what everything was — the fog bell's mechanism, the way gulls hung on the wind without seeming to try, why the light turned and turned and never got dizzy. He asked questions with his whole body, leaning into them, and when he was frightened he went silent as a stone, but those silences were coming less often now. He no longer flinched when Casper moved suddenly. He had stopped checking the door each morning to see if the sea had given anything back.

And he talked. Not much — never much — but in fragments, over bread or while she taught him his letters by chalk on the slate floor: *my father smelled of this*, holding up the lamp oil; *Marta has a dog with one eye*; *the soldiers wore red and their boots were loud*. That last one, said once, flatly, over supper, had kept Casper awake half the night. The boy remembered more than he let out. She understood that instinct intimately. She had been doing the same thing since her husband drowned.

It was Sebastian who broke the boundary between the lighthouse and the village, because a child cannot be kept behind glass like a lens. On a raw Tuesday in November, when the wind came off the water with teeth in it, Casper took him down the cliff path to Marta's cottage to trade for fish, and Marta looked at the boy, then at Casper, and said nothing at all with an eloquence that frightened her.

"You've heard something," Casper said.

Marta was a broad woman gone soft at the edges, with hands like knotted rope and a face weathered past guessing at age. Her cottage smelled of tar and haddock and woodsmoke. She poured tea without being asked, which was itself an answer, and set the cup down hard enough to slop.

"Two men came through Thursday," she said. "Gentlemen, they called themselves, though gentlemen don't wear boots that new in a fishing village. Asking after a woman and child come off the mainland. Said there'd been a death in the family and the boy was wanted home." She snorted. "Wanted home. Aye. Like a herring wants the smokehouse."

"What did you tell them?"

"Told them I keep my nose on my own nets." Marta looked at Sebastian, who had gone very still by the fire, the way he did, small and watchful as a wren. "They went up your hill after. I watched them from the window. They stood at your gate a long while."

"They knocked. I sent them away."

"And they'll come back," Marta said, "because men like that always come back, and next time it won't be asking." She refilled Casper's cup, unasked again, and her voice dropped to something quieter and older than gossip. "My Tam was pressed aboard a naval tender thirty years back. Never came home. I know what it is when powerful men decide a person belongs to them." She looked at the boy again, and her craggy face did something complicated and tender. "He'll be safe here when you need him to be. My house is the last one before the harbor wall. Nobody watches the poor. That's the one mercy of poverty — we're invisible."

Casper felt something crack open in her chest that she had spent eleven years mortaring shut. "You don't know what you're offering."

"I know exactly what I'm offering. Fish stew and a bed behind the curtain, and my grandsons' fists if it comes to fists." Marta pushed the plate of bread across the table toward Sebastian. "Eat, lad. You're thin as a gull's leg."

The boy looked at Casper — asked permission with his eyes, still, even now — and Casper nodded, and that was how it began.

* * *

After that, the village arranged itself around the secret the way water arranges itself around a stone, quietly and without any single decision anyone could point to.

Marta's grandsons came up the cliff path twice a week with fish and flour, ostensibly delivering supplies, actually serving as lookouts and couriers. Tom, fourteen, with his father's squint and his grandmother's stubbornness, could spot a strange sail an hour before anyone else and read its rigging like handwriting. His brother Jem, twelve, was small enough and quick enough to be anywhere along the coast path without seeming to arrive. They invented a system within a fortnight, entirely on their own: a white rag on Marta's drying line meant strangers about; a red rag meant stay indoors. When Casper asked where they'd learned such a thing, Tom shrugged and said, "Smugglers used it, gran says, before our time. Coast's always had its signals. Your husband knew them all, they say."

Her husband. Even dead nine years, even drowned and salted into legend, Nils Ashgrove reached out of his grave to touch everything she did. The signal-code in the logbook, the ciphered confession, and now this — the rag on the line, the language of flags that the whole coast apparently spoke in its bones. History didn't belong to other people, she was beginning to suspect. It belonged to whoever wrote it down, and everyone here had been writing it down in their own way for generations, in rags and knots and songs, because nobody official ever bothered to ask them.

The old men were worse — better — than the boys. Weathered Silas Crane, who had fished these waters for fifty years and claimed rheumatism in every joint but his tongue, appointed himself the boy's tutor in things Casper could not teach: how to gut a fish, how to read a squall, how to spit properly off the harbor wall. He came limping up the path with a rod over his shoulder and Sebastian beside him, the boy talking — actually talking, words spilling out of him like water finding a channel — and Casper would stand in the doorway of the lighthouse watching them come and feel her throat tighten at a sight she hadn't known she'd been starving for.

"He asks good questions," Silas said gruffly, the first time, handing her two cod as payment for nothing. "Better than his elders. You're doing right by him, missus."

"I'm keeping him alive," Casper said.

"Aye. Same thing mostly." And he touched his cap and went, and that was all the ceremony the village required.

She hated how exposed it made them all. Every kindness was a thread someone could pull. If the inspectors came back and started asking the fishermen instead of knocking at doors, the whole web would show itself — and webs, Casper knew, were visible precisely when they held weight. She tried to warn them. She stood in Marta's kitchen one evening and said, "You should all think carefully. What I'm mixed in could ruin a village. It's already ruined men with rank and money. There are people who burned evidence once and won't hesitate to burn witnesses."

The room went quiet around the peat fire. Marta's daughter-in-law had her arms crossed; the grandsons sat on the floor pretending not to listen, which children always do best when listening hardest. Finally old Silas, in the corner, said:

"Vane." He said the name like spitting a bone. "Colonel Vane. That's the name under all this, ain't it? We heard things. Sailors hear things in port taverns — stories from the colonies, places with names we can't spell. Villages burned. Women and children. And medals handed out after, big ones, dinner-party medals." He looked at Casper with eyes gone pale and clear as blown glass. "You think we don't know what empire does? We live on the edge of it, missus. We're what's left when it's done feeding. That boy is one of ours now. Tell us what you need."

Casper had prepared speeches. She had none left. "I need to know if anyone lands," she said finally. "Before they reach the headland. An hour's warning. That's all."

"An hour," Marta said, and smiled with all the warmth of a winter sea, "you shall have two."

* * *

They came on a Thursday, near dusk, in the grey hour when the light was changing shift from day to night and every shadow doubled itself.

Tom came pelting up the cliff path with the red rag still tied round his arm where he'd grabbed it off the line rather than waste time tying it proper. "Boat," he gasped. "Longboat, oars, six men maybe, navy pattern, coming round the point. Gran says take him now."

There was no time for cleverness. There was barely time for fear. Casper wrapped Sebastian in her dead husband's boat cloak — the boy swam in it, sleeves to his fingertips — and walked him down the cliff path at a pace carefully calibrated between urgency and innocence, past cottages where doors stood open and women stood in them, watching, saying nothing, a whole village performing unconcern like a choir. At Marta's door the boy turned and gripped Casper's hand hard.

"You'll light it tonight," he said. It wasn't a question. The light was their covenant; whatever happened below, the flame would go up at dusk, and he knew it, and somehow it steadied him more than any promise about tomorrow.

"I'll light it," Casper said. "First thing, last thing. Always."

Then Marta drew the boy inside, pulled the curtain across the alcove bed, set a one-eyed dog down in front of it like a sentry, and turned to Casper with her hands on her hips. "Now walk home slow, missus, and let them find you alone and boring. Can you be boring?"

"I've had practice."

The longboat grounded on the shingle as the first stars showed. Casper climbed her hill and lit her lamp and put herself in the doorway like a woman with nothing in the world to do, and the six men came up the path with a lantern of their own, and their leader was polite and tired and not Inspector Hale, which meant it was the other one — the cruel one, the one whose smile never reached anything above his mouth.

"Mistress Ashgrove. Out for evening air?"

"The air doesn't give me much choice. What brings the Navy ashore?"

"Routine inquiry. Missing persons. We're searching the village, cottage by cottage, very thorough, very tedious." His eyes went past her shoulder into the lighthouse, hungry. "We'll want to see inside, of course."

"Will you." Casper leaned against the doorframe and discovered, to her horror and fascination, that she was enjoying this — that some deep-buried part of her, the part that had watched her husband be destroyed by men exactly like this one and had said nothing, was awake now and speaking through her mouth. "Then you'll want a warrant, because this is a Crown station, inspector, and the light burns whether you like it or not, and I am its keeper by appointment of Trinity House, and you may search my lamp room with a writ in your hand or you may climb back in your boat. Those are the choices. The tide won't wait for either."

She saw him weigh it — see the calculation move behind his eyes like something swimming under ice. Searching a Crown lighthouse without authority was a paper trail; paper trails could be followed; and whatever else these men were, they were careful. Careful men hated leaving fingerprints.

"We'll return with proper authorization," he said.

"Do bring tea. I get so few visitors."

They went. She watched the lantern bob down the cliff path and along the row of cottages, and she saw — she would swear it to her grave — the searchers met at every door by open faces and empty hands. Old Silas sat mending a net that did not need mending, so absorbed in his work he could barely spare them a glance. Marta stood in her doorway with a baby on her hip — borrowed from a neighbor for the occasion, Casper later learned — rocking it and answering questions with the vast patient stupidity of a woman who had never had an interesting thought in her life. Behind the curtain, a one-eyed dog slept across a sleeping boy's feet, and the baby cried on cue, and the searchers, drowning in tedium, gave up the alcove after ninety seconds.

An hour and a half. Marta had promised two. Casper counted it as close enough.

When the longboat pulled away, the village came out onto the shingle and stood in the dark watching it go, forty people in silence, and nobody cheered, because cheering wasn't the style of people who lived by the sea. But Tom spat elaborately into the water, and Marta laughed once, low, and the sound carried up the cliff to where Casper stood at her door, and it was the warmest sound she had heard in nine years.

* * *

That night, after the boy came home — running up the path ahead of her, chattering about the dog, about Jem's trick with the conger eel, about Silas promising to teach him knots — Casper sat him down with bread and milk and looked at his flushed face and felt the full weight of what the village had done settle onto her.

"You understand," she said carefully, "that those people risked something today. For you. People they'd never met before this winter."

Sebastian considered this with the gravity he brought to anything that mattered. "Gran Marta says it's not risking when it's right." He tore his bread. "She says her Tam would have done it. Was Tam your husband?"

"No. Tam was her son. Lost at sea before you were born."

"Oh." The boy chewed. "Everyone's lost somebody to the sea here."

"Yes," Casper said. "Everyone." And thought: *and some of us lost them to men, and dressed it up as the sea.*

"Did you lose somebody?" he asked, direct as children are, and she could have deflected — she had a lifetime's practice at deflection — but the fire was warm and the boy was looking at her with his mother's eyes, whoever his mother had been, eyes that deserved truth.

"My husband. Nine years ago. He was the keeper here before me."

"Was he good at it?"

"He was the best I ever saw. And they treated him badly, near the end. Men in uniforms, with papers. They said he'd done wrong when he hadn't." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "I didn't fight them. I was afraid, and I was alone, and I let it happen. I've regretted it every day since."

Sebastian put down his bread. He reached across the table and put his small hand on hers, the gesture so adult, so deliberate, that her breath caught.

"You're not letting it happen now," he said.

"No," Casper managed. "I'm not."

Later, when he was asleep, she went up to the lamp room and stood a while in the turning dark, watching the beam sweep the water, sweep the village, sweep the water again. Below, lights were going out in the cottages one by one, the ordinary goodnight of ordinary people, except that nothing about them was ordinary anymore. Somewhere out in that darkness were ships carrying orders signed by decorated men. Somewhere beyond the horizon was a parliament that did not yet know what she knew. And here, in between, was a village of fishermen and widows and boys with sharp eyes, holding a secret together like a net holds water — imperfectly, temporarily, together.

She had believed history happened elsewhere, to other people, recorded by other hands. She had built a small life on that belief the way you build a breakwater, stone by stone, against a sea you cannot stop.

The stones were shifting. She could feel the tide coming for all of it.

Three days later, Tom came up the path at a dead run, white-faced, and stood in her doorway trying to catch his breath, and when he finally got the words out, they were the last words she wanted:

"Missus — there's a wreck on the rocks below the point. And there's a man washed up with it." He swallowed. "And he's wearing regiment red."
