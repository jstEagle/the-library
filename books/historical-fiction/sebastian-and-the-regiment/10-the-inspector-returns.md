## 10. The Inspector Returns

The storm came back in the last week of January, as though it had been waiting offshore all month, biding its time like a creditor. It arrived without the theatrics of the autumn gales — no screaming wind, no mountainous seas — just a low grey pressure that settled over the water and did not lift, and a swell that ran long and heavy beneath it, like something breathing under a blanket. The kind of weather, Casper thought, that made men careful. And made them come ashore.

She saw the boat first.

It was mid-morning, the light burning low but lit against the murk, and she was on the gallery with her glass when she caught the movement: a longboat pulling out from beyond the point where the fishing boats never went this season, rowed by four men in plain dark clothing who rowed the way soldiers march — together, without waste, without looking at anything but the stroke. Behind them, half-hidden in the haze, sat a larger shape. A navy cutter, or something dressed as one, riding at anchor where no honest vessel had reason to ride.

Casper lowered the glass slowly. Her hands were steady. She noted that about herself, the way she noted everything now — the way one notes the behaviour of a stranger in the house, someone whose habits must be learned before they can be trusted. *My hands are steady. Good.* Three months ago she would not have needed steadiness. Three months ago she had been only a keeper's widow, and history had happened elsewhere, to other people, in rooms she would never enter.

Now there was a boy asleep in the lamp room, curled on the old settee under her husband's boat cloak, and ciphered pages hidden inside the hollow base of the paraffin reservoir, and a name — *Vane* — written in a dead man's hand across her conscience like a brand.

She went down to meet them.

* * *

They landed at the shingle below the tower, the four oarsmen hauling the boat up beyond the tideline with an efficiency that told her everything their plain clothes tried to hide. Two of them remained with the boat. Two came toward the lighthouse across the shingle, boots crunching, and even before she could see their faces clearly she knew which of them was Hale, because he walked differently from his companion — slower, his head turning to take in the headland, the boathouse, the washing line where Marta's daughter had hung sheets two days past. The other man walked as if the landscape were beneath his notice and he merely crossed it.

She had met them both in November, when they had come asking after a missing woman and child with the smooth courtesy of men reading from a script. The tall one, Mr. Quill, had done the talking then, smiling with teeth that never reached his eyes, calling her *madam* in a voice like oiled hinges while his gaze crawled over every corner of her kitchen. Hale had stood by the door and said almost nothing, and when Quill had asked to search the tower, it was Hale who had said quietly, "We've wasted enough of Mrs. Ashgrove's morning," and taken his partner out into the rain.

She remembered thinking then: *one of these men is dangerous, and the other is worse, because I cannot tell what he is.*

She opened the door before they knocked. Better, she had learned, to be the one who opens doors.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." Hale removed his hat. Rain dripped from its brim onto the stone step. He was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that had been ordinary once and had grown less so with use — lines cut deep around the mouth, eyes the colour of slate in winter water. "Forgive us twice in one winter."

"Twice is generous counting, Mr. Hale. It's three times. Your colleague called again in December while you were away." She let that sit between them. Let him know she counted. "You'll want to come in out of it, I suppose. Whether I want you to or not."

"I'd take it kindly," he said.

Quill's absence she marked aloud, watching for it: "Your friend isn't with you?"

"Mr. Quill is detained on the mainland." Something moved behind the flatness of his voice — not quite satisfaction, not quite apology. "Official business."

*Detained.* She turned the word over as she led them into the kitchen. Detained by whom, and for what? She gave him the chair nearest the fire because refusing hospitality was a language the mainland read as confession, and she had spent three months learning to speak fluently in the grammar of innocence. She put the kettle on. Her hands performed the whole pantomime of a lonely woman glad of company, and she hated how easily the performance came now, how little distance lay between the lie and the truth of her own face.

Sebastian was in the lamp room. He knew to go there when boats came; they had drilled it like fire practice until the boy climbed those stairs in his sleep. But knowing it did nothing for the cold knot the knowledge left in her stomach — a child alone above her head, thirty feet of iron stairway between him and whatever these men were.

"You'll be wondering why we're here," Hale said.

"I stopped wondering why men come here, Mr. Hale. I've concluded it's always the same reason. Something on the mainland wants something from an island that has nothing."

He accepted the tea she poured and held it without drinking, warming his hands on the cup. Up close, in the firelight, she could see he had not slept well. There was a rawness about him, the look of a man who has been arguing with himself for weeks and losing.

"The woman we asked after in November," he said. "Ellen Vasse. The child with her. We found her."

Casper kept her face still. Somewhere above her, floorboards creaked faintly in the wind — or in a boy's restless pacing; she could not tell which, and prayed to no one in particular it was the wind.

"Found her," Casper said.

"At the bottom of the Thames, in December. Weighted." He said it the way a man reports weather. Then, quieter: "I'm telling you because I think you deserve to know what manner of people you're lying to, Mrs. Ashgrove. And because you've been lying well enough that I suspect you already know most of what I've come to say."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the swell broke on the shingle with a sound like slow applause.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," he agreed mildly. "Of course you don't."

* * *

He set the cup down and looked into the fire for a long moment, and when he spoke again it was in a different register — lower, stripped of the inspector's polish, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge.

"In thirty years' service I have buried four informants, closed two investigations I was ordered to close, and watched one colonel of the 12th Regiment receive a decoration for a campaign during which, according to every dispatch that passed through my office, nothing whatsoever happened at a place called Kessel Ford." His eyes came up and fixed on hers. "Nothing happened there, Mrs. Ashgrove, except that three hundred villagers ceased to exist, and the official account was written — signed, sealed, and dispatched — eleven days *before* the date on which nothing happened."

The words fell into the room like stones into a well. She heard them land, one after another, deeper than she had expected. Not because the facts were new — she had read them in the officer's ciphered pages, traced them in her dead husband's signal-code, line by terrible line — but because they were coming from *him*. From the empire's own mouth. From a man whose salary depended on the nothing that had happened.

"You're confessing," she said carefully.

"I'm testing," he corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference matters to both of us." He leaned forward. "Colonel Ambrose Vane is not my superior, Mrs. Ashgrove. He is my *subject*. Has been since Kessel Ford. Every report I have ever filed on the 12th Regiment has gone into a drawer in Whitehall that no one opens, because the man who holds the key to that drawer dines with the Secretary at War. I have spent six years collecting proof of what Vane did, and six years discovering that proof is worthless unless it reaches someone the drawer-key holder cannot reach. Do you follow me?"

"I follow that you're either the most honest man who's ever sat at my table or the best liar I've ever met, and I can't tell which, and neither can you entirely."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might once have lived. "That's fair."

"So make your offer, Mr. Hale. You didn't row through that sea to exchange confessions."

He drew a breath. Outside, the wind had begun to rise properly at last, moaning in the ventilators, and the whole tower hummed faintly with it, like a struck string.

"The packet," he said. "The papers the courier carried — the ones that washed ashore in December with the poor devil wearing them. My colleagues believe they were destroyed in the wreck, and I have allowed them to go on believing it, at some professional risk. If they exist — and I am now nearly certain they exist, because you are the only person on this coast who could have taken them from that body, and because you have not slept properly since Christmas — then they are worth more than either of us can calculate. They contain the order. Vane's own hand, countersigned. The thing I have hunted for six years."

"And in exchange?"

"Protection. Real protection, not the paper kind." His voice hardened. "A safe house in Edinburgh. Passage for the boy to relations who will claim him. An end to the searches, the boats, the men walking your headland at night. You hand over the documents, Mrs. Ashgrove, and by spring this island goes back to being the end of the world instead of the middle of a war."

"And the boy stays here."

"That would be arranged—"

"*Does the boy stay here.*"

The question came out harder than she intended, edged with something she recognized as it left her — not anger exactly, but its older sibling, the thing grief leaves behind when it stops being soft. Hale held her gaze and did not flinch, and to his credit he did not pretend to misunderstand.

"No," he said quietly. "Not immediately. The aunt in Edinburgh — and I believe she's genuine, Mrs. Ashgrove, I've made enquiries — would take custody within the month. That is the arrangement that protects everyone."

"Protects everyone," Casper repeated. She rose and stood at the window, her back to him, watching the grey sea work itself into ridges under the darkening sky. Below the window, the shingle gleamed wet where the boat had landed. *Everyone,* she thought. *As if the word could hold a seven-year-old who wakes screaming at red coats. As if the word had ever held anything.*

"Let me tell you what I know about protection, Mr. Hale," she said, without turning. "My husband was protected. By the Admiralty, by his commission, by twenty-two years of service and a court that wore wigs and spoke in Latin. When they needed a scapegoat for a wreck caused by rotten chain supplied by a contractor who dined with the same sort of men who dine with Colonel Vane, his protection weighed precisely as much as the paper it was printed on, and he died believing himself disgraced, and I spent nine years keeping his light because it was the only honour anyone would let me give him." She turned. "So you'll forgive me if I decline to trade a living child for a promise stamped by the same office that drowned him."

"The papers in your possession—"

"Are mine to keep. As is the boy. Both came to my door, and both stay under my roof, and if that makes me a smuggler of the truth, then so be it. I've kept harder things than documents alive in weather like this."

Hale sat very still. The fire cracked. Somewhere far overhead, faint through the iron floors, there came a small sound — the scrape of a chair, quickly stilled — and Casper saw Hale hear it too. Saw him choose, deliberately, visibly, to look back into the fire and pretend he hadn't.

"You know he's up there," she said.

"I know the wind makes old towers creak."

"Mr. Hale."

"Mrs. Ashgrove." He looked up at her then, and for the first time she saw the whole of him plainly — the tiredness, the divided man, the conscience worn smooth as beach glass from years of tumbling between duty and knowledge. "If I walk out of here and report that the packet exists, men will come who do not drink tea. Quill's replacement will not be Quill. I am offering you the only exit I can build, and you're refusing it because it doesn't save everyone, and I understand that refusal better than you know. But understand this in return: I cannot protect what I cannot control. Right now I control nothing here. You have made yourself unprotectable, and brave, and those two qualities together get people hanged."

"Then hang with me or get out of my kitchen, Inspector, but do not ask me again to weigh a boy against a bargain."

The silence stretched. Rain lashed the glass. And then Hale did something she had not expected: he took from his breast pocket a folded paper and laid it on the table between them, face down, and pushed it halfway across with two fingers.

"Sailing schedules," he said. "Mail packets out of Wexmouth for the next fortnight, with cargo manifests. Read it, burn it, do what you like. I was never here." He stood, took up his hat, and paused at the door with his back to her. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much, coming from me — the officer who wrote those pages trusted your husband completely. Enough to build his cipher on a dead signalman's code. Men like that choose keepers, Mrs. Ashgrove. Not saviours. Keepers." He settled the hat on his head. "Keep what you're keeping. God help us both, I think you may be right to."

And then he was gone, down into the rain, his boots receding across the shingle, and the longboat pulled away toward the waiting cutter with four men rowing as soldiers march.

* * *

She waited until the boat was a smudge in the murk before she climbed the stairs.

Sebastian was sitting on the settee with the boat cloak around his shoulders, white-faced, his hands knotted in the wool. He had heard everything — of course he had; sound fell down the stairwell of that tower like water. He didn't cry. He had stopped crying in December, which frightened her more than tears ever had.

"He wanted to give me away," the boy said. It wasn't a question.

"He wanted to buy papers with your future. I told him the shop was closed."

Sebastian considered this with the grave, unhurried attention he gave to everything, as though the world were a puzzle whose pieces he was still cataloguing. Then: "My papa said a man named Hale once sent him a warning. Before the soldiers came to the house. A note with no name on it." His eyes lifted to hers. "Do you think it was the same one?"

Casper knelt and took the small cold hands in hers, and thought of the folded schedule lying downstairs on her table, and of a tired man rowing away through grey water toward a war he seemed to be fighting on both sides of.

"Yes," she said. "I think it was the same one. Which means somewhere in all this, Sebastian, there's one man on their side who keeps choosing wrong and meaning right. We'll want to remember that. Days are coming when we'll need to know who to trust, and I suspect that list is shorter than either of us would like."

Below them, through the tower's bones, the storm finally arrived in earnest, and the light turned and turned above the darkening sea. On the table downstairs, the sailing schedule lay where Hale had left it, and Casper had not yet read it — and would not read it until midnight, by lamplight, with the boy asleep — but she already felt its weight in the house, the way one feels a letter from a solicitor unopened on the hall table.

Because folded inside the schedule, she would find it later: a single line in a clerk's precise hand, circled twice.

*Wexmouth mail packet — sails with the morning tide, 14th February — carrying official correspondence, War Office sealed.*

Fourteen days. And somewhere on the mainland, a colonel with a decoration on his breast was writing the ending of the story before it happened — unless a keeper and a boy and a cipher got there first.
