## 11. Fire on the Headland

The smell woke me before the shouting did.

I had learned every smell of that coast in the months since I came to Casper Ashgrove's door — salt and tar, lamp oil and wet wool, the green rot of weed at low tide, the smoke from Marta's peat fire drifting across the water when the wind turned east. I slept by smells now, the way other boys might sleep by sounds. And this one was wrong. This was burning, but not hearth-burning, not the sweet dry crackle of driftwood. It was thick and black and hungry, and underneath it ran something sharper: pitch. Burning pitch.

I was out of bed and at the window before my eyes had finished opening. The night beyond the glass was not night anymore. The headland to the south, where the boathouse stood with its upturned boats like a row of sleeping beetles, was lit orange from within. Flames stood up out of its roof and leaned hard to the west under the wind, and the smoke went off across the sky like a banner torn loose and flying.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." My voice came out small. I hated it for being small. "Mrs. Ashgrove, the boathouse."

She was already moving. I heard her bare feet on the boards, then her boots going on without lacing properly, then she was beside me at the window with her hand on my shoulder, and I felt her go still in a way I had come to know. It was not fear exactly. It was the stillness of someone measuring a thing, the way she measured the weather off the south window each morning, deciding what the day would be allowed to do.

"They'll have gone," she said quietly. "Whoever set it. They light it and walk away so the fire does the telling." She turned from the window. "Boots, boy. Coat. We're needed down there."

* * *

I want to write down honestly what I felt running along the cliff path with her lantern swinging wild between us, because later, when people called me brave, I could never make them understand that bravery is mostly made after. In the moment there was only terror with a strange cold center in it, like a stone dropped through warm water. Terror, and one thought turning over and over like a fish on a line: *they did this because of you.* They burned a building on this quiet headland because a red-coated man in London or wherever such men stood their watch had decided that a boy asleep in a lighthouse was a thing the world must be rid of. Forget the face, the note had said. The face would not be forgotten. It was being hunted by fire now.

And beneath the terror, shamefully, another feeling I did not name for years: a kind of terrible belonging. Until that night the danger had been a private thing between Mrs. Ashgrove and me, spoken low over ciphered pages, shut away like medicine in a cupboard. Now it had come outside. It had touched the village. Everyone on that beach would know, when they saw the flames, that something was aimed at the lighthouse keeper and the child she sheltered. I had brought the war to them. And they were all coming toward it anyway.

The boathouse was lost already when we arrived. That much everyone could see. Old Tam Beale's boats were inside it — two of them, tarred and sound, worth more than he'd see in five years of nets — and the fire had got into the pitch seams and was eating them with a crackling like laughter. Men stood in a ragged line passing buckets from the sea's edge, but it was ceremony more than effort; nobody believed the buckets mattered. They were there so that Tam Beale could look back afterward and say his neighbors had stood with him against it. That was the real cargo in those buckets.

Marta found us almost at once. Her hair was loose and her hands were black with soot and she pulled me against her chest hard enough to hurt, then held me at arm's length and looked me over as if counting my parts.

"He's all right?" she demanded of Mrs. Ashgrove. "They didn't touch the tower?"

"The tower's untouched. He's untouched." Then, lower, in the voice she used only for truth: "This is for us, Marta. Not for Tam. Tam is the envelope."

"I know what it is." Marta's jaw was set the way I had seen her set it against gulls and customs men and once, memorably, a tax collector. "And I know who sent it. Well. Let them read our answer in the morning."

It was while the grown men argued about whether anything inside might yet be saved that I saw it.

* * *

The sea chest sat apart from the boathouse, near the tide line where Tam kept it for want of better storage — old Tam, who rented corner-space in the boathouse and grumbled about it. It was iron-bound oak, and even in the firelight I knew it the way you know your own name. Mrs. Ashgrove's husband's chest. His letters. His signal-code books — the very books whose patterns lived now inside the ciphered pages hidden in the lighthouse logbook, the pages that named ships and dates and orders signed by heroes' hands. She kept most everything precious in the tower, but the great chest itself, too heavy for stairs, lived here among the boats, and I had seen her sit beside it some evenings with her palm flat on the lid like a woman touching a shoulder.

The fire had reached the far wall of the boathouse and was working along it toward the open door, and the wind was shoving it faster than any bucket line could answer. The chest lay maybe thirty paces off, no more, close enough that heat already breathed across the sand in long slow pulses.

Nobody else was looking at it. Everybody was looking at the flames, because flames are honest about wanting your attention.

I did not decide to run. That is the truest thing I can say about it. There was a gap in the world between standing by Marta and being halfway there, and no decision lived in the gap. My feet knew sand. My lungs knew cold smoke. The heat came at me in walls, one after another, each hotter than the last, and the fire made a sound I had never known fire made — not roaring exactly, but breathing, a vast slow respiration, drawing in over the sands and pushing flame out through the roof, and I understood suddenly that I was standing inside the breath of an enormous animal that had not yet noticed me.

My hand closed on the rope handle and the iron fittings bit cold through my glove, which surprised me — I had expected everything near a fire to be hot, but the sea side of the chest was sea-cold, streaming with the damp of the tide-line, and that coldness saved my skin if not my dignity. I hauled. The chest did not move. It weighed what grief weighs. I put my whole slight body into it, heels dug into sand, arms shaking, and it came perhaps six inches with a groan of wood on shingle.

Then hands took the other handle.

Old Tam Beale himself, soot-striped, eyebrows half gone to the flames' appetite, hauling opposite me with a strength his bent back had lied about. He did not ask what I thought I was doing. Between us the chest came free and swung and dragged, six inches, a foot, three feet, and then others were there — Marta's eldest boy Jemmy, fourteen and all elbows, and behind him two fishermen whose names I knew but had never once been given cause to speak aloud. They took the weight as if taking it were nothing, as if walking a burning building's shadow to steal back a dead man's chest were a Tuesday errand, and we got it up the shingle to the black stones above the tideline just as the roof-beams let go inside the boathouse with a crash like a ship striking rocks, and a column of sparks went up and the wind took them seaward, scattering orange over the dark water like a fleet of tiny burning ships sailing away from us toward nowhere.

We stood around the chest, panting. My hands were shaking so badly I hid them in my coat. Tam Beale looked down at me. His face was black and wet and terribly kind.

"Well then," he said. "That's the lad done a man's work tonight."

* * *

Mrs. Ashgrove came to me fast across the sand, and for one unguarded moment her composure cracked clean through, and what was underneath was not the keeper or the cipherer or the widow but simply a woman who had seen a child run toward a fire. She gripped my shoulders and turned me round once, inspecting, and her hands trembled against my coat, and when she spoke her voice was rougher than I had ever heard it.

"Don't you ever—" She stopped. Started again. "You are the bravest fool on this coast, Sebastian, and if you ever do such a thing again I will — I don't know what I will do, but it will be severe."

"You were crying," I said, astonished.

"I have sand in my eye," said Mrs. Ashgrove, who had taught me that a lie told smoothly was a weapon, and I loved her fiercely for wasting one on so small a mercy.

She knelt then, right there on the shingle with the fire painting her face orange, and opened the chest, and checked — I watched her check — that the oilcloth packet's twin hiding place within its false bottom was undisturbed, though we both knew the papers she guarded closest never left the tower. But the codebooks were there, her husband's own hand, and she laid her palm flat upon them and stayed kneeling a moment with her head bowed, and I understood that I had run into the fire for her dead husband without ever having met him, and that she knew it, and that something between us had been sealed by it that no inspector's questions would ever pry open again.

When she rose, the whole beach was watching us. Not staring — watching, the way a crew watches its officers in weather. Tam Beale spat into the embers' glow and said, loudly enough for every ear:

"Whatever quarrel mainland men have with the keeper and her lad, they've made it ours now. Burned my boats to send a message. So here's mine back: any man, woman, or child on this headland stands with the lighthouse from this night forward. Any stranger asking questions gets answers worth exactly what he pays for them, which is nothing. And if anyone comes here meaning harm to that boy—" he put his ruined hand briefly, gently, on my head "—he'll wish he'd drowned on the way over."

There was a sound from the crowd then that I had never heard directed at anything concerning me. Not applause. Something older than applause — a low murmur of assent, the sound men make around a grave or a launching, and Marta took my hand and Jemmy punched my arm in the way that meant brotherhood, and Mrs. Ashgrove stood in the middle of it straight-backed with the firelight dying behind her, and I saw that she was frightened still, and that she was not going to show it, and that neither fear nor courage was the point anymore. The point was that we were not alone, and everyone knew it now, including whoever had struck the match.

* * *

We walked home late, past the smoldering ruin, and the smell followed us all the way up the cliff path — char and salt and the ghost of pitch. From the gallery of the tower I looked back once. The boathouse was a red skeleton on the headland. Out on the black water the light swept round and round, steady as a heartbeat, touching the wreck, the sea, the village roofs where lamps were being lit in windows that had no reason to be lit at that hour except that their owners wanted it known they were awake, and keeping watch, and choosing sides.

Mrs. Ashgrove stood beside me in the wind. After a long time she said, not looking at me:

"They meant it to frighten us into giving you up before winter ends. Fire as a letter. But letters can be answered." She was quiet a moment. "Tomorrow I begin copying everything again. Three copies, like a woman preparing for her own funeral. Because whatever comes next, it will come quickly now."

I should have been afraid. Some part of me was, deep and permanent, the way the sea is deep under calm weather. But what I said, watching my breath tear away southward on the wind toward whatever mainland held the men who had burned Tam's boats, was:

"Good. Let them hurry. I'm tired of waiting to be forgotten."

Beside me, Mrs. Ashgrove went very still. Then, in the darkness, she began — softly, hopelessly, wonderfully — to laugh.
