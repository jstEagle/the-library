## 13. Reading the Whole Truth

The storm had spent itself by morning, and the lighthouse stood in a silence so complete that Casper could hear the wick of the great lamp breathing — a soft, fibrous intake, like something sleeping. She had not slept herself. She had sat through the dark hours with the logbook open on her knees and the deciphered pages accumulating beside her in a drift of foolscap, writing until her hand cramped, warming it at the lamp glass, writing again. Now the light was out, the sun was up, grey and watery behind the last scud of cloud, and the whole truth lay spread across the table in the keeper's room like a catch of fish she did not know how to gut.

Sebastian slept on the cot in the corner, one arm flung over his eyes, his breathing small and even. He had helped her the night before — that was the thing she could not stop turning over in her mind. A boy of nine, holding the signal-book open to the page she needed, pointing with one finger when she faltered, as if he had known his father's code all along without knowing he knew it. As if the cipher lived in him the way his father's face lived in him.

She made tea she did not drink and began to read it all through, from the beginning, in order, the way an enemy would read it. The way a court would have to read it.

* * *

The first pages were the officer's own hand — Captain Edmund Rill, 12th Regiment of Foot, Sebastian's father — written in the weeks before his death, in the neat, upright script of a man trained to make reports. But these were no reports. These were the account no report would carry: the truth of Kessari Point, written down because there was nowhere else for it to go.

Casper read it standing up, because sitting felt wrong, felt too much like comfort.

*On the fourteenth of August last, orders came under Colonel Vane's signature that the village of Kessari was to be "cleared." I asked cleared of what, and was told that was not my concern. The village held some three hundred souls, most of them fishermen, none of them armed beyond boar-spears and old fowling pieces.*

*I wrote to ask whether the order meant the burning of stores only. The reply came back the same evening, signed again by Vane: the clearing was to be total, and any officer who raised further objection would be relieved and reported. I understood what reported meant. Two lieutenants who had questioned earlier orders were already gone — one dead of a fever contracted, it was said, in the marshes; one sent to a station so remote that letters took half a year.*

*What was done at Kessari on the sixteenth of August was done slowly enough that every man knew what he was doing, and fast enough that no man could afterward claim he had time to think. I will not set down here the particulars. They are in my memory where God can read them, and they will be in the official dispatches dressed in language fit for church. I record instead the names of my men who wept afterwards, and the names of those who did not, because both lists matter, though I suspect only one of them ever will.*

Casper set the pages down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had expected them to shake. Outside, the gulls were working the tideline after the storm's leavings, crying their harsh cries, indifferent. Three hundred souls. She thought of the supply boatmen who had brought her news all winter — war news, victory news, the 12th Regiment praised in the papers for its firmness in the field. Firmness. She went to the window and looked at the sea for a long moment, the same sea that had taken Thomas, the same sea that had carried Captain Rill's confession across it to her door wrapped in oilcloth, and she understood suddenly that the sea had never once been indifferent, that indifference was a thing men invented so they could sleep.

She sat back down and read on.

* * *

The middle pages were the ones keyed to Thomas's signal-code, and these were different in nature entirely. These were copies — fair copies, made carefully — of documents Captain Rill should never have possessed. Orders. Requisitions. A ledger extract showing the movement of powder and shot to Kessari Point in quantities suited to nothing a fishing village could answer.

And then, on a page Casper copied out twice because she could not believe it the first time, the order itself.

*To Capt. E. Rill, 12th Foot, from Col. A. Vane, commanding: You are directed to proceed to Kessari and effect the clearing of the said place entire. No quarter is to be given to persons bearing arms, which is to be construed liberally. The operation is to be completed before the arrival of H.M.S. Meridian, whose captain has instructions to observe nothing. Acknowledge by return. — V.*

Dated the eleventh of August. Five days before the massacre. Signed. Not a rumour, not a whisper passed between officers over brandy — a signed order, in Vane's own abbreviation, with his own hand visible even in miniature, the way a man's walk is visible at a distance.

And beneath it, in Rill's smaller script, the annotation that turned the document from damning to annihilating:

*Note well the date. The official history — the dispatch printed in the Gazette, the accounts given to Parliament, the story already being told in the mess halls — describes an engagement on the seventeenth, provoked by hostile fire, regrettable losses among non-combatants. That account was drafted by Vane's aide on the twelfth of August. It was written before we marched. The history of Kessari Point was finished five days before Kessari Point existed to be described. Whatever happened there, the empire had already decided what had happened there, and we were marching into a story rather than out of one.*

The proof that the official history was written before the massacre ended. Before it began. Casper read the paragraph three times, and each time the horror shifted shape — first it was monstrous, then it was administrative, and the second was worse than the first. This was not a crime committed in the heat of a distant afternoon. This was a crime composed in advance, typeset in advance, its grief pre-measured, its regrets pre-written, waiting only for the blood to fill in the blanks.

She thought of all the histories she had read in her life — Thomas's books, borrowed volumes, the papers the boatmen left behind — and how she had always believed, without ever saying it to herself, that history belonged to other people. To men in London with seals and presses. To men who decided what had happened and then informed the world politely. And here, on her kitchen table, among tea stains and crumbs of Sebastian's breakfast bread, lay the machinery of that deciding, stripped of its casing: dates, signatures, a ledger, a draft dated five days early.

History was not elsewhere. History had been in her house since the night a boy fell asleep on her step.

* * *

The last section was the hardest. Rill had listed names.

Not only Vane. Vane was the head, but Rill had understood, with the clarity of the doomed, that cutting off a head achieves nothing if the body keeps walking. He named the adjutant who drafted the false dispatch — Lieutenant-Colonel Merrick, now mentioned in dispatches, decorated. He named the naval officer aboard the Meridian instructed to observe nothing — Commander Pell, since promoted. He named the commissary officer who had certified the requisitions as legitimate expenditure — a civilian name, a treasury name, a name with a seat somewhere and a vote somewhere else.

Fourteen names in all, arranged in Rill's hand in two columns, the weepers and the non-weepers, and beneath them a final paragraph:

*I send this by means I trust above the post. If you are reading this, you are the person I chose, or the person chance chose, and I am sorry for it either way, because this knowledge is a stone and I am asking you to carry it. Carry it to someone with the standing to speak it aloud. If no such person can be found, then carry it anyway, and give it to time. Time is the only clerk who cannot be bought.*

Casper looked over at the cot. Sebastian had turned in his sleep; his hand hung down, fingers curled loosely, and she could see in the curve of his thumb some ghost of a man she had never met. Your father trusted a stranger, she told him silently. He put you in an oilcloth packet and sent you to a woman he had never seen, because of a code your grandfather — no. Because of a code my husband built, and your father learned, and neither of them is here to see what it cost.

Thomas. She had not let herself think of the court-martial in years — the inquiry into the wreck of the *Providence*, the finding that blamed her husband's signals, the finding that was wrong, that everyone in the room must have known was wrong, that was signed anyway because signing it was easier than reopening a question with the Admiralty's fingerprints already on it. Thomas had died still carrying that finding, still a man officially at fault for a disaster officially caused by him, and she had buried the injustice with him because she had believed — she saw it now, plainly, cruelly — that people like them did not get to argue with the record. That the record was weather. You endured it.

Rill had believed otherwise. Rill, a dying man's shadow of an officer, had believed that paper could be fought with paper, that a signed order was a door that could be forced open again. And he had addressed his trust not to a lord, not to a lawyer, but to whoever stood at the far end of a signal-code — to a keeper, possibly; to a keeper's widow, certainly — because he had known Thomas Ashgrove, and he had known what Thomas knew: that the light does not choose which ships deserve saving.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon. She copied everything in triplicate, as she had promised herself she would, and the labour of it steadied her more than any prayer could have. Three full copies: the confession, the orders, the ledger extracts, the list of fourteen names, Rill's annotations, and her own covering statement — sworn before Marta's brother-in-law, who had once been a parish clerk and still owned the inkhorn for it — setting out how the documents came into her hands, dated, signed, witnessed.

One copy for the Admiralty. One for a man of standing — and here she wrote to the only name she had, a member of parliament whom the boatmen spoke of as honest, which in her experience meant merely unproven — and one to stay here, hidden where only she and Sebastian knew, in case the other two drowned, burned, or vanished, as things had a way of doing when they inconvenienced important men.

Her hand ached. The candle stubs burned down. Sebastian woke in the late afternoon and asked no questions, only brought her bread and cheese and sat across from her sharpening her spare pens with a small knife, gravely, like a surgeon's assistant, and when she finished the third copy at dusk he gathered the loose sheets and squared their edges against the tabletop without being asked, tapping them gently, the way one settles a sleeping child.

"Is it all of it?" he asked.

"It's all of it," Casper said. "Every name. Every date."

He nodded, looking at the papers the way he looked at heavy weather coming — not afraid, exactly, but measuring. Then he said, quietly, "My father said the true account would be heavy. He said whoever carried it would need strong hands."

Casper looked at her own hands, ink-stained, rope-scarred, forty-three years old, hands that had trimmed wicks through twenty winters of storms. Strong enough. They would have to be.

* * *

It was full dark when she heard the boat.

Not the supply boat — the wrong hour, the wrong rhythm of oars, and no hail called toward the slip. She blew out the lamp in the keeper's room, moved Sebastian behind the water casks without a word — he went like smoke, already practiced, which broke her heart even as she blessed it — and climbed the stair to the lantern room, where she could see down the black shoulder of the headland to the water.

Two boats. Oars muffled. Running no light.

They came on steadily toward the landing rocks below the tower, and as they slid into the lee of the point she caught, for one heartbeat, the gleam of lamplight on oiled cloth and metal — on uniforms. Red, or dark enough to be red. A dozen men at least, maybe more, coming ashore at her rock in the night, unannounced, and somewhere behind them, out past the bar where the deep water lay, she could just make out the loom of a larger vessel riding at anchor where no vessel had any business anchoring.

Casper stood in the lantern room with three copies of the truth stacked inside her coat, against her heart, and below her in the dark the first boot crunched onto the landing rock.

She reached out and lit the great lamp.
