## 14. Betrayal at the Inn

Marta Skelling had learned to read the sea the way other women read letters, and on the last Thursday of February it told her something was coming before anyone else in the village knew it. The water lay flat as poured pewter, and beneath that flatness there was a swell running from the southeast, long and patient, the kind of swell that carried no weather of its own but announced weather somewhere over the horizon. She stood in the doorway of her cottage with her hands still wet from gutting herring and watched it come, and she thought: *someone is crossing that water tonight.*

She had thought a great deal about crossing water lately. About who crossed it and why and what they carried with them when they came.

The trouble had started small, the way all ruinous things do. Her husband Tomas had been dead three years, drowned not in any grand storm but in an ordinary squall, an ordinary capsize, the sea taking its ordinary tithe — and he had left behind not only Marta and their sons but a debt at the Anchor & Chain on the mainland that grew like a barnacle on a hull. Forty pounds. Then forty-four. Then forty-nine, because the interest compounded the way interest does, quietly, in the dark, while you slept. The innkeeper, Mr. Quill, was not a cruel man, but he was a patient one, and patience in a creditor is more frightening than cruelty.

That afternoon, as she salted down the catch, she saw the boat come across from the mainland — not the supply boat, which came Thursdays, but a smaller craft, a gig with two men in it, and even at that distance she knew what they were by the way they sat. Not fishermen. Fishermen sit loose, apologizing to the sea for disturbing it. These men sat upright, rigid, like nails hammered into a board.

They went to the Anchor's sister tavern here on the point, the one the village called the Halfway House because it stood halfway between the harbor and the lighthouse road. And Marta Skelling, who had gutted four hundred herring that day and whose back screamed when she bent, found herself walking up that road behind them, telling herself she only wanted to hear what they said, telling herself that knowing a thing was not the same as being complicit in it.

Inside the Halfway House it was dim and smelled of tallow smoke and spilled ale. The two men sat in the corner booth, and one of them she recognized — or half-recognized — from the autumn, from the day the polite inspectors had come asking after a woman and child. He had been the harder one then, the one whose eyes moved over everything like a man taking inventory. His companion now was younger, with a clerk's softness about him, and between them on the table lay a ledger and a bottle neither of them touched.

Marta took her usual stool at the end of the bar and ordered nothing, because ordering nothing drew less attention than ordering something you couldn't pay for.

"—the debt," the clerk was saying, in the low voice men use when they think a room is empty. "Quill will forgive the whole of it. Forty-nine pounds, seventeen shillings. Signed and burned, if—"

"If the information proves useful," the hard-eyed one finished. "Not gossip. Useful."

There was a pause, and Marta did not turn her head, but she heard the sound of someone sitting down at the booth, someone whose boots were wet with harbor water, someone local. She knew the cough before she knew the voice. Old Piet Vosse, who netted mackerel off the eastern rocks, who had eaten Christmas dinner at her table the year Tomas died because she could not bear to eat alone and neither could he.

"Well," Piet said, and his voice had the wheedling quality of a man about to sell something he is ashamed of. "There's a boy out there. At the light. Been there since the autumn storms. Ashgrove's taken him in."

"A boy." The clerk's pen scratched. "Describe him."

"Seven, maybe eight years old. Dark-haired. Doesn't speak much. Comes down sometimes with the widow's boys to look at the nets." A pause, and Marta could hear Piet gathering himself, could hear the shame being set aside like a coat taken off before work. "And there's papers, they say. Papers the widow keeps locked up."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Marta's heart to fall all the way through her body and land somewhere below the floorboards.

"That will do," the hard-eyed man said. "Quill's debt is cleared. Don't be at the harbor tomorrow night."

Marta left without finishing her drink — she had bought none — and walked home along the water with the flat pewter sea on her left hand and fury moving through her like the long swell itself. She was angry at Piet, yes, but her anger kept sliding off him and settling where the true weight lay: on herself, on all of them, on a village so ground down by debt and tide and the indifference of the great world that a man would sell a child for forty-nine pounds and call it survival rather than betrayal. She wanted to hate him cleanly and could not. That was the worst of it. If he had been a monster she could have hated him and been done. Instead he was just a poor man drowning slowly, and he had grabbed the first rope offered, never mind who else was attached to the other end.

Her sons were splitting kindling when she got in. Joren, fourteen, all elbows and silences; Ansel, eleven, all questions. She looked at them a long moment, these two boys of hers, and made a decision that felt like stepping off a boat into black water.

"Put down the axe," she said. "We're going to the lighthouse."

* * *

Casper Ashgrove opened the door before Marta knocked. Perhaps she'd seen them coming up the path; perhaps the lamp above them threw their shadows long across the yard. Up close, the keeper looked thinner than she had in the autumn, worn down to something essential, the way driftwood is worn — but her eyes had not worn down at all.

"You've heard something," Casper said.

It was not a question. Marta realized, standing there with the wind pulling at her shawl, that she had rehearsed a whole speech on the walk up and that every word of it was now useless, because this woman read faces the way Marta read water.

"Piet sold you," Marta said plainly. "Sold the boy. To soldiers. They're coming tomorrow night — maybe sooner. There's a debt at the Anchor & Chain and Quill forgave it this afternoon, and Quill doesn't forgive anything for free." She glanced at Sebastian, who stood half-hidden behind Casper's skirts in the lamplight, watching her with the solemn, unblinking gaze of an owl. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry it took money to make me come. It shouldn't have. I'd have come anyway, but I'd have come slower, and slower might have killed you."

For a moment Casper said nothing. Then she stepped back from the doorway.

"Come in, Marta Skelling. All of you. It's cold, and we have planning to do, and I find I can't do it alone anymore."

They planned around the keeper's own table, with the great lamp turning overhead — Marta could feel it through the ceiling, that slow patient weight going round and round, sweeping its beam across the dark like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. Joren and Ansel were sent up to the lamp room with Sebastian, ostensibly to see the mechanism, actually to keep the boy calm and away from adult voices. Down below, Marta laid it out: the gig, the two men, the words she'd heard, the timing.

"They'll land after dark," she said. "Small party — six men at most; a gig won't hold more, and they'll want quiet, not a landing force. They'll come up the harbor road, and they'll want to take the boy without waking the village, which means fast and means frightened, because a village that wakes up is a village that remembers faces."

Casper had gone very still, the stillness of deep water. "You know this how?"

"I know fishermen, and I know soldiers, and I know what men look like when they're doing something they'd rather not be seen doing." Marta folded her rough red hands on the table. "You can't run. Running confirms everything and gets you caught in the open with a child's legs. You can't fight — they'd call it assault and come back with thirty. So you delay them. You give them nothing and you waste their night, and meanwhile—" she nodded upward, toward the turning lamp and the boy above it, "—meanwhile the boy isn't where they think he is."

"And where is he?"

"With my sister-in-law Brechtje, up the coast past the shingle spit. Her cottage sits alone; no one passing would mark it. Joren can row him around tonight, before the moon sets. By morning he'll be a nephew visiting an aunt, and nephews are everywhere."

Casper looked at her for a long time. "They'll ask questions in the village afterward. They'll want to know who helped."

"They will," Marta agreed. "And I'll tell them whatever keeps them looking at me instead of at my boys." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile she wore when hauling nets against a contrary current. "I've been a widow on this coast nine years, mistress. Men underestimate grieving women. It's the one gift the sea ever gave me."

* * *

They came the next night, as she had known they would, and Marta was ready for them, though ready turned out to mean something different than she had imagined.

It began well enough. Joren had rowed Sebastian around the point in the dark hours, wrapped in sacking among the crab pots, and returned soaked and grinning and silent about it even to Ansel. The boy was safe at Brechtje's, fed on barley soup, learning the names of her goats. When the gig slid into the harbor an hour after full dark — six men, oars muffled with cloth, exactly as predicted — Marta was waiting on the harbor steps with a lantern and a face full of honest confusion.

The hard-eyed man from the tavern came ashore first. Up close he had a name, apparently: Sergeant Brace, though he offered it the way a man offers a fist.

"Evening," he said. "We're looking for a runaway. Boy, eight years old, dark hair. May be sheltering with the lighthouse keeper."

"At this hour?" Marta let her voice go thin and foolish, the voice of a woman whose wits were mostly wool. "In this cold? Whoever lost him should be ashamed."

"The keeper, then. Is she at home?"

"She's always at home. She's a keeper." Marta lifted the lantern and peered theatrically into his face. "Is the boy yours? He's not. I can tell. Boys don't run from people who are kind to them."

Something flickered behind Brace's eyes — irritation, yes, but also a kind of professional caution. This was what she had counted on. A man doing secret work cannot afford to make enemies loudly; noise brings witnesses, witnesses bring records. So he smiled instead, a paper smile.

"Madam, I'll ask you once more, plainly. Is the boy at the lighthouse?"

"I couldn't say. I sleep at night. Everyone here sleeps at night. We fish at dawn." She turned and called up the harbor road, pitching her voice to carry to the cottages, to the windows where lights were already beginning to show because she had sent Ansel running door to door an hour earlier with the news that strangers were landing: "Daan! Daan Hendriks! You awake? Soldiers are here asking after a lost child!"

Doors opened. Figures appeared in lamplight. Not many — the coast was too used to minding its own business for that — but enough. Enough faces in doorways to make a secret operation feel suddenly public.

Brace's jaw tightened. "Search the lighthouse, then. Quickly."

But here the night began to go wrong for him, and right for Marta, in ways she had not fully planned and would remember all her life. Because the tide that evening was a spring tide, and the harbor road's lower stretch was already awash, and the only way to the lighthouse on foot was the cliff path, which in February wind, in the dark, with a rising sea booming below it, was a thing even soldiers hesitated at. Two of the six refused outright. Another slipped on the wet rock and gashed his palm and spent ten minutes being bandaged with Marta's own apron strips, which she tore with great ceremony and terrible slowness, clucking like a hen the whole while.

By the time they reached the lighthouse, Casper Ashgrove was standing in her doorway with a lamp held high and a document in her hand — Marta learned the shape of it later, a keeper's warrant, some old paper giving her authority over her own ground — and she received the soldiers with a courtesy so glacial it might have preserved them for science. Yes, they might search. No, there was no boy. Yes, she would accompany them herself, room by room, because she trusted no man alone in her house, and if they damaged so much as a wick she would write to the Board and to the Admiralty and to the newspapers, and she recited the newspapers by name.

The search took two hours. It found a sleeping loft, a cold stove, jars of preserves, a logbook open on its stand — open, Marta noted, to pages of perfectly innocent weather entries — and no boy at all. Brace stood in the empty kitchen at the end of it, and for a moment his composure cracked and Marta saw underneath it, saw a tired man in a dirty job who suspected, perhaps, that he was the instrument of something filthy. Then it sealed over again.

"We'll return," he said at the door.

"People usually do," said Casper Ashgrove.

* * *

The reckoning came for Marta three days later, and it came not with violence but with paperwork, which on this coast was the more frightening thing.

A cutter appeared in the roads at noon — a proper naval vessel this, flying the ensign, carrying an officer with a list. The list concerned seaworthiness. Marta's boat, the *Tomas's Luck*, named for a drowned man and the only thing of value he'd left besides his sons, was declared unseaworthy upon inspection, condemned, and seized under some regulation older than anyone present, pending a survey that everyone understood would never happen.

"It floats," Marta said to the young officer, standing on the shingle while strangers put a chain on her boat. "It has floated for nineteen years. My husband built her."

"Regulations are regulations, madam."

"Yes," she said. "I've noticed they usually are, when they want something."

Joren had to be held back by Ansel, and Ansel had to be held back by the sight of his mother's face, which had gone very quiet, very level, the levelness of deep water before it closes over something. She watched them tow the *Tomas's Luck* out to the cutter, watched it grow small, watched it disappear, and she did not cry, because crying was a luxury and also because she had decided — in the way one decides such things without deciding them — that if she wept in front of these men, they would win twice.

That evening Piet Vosse came to her door. He stood on her step twisting his cap in his hands, an old man made suddenly older, and he could not meet her eyes.

"Marta," he began. "I didn't know they'd—"

"You knew," she said, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. "You knew what they were. You just didn't know what you were. There's a difference, Piet, but it's a small one, and it lives in a person's chest, and either it's there or it isn't." She looked past him at the darkening sea, where the swell was still running from the southeast, longer now, more patient. "Go home. Eat something. And if they come asking you again — and they will — remember that the debt they bought you with can be sold again to someone worse. Debts don't die, Piet. Only debtors do."

He went. She stood a while longer in the doorway, listening to the sea work against the shingle, thinking of the boy at Brechtje's cottage learning the names of goats, thinking of Casper Ashgrove alone in her tower with her papers and her terrible knowledge, thinking of a boat named for her husband riding at a stranger's stern.

Then she saw, far out past the point, a light she did not recognize — a signal, short and sharp, three flashes and a pause, three flashes and a pause — answering itself from somewhere down the coast. Not the lighthouse. Something else. Something moving.

Marta Skelling went inside and woke her sons, because whatever was crossing that dark water tonight, she had the sudden, certain feeling that the village would need every pair of hands it had by morning.
