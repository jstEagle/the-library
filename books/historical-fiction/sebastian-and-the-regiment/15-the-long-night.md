## 15. The Long Night

The knock came at half past eleven, three blows with the butt of a musket, and Casper knew before she opened her mouth to answer that the night would be long.

She had been in the lamp room when she first saw them — a line of lanterns strung down the cliff path like beads pulled off a broken necklace, eight men perhaps, maybe ten, moving with the unhurried confidence of those who have never once been told no by the sea. She had time enough to do only what mattered. She banked the fire in the stove, took Sebastian by the wrist without a word, and climbed.

Now they were below, and the boy was folded into the space behind the great lens where the shadow fell thickest, among the spare wicks and the brass fittings and the smell of hot oil that had been his whole world these many months. She had wedged him in with the oilskins draped over him and told him, kneeling so their eyes were level, *whatever you hear, whatever you hear, you are stone, you are part of the light itself, and no one questions the furniture of a lighthouse.* He had nodded. His eyes in the lamplight were enormous and dark as tide pools, and he had not trembled, which frightened her more than trembling would have done. A child learns early what terror looks like when it has been worn smooth by repetition.

Then she had gone down to open the door, because a keeper who does not open her door invites them to break it, and a broken door is a story, and stories travel to the mainland faster than any boat.

* * *

They filled her kitchen the way water fills a hull — quietly at first, then all at once, and everywhere.

There were nine of them. She counted without seeming to count, the way her husband used to count gulls off the headland: a lieutenant with a face like a ledger, two sergeants, and six soldiers who stood where they were put and moved when moved, their boots leaving crescents of wet sand on her scrubbed flags. The wind outside was rising from the southwest, and it found every gap in the old tower and made the whole structure hum like a struck bell, so that even standing still, the lighthouse seemed to be moaning its warnings into the dark.

"Mrs. Ashgrove." The lieutenant consulted a paper he did not need to consult. "We're searching for a runaway boy. Small, dark-haired, foreign-looking. There's reason to believe he was put ashore along this coast."

"Was he." Casper kept her voice flat as the horizon. She had learned this much about lying since autumn: it was not performance but arithmetic. You subtracted everything true and what remained was safe. "I see few children here. The supply boat comes fortnightly and brings flour and oil and gossip, in that order."

"We'll need to look through the station."

"The station is mine to keep," she said. "By warrant of the Trinity House and the Crown. But I'm a widow alone, Lieutenant, and I won't stand between the King's men and the King's orders. Mind the stairs. Mind the oil. It burns fast and it burns *everything*, and there is no village here for four miles if you set us alight."

It was the truth shaped into a threat, which was the most honest lie she knew how to tell. She watched it land. Soldiers respect fire above officers; it is the one authority they have all seen act arbitrarily.

They went up in pairs, boots ringing on iron treads, and Casper followed with her lamp held high, playing the hostess, narrating as they climbed — *the watch room, my late husband's charts, mind the draft, the door sticks* — her voice steady while beneath her breastbone her heart beat like something trying to get out of a trap.

Above them, behind the lens, in the blind spot where the rotating flame's reflection could not reach, a boy lay among oilskins and did not breathe.

* * *

The hours after that were the longest of her life, and she had buried a husband, which is to say she had already lived through nights that did not end.

The search was thorough in the manner of men performing thoroughness for an audience. They looked in the store room and the coal cellar and under Marta's boy Tom's abandoned netting pile. They opened the sea chest — her husband's sea chest, scorched at one corner from the boathouse fire, rescued from the flames by Sebastian's own hands — and went through Casper's linens and Jonas's old coats with hands that were careful and incurious, looking for a boy and finding instead the ordinary archaeology of grief: a pipe, a fob watch stopped at some unrecorded hour, letters tied with string that the lieutenant glanced at and, God forgive him or don't, returned to their place.

But they did not find the logbook. They did not know to look for it. To them it was a ledger of weather and lamp-hours, and it sat in plain view on its shelf in the watch room, ciphered columns marching down its pages like rain against a window, holding within it the names of decorated heroes and the dates of a massacre written in a dead signal-code — and nine soldiers looked at it and saw nothing, because the empire has always been able to look directly at a thing and see nothing, so long as seeing would cost it something.

Casper stood in the doorway of the watch room while two of them riffled the chart drawers, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter: *you could burn this whole tower and the paper would outlive you.* Paper is patient. Paper goes to London. Paper waits.

"Your light," the lieutenant said suddenly, and she turned. "You're due to light it."

"I am always due to light it," Casper said. "I light it in war and peace, in storms and searches, for smugglers and admirals alike. That is the whole of the office. Shall I go up?"

He hesitated. She watched him weigh it — the order in his pocket said *find the boy*, and a lit lamp meant workmen climbing, movement, complication. But the chart on the wall behind him showed the shoals, and the wind was swinging south-southwest, and somewhere out in that black waste were ships whose masters trusted a fixed white light on this headland more than they trusted the Admiralty itself.

"Go up," he said. "Two men will accompany you."

"Of course," said Casper, and picked up her oil can with hands that did not shake, because she had decided years ago, at her husband's funeral, in front of men in red coats who would not meet her eyes, that her hands would never shake where they could be seen.

* * *

The lamp room was a drum of glass and brass at the top of the world, and the storm was arriving.

Through the glazing she could see the clouds coming off the sea in long grey ranks, and the first spit of rain ticking against the panes like fingernails. Below, the tower shuddered. Above, nothing but weather. She went about her ritual — trim the wick, check the oil feed, wind the rotation clock, touch the flame to the wick and watch it take, that small gold bloom that meant, as it had meant every night for seven years, *someone may live because of this.*

The two soldiers stood awkwardly by the door, out of their element. One of them was young. He kept looking at the lens with something close to wonder, and then catching himself and putting the soldier back on his face.

"You've a boy here," the young one said abruptly, too loud for the room. "I mean — a child was reported—"

"I heard the report," Casper said, feeding oil with a steady hand. "I also heard the wind. Both are beyond me."

The other soldier shifted. "Ma'am. We're to ask direct. Have you seen him?"

And here was the moment. She had known it would come and had rehearsed nothing, because rehearsal shows. She turned and looked at them both, this woman alone at the top of a tower with a flame between herself and the empire, and she let the silence stretch until it was uncomfortable, and then she said:

"Gentlemen. I have seen forty wrecks from this gallery. I have pulled drowned boys out of the surf and laid them in my kitchen and written to mothers who never wrote back. If a living child came to my door, I would feed him, warm him, and send word to the parish — because that is what a person does, and what your regiment, tonight, in my house, with its boots on my clean floor, appears to have forgotten." She lifted her chin toward the dark glass. "Now. Out there, past the shoal, there is a brig running for shelter with no idea how hard this wind is about to hit. Every minute that lamp burns properly is a minute I do not have to add another name to that list. So you may stand there and guard me from the oil can, or you may wait below. Either way, the light stays lit."

The young soldier looked at the older one. The older one looked at the floor. And Casper understood, with a strange sinking tenderness, that these men were not monsters; they were instruments, and instruments can be aimed, and the hand aiming them tonight was far away and wearing a colonel's coat.

They waited below.

* * *

The storm arrived fully at two o'clock in the morning, and with it the hardest hour.

The wind came around and struck the tower broadside, and the whole structure leaned into it and groaned, a sound like a ship's timbers, like a living thing in pain. Rain drove horizontally against the lantern glass. The flame guttered in spite of every mechanism built to protect it, ducked low, flared, ducked again, and Casper moved through the shrieking drum of the lamp room doing the work her body knew better than her mind: adjusting the draught vents, wiping salt spray from the lower panes, coaxing the wick.

And between tasks, when the soldiers' boots were faint on the stairs below, she knelt by the lens and spoke softly into the dark.

"It's me. Storm's come. Hold still, hold warm." She passed a biscuit and a swallow of water through the gap in the oilskins. Small fingers brushed hers and withdrew. "You're doing beautifully. You're braver than the whole Ninth—"

"*Twelfth,*" came the whisper, fierce and immediate, and she nearly laughed, nearly wept. Even now. Even crouched in the dark with soldiers hunting him, the boy kept the regiment's shame straight in his head, because it was *his* — his father's disgrace and his father's truth, and he carried it the way she carried the light: nightly, faithfully, without being asked.

"Yes," she whispered back. "The Twelfth. Sleep if you can. Morning's coming."

Whether he slept she didn't know. She worked.

That was the whole of it, and she understood that it was the whole of everything: she worked. The clockwork needed winding again at three. The oil needed feeding at half past. At four the rain turned to sleet and began to crust the gallery rail in white, and she went out onto the gallery itself with the safety chain biting her waist, hunched against the wind, chipping ice from the ventilators with the back of a knife while the sea heaved below her like something dreaming violently. Once, through a rift in the sleet, she saw a sail — a small coaster running north, badly, too close inshore — and she watched it the way you watch a held breath, until it cleared the shoal buoy by a cable's length and was gone into the murk.

*Saved*, she thought. *Or delayed. Or lucky. It doesn't matter. The light doesn't get to know. The light only gets to burn.*

She had spent seven years believing history happened elsewhere — in parliaments, in colonies, in court-martial rooms where verdicts were decided before witnesses spoke. Standing on the ice-rimed gallery with the wind trying to peel her off the tower, she finally let go of that belief entirely, and it left her lighter, not heavier. History was here. History was a boy behind a lens and a ciphered logbook on a shelf and a flame she would defend with her body, and history, tonight, was being kept — tended like a wick, fed like a flame — by a woman the record did not yet know existed.

*It will*, she thought, going back inside. *Before spring, it will.*

* * *

They came up at dawn, or what passes for dawn in a gale — a bruised grey suggestion of light, the storm easing just enough to show the sea's full fury beneath it. The lieutenant was pale from a sleepless night on a bench, and something in his face had changed. Not softened, exactly. Complicated.

"No boy, Mrs. Ashgrove."

"No," she agreed. "No boy."

"He was seen landed near this coast in October."

"Lots of things are landed near this coast. Most of them dead." She poured him tea because refusing hospitality would have been a confession, and because her hands wanted occupation. He took the cup and did not drink it.

"My orders extend to a second visit," he said. "Within the fortnight. With a wider party." He paused, and lowered his voice, and for one moment the ledger-face slipped and she saw the man underneath, tired and uncertain. "The officer who signed these orders — Mrs. Ashgrove, do you know anything of the Twelfth Regiment? Of its... recent history?"

"I know what the newspapers print," Casper said carefully. "Honours. Victories. A colonel much commended."

Something moved behind his eyes — a door opening onto a corridor with more doors behind it. "Yes," he said. "That is what the newspapers print." He set down the untouched tea. "Mind your lamp, Keeper."

They went down the cliff path into the wreck of the morning, nine red coats dwindling along the rocks, and Casper stood in the doorway until the last of them rounded the headland and was gone.

Only then did she climb. Only then, with her legs shaking so badly she had to sit on the watch-room stair halfway up, did she let the night have her — the fear arriving all at once, belated, like a wave hitting a boat that has ridden over its swell. She sat with her head in her hands and breathed until it passed.

Then she went up to the lamp room, and pulled back the oilskins.

Sebastian was exactly where she had left him, folded into the smallest possible shape of himself, eyes open, awake, having heard every word spoken in that room for six hours including her speech to the soldiers and her whispers and, God help her, probably her crying on the stairs. He crawled out stiffly, rubbing his knees, and looked up at her.

"They'll come back," he said. Not a question. He was nine years old and he already knew how the empire worked.

"Yes," Casper said. "In a fortnight, with more men."

"Then we haven't got a fortnight."

She looked at him — this scrap of a boy with his father's stubborn jaw and his mother's watchful eyes, who had survived soldiers and saboteurs and a winter at the edge of the world — and she did not insult him by pretending otherwise.

"No," she said. "We haven't. Which means everything must go to sea before they return. All of it. And there's only one ship that carries post to London regular—" She stopped. Her mind, storm-battered and sleepless, snagged on a fact it had been carrying for days without weighing it: the mail packet, the *Providence*, sailing on the morning tide, day after tomorrow. Carrying dispatches. Carrying, very possibly, a certain official account, sealed and franked and bound for the desks of men who would read it and nod and file the massacre away forever.

Sebastian was watching her face. "What?" he said. "What is it?"

Casper looked out through the streaming glass at the grey, roaring sea, and felt the shape of the next two days assemble itself, terrible and precise, like a knot she already knew how to tie.

"The mail ship," she said, "leaves on Thursday's tide."
