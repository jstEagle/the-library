## 17. What Must Go to Sea

The barometer had begun to fall on Tuesday, and by Thursday it fell the way a man falls from a mast — not gradually but all at once, with a sound like something tearing. Casper watched the needle slide into the red and thought: *not now. Not this week.*

She said it aloud to the instrument as if it were an old acquaintance she could reason with. "You have held your tongue through two wrecks and a winter of gales. Hold it four days more."

The needle did not answer. Needles never do. But outside the glass the sea was already changing its mind about itself — the long grey swells coming in from the southwest shorter and steeper than they should have been for so light a wind, the way water gathers itself before it decides to break something.

Three copies lay before her on the kitchen table, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with tarred twine. She had made them over six nights by lamplight, copying her own hand until her wrist ached and the letters swam, until she could recite whole passages in her sleep — dates, ship's names, the order signed in Colonel Vane's neat, upright script, the account written three days *before* the village burned. Three times she had written that sentence out. Three times it had looked no less impossible in the morning.

One copy would go to sea. One to the Admiralty, by way of a fishing boat and a trusted cousin and a mail coach whose route Marta's eldest son had memorized like a psalm. One would stay hidden in the lighthouse, inside the hollowed base of the spare lamp stand, where fire could not easily find it and neither could men.

But the copy that mattered most — the copy that had to reach London while the official dispatches were still aboard the *Ariel*, still sealed, still believed — that one needed a man, and the man was Mr. Ellery Voss, Member of Parliament, and he was presently somewhere on the mainland behind a falling barometer and forty miles of flooded road.

* * *

Hale had sent word by the fisher-boy Tam, folded small inside a twist of tobacco paper: *Voss confirmed aboard. Ariel mails Thursday next, tide at dawn. He will expect delivery at the harbour steps, no later than Wednesday night. After that I cannot hold anything open.*

No signature. Hale did not sign things now; signing was how men got hanged. But Casper knew his hand, the cramped downward slant of it, the way he crossed his t's like a man striking matches. He had read the pages. Whatever he had been when he came ashore last autumn asking polite questions about a missing woman and child, he was not that man anymore. She had seen him read the deciphered order — the one that sent soldiers into a sleeping village and called it pacification — and she had watched something go out of his face that had perhaps been holding him up for years.

*He will expect delivery.* As if the truth were a parcel of kippers.

She stood at the window with the note burning slowly in the stove below her — old habit; nothing written stayed written in this house — and watched Sebastian on the headland path, hauling the day's water up from the cistern with the grim economy of a much older child. Eight years old and he carried buckets like a stevedore. Eight years old and he checked the horizon every time he straightened, without seeming to, the way soldiers check doorways.

He had grown into his silences this winter. They were no longer the silences of fear. They were the silences of a boy learning to count what mattered: the number of boats out, the colour of the sky to the west, whether Mrs. Quill's smoke rose regular from her chimney down the bay. Casper recognized the education. It was her own, taught to her by grief, and she hated seeing it fitted onto shoulders so young.

Wednesday night. Tide at dawn Thursday. And the sea already lifting its shoulder.

* * *

Marta came up the cliff path at dusk with her shawl pinned wrong and her face set in the expression Casper had learned to dread, because on Marta it meant either very good news or very bad news delivered in the same flat voice.

"Boat's ready," she said. "Tam's brother will run you across to the harbour Wednesday if the weather holds. If it doesn't—" She shrugged, and the shrug said everything the sentence wouldn't: *if it doesn't, we are finished, and there is no other plan, because we are fishermen's widows and boys, and the empire does not wait upon our convenience.*

"The weather won't hold," Casper said.

"No," Marta agreed. "It won't."

They stood in the doorway together, watching the first spits of rain come in slantwise off the water. Below them the village lamps were lighting one by one, little orange convictions against the dark. Casper counted them out of habit. Eleven. All winter they had been eleven lamps, and every one of those households knew now — not everything, but enough — enough that when the soldiers came in February, half the village had lied to their faces with the serene fluency of people who had been lied to by their betters for generations.

"You could send me instead," Marta said suddenly. "To the harbour. I've a face they don't know."

"They know every face on this coast. That's rather the difficulty."

"I mean they don't know me as *yours*. A woman carrying a package is nobody. A woman carrying *that* package is a hanging."

"It isn't yours to carry." Casper heard the steel come into her own voice and did not soften it. "I found it. I read it. I wrote it out three times with my own hand. If it costs anyone, it costs me."

"That's pride talking."

"That's arithmetic." She turned back to the room, to the table where the three bundles sat like sleeping animals. "My husband went to his grave believing the record would right itself if only a man told the truth plainly enough. He told it plainly. The record buried him twice. I am not going to stand on these cliffs and hand the second burial to someone braver than I need be."

Marta looked at her a long moment, rain dripping off her shawl onto the flags. "Casper Ashgrove," she said at last, "you were a different woman in October."

"Yes," Casper said. "October drowned her too."

* * *

Sebastian did not cry, which somehow was worse. He stood very straight by the stove with his hands behind his back — his father's stance, though he could not have known it, or perhaps he could, perhaps it lived in the blood like the flinch at red coats — and listened while Casper explained, again, why he could not come to the harbour.

"The boat might be stopped."

"I'm small. Nobody searches small people."

"That is precisely why they'd search you."

"They searched Marta's boat in February and found the brandy in the flour barrel and didn't look in the fish box."

"And if they'd looked in the fish box?"

He considered this with the terrible seriousness of childhood chess. "Then Marta would have lost the fish box too."

Casper turned away to trim the lamp wick, which did not need trimming, because there were moments when a woman needed her hands occupied or they would shake where the child could see. "Sebastian. Look at me."

He looked at her. Grey eyes, his mother's according to the aunt, steady past all reason.

"If this goes wrong — if the boat is taken, if I am taken — you go to the hollow under the lamp room floor. You stay there until Aunt Ruth comes or until Tam comes, whoever comes first. You do not come out for my voice. Do you understand? Anyone can make my voice."

"Anyone can't make your voice," Sebastian said. "Your voice sounds like the lamp room door. Creaky, but it always opens."

Something went through her chest that was not quite laughter and not quite grief, some third thing that had moved in this winter and refused to give its name. She knelt and took his thin shoulders in her hands.

"Promise me the hollow. Promise it properly, the way you promised about the boathouse fire."

"I promise." And then, because he was eight and merciless: "But you'll come back. You always come back. You're the keeper. The keeper comes back."

"The keeper comes back," she agreed, and hated herself for the ease of it, and kissed the top of his head where the hair smelled of lamp oil and salt, the same smell, she understood now, that had made him trust her on the first terrible night — his father's smell, inherited by accident into her house, a whole dead man distilled into a scent.

* * *

Tuesday the wind came round to the southwest and began to blow in earnest, and the barometer's needle lay flat along the bottom of the arc like a fainted thing. The supply boat that should have run Wednesday did not run. The harbour at low tide showed weed-slick stone and white water standing off the mole in broken columns, and Casper stood at the lantern rail with a glass pressed to her eye and felt the plan shrinking beneath her like sand under a wave.

*Voss sails Thursday dawn. Delivery Wednesday night. There is no Wednesday night if no boat crosses.*

She spent Tuesday evening doing what keepers do in storms, which is everything: trimming, filling, wiping salt rime from the glass, logging the wind each hour in a hand steadier than her heart. The light turned and swept and turned, and each sweep showed her the same picture — the sea gone from grey to a green-black like wet slate, the combers coming through the outer shoal in ranks, the whole coast braced like a man awaiting a blow.

At midnight she found Sebastian awake in his cot, sitting up, listening.

"It sounds angry," he said.

"It is angry. It has a right to be. So do we."

"Will it stop by tomorrow night?"

She could lie to inspectors. She could lie to colonels. Her throat closed around lying to him. "I don't know."

He nodded, absorbing this the way he absorbed everything now, filing it among the other things adults would not promise. Then: "If the storm doesn't stop, does the truth stop?"

And there it was — the question underneath the winter, asked in a child's plain grammar because no adult would dare ask it. Does the truth stop?

"No," Casper said. "Storms stop. Ships sink. Governments forget. The truth doesn't stop. It just waits for somebody stubborn." She tucked the blanket under his arms the way her mother had done forty years ago in another century of storms. "We are being extremely stubborn."

He almost smiled. On Sebastian's face an almost-smile was a sunrise.

* * *

Wednesday dawned the colour of dishwater, and by noon the wind had done the thing winds do on this coast — backed suddenly east, dropped to a hard cold blow, and left behind it a sea like a shaken bed, confused cross-swells from three directions, the worst kind of water for a small boat and the best kind of excuse for every authority between here and the harbour to close the crossing entirely.

Casper stood on the landing stage with the primary bundle strapped inside her coat, against her ribs, where it had ridden since dawn like a second heartbeat. Tam's brother Ewan stood beside his boat, a fourteen-foot lugger named — with the fatalism of his people — the *Maybe*. He looked at the water the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten him twice.

"She'll take us across," he said. "Question is whether she'll take us back."

"Then we don't come back tonight. We come back after."

"After the mail packet's sailed." He squinted eastward, toward the harbour mouth five miles down the coast, invisible beyond the headland but present in his mind as clearly as his own hands. "After, then. If the *Ariel* sails."

"She'll sail. Mail ships sail in weather that sinks everyone else. It's the one reliable virtue of the postal service."

Ewan grinned despite himself, spat ceremonially to leeward, and held the boat against the stage while she stepped down into a bilge full of nets and herring stink and one anxious hen. Sebastian stood above on the rocks, holding the rope that must not be let go until the last moment, his chin up, his eyes enormous.

"The keeper comes back," he called down.

"The keeper comes back."

The *Maybe* went out through the harbour race with the wind on the quarter and the spray coming over green and solid as paving stones. Casper crouched in the stern with her arms around the bundle and the bundle around her heart, and watched her lighthouse shrink behind them — her tower, her husband's tower, her whole deliberate small life — until the headland took it, and there was only the sea, and the harbour ahead, and Wednesday night running out like sand in a glass.

Five miles. One crossing. One member of Parliament who had better keep his appointment, one tide that had better behave, and one storm behind them gathering its breath to follow.

In the bottom of the boat, among the nets, the hen laid an egg. Ewan looked at it, then at her, and laughed until the boat shipped water.

"Aye," Casper said grimly, gripping the gunwale as the *Maybe* climbed the next grey wall. "Even that's more certain than the post."

Ahead, through the flying scud, the harbour lights of the town were just beginning to show — and riding at anchor beyond the mole, black against the last light, the masts of the *Ariel*, tall and patient as a gallows.

Tide at dawn. Hours left. And somewhere on the quay, she would learn within the hour, a man in a red coat asking quiet questions about a woman from the lighthouse.
