## 19. The Morning Tide

**CASPER**

The fog came in before dawn, the way the worst things always did — without announcement, without mercy, folding itself around the headland like a shroud drawn up to the chin. Casper Ashgrove stood at the lantern rail and watched it swallow the sea whole, and she thought: *of course*. Of course the morning that mattered most would be the morning the light could not be seen.

She had kept this lamp through eleven years of storms. She knew its every mood, its hunger, the way the flame leaned east when the wind backed round. And she knew what fog meant for a ship standing in toward the sound with no bearings but a bell-buoy somewhere off her bow. The *Alderney Star* was due on the morning tide, mail ship and packet boat both, carrying His Majesty's correspondence and one sympathetic Member of Parliament who had agreed — at considerable risk to his career and possibly his neck — to carry a sealed oilcloth satchel into London.

That satchel lay against Casper's chest now, wrapped in oilskin beneath her coat, warm from her own body. Three copies. One to the Admiralty by way of Marta's cousin in Penzance. One buried in the lamp room floor under the spare glass. And this one, the true original, going out on the tide.

Below her feet, Sebastian slept in the oil store, curled among the empty tins like a kitten in a drawer. She had not told him he might yet be taken from her today. Some mercies were lies, and she had decided she could live with that.

The bell-buoy sounded, far off, muffled to almost nothing.

"Come," she whispered to the fog, to the ship inside it, to whatever god kept ledgers of his own. "Come and take it."

* * *

**MARTHA'S SONS**

Tom and Jory Penhalick had rowed through surf before — they'd been born into a boat the way other children were born into shoes — but never at slack water in a fog so thick they could not see their own oar-blades strike.

"She'll be standing in past the bell," Tom said. He was nineteen and pretended not to be afraid, which was the same as being afraid, which was fine.

"Then we'd best be between her and the rocks when she comes." Jory, sixteen, pulled with his jaw set, listening the way you listened in fog: not with ears but with the back of your neck, with the sea's breath against your cheek.

Their mother had put them off herself, wading to her knees beside the boat, and pressed the oilcloth bundle into Tom's oilskins and said nothing at all except, "If you're boarded, let them have the boat. Let them have your coats. That bundle goes in the water with you if it must, and you swim, do you hear me? Both of you. You swim."

They heard her.

Out past the point the fog thinned and thickened like breathing. Somewhere in it was a ship of two hundred tons with a captain who did not know what he carried toward him in a rowing boat — or perhaps he did. The MP had signaled from the headland last night with a shuttered lamp; three long, three short. *Understood*, had come back. *We will lie off the buoy.*

The bell sounded again, closer now, and beneath it — faint, then less faint — the creak and slap of a hull working at anchor, canvas being hauled down, the wet roar of sails flogging once and falling silent.

Jory found the ship's side by bumping it, which was how their father had always done it, God rest him, and a voice above said, "Who in blazes is that?" and another voice, dry and educated, said, "That, I believe, is our post."

* * *

**HALE**

Inspector Edmund Hale had been awake all night, and not because of nerves. Nerves were for men who still had something to lose. He stood at the harbor wall in the grey half-light with Vane's two troopers — Brandt and the young one, Ellery — and gave orders in the flat tone of a man following instructions, while inside himself he laid each instruction down like a card face-up on a table where only he could see it.

"The woman first," Brandt said. "Vane wants the boy before the tide."

"The boy's at the lighthouse," Hale said. "The lighthouse is a mile along the cliff path. The path is mud after last night's rain. We go by the road, and we go carefully, because if the boy bolts across country we lose him, and the Colonel does not pay for failure. Do I make myself clear?"

It was good reasoning. It was even true. It bought them an hour, maybe more, and every minute was a rope paying out behind the rowing boat he could hear — or imagined he could hear — out in the white blindness beyond the wall.

Ellery shifted. "Sir. The Colonel said—"

"I know what the Colonel said." Hale turned and looked at the young trooper until the boy dropped his eyes. He had read the deciphered pages at three o'clock that morning, by candle, in the inn's back parlor, and he had not slept since, and he would never sleep properly again, and that was a small enough price. Villages burned to save a colonel's reputation. Women and children shot in a ravine and entered in the dispatches as *action against hostile parties*. Orders signed in a hand Hale knew, because he'd seen it on his own commission.

"You'll walk behind me," he told them. "You'll say nothing to anyone. If we meet anyone on the road, we are inspecting coastal lights for the Board of Trade, and I will do the talking."

They met Marta Penhalick on the road, hauling creels, broad as a door and twice as immovable.

"Morning," she said, and did not move aside.

Hale touched his hat. "Madam."

"Inspectors." Her eyes went over the three of them like a fishwife pricing herring. "Fog's thick. Hope you've no mind to drive anywhere."

"We're walking to the light."

"Long walk in fog." She shifted her creels deliberately into the middle of the path. "Mind the mud. It's treacherous past the chapel. Took my husband's cart axle clean off one winter, and him with it." She said it flatly, without grief, daring them to make something of it, and Hale understood that she was telling them: *my husband died on this road, and so might you, slowly, and I would watch.*

"Thank you," Hale said gravely, and stepped around her creels, and gave her half a second — no more, just half a second — in which his eyes said what his mouth could not. *I'm sorry. I'm slow. Forgive me.* She looked back at him with the same message returned: *Be quicker than sorry.*

* * *

**SEBASTIAN**

Sebastian woke because the light stopped turning.

He knew the sound of it the way he knew his own heartbeat — the great clockwork groan overhead, the tick and sweep of the lens riding its bath of mercury — and when it faltered, when the machinery wound down and the silence pressed in, he was out of the oil store and up the stairs before he knew his legs were moving.

Mrs. Ashgrove stood at the lamp, feeding the winding weight, her hands steady, her face pale as the fog beyond the glass.

"Fog signal duty," she said. "The mechanism needs me every twenty minutes in fog, or the lens stalls. Go down and fetch me the coffee flask, there's a good lad."

But he didn't go. He stood looking at her, and she looked back, and something passed between them that neither of them would ever quite describe afterward — not farewell exactly, and not fear either. Recognition, maybe. Two people standing in the same weather.

"They're coming," he said.

"Yes."

"For me."

"For what you know," she said. "Which is worse, and better." She finished the winding. The lens sighed and began its turn again, sweeping its blade of light into the fog, cutting nothing. "Sebastian. Listen to me. Whatever happens today — whatever anyone tells you — the truth is already moving. It's on the water. Truth travels faster than soldiers when it has a fair wind, and today it has one. Your father wrote it down so it couldn't be killed with him. All anyone can do now is try to catch up to it, and they will find it ahead of them, everywhere, forever."

He nodded. Then, because he was nine years old and brave in the way children are brave, entirely and without knowing the cost: "Will you be here after?"

She knelt and took his shoulders. She smelled of lamp oil and salt, of home, of everything he had left of his father.

"Nowhere else," she said. "Nowhere else on God's earth."

* * *

**CASPER**

They came up the cliff path at half past seven — three figures out of the fog, resolving slowly, and Casper counted them from the gallery with a keeper's eye: the tall one walking first, the heavyset one behind, and the boy soldier trailing, and she thought, *where is the fourth? Where are the six he landed with?* and answered herself: scattered, watching the village, watching the roads, watching boats. But not watching the water, because no one watched the water in fog. No one but keepers.

She had the logbook open on the desk below, innocently official. She had Sebastian sent up to the lamp room with instructions to wind the mechanism and touch nothing else, which he understood perfectly well meant *be ready to bolt to the north door and down the goat path to Marta's*, and she had the poker within reach of her right hand, and she was ashamed of none of it.

Hale knocked. Of course he knocked. He had knocked the first time too, months ago, hat in hand, polite as a funeral.

"Mrs. Ashgrove."

"Inspector. And colleagues. Fog's bad. Will you come in for coffee?"

"We won't." He stepped inside anyway, dripping, and his eyes went around the room — the desk, the logbook, the stairs, the closed door of the oil store — cataloguing with the terrible competence she remembered. "Where is the boy?"

"Asleep."

"In the oil store?"

"In a house," Casper said. "Somewhere in it. Boys are difficult to pin down; I should think a regiment would know that better than most."

Brandt moved toward the stairs and Hale's hand came up, casual as swatting a fly. "Not yet." He looked at Casper, and for the first time since she had known him, Inspector Hale looked tired. Not defeated — tired, the way a man looks who has been carrying something heavy in the dark and has finally set it down. "Mrs. Ashgrove. I'm going to ask you plainly, and I want you to consider that I am asking at all. The Colonel arrives on the noon boat. My orders are to have the child and all papers in hand before he lands. If I report failure—" He stopped. Started again. "There is a copy of everything, isn't there? There always is, with careful people."

Casper said nothing. The lamp turned above them. Somewhere out in the fog, faint as memory, she heard — or dreamed she heard — oars.

"There is a ship leaving on this tide," Hale said quietly. "Carrying dispatches. Carrying the Colonel's account of certain events, signed, sealed, and bound for the War Office and the printers who make history out of such things. Once that account is ashore in London, nothing any of us holds here will matter. Inquiry or no inquiry, the record will already be written." He paused. "Unless something reaches the record faster. Or alongside it. Or — I'm told the MP aboard has some interest in naval matters — *within arm's reach of it*."

The fog pressed against the windows. Casper felt her heart going like a hammer in a storm, and kept her face like the lamp glass: clear, steady, giving back only what was shown to it.

"You knew," she said. "Last night. When you passed the schedule under my door."

"I read those pages, Mrs. Ashgrove. All of them. I've spent twenty years believing I served gentlemen." He straightened his hat. "I find I was mistaken, and I find I mind."

"So you'll stall them."

"I'll stall them," Hale said, "badly. I'll misread my orders, lose my way on the road, quarrel with my colleague about jurisdiction. I'll be incompetent at a level that will end my career, and possibly see me tried. And in exchange you will give me your word — yours, Mrs. Ashgrove, a keeper's word — that when this goes before Parliament, my name stands beside the testimony. Not as a hero. As a man who was slow, and late, and finally arrived."

"You'll have it," she said. "In ink."

"Ink," he agreed, almost smiling. "God. Yes. Ink."

Behind him, Brandt's patience broke. "Sir, the boy—"

"*Is asleep,*" Hale snapped, with magnificent theatrical fury, "*and will remain undisturbed until the Colonel lands, per orders, which YOU will follow, Corporal, or explain to HIM why the search was bungled—*" and he swept Brandt toward the door with sheer noise and rank, and at the threshold he turned, and bowed slightly, and was gone into the fog with his two unhappy soldiers, arguing loudly all the way down the cliff path about whether the road went past the chapel or the creek, getting lost on purpose within sight of the door.

Casper stood alone in the lamp room with her hand pressed flat against her sternum, over the oilcloth satchel beneath her coat, and allowed herself ten seconds of shaking.

Nine. Ten.

Then she went down and out the north door, and ran.

* * *

**MARTA**

Down at the creek, Marta Penhalick stood in the shallows holding her dead husband's boat against the drag of the tide, and watched the fog for her sons, and prayed in the manner of fishing widows, which is to say bargaining, bluntly, with terms.

*Let them come back and I'll never curse the sea again. Let them come back and I'll forgive the Admiralty its confiscations. Let them—*

Oars. Faint. Then the shape of them, ghost-grey, materializing like something rising rather than arriving, and her heart went sideways in her chest, and then Tom's voice, hoarse with salt spray: "Mother! Mother, it's done! It's aboard!"

She caught the painter and hauled, and Jory scrambled out into the shallows laughing and crying at once, soaked to the ribs, seventeen kinds of triumphant, and for one moment she held them both, one under each arm, smelling of seaweed and boys and life.

"What happened," she demanded, "aboard? Tell me true."

"Captain near had us thrown off," Tom gasped. "Till the MP came down the ladder himself, took the satchel in his own two hands, and says, 'Gentlemen, this is post of the highest national importance, and if a drop of sea-water touches it I'll have the lot of you broken.' Locked it in his cabin. Chained to the desk, Mother. Chained."

"And then," Jory said, grinning through the tears, "then the fog started lifting."

Marta looked up. Out past the point, the whiteness was thinning, tearing, and through the rents showed grey water and the shape of the *Alderney Star*, anchor coming up, canvas rising black against the brightening east.

And behind her, from the harbor road, hoofbeats.

* * *

**CASPER**

She reached the headland as the fog tore open, and saw it all at once, the whole board laid bare:

The *Alderney Star* standing out on the ebb, topsails filling, a quarter mile of cold green water opening between her and the shore.

Colonel Vane's boat already launched from the harbor, pulling hard for the ship — too late by minutes, minutes Hale had bought with a career — oars bending, a uniformed figure in the stern, and even at this distance she could feel his fury coming off the water like heat off a stove.

And between the ship and Vane's boat, absurdly, gallantly, a single rowing boat crossing its line — Tom and Jory, gone out again without being asked, putting themselves and their father's boat square across the Colonel's course, shipping oars, drifting, fouling his water, pretending with magnificent insolence to be fishing.

Vane's coxswain swore and sheered. Lost thirty seconds. Forty.

On the headland beside Casper, breath ragged, Sebastian appeared out of the gorse — Marta had brought him up the low path, or he had run from her, it hardly mattered — and together they watched the mail ship lean to the freshening wind, gather way, and stand out toward the open sea, growing smaller and more certain with every swell.

"He made it," Sebastian whispered. "It's really going."

"It's really going," Casper said. Below them Vane's boat gave up the chase and swung back for the harbor, the oarsmen pulling with the mechanical bitterness of men who would bear the blame. The fishing boat rowed home in triumph. The fog lifted off the water like a curtain rising, and the sun came through, ordinary and golden, on an ordinary morning sea.

Sebastian's hand found hers. Small, cold, gripping hard.

"It worked," he said, wondering, as if he had never quite believed grown-ups could make a thing work. "Mrs. Ashgrove. It actually worked."

She knelt down in the wet grass and looked at him — this child abandoned at her door with a note that said *forget the face*, this witness, this boy, this reason — and she laughed, or sobbed, or both, and pulled him against her shoulder.

"Not yet," she said into his hair. "Not 'worked' yet. Now the hard part begins, my love. Now it has to survive London."

Far out on the water, the *Alderney Star* raised more sail, running free before the wind, carrying toward the capital a dead officer's testimony, a keeper's copies, and the name of every man who had believed the sea would keep their secret.

And behind them, from the direction of the harbor, came the sound of horses — many horses, ridden hard — and Marta's voice, high and sharp across the headland:

"CASPER! CASPER, HE'S LANDED AT THE QUAY — HE'S COMING FOR THE LIGHT!"
