## 20. Reckoning

The news came the way most news came to the light: secondhand, salt-stained, half a week old.

Marta's eldest boy, Jory, came up the headland path on a morning of hard blue sky, running the last stretch with his cap in his fist and shouting before he was even through the gate. Casper heard him from the gallery, where she was cleaning the storm panes, and her first thought — shameful, immediate — was that something had gone wrong. That after all these weeks of quiet, the quiet had been only the sea drawing back its breath.

"Mrs. Ashgrove! Mrs. Ashgrove, there's word on the packet boat!"

She came down the stair slowly, wiping her hands on her apron, making herself move at the pace of a woman who had not spent a winter waiting for the empire to answer. Sebastian was in the yard below, mending a net with Marta's daughter, and he dropped it when he heard the shouting, and then stood very still, the way he did, one hand curled around the strap of the oilskin satchel he still carried everywhere though there was nothing left in it to carry.

"Say it," Casper said.

Jory was breathless and glowing. "Inquiry. A proper one — parliamentary. They've announced it in the papers, Mr. Treloar read it out at the inn. The massacre and the cover-up and everything. There's going to be witnesses called to London." He gulped air. "And the other thing. The colonel. Vane."

The name landed in the yard like a stone into still water. Even the gulls seemed to go quiet over the headland.

"He's been arrested," Jory said. "They took him off his own ship. At Portsmouth. In front of everyone."

* * *

Casper had imagined this moment so many times during the winter that she had assumed that when it came, it would feel like triumph. She had rehearsed it lying awake: the news arriving, the vindication spreading outward from their rock like rings from a dropped stone, herself standing straight-backed in the yard while the world admitted what it had done. She had imagined she would weep, or laugh, or shout until her voice cracked against the cliffs.

What she felt instead, standing in the cold spring sunshine with Jory's words still hanging in the air, was something smaller and stranger. It felt like the end of a long calculation — the moment when the numbers finally balanced, and you saw they balanced, and understood that balancing them had cost what it cost and nothing could give that back.

Thomas would have understood that. Thomas, who had stood in a courtroom eleven years ago while men in good coats explained why his signal-code system was a breach of protocol rather than an innovation, why his commission should be suspended rather than honored, why a career could be ended by a signature without anyone ever having to say they were sorry. He had come home from that court-martial gray-faced and quiet, and he had said, *It's done now, Casper. It goes in the record and no one will ever read it again.* He had been wrong about many things in his life, but never, she thought, about how records worked. Records were not read. They were kept. They sat in rooms in London like bodies in ledgers, and the living walked past them.

Unless someone made the reading happen.

"Mrs. Ashgrove?" Jory said. "You've gone strange."

"I'm quite well," she said, and found that she mostly meant it. "Run down and tell Marta I'll bring the lamp oil she wanted tomorrow. And Jory—" he had turned, and she looked at him, this boy who had rowed dispatches through surf because she'd asked him to, "—tell your mother thank you. From me. From both of us."

Sebastian had come to stand beside her. He did not say anything. He had grown taller since autumn, or perhaps she had simply stopped measuring him against the frightened child at her door; either way, he stood now nearly to her shoulder, and he watched the horizon with an expression she recognized because she wore it herself. It was the look of a person checking whether the world had actually changed, or whether the change was only weather.

"They took him in front of everyone," Sebastian said at last. His voice was steady, which was new too. "His own officers. And nobody stopped it."

"No," Casper said. "Nobody stopped it."

He nodded once, slowly, as if filing the fact somewhere permanent. Then he picked up the net again, because there was work, and work was how this family — this strange, improvised, sea-battered family — knew how to hold itself together.

* * *

The inquiry announcement brought more than papers. It brought people.

They began arriving within the fortnight: a correspondent from a London daily who stayed three nights at the inn and walked the headland taking notes with numb fingers; a clerk from the Admiralty, polite and exhausted, who asked to see the lighthouse logbook and went pale when Casper produced not only the official log but the ciphered pages and the triplicate copies she had made through the worst weeks of winter. He copied everything again by hand in her kitchen, refusing tea twice, and when he finished he sat back and looked at her with an expression she had learned to recognize by now — the look of a man realizing that history had happened here, in this room, between a widow and a child and a stack of oilcloth.

"You understand," he said, "that your name will be entered into the record. As witness. As custodian of the documents."

"I had hoped so," Casper said.

"It is not—forgive me—it is not usual. Women are rarely formally cited in such proceedings."

Casper poured him the tea he'd refused. "Then the record will be unusual," she said. "It has already had to be."

He smiled then, briefly, and wrote her name carefully, spelling it back to her twice.

* * *

Hale came on a gray afternoon near the end of the month, alone, in plain clothes, looking older than the man who had stood in her kitchen in winter offering bargains. He had testified, the papers said — three days before Vane's arrest, he had walked into the inquiry's temporary offices and laid out the sailing schedules, the orders he'd carried, the instructions he'd refused. They called it defection in some columns and conscience in others. Hale himself called it, when she asked him directly, "a late correction."

He stood in her doorway holding his hat, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The wind moved the grass on the headland behind him with a sound like whispering.

"I'm not staying," he said. "I only wanted—I wanted to see it. The light. From close." He glanced up at the lantern room, white against the pale sky. "I spent four months trying to put it out, in a manner of speaking. Seemed fitting to look at it properly, once."

"Come in," Casper said. "There's bread and there's tea, and the boy will want to shake your hand whether you want him to or not."

Hale's face did something complicated. "I don't deserve—"

"No," Casper agreed, opening the door wider. "Very few of us do. Come in anyway. That's rather the whole lesson, isn't it?"

He ate two slices of bread and drank his tea and let Sebastian solemnly shake his hand, and when he rose to leave he paused at the door and said, without turning around: "The hearing in London. They'll ask you to testify, Mrs. Ashgrove. They'll ask the boy too. It will be ugly in ways the papers won't print. Men like Vane have friends even in disgrace." He settled his hat. "But the record is the record now. Whatever else happens — whatever they decide, however they twist it — it's in the record. No one can unread it."

"No," Casper said. "No one can unread it."

He went down the path, and she watched him until the fog took him, and found she was glad, in an unsentimental way, that he had seen the light burning.

* * *

Miss Ellery — Sebastian's aunt, née the same surname as the dead officer, though she had reclaimed her maiden name in the papers and signed herself *Margaret Ellery* in a firm, upright hand — arrived to stay through the end of the season, and the cottage rearranged itself around her presence the way water rearranges itself around a new stone.

Casper had expected jealousy of the arrangement, or awkwardness, or at minimum the careful diplomacy of two women sharing a child's allegiance across a table. What she had not expected was how easily Margaret fit. She was her brother's sister in the face and nothing like him in temperament — practical where he had been dreamy, blunt where he had been careful — and she took up residence in the second bedroom and within a week had reorganized the kitchen stores, repaired the gutter Jory had broken in November, and begun teaching Sebastian proper penmanship, which he resisted and secretly practiced.

It was Margaret who raised the guardianship formally confirmed by letter from London — the solicitor's letter arrived under seal, with copies for the parish — and it was Margaret who came to Casper on the evening she received it, and sat down across the kitchen table, and pushed the letter aside as if it were of secondary importance. Which, Casper realized, watching her face, it was.

"The solicitors say guardianship is mine," Margaret said. "Paper, signatures, all correct. He'll come to London with me when the hearing's done, or stay here through the summer if he chooses — the lawyers allow it either way, which tells you how much they understand children." She folded her hands. "None of that is what I came to say."

Casper waited.

"What I came to say is this." Margaret's voice was level, but her eyes had gone bright in the lamplight, and Casper saw suddenly how much it was costing her to say it plainly, how much pride and fear were tangled together underneath. "I am a stranger to that boy. I loved my brother and I never met his son until March. You are the woman who opened her door to him in a gale and kept it open all winter while men with torches circled your house. Whatever the paper says, the truth is that he has a mother in this house already, and I would be a fool and worse than a fool to try to take him away from her."

"Margaret—"

"Let me finish." She held up a hand. "I'm not asking you to give him up, and I'm not asking you to pretend I don't exist. I'm asking for something harder than either. I'm asking us to share him. Properly. Him coming and going between us, letters in between, summers here, winters in London while he's at school — all of it, the whole untidy arrangement, for as long as it takes and longer. Because I think he loves you, and I think he's beginning to love me, and I think the only way either of those survives is if we refuse to make him choose." She stopped. Her hands had tightened on each other. "So. Will you? Share him?"

Outside, the sea made its long, patient sound against the rocks. Somewhere below, Sebastian was laughing at something one of the fishing boys had said — a real laugh now, unguarded, the laugh of a boy who had forgotten to be afraid.

"Yes," Casper said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended, and she did not trouble to smooth it. "Yes. Gladly. All of it."

Margaret exhaled, and laughed, and wiped her eyes briskly with the heel of her hand as if tears were a household problem she'd deal with later, and the two of them sat together in the lamplight while the kettle sang, and neither of them mentioned that the hearing was three weeks away, or that testimony was a kind of tide that once summoned must be answered.

* * *

On the last night of April, Casper climbed to the lantern room alone.

It was her habit now, on quiet evenings, to sit awhile in the gallery after the lamp was lit and the mechanism wound, watching the beam swing out over the darkening water. But tonight she brought the logbook with her — the true logbook, the one that had started as a cipher and become a testament — and she sat on the cold iron bench with it open on her knees while the great lens turned above her head with its sound like slow breathing.

Weeks ago, Hale had told her the record could not be unread. Weeks before that, a clerk had written her name into a government ledger. But there was one record left unwritten, and it was hers.

She uncapped her pen. Below the final deciphered entry, below the names and dates and the order signed by a colonel who was now sitting in a cell awaiting trial, she wrote:

*Let it be entered in this log, and known to any who keep or read it, that the truth herein contained was preserved through the winter of 1847–48 by the keeper of this light and by those who aided her — a widow, a child, a fishing family, and one officer of conscience — against those who came to bury it. The light was kept burning throughout.*

She read it over. The ink dried quickly in the sea wind. Above her the beam swept its patient arc — dark, bright, dark, bright — out over the water where Thomas lay, where the courier had washed ashore, where Marta's sons had rowed the truth through surf toward a departing ship. Out there, somewhere past the horizon, the machinery of Parliament was grinding toward a hearing; men were drafting questions for a widow from a rock; a boy was learning to write his father's name in a fair round hand.

Justice, she thought, was not arriving. It had never once in her life arrived — whole, complete, on schedule. It crept. It leaked. It came in fragments, carried by tired clerks and defected inspectors and fishermen's sons in rowboats, imperfect every time, late almost always, but real — really real, weight-bearing, load-carrying real. Not the justice of ballads. The justice of the tide: indifferent to hurry, obedient to no one's schedule, and impossible forever to hold back.

She closed the logbook and sat on in the dark and the light together, listening to the sea, until footsteps on the stair announced Sebastian, climbing, with his aunt's voice floating up behind him — and with him, folded in his jacket pocket, a letter bearing the crest of the inquiry commission, addressed to *Mrs. C. Ashgrove*, summoning her to London.

He held it out to her with both hands, gravely, the way he had once held out a note that read *forget the face*.

"They want you to speak," he said. "Both of us. On the same day."

Casper took the letter. The beam swung overhead, painting the gallery white, then blue-dark, then white again, and she looked from the summons in her hand to the boy beside her — her son in every way that mattered, bound for the city where his father's killers would be called to account — and heard herself say:

"Then we'll go together, and we'll tell them everything."
