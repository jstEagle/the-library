## 2. Ships at the Horizon

The storm had spent itself in the night, and morning came up the color of pewter, the sea lying flat and gleaming like spilled mercury all the way to the edge of the world. Kestrel Villalobos climbed the tower stairs with her coffee going cold in one hand and the lamp key warm from her pocket in the other, as she did every dawn, as she had done every dawn for six years now, and it was on the landing — halfway between sleep and duty — that she saw them.

Ships.

Three of them, gray against gray, anchored off the point in a line so neat it might have been drawn with a rule. Not fishing boats; she knew every hull that worked these waters, knew them by the way they sat in the swell and the color of their sails and the names their owners painted crooked along the bows. These were warships, low and dark, funnels breathing thin smoke into the cold air, and each one flew the flag at a height meant to be seen.

She stood very still. The coffee steamed faintly. Somewhere below her, the sea went on doing what the sea always did — folding itself against the rocks in long gray sleeves — but above the waterline everything had gone quiet in a way that made her ears ache.

*War preparations,* Old Marta had said last market day, spitting into the gutter with the relish of a woman who enjoyed being right about terrible things. *They're building forts down the coast. Mark me, girl, there'll be soldiers before Christmas.*

Kestrel had laughed. She'd actually laughed, and bought her flour, and gone home to trim the wicks. Wars were things that happened to other coasts. Her promontory was too far north, too poor, too weather-eaten to matter to anyone who made decisions in marble rooms. The town's whole history fit inside Tomás's ledger — births, debts, deeds, quarrels over fence lines and crab pots — and none of it, not one line of it, had ever been worth a cannon's attention.

Now three warships sat off her point like crows on a fence rail.

She finished lighting the lamp out of pure habit — though in this daylight it burned invisible — and stood a moment longer with her hand flat against the cold glass of the lantern room, looking down. From this height the town revealed itself the way it always did: the crooked streets, the harbor cradled in its arms of rock, the smoke rising thin from forty chimneys. The workshop sat below the cliff like a held breath, its roof green with salt moss, holding six years of her husband's careful handwriting.

*They'll pass,* she told herself. *Ships pass. That's what ships do.*

But even then, even before the first longboat touched the shingle, some animal part of her — the part that read weather in the color of the water — knew they would not.

* * *

The column came at noon, marching up the coast road in a haze of dust and boot-rhythm, and by the time Kestrel reached the square the whole town had gathered the way gulls gather at a gutting table.

There must have been two hundred soldiers. They came in perfect order, bayonets catching the weak light, supply wagons grinding behind them, and at their head rode a man on a chestnut horse — broad, silver-templed, with a face like something carved for a courthouse pediment. He wore his rank easily, the way a man wears an old coat, and when he reined in before the fish market he surveyed the crowd with the mild patience of someone who has announced many things to many small places.

"I am Major Halloway," he said, and his voice carried without effort, trained for exactly this. "By order of the War Office, this township and its harbor are hereby requisitioned for coastal defense."

The words dropped into the square and lay there.

Old Marta, beside Kestrel near the well, whispered, "*Requisitioned.*" She said it the way you'd say a diagnosis.

"Hostilities may come to this coast," Halloway went on. "The Crown requires your harbor, your high ground, and your cooperation. This is temporary. When the danger passes, normal life resumes." He unfolded a paper. "Seizure notices will be posted on public buildings. Certain structures will be required for military use. Compensation schedules will be published in due course."

Kestrel heard the words, all of them, and understood almost none of them, because her attention had snagged on something else — on the soldiers themselves. While the major spoke, a dozen men had already peeled away from the column. They weren't posting notices or unloading wagons. They were walking the harbor's edge with instruments — tripods and brass telescopes on jointed legs, chains, notebooks bound shut against the spray. One knelt at the waterline making marks on the stone with chalk. Another stood on the breakwater taking sightings across the anchorage, muttering numbers to a companion.

They were measuring the harbor.

Not the coastline. Not the cliffs where guns might sit, not the road where an enemy might land. The *harbor* — its depth, its width, the exact reach of the moorings, the shape of the quay. Measuring it the way you measure anything you intend to buy.

The thought arrived whole and cold, like a fish pulled gasping from deep water, and Kestrel could not have said why it frightened her more than the bayonets did. Bayonets were honest. They told you what they wanted.

"Temporary," she repeated under her breath, and Marta gripped her arm with fingers like gull claws.

"See?" the old woman said, satisfied. "What did I tell you. Soldiers before Christmas."

It was only September.

* * *

By evening the notices were everywhere — nailed to the church door, pasted to the fish market wall, glued even, with a thoroughness that felt like contempt, to the side of Tomás's workshop below the cliff. Kestrel walked down the cliff path at dusk and stood before the paper in the failing light, reading it twice through because the first time the words refused to arrange themselves into sense.

*NOTICE OF REQUISITION. By order of the War Office... all lands, structures, and appurtenances within the designated zone... subject to immediate military control... owners to present documentation of title...*

Documentation of title.

She looked at the workshop door behind the notice. Through its warped planks she could smell the familiar smell — lamp oil and paper dust and the ghost of Tomás's pipe tobacco, soaked so deep into the wood that six years hadn't washed it out. Inside, on the long bench under the north window, lay the ledger.

Tomás had kept it the way he kept everything: meticulously, without flourish, in a hand so even it looked printed. Every family in the town was in that book. The Brandts' deed to the net shed, dated forty years back in his father's hand. Marta's claim to the spring above the mill, argued and won and written down. Debts forgiven, debts outstanding, children born and named, the slow accumulated weight of ordinary lives recorded because *someone ought*, because Tomás Villalobos had believed — quietly, stubbornly, without ever once saying it aloud — that a place existed only if somebody wrote it down.

He'd started it after the fever winter took their daughter, when half the town buried people whose graves had no markers anyone could find afterward. "Paper remembers," he used to say, tapping the page with his pencil stub. "People forget. Stone wears away. Paper remembers, Kes."

And here was a paper nailed to his door saying the town had never properly existed at all.

Kestrel put her palm flat against the notice. The glue was still tacky. Behind her the tide came in with its long patient sound, and the warships' riding lights had come up off the point, three yellow stars that didn't belong to any constellation.

*Temporary,* the major had said.

She thought again of the men with their chains and tripods, pacing the harbor's rim, writing numbers in books bound shut against the weather. You measured a thing carefully, exhaustively, lovingly almost, only when you intended to keep it. She had watched buyers do it at auction — walk the lots, test the wood, run a thumb along the grain — before the bidding ever began. Nobody measured what they meant to give back.

The wind shifted, carrying coal smoke from the ships offshore, and somewhere behind her a soldier's voice called a number to another soldier, and another number answered, and the harbor lay between them being measured inch by inch like cloth on a merchant's table.

Kestrel went inside the workshop and lit the lamp and took down the ledger, and held it a while against her chest, feeling its weight — heavier than a book should be, the way things get when they're asked to carry more than pages.

Then she began, without quite deciding to, to copy the index into her own notebook, name by name, in case paper forgot.

* * *

The next morning brought more ships' boats, more surveyors, and a new notice on the market wall listing the buildings requisitioned for immediate use: the customs house, the net shed, the granary, the chapel vestry. The townsfolk read the list and shook their heads and went back to their nets.

"They'll be gone by spring," said Henrik Brandt, mending a trawl with the serenity of a man discussing weather. "These things always blow over. Remember the press gang scare of '71? Nothing came of it."

"Aye," said his brother. "Nothing ever does."

Kestrel said nothing. She stood in the doorway of the market with her basket on her arm, watching a lieutenant — young, fair, with ink stains on his fingers that no uniform quite hid — direct two soldiers as they drove iron stakes into the ground at the harbor mouth. A boundary. The rope they strung between the stakes was thin and pale and looked like nothing at all, and yet when a fisherman ducked under it to reach his boat without breaking stride, both soldiers turned to watch him go, and wrote something down.

That night the lamp turned above the dark water, and the warships rode at anchor beneath it, and Kestrel sat in the workshop until the candle guttered, copying names.

On the third day, a wagon stopped at the workshop door, and an officer stepped down, and read aloud from a paper that the ledger — all records, all documents of title and debt and deed within the requisitioned zone — was to be surrendered to military custody for cataloguing.

"For safekeeping," he said, and almost managed to mean it.
