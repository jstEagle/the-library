## 3. What the Paper Says

The notice went up on the chapel door on a morning so still the sea looked poured rather than moving. Kestrel read it twice, once for the words and once for what the words were doing underneath themselves, the way a tide chart tells you not only where the water is but where it intends to be.

BY ORDER OF THE WAR DEPARTMENT, it began, in a hand too neat to have been written by anyone who had ever hauled a net or coaled a light. THE SETTLEMENT KNOWN AS BRIDLEPOINT HAVING NO RECOGNIZED INCORPORATION, NO REGISTERED CHARTER, AND NO LEGAL RECORD OF ITS INHABITANTS, ALL LANDS, STRUCTURES, AND IMPROVEMENTS WITHIN THE BOUNDARIES HEREIN DESCRIBED ARE HEREBY PLACED UNDER MILITARY ADMINISTRATION UNTIL SUCH TIME AS TITLE MAY BE PROPERLY ESTABLISHED.

*No legal record of its inhabitants.*

She stood in the cold with her breath making small ghosts and read that line a third time, because the first two readings had refused to be true.

Behind her, the town had begun to gather — Denny Croft with his hands still smelling of bait, the Pell sisters in their matching gray shawls, old Marta leaning on her stick as if she had walked out of her own house purely out of spite for the army. They murmured at the notice the way people murmur at weather: something to be endured, waited out, complained about over supper.

"Never incorporated," Denny said, half-laughing. "We've been here since before the county had a name."

"Paper doesn't know that," said one of the Pells.

Kestrel said nothing. She was looking past the notice at the young soldier who had nailed it up, at the way he'd driven the nails with quick, embarrassed efficiency, the way he wouldn't meet anyone's eyes. He knew. Or he suspected. Either way, someone had told him not to answer questions, because when Marta planted herself in front of him and demanded to know by what right the War Department claimed a town that had buried four generations of its dead in the hillside cemetery, he touched his cap and walked away at a pace just short of running.

Kestrel turned toward the cliff path and the workshop below.

She did not run. Running would have meant admitting what she already knew, and some part of her — the part Tomás had always called her stubborn keel — wanted ten more minutes of the world as it had been. So she walked, past the salt-crusted fence rails, past the gorse gone bronze with winter's approach, down the switchback path where the wind came off the water with its old familiar authority, and let herself pretend, step by step, that the ledger would still be where it had lived for nineteen years: on the oak desk under the south window, beside the lamp with the green glass shade, its spine cracked from use, its pages soft as cloth at the corners.

The workshop door stood open. She never left it open.

Inside, three soldiers were packing the room into crates.

They were not vandals; that was somehow worse. They worked with the careful, apologetic competence of men moving furniture for a widow — wrapping Tomás's tools in sacking, lifting the brass fittings into straw, stacking his charts flat so they wouldn't crease. One of them held the ledger.

He held it the way you hold something you've been told is valuable but not important. His thumb rode along the page edges, and when Kestrel crossed the threshold he looked up with the expression of a man caught taking biscuits.

"Mrs. Villalobos." He had a sergeant's stripes and a voice pitched carefully neutral. "I'm sorry, ma'am. Requisition order. All records of property and population come under military inventory."

"That book is not military anything." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Inside, something was moving like a storm swell under ice. "That book is this town. Every family in Bridlepoint is in those pages. Births. Deeds. Who owes whom for what, going back to my husband's father. You cannot requisition a memory."

"Begging your pardon, ma'am." The sergeant turned the ledger over, and she watched his eyes find the flyleaf, where Tomás had written in his beautiful drafting-hand: *For Bridlepoint, that no one be forgotten.* The sergeant read it, and something flickered across his face — doubt, maybe, or just discomfort — before training smoothed it away. "Orders are orders. There'll be compensation assessed once title's established."

"Title." She tasted the word. "By whose establishment? The paper on the chapel door says we don't exist. Now the only proof we exist is being carried out my door in a soldier's hands. Do you see the shape of that, Sergeant? Do you see what's been built there?"

He did not answer. He wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, set it in a crate between Tomás's sextant and a jar of preserved quills, and drove the lid home. The sound of the nails was very loud in the little room.

They took other things too — the deed copies Tomás had kept for neighbors who couldn't read, the marriage lines, the baptismal notes Marta had dictated in her cracked voice while Kestrel wrote them down. They left the tools, eventually, though they inventoried every hammer and plane as if a chisel might constitute a threat to the Crown. And then they were gone up the path, boots crunching, and Kestrel stood alone in the emptied workshop with the green-shaded lamp and the smell of oak shavings and the long pale rectangle of unfaded wood on the wall where Tomás's ledger shelf had hung.

She sat down at the desk and put her head in her hands and let herself feel it — properly, fully, the way she had not allowed herself to feel anything since the sea took him. Not grief exactly. Something colder. A grief that had been handed a weapon and shown how to aim it.

* * *

That evening the officer came.

Not Major Halloway — a younger man, a lieutenant with ink-stained fingers and a coat slightly too big for him, carrying a leather folio under one arm like a schoolmaster late for lessons. He introduced himself as Lieutenant Ashe, assigned to conduct the civil inventory, and asked if he might come in, and Kestrel, standing in her doorway with the wind pulling at her hair, considered telling him the sea itself could not make her more inhospitable than the army had already made her, and instead stepped aside.

He sat where she indicated, at the kitchen table, and laid the folio down without opening it, which she noted. Men who open their papers immediately want you to look at them. Men who keep them closed want to watch you first.

"I'm required to interview each householder," he said. "Names, ages, occupations, claims to land. It's for the compensation process."

"And after the compensation process?"

He blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"After. When the war ends — if there is even a war to end, which I begin to doubt, since I've yet to see an enemy nearer than the horizon — when the ships weigh anchor and the major takes his band somewhere else. How do we get our names back?"

"The requisition will be dissolved in due course, ma'am, and—"

"In due course." She folded her hands. "Lieutenant, do you know what a lighthouse keeper does all day? People think it's lighting the lamp. That's ten minutes' work. The rest of it is cleaning, polishing, winding, recording — the same things, every day, whether the weather rewards you or not. Keeping a thing alive is boring, patient work. Losing one is fast. Your major has lost my town's whole history in an afternoon, and he proposes to give it back *in due course*. I should like to know what course, and who is steering."

The lieutenant's ears had gone faintly red. But to his credit he didn't retreat into the wooden voice. He opened the folio instead and drew out a copy of the requisition order, and slid it across the table.

"You asked who's steering," he said quietly. "I can't answer that. But you should read this. All of it. Most people stop at the seizure clause."

She read it. The boundary description came first — and it was wrong, subtly wrong, drawing its eastern line through the harbor flats rather than along the ridge above them, swallowing the fishery sheds and the drying yards and the deep-water anchorage that was, she now realized with a slow cold turn of her stomach, the only part of Bridlepoint worth stealing. The rest of the town was a pretext. The town was the shell around the oyster.

Then the legal recitals. *Whereas no charter of incorporation exists for said settlement. Whereas no register of freehold title has been lodged with the county. Whereas the settlement therefore constitutes unencumbered crown-derelict land…*

"This is false," she said.

"It's what the record shows."

"The record shows nothing of the kind. We petitioned for incorporation in 'seventy-one. My husband carried the petition himself to the county seat — sixty miles each way, twice, because the first clerk lost it. The charter was granted. There are papers."

"Where?" And he asked it gently, genuinely, a man asking directions rather than laying a trap, and that gentleness frightened her more than Halloway's trumpets would have.

"I don't know," she said slowly. "Tomás kept everything. If he kept them, they were in the workshop, and—" She stopped.

The workshop had been inventoried to the last gimlet. Nothing resembling a charter had been listed, seized, or mentioned.

"When did your husband carry this petition?" Ashe asked. He was writing now, quickly.

"Spring of 'seventy-one. Why?"

"Because—" He hesitated. Whatever he was about to say, she watched him decide against it, and she marked the decision the way she marked a change in wind: not knowing what it would bring, but knowing it would bring something. "Because the order references a survey report," he finished instead. "A valuation of these lands commissioned in 'seventy-two. By a firm called Corven & Associates."

Corven. The name landed in her like a stone into a well, dropping down through years to strike something at the bottom. She saw again a wet autumn afternoon, a carriage stuck to its axles in the lane below the church, a broad smiling man in a city coat who'd bought half the village drinks at the Anchor while his clerk measured the harbor with instruments no fisherman had ever needed. She remembered Tomás coming home that week grim and quiet, saying only: *That man asked me where the incorporation papers were lodged. Asked me twice, casual as asking the time.*

Six months later, the county seat had burned. A chimney fire, the papers said. The registry office, of all the buildings in a town of forty, was the one the flames chose.

"They destroyed them," Kestrel said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm as deep water. "Twenty years ago. Before any of this. Corven burned our charter out of a registry office and sat on the knowledge like a man sitting on a seed, waiting for the season to turn. And now the season has turned, and the government arrives with a paper that says we were never real, and the author of that paper is drinking brandy in the major's quarters."

"Ma'am, I can't—"

"I'm not asking you to confirm anything, Lieutenant. I'm telling you what I know, and I have known it since before you could grow that mustache." She rose, and he rose with her, flustered, and she took the requisition copy and held it up to the lamplight. "You wanted names for your inventory. Very well. Here is mine: Kestrel Villalobos, keeper of the Bridlepoint light, widow of Tomás Villalobos, who carried this town's charter sixty miles through the rain and back, and whose book your soldiers nailed shut in a crate today. Write it down. Because I am going to write it down too — every name, every deed, every debt in this town, from memory and testimony if I have to — and when the day comes that somebody asks for proof of who we are, there will be proof. Not in due course. In my course."

The lieutenant looked at her for a long moment. The lamp flame stood perfectly still between them.

"Mrs. Villalobos," he said at last, gathering his folio, "the inventory interviews begin tomorrow. I'll start at the north end of the street." He paused at the door, not quite looking back. "I'll start at the north end. The south houses may take some time to reach."

And then he was gone into the dark, and Kestrel stood alone with the dying fire, understanding that she had just been given either a warning or a promise — and that tomorrow, whatever else happened, she would begin at the north end of the street, door by door, name by name, rebuilding from living memory the thing they had carried away in a crate.

Somewhere out beyond the point, the great light turned, steady as a heartbeat, sweeping its white arm across a sea full of anchored guns.
