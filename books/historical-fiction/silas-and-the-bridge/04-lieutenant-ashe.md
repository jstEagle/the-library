## 4. Lieutenant Ashe

The maps arrived before the war did.

Lieutenant Elias Ashe discovered this on his third morning in the requisitioned customs house, a building that smelled of salt rot and someone else's tobacco, with a crate of survey charts balanced on his knee and the wind worrying at the shutters like a dog begging to be let in. He had been sent to inventory a town. What he held, instead, was an argument.

The chart in his hands was dated the fourteenth of March. The war order — the one that authorized the coastal defense requisition, the one that had brought Major Halloway and two hundred men and a train of artillery up the coast road — was dated the second of June. Nearly three months. He checked it twice, then a third time, tilting the paper toward the gray window light as though the date might rearrange itself under scrutiny. It did not. The seizure map, with its neat red boundary lines running not along the coastline but through the harbor, through the fish yards, through what he understood to be private holdings, predated the order that supposedly required it.

He sat for a long while with the crate open at his feet, listening to the gulls quarrel on the roof.

There were explanations, of course. There were always explanations. Surveys preceded orders; staff officers planned ahead; a prudent engineer mapped terrain before anyone knew it would be needed. He had been taught all of this at the academy, had recited it back in examinations, had believed it the way one believes in the load-bearing walls of a house without ever inspecting them. And yet. A defensive survey mapped the coastline. It measured sightlines and surf conditions and the bearing of enemy approaches from open water. This map measured the harbor depth. This map noted, in a small precise hand, the tonnage a pier could bear. This map marked parcels by number, with acreage calculated to the hundredth — figures no gunner would ever need, but which any buyer would want very much to have.

Ashe set the chart down and pressed his palms flat against his knees and told himself, firmly, that he was a lieutenant of engineers, twenty-six years old, and that his business was bridges and emplacements and the honest arithmetic of loads, not speculation about the intentions of his superiors. Then he opened the next folder, because the work did not care what he believed, and found another map, dated February, and then another, dated January, each one drawn months before there was a war to prepare for, each one more detailed than the last, like a man rehearsing a theft until he could perform it blindfolded.

* * *

They gave him the lighthouse because no one else wanted the climb.

It stood at the end of everything — the road, the town, the habitable world — a white tower rising from black rock with the sea working at its foundations day and night, patient as a creditor. His orders listed it as Government Property No. 7, keeper's quarters included, and instructed him to conduct a full inventory of the structure and its contents. The keeper was a widow. Villalobos, the records said. The records said little else.

She met him at the door of the lower gallery with an oil rag in one hand and an expression that suggested she had been expecting him for some time and had already grown tired of him.

"Lieutenant." Her voice was level, neither warm nor cold. She was perhaps fifty, tall, with gray coming into dark hair pinned back without ceremony, and eyes the color of the water over deep rock — you could look straight into them and see nothing but your own reflection, slightly warped. "You'll want the lens room. Mind the brass. It polishes easier than it looks."

"I'm here for the inventory," he said. "Structure and contents."

"Then you've come to the right tower. We're short on both."

He followed her up the spiral stair, noting in his ledger-book the iron treads worn to a bright groove by decades of feet, the paint peeling in long curls from the walls, the smell of burning oil and salt that lived in the masonry itself. She climbed ahead of him without hurrying, without pausing, and he found himself matching his breath to hers and resenting it a little.

In the lens room the great lamp sat under its crystal housing like something sleeping, and beyond the glass the whole gray world turned: sea, sky, the drowned rocks offshore where the swell broke white and endless. She watched him rather than the view.

"You'll want to write down the light," she said. "That's what they do, isn't it? Put things on lists so they can be taken properly."

"The requisition is temporary," he said, and heard how the sentence sounded — recited, weightless, a coin passed without counting it. "For the duration of the emergency."

"Of course." She took up her rag again and worked it along the base of the housing in slow circles. "And when is the emergency dated from? I'd like to mark my calendar. People forget to mark their calendars, and then emergencies go on longer than anyone intended."

He wrote *one (1) first-order Fresnel lens, condition excellent* in his book, because his hand needed somewhere to go. "Mrs. Villalobos—"

"Kestrel," she said, not looking up. "My husband's name was Tomás. You can use either; I'll answer to both and mean neither."

* * *

He came back the next week, because the inventory required it, and then the week after, because he told himself it still required it. The truth, which he did not examine closely, was that she was the only person in the town who spoke to him as though he were a mind and not a uniform, and that being spoken to that way was a kind of thirst he hadn't known he had.

On the fourth visit she made tea — real tea, hoarded from some private reserve — and set out two cups on a scarred table in the keeper's quarters, and asked him questions.

Not the way townspeople asked, with fear or flattery. She asked the way examiners ask.

"You measure harbors," she said, pouring. "In your profession."

"When they require defense, yes."

"And who decides when they require it?"

"The War Department."

"Mm." She sipped. "And does the War Department decide before or after it tells the newspapers there's an emergency?"

"The emergency is real, Mrs.— Kestrel. There are ships off the point. There is a war coming."

"There may be," she agreed pleasantly. "Ships are easy to find off any point if you look for them. Tell me — when your engineers surveyed this harbor, did they also survey the tide tables for the fish landings? Because I'm told your maps include the tonnage our piers can carry, and I confess I've never known a cannon to need a pier."

The wind filled the pause for her. He looked at his tea and found he had no answer that was both true and safe, and discovered, with a small internal shock, that she had known he would not — that the question had been built the way his bridges were built, with the load applied exactly where the span was weakest.

"The maps are standard issue," he said finally.

"Drawn in January?"

His head came up. She was watching him over the rim of her cup, and there was nothing cruel in her face, only a kind of weary precision, the patience of someone who kept a light burning against storms and expected them.

"How do you know the dates?" he asked.

"A widow sees a great deal from a tower. Men walk past the customs house with rolled papers under their arms, and they don't walk like soldiers carrying orders. They walk like clerks carrying accounts." She set down the cup. "Here is my question, Lieutenant, and you may answer it or drink your tea and leave, and we will remain polite either way. If this requisition is for defense — whose defense? Because from where I sit, every night, with the best glass on this coast, I have watched your men measure the harbor, the piers, the fish yards, and the boundaries of forty-one private holdings. Not once have I seen them measure the sea."

The lamp above them turned its slow wheel of light across the ceiling. He thought: *I should report this conversation.* He thought: *Report it to whom?*

"The sea doesn't need measuring," he said. "It's already there."

"Yes," Kestrel Villalobos said softly. "So are we. But someone seems to feel otherwise."

* * *

That night he did not sleep. He lay in the requisitioned billet with the shutter knocking its slow rhythm and went back through everything in the crates, memory turning pages: January, February, March, April. The maps growing finer. The boundary lines tightening around the harbor like a hand closing. And underneath it all, the date of the war order sitting fat and official in June, arriving months late to a theft that had been rehearsed without it.

A prudent officer would note the discrepancy and forward it up the chain, where it would pass through the hands of Major Halloway, whose signature was on the requisition, and die there between one desk and the next.

A foolish officer might keep a copy.

He got up, lit the lamp, and sat with his pen poised over a fresh sheet for a long time. Outside, far down the promontory, the lighthouse swept its beam across the water, once every thirty seconds, patient as a heartbeat — a light that existed for the sole purpose of telling the truth about where the rocks were.

He began to write. Not a report. A list. Dates, map numbers, parcel numbers, the tonnage figures no gunner would ever need. Just the facts, in his own small precise hand, folded twice and sewn into the lining of his kit bag, where no inventory would think to look.

It was, he understood, a small treason. It felt, for the first time in three years of service, like an honest act.

Three days later, a clerk from headquarters came to the customs house to collect the survey crates for "consolidation," and Ashe stood aside and watched eleven months of evidence carried out the door — and understood that someone, somewhere, had decided what he was permitted to see.

That evening he walked the cliff path to the lighthouse, uninvited, with nothing in his hands but his cap, and found Kestrel Villalobos in the doorway of the workshop below the tower, waiting as though she had counted on his arrival to the minute.

"Well, Lieutenant," she said. "You look like a man who has finally read the dates. Come in. There are names you ought to learn."
