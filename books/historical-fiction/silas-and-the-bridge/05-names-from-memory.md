## 5. Names from Memory

The first page I wrote was Tomás's.

I sat in the lighthouse gallery with the storm glass clouding and clearing at the window beside me, a board across my knees for a desk, and I wrote his name the way he had taught me to write it — *Tomás Villalobos, keeper of the light* — and beneath it the date of our marriage, which I knew better than my own heartbeat, and the date of his death, which I knew better than that.

The ink shivered because my hand did. That was all.

A ledger is only names, Tomás used to say, but a name is only a promise that someone was here. He had kept the town's book the way he kept the lamp: nightly, without ceremony, without fail. Births in one column, deaths in another, deeds and debts and christenings and quarrels settled over herring prices in the margins. When the soldiers carried it off in its oilcloth wrapping like something they'd caught and killed, they took more than paper. They took the proof that we had ever been.

So I would make it again. Not from capitals or battlefields. From memory, which no requisition order had yet learned to seize.

I began with what lived closest to my own skin. The Villalobos line, four generations of keepers back to old Esteban who built the first tower of timber and lost it to a gale and built it again of stone out of pure stubbornness. The Amberdales, my mother's people, shipwrights gone now but their keels still under half the boats in the harbor. Then outward, name by name, street by street, the way a lamp throws its beam — near things sharp, far things dimmer but there.

By the third night I had forty-one names and I hated every one of them, because forty-one was nothing. The town held better than two hundred souls. And memory alone, I knew, was a lamp with a cracked lens: it threw light, but it bent it. I needed other eyes. Other hands.

I needed the town.

* * *

Marta Ossian's cottage leaned against the cliff path like an old woman leaning on a fence, and Marta herself matched it. She was ninety-one by her own account and by mine, though she claimed to have subtracted years during some long-ago quarrel with the priest and never reconciled the figures. Her father had been the harbormaster before the harbormasters were called anything so grand.

She opened her door to my knock and looked past me first, down the path, to where Private Denny stood his morning watch at the corner, stamping his boots against the cold and pretending not to see us.

"They've set a boy to watch you," she said. "Come in before he learns something."

Inside smelled of peat smoke and dried thyme and sixty winters. She poured tea neither of us wanted and sat across from me with her hands folded like a woman preparing to hear confessions, or give them.

"You're rebuilding the book," she said.

It wasn't a question. Word traveled this coast faster than weather.

"I'm trying."

"Then you'll want the Ferrin deed." She closed her eyes, and behind the lids I could see her walking through decades. "Spring of 'sixty-one, before your time. Old Ferrin sold the north pasture to the Brennans, and there was a paper, signed at my father's table because Ferrin's house had flooded. My father witnessed it. So did Father Anselm, God keep him. Half the town said the Brennans paid too much and half said too little, and Ferrin himself said nothing because he was dead of a lung fever by June." Her eyes opened. "No one else living remembers that sale. The Brennans' sons think the pasture came to them from God."

"Marta—"

"And the Quill boat debt," she went on, relentless as tide. "'Seventy-eight. Young Aldous Quill borrowed against his hull from the Merripen fund and repaid it in fish over four seasons, and the mark was made in your husband's ledger each quarter, because your husband kept the fund's accounts too, though he'd have bitten his tongue off before admitting he did work for the Merrypens after what was said at the wedding."

She talked for two hours. I wrote until my fingers cramped. Deeds and debts, marriages and mended fences, the price of a coffin in 'eighty-two and who had paid it and who still owed. Somewhere in the middle of it she stopped being a witness and became a conspirator, and I felt the change in the room like a shift of wind.

"They can burn paper," she said at last, quietly, looking into her cold tea. "They burned my mother's letters when the cholera year ended. Said it was sanitation." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Paper burns. I'm still here. Write it all down, Kestrel. Write down that I am still here."

* * *

The fisherfolk gave me dates.

That was their gift, and I hadn't expected it. I had thought of memory as an old woman's thing — soft, story-shaped, unreliable at the edges. But men whose lives depend on reading water and sky keep accounts differently. They remember storms the way priests remember saints' days.

Colm Brannick met me at the harbor wall with three of his crew, out of earshot of the sentry by maybe ten yards, speaking low and fast while gutting knives worked in their hands.

"The Gale of the ninth of November, eighty-nine," Colm said. "You'd have that in the book, keeper's wife — losses that winter. The *Sweet Amity*, the *Kestrel's Grace* — no relation to you, that was Pellow's boat — and the *Marguerite* run aground on the Teeth with all hands saved and the hull lost. Your Tomás recorded every hull lost since he took the light. Ask any man here and he'll tell you the same list."

And they did, one after another, the same list in different mouths, correcting each other on tonnage and insurance claims and which widow was owed what from the mutual fund and whether the payment had been made at Michaelmas or a fortnight later. Dates fell from them like fish from a net. Debts, repayments, shares, the exact split agreed between the Brannicks and the Hallorans for the salvage of the *Amity*'s cargo — disputed for a decade, settled finally in Tomás's ledger, and now remembered by both families with perfect clarity and opposite conclusions.

"That's why they took the book," Halloran said suddenly, pausing with his knife in the air. "Not for the army's sake. If the record's gone, every debt's open again. Every boundary's open. Every claim anybody ever settled." He looked around at the others. "We're not just names to them. We're unpaid accounts."

The wind came off the water then, hard enough to sting, and nobody spoke for a moment. Down the quay, Private Denny shifted his weight and turned away, giving us his back — and I understood, in the way you understand a tide change without seeing it move, that the boy had heard everything, and had chosen not to have heard it.

* * *

I went door to door through the whole gray length of that week, and the week after, up the hill lanes and along the shore road, with my board and my ink bottle wrapped in wool against the cold. Soldiers watched me everywhere — at corners, at the well, lounging outside the chapel yard where I copied gravestone inscriptions because stone remembers even when paper doesn't. They watched, and I let them watch, and I learned something about watching: it goes both ways.

At first the doors opened slowly. People were afraid; I don't pretend otherwise. Fear sat in this town like fog, in every parlor, in every pause before anyone spoke above a murmur. The Brennan widow crossed herself before she let me in, as if testimony might be a sin the army had ruled punishable. Young Mrs. Pellow wept through her whole account of the *Kestrel's Grace* and apologized for it, twice.

But fear, given somewhere to go, becomes something else.

It happened gradually, and then all at once. The cooper's wife told me about the deed to the cooperage and then, lowering her voice, about the letter her brother had received from a land agent in the capital — an offer, dated *before* the warships ever anchored, offering to buy their property "contingent upon government acquisition." She had thought it a swindle and thrown it in the fire. She described the seal anyway, from memory, and her hands shook as she did it.

Old Piet Halloran drew me a map of the harbor lots as they were parceled in his grandfather's day, on the back of a fish invoice, and made me swear to copy it and return the original to him, which I did, and which he hid inside the lining of his best coat, the coat he'd be buried in, he said, where no requisition order would follow him.

The schoolteacher, Miss Ferrow, brought me her class registers going back thirty years — birthdates, parentages, the whole spine of a generation — and asked only that I note in the new ledger that she had contributed them. "Put my name in it," she said. "If they take it again, let them take my name knowing it was given freely."

Evening by evening I climbed the cliff path with my pockets full of scraps — invoices, letters, drawings, recitations taken down in shorthand — and climbed the tower stairs, and transcribed by lamplight until the oil ran low. Forty-one names became ninety. Ninety became a hundred and thirty. The margins filled with Marta's deeds, Colm's storms, the cooper's seal, Miss Ferrow's registers. It wasn't Tomás's ledger. His hand had been steady and certain, sure of its authority. Mine was a raft built of other people's courage, patched nightly, barely seaworthy.

But it floated. And it grew.

On the twelfth night, copying the Ferrin deed from Marta's dictation for the second time — I always took the oldest testimonies twice — I heard boots on the tower stairs. Not the sentry's tread. Slower. Hesitant.

Lieutenant Ashe stood in the doorway of the lamp room, cap in his hands, snow melting on his shoulders, and looked at the pages spread across my table with an expression I couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to.

"Mrs. Villalobos," he said. "There are things I need to ask you. About the maps."

He stepped inside, and pulled the door shut against the storm behind him.
