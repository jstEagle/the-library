## 6. The Speculator's Shadow

The quartermaster's tent smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and by the third week of the occupation Lieutenant Elias Ashe had learned to hate it more than he had ever hated any trench or drill field, because at least those places were honest about what they were. This was a room of paper pretending to be a war.

He sat at a trestle table beneath a hissing lantern, the inventory ledgers open before him in ranks — harbor lots, net sheds, the salt-house, the cooperage, forty-one parcels of the town below the cliff, each with its assessed value entered in a neat column in his own hand. He had been trained at Woolwich to make columns like these: exact, unimpeachable, dull as ballast. A good inventory was supposed to be the most boring document in the army. So why, he asked himself for the hundredth time, did this one feel like a loaded gun?

He turned back to the seizure maps. They had come with the requisition order from the District Office, folded into oilcloth, and they were beautiful work — surveyor's ink, precise bearings, every parcel numbered. Too beautiful. Ashe had surveyed enough coastline himself to know how long such work took: the soundings, the triangulation, the fair-copying. Months. A year, in weather like this coast's weather.

And the requisition order itself — the order that had supposedly set all this in motion — was dated the eleventh of September.

He laid a tracing sheet over the map corner and copied the surveyor's mark in pencil. Then he pulled the order across the table and wrote beside it: *11 Sept.* The maps bore a completion date of *March*. Six months before the order that authorized them. Somebody had drawn up plans to seize this town half a year before anyone decided there was a reason to.

Unless the reason came first, and the order was written to fit it.

Ashe sat very still and listened to the rain on canvas, and felt something shift in him the way a footing shifts on scree — not a fall yet, only the first small giving-way.

* * *

Corven arrived on a Thursday, unannounced, which was itself an answer to a question Ashe hadn't finished asking. Civilians did not simply walk into a requisitioned military district. But Ambrose Corven came up the harbor road in a hired trap with mud to the axles, wearing a gray coat too fine for the weather, and the sentries saluted the trap before they saluted the man inside it.

Ashe watched from the quartermaster's flap as Corven was shown into Major Halloway's quarters without so much as a signature in the visitors' book. He noted that too. He was beginning to note everything; it was the only defense left to a man whose reports kept vanishing.

For Corven's name had begun appearing in the files like water appearing in a hold — nowhere in particular, everywhere at once. A valuation certificate countersigned *A. Corven, Land Agent* on three parcels. A letter of inquiry about "surplus coastal holdings" on District stationery, unsigned but in a hand that matched the valuations. And yesterday, wedged in the back of the compensation ledger where no one was meant to look, a schedule of sale — eleven parcels listed at prices so far beneath their assessed value that Ashe had checked his arithmetic twice, then a third time, thinking he must have misread shillings for pounds.

He had not misread anything. The fisherman Aldous Renwick's holding, assessed at two hundred and ten pounds, scheduled for sale at thirty-five. The salt-house, four hundred, offered at sixty. Eleven parcels, and behind each figure, in faint pencil, the same initials: a syndicate called the Meridian Coastal Company, registered in the capital, directors unnamed, address a lawyer's chambers.

Requisitioned land, bought cheap from the Crown by men who had known it would be requisitioned before the Crown did.

That evening Ashe walked the cliff path in the dark rather than go to the officers' mess, because he could not sit among men drinking port and making jokes about fishwives while the arithmetic burned in his pocket — he had taken to carrying the figures on his person, written small on a card, after his first memorandum went missing from the district file box between one morning and the next. The sea below was black and enormous. The lighthouse above him turned its white blade through the rain, steady as a heartbeat, and he found himself watching it the way a drowning man watches a shore.

He had interviewed its keeper once. He remembered her questions better than his own answers.

*Who measured the harbor, Lieutenant? And when?*

He hadn't answered honestly then. He was no longer sure he knew what an honest answer looked like, when the honest answer might end his career — or worse, change nothing at all except who got ruined for saying it.

* * *

Halloway summoned him at nine the next morning, and Ashe knew before he crossed the threshold, from the set of the major's shoulders behind the desk, that the summons was a warning dressed as a courtesy.

"Lieutenant." Halloway did not offer him a chair. The major was a heavy, ruddy man with a voice built for parade grounds, and he used it now at quarter-volume, which was somehow worse. "Your inventory is complete?"

"Complete as of Tuesday, sir."

"And your supplementary memoranda?" A pause, delicately weighted. "I seem to be missing some correspondence."

"My memoranda are submitted through channels, sir."

"Through channels." Halloway smiled without warmth. He rose and stood at the map of the coast pinned behind his desk — Ashe saw, with a cold drop of the stomach, that it was the same survey as the seizure maps, same hand, same March date — and tapped the promontory once. "You're a diligent officer, Ashe. That's why you're here. Diligence is a virtue in an inventory clerk." He turned. "It becomes a vice when it outgrows its posting."

"Sir, I don't—"

"You've been pulling valuation schedules," Halloway said. "Cross-referencing registration dates. Writing letters to the Registry Office under your own name." He let that last word hang. "A civilian firm has contracted to purchase certain surplus parcels. Perfectly regular. The Treasury approves such transfers weekly. It is *not*, Lieutenant, the business of a subaltern to audit the Treasury."

"The parcels are being sold at a fraction of assessed value, sir. On paper signed before the requisition existed. Someone should—"

"*Someone*" — Halloway came around the desk, and though he never raised his voice, the room seemed to contract around him — "is Mr. Corven, who advises this command at the personal direction of the District Office, and whose transactions you will treat exactly as you'd treat orders. Which is to say: you will execute them, record them, and keep your conclusions in your head, where they can do no damage." He stopped an arm's length away. He smelled of bay rum and something underneath it, older, like a cellar. "You have a career ahead of you, Ashe. I've read your file. First in surveying. A name worth keeping clean. Don't write things down you wouldn't want read back to you at a court of inquiry."

"Am I under inquiry, sir?"

"You're under *orders*." Halloway returned to his chair, and the warmth of a host reassembled over his face like ice reforming on water. "Redraft the compensation ledger. Clean copy. Some of the earlier entries contain errors. You'll initial the corrections."

Errors meaning figures corrected upward, Ashe understood, so the sale prices would look less like robbery. Errors meaning his own careful numbers rewritten by another hand and made official under his initials.

"Yes, sir," he heard himself say.

He walked back to the quartermaster's tent through the rain with his ears roaring, and only when he was inside did he understand that his hands were shaking — not from fear, he told himself, but from the peculiar violence of having seen a thing plainly and agreed to unsee it. Power did not always corrupt by tempting. Sometimes it corrupted by simply informing you what your career weighed, in pounds and shillings, and waiting to see if you'd take the money or the truth. He had stood in that warm office and he had said *yes, sir*, and some part of him had already begun, quietly, to rot.

He refused to let all of him do it.

* * *

That night he began to copy documents.

Not steal them — he was done with originals; originals vanished, and their disappearance accused no one but the file clerk. Copies. He worked after midnight by shielded lantern, transcribing onto tissue-thin paper in a hand smaller than his natural one: the sale schedule with all eleven parcels and both sets of figures. Corven's valuation certificates. The survey mark and its impossible date. The draft of Halloway's order to the Registry Office declaring the town's incorporation records nonexistent — a declaration, Ashe now realized, that must have preceded the destruction of whatever papers had actually existed, since the keeper woman spoke of deeds and debts as things she had held in her hands.

Where did he hide them? Not in the tent, not in the district files, not anywhere with a lock, because locks invited keys and keys belonged to other men. In the end he sewed a oilcloth packet inside the lining of his greatcoat, flat against the small of his back, and wore his evidence like a second spine. He slept badly. He woke at every footstep. But the coat went everywhere he went, and no one ordered a lieutenant to strip in front of his major, not yet.

On the fifth night, copying the Registry declaration by lantern light, he heard footsteps outside the canvas and killed the flame and sat in absolute darkness with his heart slamming, listening to a sentry's boots pass and fade. When the light came back, the ink had blurred where a drop of rain through a seam had fallen on the page, and he blotted it carefully, and finished.

He thought, as he sewed the pages in, of the widow Villalobos sitting across from him with her hands folded, asking her quiet terrible questions. She had said — he could still hear it — *"Paper is just names wearing uniforms, Lieutenant. The question is whether the names remember what the uniforms forget."* He had thought it rustic poetry then. Now he understood it as instruction. Names were the whole of it. His name in a file. Her town's names in a ledger someone had burned or buried. Corven's name on certificates signed months before the law allowed.

There was a knock at the tent pole. Two knocks, unhurried.

"Lieutenant Ashe." A voice he didn't know. "Major's compliments. Your redrafted ledger is wanted first thing. And, sir—" the pause was perfectly polite, perfectly menacing— "the major asks that you submit no further correspondence through channels. Hand-carried only, from tonight. For security."

Security. The word settled over him like snowfall, soft and total, burying everything it touched.

"Ashe acknowledges," he said, and listened to the boots retreat, and sat down again at the table. Before him lay the last item he had not yet copied: a single sheet found tucked inside Corven's valuation folder, undated, listing the Meridian Coastal Company's intended purchases *in full* — every parcel in the town, the lighthouse point included, marked with a penciled note in a confident hand:

*Light to be decommissioned upon transfer. Structure expendable.*

Structure expendable. The workshop below the cliff. Perhaps the light itself.

Ashe looked at the words a long time. Outside, the storm that had been building all week began to arrive in earnest, rain driving against the canvas like flung gravel, and somewhere above the cliff the great lamp swung its beam through the chaos, patient, relentless, keeping faith with ships that had no idea what men on land intended.

He folded the sheet along its old creases, took up his needle and thread, and began to sew it into the lining of his coat with fingers that, by the last stitch, had gone quite steady.
