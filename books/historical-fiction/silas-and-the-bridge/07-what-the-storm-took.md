## 7. What the Storm Took

The storm came in from the southeast on a Tuesday, the way bad news always seemed to, arriving before anyone had properly braced for it. Kestrel felt it first in her teeth — an old ache that had lived there since Tomás's funeral, since she had stood at the graveside in rain and heard the sea say what it always said: *I take what I want.* By dusk the wind was worrying the lamp room like a hand testing a lock, and by full dark it was screaming down the chimney of her kitchen, scattering ash across the hearthstones.

She did not mind. The storm gave cover.

For eleven weeks now — she counted them the way other widows counted coins — she had been building a book out of nothing. Out of memory, out of Marta's cracked voice reciting deed dates from forty years past, out of the fisherfolk's debts scratched onto slate behind the chandlery door, out of pages salvaged from the ledger's burning-corner when the soldiers had come for it in October and left only scraps. The reconstruction lived in a canvas oilskin bag beneath her bed by night and traveled with her by day, wrapped in her shawl like a sick child. Every name she wrote into it was an act of quiet treason. She knew this. She wrote anyway, in Tomás's old hand, which hers had grown to resemble over the years until sometimes she could not tell whose letters were whose.

Tonight the wind would keep the garrison indoors. Tonight she meant to finish the Aldous family entries — four generations of net-menders, all their debts and dowries — and then copy out the harbor leases from the papers Old Marta had sewn into her petticoat hem like contraband lace.

She had the ink open when the knock came. Three blows, spaced like artillery. Not a neighbor's knock. A neighbor knocked twice and called your name through the wood.

Kestrel capped the ink without hurry. She slid the ledger into its place between the flour sack and the wall — not under the bed tonight; too obvious — and put the kettle on the stove as though she had been doing nothing all evening but waiting for tea. Then she opened the door.

Two soldiers stood in the squall, rain streaming off their cap brims, and behind them, half-sheltered under the eave, a sergeant she recognized from the inventory detail. Pell. A thin man with a clerk's hands and no imagination, which made him dangerous in the way a straightedge is dangerous: he could draw only one kind of line.

"Mrs. Villalobos." He raised his voice against the wind without stepping inside. "We're searching dwellings tonight. Requisition order, section nine."

"Searching for what?" she asked, though she knew, and he knew she knew, and they both let the lie stand because the lie was the whole architecture now. The requisition claimed there was no record of the town's names. If no record existed, there was nothing to search for. The fact that they searched proved everything the paper denied.

"Contraband documents," Pell said. "Unauthorized writings pertaining to property."

"You mean names." The wind took her voice and threw it back at her. "You're looking for people, Sergeant. Written down where your masters can't burn them."

Pell's jaw tightened. "Are you going to admit me, or do I fetch the major's warrant?"

She stood aside. What else could she do? Refusal was a door slamming, and slammed doors brought more doors, brought men who did not knock at all.

* * *

They went through the lighthouse like a tide going through a wreck.

It was worse than she had feared and better than she'd dreaded. They turned out her drawers, ran bayonet points along book spines — Tomás's books, navigation tables, the Bible with his family births inscribed inside the cover, which one soldier read aloud with a sneer before Pell told him to put it down, names in a family Bible being apparently beneath suspicion. They opened the salt box. They prodded the mattress with rifle butts. One of them found Tomás's pipe on the mantel, packed it absently with his thumb, and set it down again, and Kestrel felt that small desecration more sharply than all the rest — the thought of another man's fingers in the bowl where her husband's tobacco had burned.

Through it all she sat at the table with her tea cooling, and she kept her face the way she kept the light: steady, revolving, revealing nothing.

But they saved the workshop for last, and standing at its threshold Pell went still in a way that told her everything. Of course. The workshop below the cliff was where the ledger had lived for thirty years. It was where a man rebuilding a ledger would work. They had come for the reconstruction, and they knew exactly which stone to lift.

"Light the lamps," Pell said.

The workshop smelled as it always smelled — linseed oil, cedar shavings, the ghost of Tomás's pipe smoke soaked so deep into the beams that three years of absence had not driven it out. She lit two lamps with hands that did not shake, and hated herself a little for how well she performed calm, and watched the soldiers begin.

They emptied the tool chests. They unrolled drawings on the floor — Tomás's old plans for the harbor winch, for the chapel roof, harmless things — and studied each one as though a list of nails might hide a nation. They pulled books off the shelves and fanned the pages. And Kestrel stood among them with her heart going like the fog bell, because the canvas bag was not here, it was under the flour, it was safe, it was safe —

Except.

On the workbench, half-buried under a drop cloth, lay her practice sheets. Eleven weeks of discarded drafts: trial alphabets, test entries, a page where she'd copied out the Brannock debt twice to get the figures even. Nothing incriminating alone. But together, spread under a lamp — together they said *someone is writing this town down again.*

Pell gathered them up slowly, square by square, the way a man gathers cards he suspects are marked.

"Copious notes," he said softly. "For a woman with no records."

"I'm a widow," Kestrel said. "I write letters. I write lists. I write anything at all to fill the hours you've given me, since you've taken my husband's work and my husband's house is a barracks and my husband's town is a fence." Her voice did not rise. That was the trick of it, the thing Tomás had taught her without meaning to: the light does not shout. "If lists are contraband, arrest me for the shopping."

Pell looked at her a long moment. Rain hammered the little window above the bench. Somewhere overhead the great lens turned, sweeping its beam across the chaos of the sea, and the shadow of it wheeled slow through the workshop like the hand of an enormous clock.

"We're done here," he said at last, and pocketed the practice sheets, and she understood: he had nothing, and he knew he had nothing, but the sheets bought him a report to write and a reason to come back. That was how they worked. Not with proof. With appetite.

They tramped out into the storm. She bolted the workshop door behind them, stood in the dark smelling linseed and wet wool, and listened to her own blood.

Then she went upstairs, knelt, and moved the flour.

* * *

The oil store was a stone chamber at the tower's base, built into the living rock, where the winter's colza oil stood in iron tanks and the air was thick enough to taste. Oil-soaked rags hung in a ventilated cabinet. The walls sweated. It stank of fat and mineral sharpness, and it was the one place in the lighthouse no soldier would rummage willingly — not with their uniforms and their paper documents, not with fire regulations drilled into every noncom. Pell might imagine a hidden ledger. No one imagined it in a room built for combustion.

She worked fast. From the false bottom of Tomás's sea chest she lifted out the packet she had prepared weeks ago and never needed until now: oilcloth, tarred twine, a tin document box that had once held chronometer spare parts. Into the tin went the ledger — four hundred pages, heavy as a brick, damp-edged despite all her care — and around it the testimonies, Marta's sewn papers, the fisherfolk's slates rubbed clean and rewritten on proper stock. She sealed the tin with tallow, wrapped it in oilcloth, tied it with the patience of a woman rigging something to survive a drowning, and lowered it into the gap between the second oil tank and the wall, behind a loose stone Tomás had mortared crooked on purpose, years ago, for storing valuables away from thieves.

*He always planned for loss,* she thought, kneeling in the reek of oil with her arms black to the elbow. *He just never thought the thief would be the army.*

She reset the stone. She wiped the tank rim. She climbed the stairs into the wind and stood a moment in the dark of the entry hall, shaking now that it was done, the shaking coming up from somewhere below discipline.

That was when she saw the letter.

It had fallen behind the sea chest — dislodged, she supposed, when she'd opened the false bottom, or perhaps by the soldiers' rifling earlier, or perhaps it had waited there for years the way some truths wait, patient as seed. A single envelope, foxed with damp, addressed in a hand she knew better than her own heartbeat: *Mrs. K. Villalobos*, and beneath it, smaller, in the same hand: *to be opened when I am wrong about something.*

Tomás's writing.

Her knees went to the flagstones. She held the envelope without opening it for what felt like a full turn of the lamp above her — one full revolution of the light, thirty seconds of dark and bright, dark and bright. Then she broke the seal.

*My Kess,* it began. *If you're reading this, then either I've died a fool or lived long enough to be proven one, and I'd wager on the former, knowing my luck.*

*I've been to Whitstable. I've seen the maps Corven carries — the ones he says are for the railway concern. They mark our harbor, our point, our cliff land, and the boundaries drawn have nothing to do with any railway. They match the old incorporation grant, line for line, except the grant leaves us out. Someone has redrawn the town to make it look like it never legally existed. He means to have it declared empty on paper and sold filled with people. I've written to the county registrar. I don't know if it will matter. Paper against paper, and his is newer.*

*Keep the ledger current. Keep it true. A town is not its buildings, love. It's the record that anyone was ever here at all. If they take everything else, don't let them take that.*

*Yours entirely, T.*

Dated March. Tomás had died in June. Three months he had carried this knowledge, writing letters to registrars, watching Corven's maps, saying nothing to her — protecting her, she supposed, or sparing her, or simply hoping to fix it himself the way he fixed everything with his hands, quietly, before she ever noticed it was broken.

Grief came up in her like groundwater, sudden and salt. She let it. She knelt on the cold flags with the storm shouting at the tower and cried for the man who had seen the flood coming and died with his boots dry and his warning folded away behind a sea chest.

And then, because she was her father's daughter and Tomás's wife and keeper of a light that had not missed a single night in thirty-one years, she stopped crying, read the letter twice more, and began to think.

Corven's maps predated the war order. She had suspected it; now she had Tomás's word, in his own hand, dated. The registrar at Whitstable might hold whatever reply Tomás received — or the copies Tomás sent. Evidence existed outside this promontory, outside Halloway's reach. Evidence with dates on it.

She folded the letter into her bodice, over her heart, where she could feel it with every breath. The ledger slept in the oil store below, wrapped like a body prepared for burial — no, she corrected herself, climbing toward the light. Wrapped like a body prepared for waking.

Outside, the storm was turning. She could feel it in the tower's bones, the pressure shifting, the wind swinging north with a new and colder voice. The glass in the lamp room was falling faster than she liked, and the sea beyond the point had gone that particular black-and-green that meant the weather had not yet spent half its fury.

Three days, the fishermen had said, looking at the sky and not liking what they read there. Maybe four. A storm to end storms, the kind the oldest charts marked with a cross.

In the garrison headquarters down the hill, she would have wagered the demolition orders were already signed, waiting only for decent weather to serve as their excuse.

Let them come, she thought, trimming the wick, watching the flame stand tall against the hammering dark. Let them bring their matches. They should know by now what a keeper does with fire.

She had three days to get word to Lieutenant Ashe — and she intended to spend every hour of them.
