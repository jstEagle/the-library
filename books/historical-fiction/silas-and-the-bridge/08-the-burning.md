## 8. The Burning

The storm came out of the west at dusk, and it came like something that had been waiting.

Kestrel felt it first in the lamp room, a shudder through the glass that made the flame gutter though every draft was sealed. The barometer in Tomás's old case had fallen all day, the needle sagging lower and lower until it seemed to apologize. By four o'clock the sea had gone a strange bruised green, and by six the wind arrived — not gusting but *arriving*, whole, as if it had crossed three hundred miles of open water without once touching land, saving itself entire for this coast, this night, this town.

She trimmed the wick and set the clockwork turning. The light swept out into the murk and was swallowed almost at once, its beam bent sideways by rain moving horizontal as thrown gravel. Somewhere below, the town was shuttering its windows. She could not see it, but she could feel it, the way one feels a hand pressed flat against one's own chest — the whole settlement holding itself down against the dark.

The demolition order had come at noon.

A corporal had nailed it to the customs house door while the first drops were falling, and Kestrel had stood in the street reading it twice through the wet. EFFECTIVE UPON RECEIPT OF FURTHER ORDERS: ALL STRUCTURES BELOW THE HIGH-WATER ROAD TO BE CLEARED. Which meant the fish sheds. The salt houses. And the workshop — Tomás's workshop, with his bench still bearing the grooves of his chisels, the smell of cedar shavings that ten years had not driven out.

*Upon receipt of further orders.* The phrase was doing a coward's work. It meant whenever Corven pleased. It meant soon.

Kestrel climbed down from the lamp room as the storm found its full voice. Below the cliff, half-hidden in the rain, she saw lanterns moving along the shore road — soldiers, working fast, hammering planks over windows they intended soon to remove entirely. And among them, unmistakable even at distance by his height and the way he walked slightly ahead of his escort rather than behind it, Lieutenant Ashe, going door to door in the deluge, speaking to people through cracks in doors, writing in a book of his own.

She watched him a long moment. Whatever game the boy was playing, he had looked at her on Tuesday with the face of a man carrying a weight he could no longer set down.

It did not matter tonight. Tonight there was only the ledger, finished at last after five months of memory and testimony and Marta's crooked recitations, wrapped in oilcloth and sleeping inside the oil store where no search had thought to look. Two hundred and eleven names. Every deed, every debt, every birth and burial this gray promontory had ever claimed for its own.

Tonight she would bring it up to the light itself. If the storm wanted the town, let it find her standing in the middle of it, holding the proof.

* * *

Ashe had known the fire would come before he knew anything else about that night.

He understood it the way one understands a lie in a dispatch — not from evidence but from the shape of the silence around it. At nine o'clock Halloway's adjutant had handed him a sealed envelope and told him to remain at the quartermaster's office until relieved. No patrol assignment. No inventory work. Just: *remain*. In five months he had never once been told to do nothing, and he had learned what ordered idleness meant on this coast. It meant someone else had been given a task that would not bear witnesses.

He waited forty minutes. Then he put out his lamp, went out the back, and ran into the teeth of the storm.

The workshop stood at the base of the cliff path, black against the blacker sea, and Ashe saw the problem immediately: two soldiers with a covered lantern loitering near the salt shed, pretending a casualness their boots betrayed. He circled wide through the dune grass, rain driving so hard it left welts on his face, and came at the building from the seaward side where the cliff's shadow pooled thickest.

He smelled it before he reached the door. Coal oil — not lighthouse coal oil, which was clean, but the cheap yellow stuff from the army stores. Freshly spilled. Soaked, from the smell of it, into the sill and the shingles beneath the east window.

His heart did something complicated then — a single beat that contained fury and fear and a terrible clarity all at once. They were going to burn it with the ledger inside. They believed the ledger was inside. Someone had talked, or someone had guessed, or Corven had simply decided that a building was easier to lose than an argument.

The two soldiers at the salt shed struck a match.

Ashe did not decide anything. Later, when he tried to reconstruct the moment, he would find no decision in it at all — only motion, only the animal certainty that some lines, once crossed, cannot be uncrossed. He came out of the dark at them shouting, using his rank like a weapon because it was the only one he carried: *"Lieutenant Ashe! Step away from that structure! That is a direct order!"*

They turned. The match died in the wet. For three seconds nobody moved except the rain.

"Sir," said the older one carefully, "we've got our instructions."

"You've got mine now. Fall back to the shore road. If anyone asks, Lieutenant Ashe ordered a perimeter check." His voice sounded strange to him, steadier than he felt. "Go."

They went — slowly, exchanging a look he chose not to interpret, and perhaps choosing not to interpret it was the last act of the officer he had been that morning.

Then the window above him bloomed orange anyway.

Someone else had come from the other side. Or a fuse already laid had simply needed its hour. It hardly mattered. Fire took the workshop's interior in one long exhalation, and through the glass Ashe saw the flames find Tomás Villalobos's bench, his tools, the cedar smell that must have gone up like a sacrament — and he turned and ran for the cliff path, because if the ledger was in there, then everything was in there, and there was no tribunal yet, no record, nothing but ash and a speculator's clean hands.

* * *

Kestrel was halfway down the cliff stairs when the workshop caught.

She had the ledger strapped to her back under her oilskins, and the weight of it — eleven pounds of paper and ink and other people's lives — swung against her spine as she stopped dead on the wet stone steps. Below her, fire stood up out of the workshop roof like something being born, orange against the rain, and the heat reached her even at fifty yards, even through the deluge, a breath of summer in the middle of all that winter.

She did not scream. She noted, distantly, that she did not scream, and that this seemed somehow the worst part — that grief had burned itself down to coals so banked and old that even this could not wake it fully. Ten years ago she had lost Tomás to the sea and learned that the body does its loudest work alone, in the years after, not the minutes.

But then she saw movement at the burning door. A figure — tall, coat streaming, hand raised against the heat — hauling something out. Not a thing. *Someone.* Old Marta, God help her, Marta who slept sometimes in the workshop's back room since her own cottage flooded in October — Marta stumbling out into the rain with the lieutenant's arm around her, both of them coughing, alive, alive.

And behind them the flames went through the roof, and the workshop became a lantern, and everything Tomás had ever touched turned to light and went up into the storm.

* * *

They met in the lee of the sea wall, the four of them — Kestrel, Ashe, shivering Marta wrapped in his officer's cloak, and the storm shouting overhead. Embers from the workshop were flying inland like a migration of small red birds, dying one by one on the wet grass.

"The ledger," Ashe said. Rain ran off his chin. He was looking at the strap across her chest, at the shape beneath the oilskin, and his voice cracked on the word. "Tell me you have it."

"Why do you think I'm standing here instead of running into that fire?"

Something went out of his shoulders then — a tension held so long it had become architecture. He laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and put his hand briefly over his eyes.

"I couldn't stop it," he said. "There were men on both sides. I got Marta out and I couldn't stop it."

"I know." She looked past him at the burning building, at the silhouette of the chimney still standing, absurd, upright, keeping its own kind of watch. "I saw you go in for her."

"It wasn't for her." His jaw worked. "It was — I don't even know what it was for. I've spent five months writing reports that disappeared before they reached anyone who mattered, and tonight I finally disobeyed a direct order and saved one old woman and lost a building, and none of it—" He stopped. Breathed. "None of it is enough. Unless the tribunal happens. Unless this ends."

Kestrel unwound the strap and drew the ledger out into the thin shelter of the sea wall, peeling back the oilcloth just far enough to show him the first page. *A Record of the Names, Deeds, and Debts of the Town at Gray Promontory, Begun by Tomás Villalobos, Keeper, and Continued by Kestrel Villalobos, Keeper.* Two hundred and eleven names in her hand and Marta's and the fisherfolk's, each one witnessed, each one dated.

And beneath the ledger, second bundle, smaller: the papers she had taken from Corven's dropped satchel in October, the ones Ashe had helped her identify — the forged incorporation certificates, the syndicate's purchase options dated months before any war order existed. Damp at the edges. Whole.

"The fire took the past," she said. "It didn't take the proof."

Marta, coughing, pulled the cloak tighter and said, in her cracked voice that carried anyway, "Then it's tomorrow, is it? The tribunal?"

Ashe looked at the burning workshop, at the soldiers' lanterns now converging along the shore road, at the storm that had swallowed the moon and given them this one dark hour to be honest in.

"At dawn," he said. "Halloway convened it himself — he thinks Corven's evidence will bury me. He doesn't know what we're carrying." He met Kestrel's eyes. "Mrs. Villalobos. When you stand up in front of those men and read those names — read mine too. Write me into it. Because whatever happens in that room, one of us is walking out of it erased, and I have decided it will not be this town."

Above them the workshop's chimney finally leaned, hung, and fell into the sea, and the sound it made — a long stone sigh, swallowed by the surf — was the last sound the old building ever gave. Kestrel put her hand flat on the ledger's cover, feeling through the oilcloth the faint warmth the fire had left in the world.

"Dawn, then," she said. "Bring your courage, Lieutenant. I'll bring the names."
