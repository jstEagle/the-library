## 10. Keeper of Names

The ice went out of the harbor in the third week of March, and with it went the army.

Kestrel Villalobos stood at the gallery rail of the lighthouse and watched the transports take on the last of the soldiers. The morning was pale and rinsed clean, the kind of light that comes after a long winter and seems almost apologetic about it — thin gold on gray water, gulls wheeling and crying over the masts. Below her, the town had turned out to watch them go, though no one cheered. There was nothing triumphant in it. The soldiers were mostly boys, she saw now, young faces at the rail looking back at a coast they had been sent to measure and steal, and some of them looked sorry. One of them raised a hand — not quite a wave, not quite a salute — and Kestrel found herself raising hers before she could decide whether she meant it.

Lieutenant Ashe stood at the foot of the gangplank. Even at this distance she knew the set of his shoulders, that careful, upright bearing he wore like a uniform under his uniform. He did not board until the last crate was swung up and stowed. Then he paused, turned toward the town, and looked up — not at her, precisely; a man could not have known where she stood behind the glass — but at the light itself, the tower, the whole stubborn architecture of it. He touched his cap. Then he climbed aboard, and the transport's engines woke the water, and by noon the horizon held nothing but horizon.

She stayed at the rail a long while after. The wind smelled of thaw and kelp and, faintly, of tar from the burned ruin of the workshop below the cliff, where the charred beams still stood like ribs picked clean. She had not gone down there since the tribunal. She told herself there was nothing left to salvage. She told herself the ledger was safe, copied twice, locked in the strongbox at the harbor office and again beneath her bed. But grief is a tide; it goes out and it comes back whether you consent to it or not, and some mornings she woke reaching for Tomás across cold linen, and some mornings she woke reaching for the workshop — for the smell of ink and cedar shavings, for the scratch of his pen heard through the floorboards of her sleep.

That morning, for the first time, she made herself walk down.

* * *

The path was soft with mud and new growth. Snowdrops had pushed up through the ash field around the foundation, white and absurd and utterly indifferent to what had been burned above them. Kestrel crouched among them and did not weep, though her throat worked at it. Instead she did what Tomás would have done: she looked closely.

Under a fallen rafter, protected by its own char, lay the iron strongbox he had built for deeds and title papers — warped but whole, its lock fused shut. And wedged in the corner of what had been the drafting table, half-melted but legible, a page of his hand. Not the ledger. Something older. A letter draft, the one the soldier's raid had missed because Tomás had hidden it in the one place no searcher would think to look: in plain sight, filed as scrap. *They mean to make us unrecorded,* he had written to a cousin in the capital, years ago now. *A town without papers is a town without a past, and a town without a past can be sold.* He had suspected it all along. He had died still trying to put the names somewhere they couldn't be taken.

She sat down in the ashes with the page in her lap and let the wind move through the ruined walls, and after a time she understood that this was not a grave. It was a beginning. The workshop had been his. What came next would be theirs — hers, and Marta's, and the fisherfolk's, and every household that had signed the new book — and it would not be kept in any single room that fire or soldiers or time itself could reach into and take.

She folded the letter into her coat, over her heart, and walked back up the path through the snowdrops.

* * *

Incorporation day came in late April, when the mayflowers bloomed along the cliff path and the harbor filled with fishing boats again, their paint bright after the winter's grime.

It was decided — by Marta, who had appointed herself the engine of all civic ceremony and permitted no objection — that it should happen in the open air, on the green above the harbor, where the seizure notices had once been nailed to the post. The post itself had been pulled up and split for kindling within a week of the requisition's collapse; Halloway's disavowal of Corven had come swift and self-serving, the major eager to bury the syndicate's forged papers under his own testimony, and Corven had been taken east in irons to answer for it. Whether justice would be done there, Kestrel doubted. But the paper trail Ashe had copied and smuggled out — page by page, night by night, at the cost of everything a career was supposed to mean — had done its work. The requisition was voided. The land titles were confirmed. And the territory of Grayhaven Point, population two hundred and eleven souls, was incorporated by act of the legislature, with boundaries surveyed by an engineer who was not, this time, measuring for anyone else's profit.

The magistrate read the charter aloud in a voice pitched against the sea wind. Then came the part Kestrel had insisted upon, over Marta's suggestion that a simple signing ceremony would suffice.

"Every household will sign," she said, standing before the long table where the new ledger lay open. "Not the magistrate alone. Not me. Every hand that wants its name in this book will put it there themselves."

They came up one by one. Old Marta signed first, gripping the pen in a fist gnarled like driftwood, writing her name in letters large enough to read across a harbor — MARTA OKONKWO, and beneath it, unbidden, *born on this coast, as was my mother, as was hers.* The Hollis brothers signed together, pressing both hands on the page. The Widow Crane, who had lost her son to the winter and her boat to the storm, signed so small her name was barely a whisper of ink, and Kestrel leaned close and said, "Loudly, Agnes. In here, everything speaks." Agnes looked up at her with wet eyes and wrote it again, larger.

The children signed too — or made their marks, which Kestrel entered alongside their names in her own careful script, the way Tomás had always done, recording the mark and the name together so that neither could be separated later by anyone with an eraser and an appetite. It took three hours. Nobody left. Someone brought cider, then bread, then a fiddle, and the incorporation of Grayhaven Point became, without anyone planning it, the first festival the town had held since before the warships came.

Last of all, when the line was gone and the light going amber over the water, Kestrel signed herself. KESTREL VILLALOBOS, KEEPER OF THE LIGHT. She looked at the name a moment — her own name, restored to a record that had once been altered to erase it, the ink of Corven's forgery cut away by order of the tribunal — and beneath it, in smaller letters, she added: *and keeper of this book, until someone worthy takes it from me.*

* * *

The apprentice arrived in May, and she was not what Kestrel expected.

Her name was Ilsa Brandt, daughter of the Hollis brothers' sister, fourteen years old, all elbows and impatience, with ink already permanently stained into the side of her right hand from copying school exercises. She had volunteered, Marta reported, with the terrifying directness of the young: *"I want to learn the book."* Not the light. The book.

"You understand," Kestrel said on the girl's first evening, standing in the rebuilt records room — for the town had voted to raise a new structure on the old foundation, stone this time, low and windowless on the seaward side, fireproof as human hands could make it — "that this is not a duty of honor. It is a duty of suspicion. You will love these names, and you will trust no one who asks to borrow them."

Ilsa nodded too quickly, the way the young nod when they think they understand.

Kestrel set the ledger on the table between them, opened to a fresh page, and laid out Tomás's tools beside it: the good pen, the blotting sand, the ruler of whalebone worn smooth by thirty years of his grip. "Your husband's methods?" Ilsa asked, eyeing them.

"My husband's rules," Kestrel said. "There are four. First: write everything the same day you learn it, because memory is a speculator too — it sells the past cheap and keeps the difference. Second: record the mark and the name together, always, so no clerk can ever say the hand and the name don't match. Third—" she tapped the whalebone ruler— "date everything, even what seems undatable, because a lie about when is how every theft begins."

"And fourth?"

Kestrel looked at the girl a long moment. Outside, the light was turning; soon she would have to climb the tower and trim the wicks and wind the clockwork, the same rounds she had kept through storm and siege and grief, the same rounds she would keep tomorrow. The first stars were pricking through over the water where the warships used to be.

"Fourth," she said, "keep a copy somewhere they'll never think to look. Because the men who erased my name out of that old ledger were not monsters, Ilsa. They were clerks. They had stamps, and forms, and very reasonable voices, and if there had been only one book, there would be no town." She slid the pen across the table. "Now. First lesson. Tell me your full name, your mother's name, and the date of your birth — and mind you get the dates right, because in fifty years, some child of yours will be standing exactly where you're standing, checking."

Ilsa took up the pen the way one takes up a tool one intends to use for life. Her hand was steady. The ink went down dark and certain: ILSE-MARIE BRANDT, and the date, and beneath it, unprompted, in letters that would still be legible when they were both dust — *born on this coast, and meaning to stay.*

Kestrel watched the words dry, and lit the lamp, and began.
