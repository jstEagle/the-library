## 1. What Can Be Carried

The trick was to own nothing that couldn't be gone in ninety seconds.

I had learned it before I had a name for it. My mother taught me without meaning to — the way she kept her one good dress folded flat at the bottom of her trunk, the way she never let a pot sit too far from the door. When you live in a company town, on company salt, under a company roof that the company could take back any Tuesday it liked, you learned to measure your life by what fit in your hands.

So on the last evening of the season, while the gulls screamed over the drying racks and the smell of brine and tar settled over everything like a second skin, I sat on my cot in Barrack C and counted what I had.

Item: a shell comb, rose-pink, its teeth worn thin from years of use. My mother's. She'd traded three days of oyster shucking for it when I was small enough to still believe the sea gave gifts freely. It fit in my palm like a piece of sunset that had decided to keep working.

Item: a diver's knife, bone handle gone smooth and dark where a hand had gripped it for twenty years. My father's. The blade had been resharpened so many times it was half the width it started as, a knife whittled down to its own stubbornness. The sheath cracked at the seam; I kept it wrapped in waxed thread.

Item: my indenture papers. One sheet, folded into eighths, soft as cloth at the creases. MARGUERITE QUINTANA, it said, in the Voss Trading Company's tidy clerk-hand, INDENTURED TO THE VOSS TRADING COMPANY FOR A TERM OF TWELVE YEARS FROM THE DATE BELOW. Below was a date eleven years and four months old. Eight months left. I had done the arithmetic so many times the numbers had worn grooves in me.

That was all. Everything else — the tin cup, the blanket stamped VOSS PROPERTY in faded letters, the spare shirt, the bar of tallow soap — those were the company's things, lent to me the way weather is lent to everyone. If the orders came tonight, I would take the comb, the knife, the papers, and walk out of Barrack C lighter than most men woke up.

People pitied me for it. I could see it in their faces sometimes, the newer divers especially, the ones still young enough to want things — a brass lamp, a painted box, a photograph in a frame. *Look at him,* their faces said. *He owns nothing.* But they had it backwards. Owning nothing wasn't poverty. It was armor. A man who can carry his whole life in two hands cannot be robbed of anything but his life, and the company, whatever else it took, had not yet figured out how to bill you for that.

My name is Marguerite Quintana, and if that seems an odd name for a pearl diver's son, it is because it is an odd name. My mother chose it off the bow of a wrecked schooner that came apart on the reef the winter I was born — the *Marguerite*, out of some port none of us would ever see, its figurehead a carved woman with flowers in her hair, staring at us with painted eyes while the surf ate her ship behind her. My father had wanted to name me after his brother. My mother said no brother of his deserved it, though she never said why, and my father, who argued with waves and winced at my mother, did not press the point.

The name marked me. Everyone in the compound knew which boy belonged to it. That was fine. Being known and being noticed are different animals, and I had spent eleven years learning to confuse them — to be present the way furniture is present, useful, unremarkable, beneath remark. When the clerks needed a runner to the quay, they called Marguerite. When the sheds needed counting, Marguerite did the counting. And when the overseers' voices went quiet and dangerous, when someone had to stand close to a man being told his debt had grown again, somehow it was always Marguerite standing there, saying nothing, seeing everything, forgotten before he'd finished being seen.

Invisibility is a craft. Nobody thanks you for it, which is how you know it's working.

* * *

The Voss compound sat on the harbor's north shore like a fist closed around a coin. You could read the whole company in its layout if you knew how to look, and looking was the one luxury I allowed myself.

At the center, the counting house: brick, two stories, shutters always half-lowered even in full sun, as though the building itself were squinting at something it didn't care for. That was where the ledgers lived, and where men went in owing six months and came out owing eighteen, blinking in the daylight like moles, unable to explain what had happened to them in there except that the numbers had moved.

Around it, the warehouses — pearl, shell, dried fish, copra — long low buildings with roofs greened by salt air, guarded by dogs that were fed better than the divers. Then the drying racks, the sheds, the cooperage, the smithy. Then the barracks: five long rooms of bunk and cot and company stove, housing two hundred souls whose names lived in the counting house more durably than they lived anywhere else.

And then the waterfront shops, strung along the quay's landward edge like barnacles on a hull's waterline. The chandler. The mender of nets. The cookhouse that sold food the mess didn't provide, at prices designed to keep a man's account exactly where it was. The letter-writer.

I'll come to him.

Behind it all, cutting across the neck of the peninsula, ran the causeway bridge — timber on stone pilings, half a mile of planking that connected our island town to the mainland road. In season it carried carts and mules and the company's wagons heavy with pearl-shell, and it groaned day and night like an old man turning in bed. Every year the storms chewed a little more of it. Every year the company repaired exactly as much as it had to and swore the rest was sound.

This year the storms had come early and mean. Three days ago the engineer's men had roped off the middle span, where a piling had shifted and the planks had begun to sag like a hammock holding a sleeping bear. CLOSED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE, said the sign, in the same tidy clerk-hand as my indenture papers. The company's handwriting got everywhere. Sometimes I thought it was the only thing the place grew naturally.

It didn't matter much yet. The bridge closing meant the carts stopped, but the ships still came — the harbor faced open water on the south side, and the sea, unlike the road, had never signed anything with anyone. The season's last shipment was due within the week, and every indentured hand in the compound knew what that meant, because we had all heard the speech before: the ship waits one tide. Be aboard or forfeit. Forfeit, in the company's vocabulary, meant your remaining term converted to cash-debt, and cash-debt, in the counting house's arithmetic, grew like mold in the dark.

Eight months left on my papers. One tide between me and eight months becoming something longer. I lay on my cot after lights-out doing the arithmetic again, as if the answer might have changed since supper, and listened to Barrack C breathe around me — forty men coughing, shifting, muttering in three languages, dreaming whatever divers dream about.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain that hadn't arrived yet. I was at the shell shed before the bell finished, because invisibility means being early — a late man gets looked at; an early man gets used.

The morning passed in the usual litany. I counted crates of mother-of-pearl into the warehouse, checked seals, signed nothing — signing was for clerks; my job was to make sure the counts matched what the tally-boards said, and if they didn't match, to say so quietly and then forget I'd said it. By midday the rain had arrived, thin and needling, drumming on the warehouse roof like fingers practicing patience.

That was when Clerk Fenwick found me.

Fenwick was sixty if he was forty, a narrow man assembled entirely of angles and grievance, with ink permanently staining the first knuckle of his right hand. He held a ledger against his chest the way other men held Bibles.

"Quintana." He said my name the way he said everything, as though it owed him money. "Runner work."

"Yes, sir."

"Ink. The good stuff, not the wash. And paper stock, one ream." He consulted the ledger without opening it, which was a skill of his. "For Okafor. The letter-writer."

Something turned over in my chest, small and quick, like a fish under a boat. I kept my face where it was. "The shop past the quay?"

"The shop past the quay," Fenwick said, as if I were slow. Maybe I was. Maybe the fish under the boat was just hunger. "Company accounts carry his supplies. Don't ask me why; I don't set policy. Take the handcart. Bring back a receipt, signed, or don't bother coming back."

"Yes, sir."

"And Quintana." He lowered the ledger an inch. His eyes had the wet shine of a man who enjoyed this part. "Okafor's been strange lately. Talks to himself. Orders more ink than a dying trade needs. If he says anything to you — anything — you bring it to me. Company ears, understand? Not your own."

"Understood, sir."

I took the handcart out into the rain, and the wheels sang on the wet cobbles of the quay road, and I told myself the turn in my chest was nothing. Just weather. Just the season ending. A runner runs; a receipt comes back; the world stays the size of a delivery.

But I'll admit it here, where no clerk can read it: I walked slower than I needed to.

* * *

Silas Okafor's shop sat at the very edge of company land, the last building before the rope-fence and the scrub beyond, as though it had been walking away from the compound for years and had simply gotten tired partway. It was narrow, two stories, the lower story all window — panes so old and warped that everything seen through them arrived slightly melted. A sign hung over the door, hand-lettered, the paint gone the color of weak tea:

**LETTERS WRITTEN & READ — FAIR RATES — ALL TONGUES**

Beneath it, smaller, added later in a different hand: *No job refused.*

Through the melted glass I could see lamplight, though it was barely afternoon, and a shape bent over a desk that might have been a man or a heap of coats. Rain ticked on the awning. Somewhere behind the quay, a ship's bell counted the hour, muffled by weather.

I had delivered ink to this shop maybe thirty times in eleven years. I had never once gone inside. Deliveries to Silas Okafor went to the door, where a hand appeared through a gap to take the parcel and pass out a receipt already signed in a hand so fine it looked printed. The hand was brown and old and spotted, and the signature changed slightly every time, and I had never wondered why until that moment, standing in the rain with a ream of paper going soft in its wrapping, wondering why a man who wrote letters for a living would sign his own receipts in thirty different versions of his own name.

A man who owns nothing has time to wonder. That's the other thing nobody tells you about carrying your whole life in two hands: it leaves your eyes free.

I knocked.

The shape inside didn't move. I knocked harder, and the rain filled the silence, and just when I'd decided the old man was dead at his desk — which would have been, in its way, the simplest delivery of my career — the door opened inward and the smell hit me: ink and beeswax and pipe smoke and something underneath, medicinal and bitter, like herbs boiled too long.

Silas Okafor was smaller than the shape suggested, a compact old man in a coat too warm for the season, with white wool hair cropped close to his skull and eyes that took my measure in the time it took the door to swing. His gaze went to the cart, to the parcels, to my face, and stopped there a beat longer than any clerk's ever had.

"You're the Quintana boy," he said.

"I'm the runner, sir. Ink and paper stock. From the company stores."

"Quintana." He said it differently than Fenwick had — slowly, tasting it, like a word from a language he was deciding whether to remember. "There was a Quintana in the diving rolls. Years back. Big fellow. Lungs like bellows, they said."

The rain came down harder. Behind him, the lamplight showed a desk buried in paper, walls lined with pigeonholes stuffed with letters in a dozen scripts, and a ledger lying open that I would think about later, at night, for a long time.

"That was my father," I said.

"Yes," said Silas Okafor. "I know."
