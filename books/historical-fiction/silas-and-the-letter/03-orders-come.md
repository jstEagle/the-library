## 3. Orders Come

The bell at the Voss compound rang wrong that morning, and everyone knew what it meant before anyone said a word.

There were two bells in Port Sable. The harbor bell rang slow and even, three strokes for the tide, and you could sleep through it your whole life without it meaning anything but water. The company bell was different. It rang in patterns — one stroke for pay, two for inspection, three for the boats coming home. This morning it rang four times, then stopped, then four again, and Marguerite Quintana set down the crate he was carrying and felt his stomach go to iron.

Four strokes meant assembly. Four strokes, this late in the season, with the light already going thin and gold and the air smelling of cold salt instead of warm — four strokes meant orders.

He had learned the bells before he'd learned his letters. That wasn't quite true; he had never properly learned his letters, which was a thing he kept folded away like a garment too fine to wear. But the bells, yes. His mother used to say he could tell you what the day would ask of him before the sun cleared the warehouse roofs. She said it with pride once. Later she stopped saying it at all.

Around him the compound yard began to move. Men came out of the barracks blocks still buttoning their shirts, divers and haulers and rope-walkers, forty years of hands and twenty years of hands and boys of fifteen pretending to be men of twenty. They moved the way water moves when it finds a low place — no one deciding, everyone arriving — and within minutes the whole indentured population of the Voss Trading Company stood ranked in the yard in front of the counting house, breath steaming, nobody talking above a murmur.

Marguerite found his place the way he always did: third rank from the back, half a body behind Osei's broad shoulder, where the overseers' eyes slid off him like rain off oiled canvas. He had spent nineteen years learning how to be nearly not there. It was a skill like any other. Some men could dive on one breath; some could carry two hundred pounds of shell down a gangplank without swaying. Marguerite could stand in a crowd of three hundred and be missed by every eye that passed over him.

It was the most useful thing he owned, and it was not portable.

The counting house door opened and Mr. Voss himself came out onto the steps.

Old man Voss rarely came out for anything less than money or weather, and this morning he brought both, though only one of them was spoken aloud. He was a tall ruin of a man, straight-backed the way furniture is straight-backed, with white hair cut short as a convict's and a voice that carried across water because it had spent thirty years doing nothing else. Beside him stood Mr. Calloway, the factor, holding a board with papers weighted against the wind, and behind them, at the edge of the steps where clerks stand when they are not wanted further forward, stood a young clerk whose name Marguerite didn't know and whose face he memorized anyway. Habit. You watched everyone. You trusted none of them to watch back kindly.

"Hands," old Voss said, and the murmur died so completely that Marguerite could hear the gulls working the tide line beyond the wall.

"The *Cormorant* makes ready for the season's last run. She sails on the morning tide, five days hence."

Nobody breathed. Everybody breathed. The words went into the crowd like a hook into a net full of fish, and every man in that yard felt the pull of it somewhere different — in his chest, in his gut, in the place behind the teeth where fear keeps its office.

"She carries the season's take south," Voss continued, "and she carries our contracted men south to the winter works at Cape Ferren. All indentured hands are required aboard. Those who board will have the year credited to their term and provisions laid in for the passage." He paused, and the pause was a tool, and he used it well. "Those who do not board forfeit their contracts entire. Forfeit means the debt stands, the time does not count, and the company is released from all obligation to carry you again. There is no second ship. There is no appeal. The bridge closes when the storm line comes through — likely tomorrow night, by the look of it — and after it closes there is no road north until spring."

He let that sit. Somewhere in the ranks a boy began to cry, quietly, the way children cry when they've been taught that crying costs more than it buys, and an older man's hand came down on his shoulder and stayed there.

"You'll receive your boarding chits at the factor's window by noon tomorrow. Chit, kit, and person aboard before the tide turns, or the contract's void. That is all."

Voss turned and went inside, and the door shut, and the yard stayed exactly where it was for a long moment — three hundred men standing in the shape of a decision they hadn't made yet, most of them knowing already what the decision would be, because it had been made for them years ago, in ledgers they had never seen, in sums they could not read.

*Forfeit.* The word moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. Marguerite heard it repeated in four languages before the ranks broke. Forfeit meant another year added to the far end of a term that never seemed to reach its own end. Forfeit meant starting over. Forfeit, some men whispered, might even mean worse than starting over, because a man without a contract was a man without a value, and the company had ways of making valueless men valuable again.

Marguerite did not whisper. Marguerite was already walking.

* * *

Here is the thing about packing when you have spent your whole life practicing: it takes eleven minutes.

He had done it before, at fourteen, when the winter works took him the first time; at sixteen, when the *Petrel* ran aground and half the compound was reassigned overnight; at eighteen, when his father drowned and the company, with a kindness that was worse than cruelty, offered to move his mother into smaller quarters "for her convenience." Each time the same eleven minutes. Each time the same inventory.

He knelt on the floor of his bunk room — six feet by eight, a cot, a shelf, a hook for his coat, a window high in the wall that showed a slice of gray sky and, if you leaned, the top of Silas Okafor's crooked chimney at the edge of company land — and he began.

The diver's knife first. His father's knife, bone-handled, the blade worn thin as a promise from years of prying pearl meat loose and cutting rope and, once, according to a story his father told only twice, opening a letter. It lived wrapped in oilcloth under the cot, and Marguerite unwrapped it, checked the edge out of pure ritual, and put it in the canvas bag. Weight: almost nothing. Value: everything.

The shell comb. His mother's. Pink nacre gone soft at the teeth from thirty years of hair, smelling faintly of the coconut oil she used when the trade ships brought it and salt water when they didn't. He had taken it from her room the week after the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral — the company did not hold funerals for drowned divers, only adjusted the ledgers — and he had carried it every season since without ever once using it. It went into the bag in its cloth. Weight: nothing. Value: not speakable.

The indenture papers. Folded in oilskin, tied with string, worn soft at the creases from being read and re-read by a boy who couldn't read them himself but had memorized the shapes of the words the way you memorize the shapes of clouds. His name was on them — *Marguerite Quintana* — in a clerk's copperplate, and below it a sum, and below the sum a term, and below the term a clause that he had once asked a literate deckhand to translate and had wished afterward he hadn't. He put the papers inside his shirt, against his chest, where they rode always, sleeping and waking. Weight: nothing. Value: his whole life, priced.

Then the rest, the small arithmetic of a small life. Two shirts. A sweater knit by his mother the winter before she understood she was dying, the wool gone thin at the elbows. A bar of soap. A tin cup. A coil of good cord, because cord was wealth anywhere on this coast. His mother's little brass mirror, cracked, kept not for vanity but because it was hers. And at the bottom of the bag, wrapped separately, a thing he told no one about: a scrap of paper maybe four inches square, salvaged years ago from the factor's waste basket, blank, good stock, worth more per ounce than anything else he carried. Blank paper was a currency he couldn't spend. He kept it the way a drowning man keeps air in his cheeks.

Eleven minutes. Done. He sat back on his heels and looked at the bag and felt the thing he always felt, which was not relief and not grief but a kind of smooth, practiced emptiness, like a drawer sliding shut.

Five days. The *Cormorant*, the morning tide, Cape Ferren and the winter works, and a year credited toward a number that never came down. He had done it four times before. He could do it again. You packed your life into a bag that weighed less than a diving belt, you walked up a gangplank, you became cargo that could sign its name — or make its mark — and in the spring the bridge opened and the cycle closed over your head like water.

It was a good arrangement. It was the only arrangement. He had made his peace with it so thoroughly that the peace had gone invisible, the way a thing you carry every day stops having weight.

That was what he told himself, kneeling on the floor of a room he'd live in for five more days, while outside the compound the wind came up off the strait smelling of the storm that was coming to close the bridge.

That was what he told himself right up until the moment he remembered the ink.

* * *

He had forgotten it in the yard, in the noise, in the four-stroke bell and the word *forfeit* — but now it surfaced whole: the delivery slip in his pocket, the stoneware jug of ink under his arm since midmorning, undelivered, sitting this very minute on the floor of the storeroom where he'd set it down when the bell rang.

Ink for the letter-writer. Silas Okafor, shop at the edge of company land, past the ropewalk, where the ground got marshy and the company's rules got thinner. The slip had come that morning with the first work orders, ordinary as bread. Take the jug to Okafor. Sign the slip. Come back.

Ordinary as bread. Except that two days ago he had stood in that cramped shop smelling of paper and lamp oil and something medicinal underneath, and the old man had looked at him across a ledger open on the desk — an old ledger, a dead year's ledger — and said a name.

His father's name. *Emile Quintana,* written in a column of figures, and Silas Okafor had gone quiet looking at it, quiet in a way that made the shop feel suddenly too small, and then he had said, "You're his boy," not asking, and then he had said something else, something about entries and erasures and how ink could be made to forget, and Marguerite had left quickly, the way you leave a room where the floor has begun to creak.

He should deliver the ink tonight. It was on the slip; it was his task; undone tasks had a way of becoming marks against you, and marks against you had a way of becoming sums. That was the whole grammar of his life and he spoke it fluently.

And besides, in five days he would be gone. Whatever the old man had started to say, whatever lived in that ledger, it would keep until spring or forever. It was not his. Nothing at the edge of company land was his. His whole world fit in a canvas bag, weighed less than a diving belt, and was already packed.

Marguerite picked up the bag and set it by the door, squared, corners aligned, ready. Then he went down to the storeroom and collected the jug of ink.

It was heavier than he expected. Or lighter. He couldn't have said which.

He signed the slip at the gate — the gate guard marked it, actually, since Marguerite's signature was a laborious, lopsided *M Q* that shamed him each time he made it — and walked out along the compound wall with the jug under his arm, and the wind followed him the whole way, smelling of iron and coming weather, and above the rooftops ahead the crooked chimney of the letter-writer's shop stood up against a sky the color of unpolished silver.

Five days, he thought. Five days and the tide takes me, and I will be small and useful somewhere else, and this town and its bells and its crooked chimney will close over like water.

At the shop door, he raised his hand to knock — and paused. Through the window, lamplit against the early dark, he could see the old man bent over his desk, pen moving, writing other people's names. And beside the ledger, stacked neat as bricks, a pile of sealed letters. More letters than Marguerite had ever seen on that desk. Dozens. Waiting.

Silas Okafor looked up, as if he'd heard the thought and not the knock, and beckoned him in.
