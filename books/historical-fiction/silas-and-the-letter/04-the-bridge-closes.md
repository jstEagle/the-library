## 4. The Bridge Closes

The storm came up the channel like a debt collector.

Silas Okafor heard it before he saw it — the water slapping the pilings under his shop in a rhythm too fast for tide, too angry for wind. He had lived at the edge of Voss Landing long enough to know what that sound meant. The sea was moving things it should not move. By morning there would be wreckage, and by afternoon there would be men from the company counting it and writing down numbers that would never be true.

He got up from the desk where he had been copying — he was always copying now, even when his hands shook so badly the letters wandered off their lines like drunk men leaving a tavern — and went to the window.

The bridge was still standing. That surprised him. It was an old thing, the causeway road across the narrows, timber on stone piles older than the company, older than the town, older probably than Silas himself, though not by much. Every season it carried the divers out and carried the money back in. Every season the company swore it would rebuild it properly, and every season they patched it instead with green lumber and prayer.

Tonight it was carrying rain sideways and a wind that bent the mangroves along the bank flat as if they were being interrogated.

Silas watched it sway. Not fall — sway. A slow, sickening lean toward the water, hold, then back. Like a man deciding whether to faint.

*Go on,* he thought. *Fall. Do us all a favor.*

But the bridge did not fall for him. It fell, or half-fell, two hours later, at the far span, where the storm surge drove a derelict barge against the pilings with a crack Silas felt through the floor of his shop and into the bones of his feet. He knew what it was immediately. Everyone in Voss Landing knew. You could hear the whole town change its breathing — doors opening, voices calling down lanes, then the particular silence of people who have just understood something and do not yet know whether it is good news or bad.

For most of them it was bad news. The divers' families crowded along the waterfront, staring across the black water at the broken span, because the ship that would carry their men away came at the morning tide, and if the road was closed, how would anyone reach the anchorage? For most of them it meant confusion, delay, wages lost, contracts argued over by candlelight.

For Silas Okafor it meant something else entirely.

For Silas Okafor, the broken bridge was the first piece of good luck the sea had handed him in forty years.

* * *

He sat back down at the desk after the noise died and took stock, the way he had taught himself to take stock every night since the cough started — not of money, which he had none of, but of work. What was finished. What remained.

Eleven letters.

He said it aloud, because saying it made it real, and making it real made it urgent, and urgency was the only medicine that worked on him anymore.

"Eleven," he told the empty room.

The shop smelled of ink and lamp oil and, underneath both, the sweetish rot smell he had been trying not to notice for three months and could no longer ignore. He coughed, deep, the kind that felt like hauling something heavy up from a flooded hold, and spat into the rag he kept for the purpose. There was blood in it again. More than yesterday. He folded the rag over neatly, the way he did everything, and put it under the desk where no one would see.

Dying was a paperwork problem, when you thought about it. Everything was.

He pulled the ledger toward him — the true one, the one nobody but him knew existed, kept in a false binding stamped *Voss Trading Co., Provision Records, 1889–1894*, dull enough that even a thief would leave it alone. Inside, in eleven different hands over nine years, was the other history of Voss Landing. The real debts. The real dates. The terms of indenture as actually signed, next to the terms as actually enforced.

A diver signed for five years, the ledger said, and served nine, because the company charged him for his lamp oil, his salt, his boat patch, his funeral insurance he never collected, and the interest compounded on all of it faster than any man could dive pearls out of the dark. A diver signed for five years and drowned in year four, and his debt passed to his widow, who had never signed anything in her life.

That was the fraud. Not hidden, exactly. Just distributed — spread thin across hundreds of small cruelties until no single cruelty seemed worth a riot. Silas had spent nine years gathering it back together, thread by thread, into one account a man could hold in his two hands.

And eleven names still needed freeing.

He opened the drawer where the finished letters lay in their oilcloth wrapper: nineteen of them, each a certificate of manumission, each written in the hand of someone with the authority to sign such a thing — a magistrate dead six years, a company factor dismissed for drunkenness whose signature Silas knew better than his own, once even the old harbormaster's hand, God rest him, copied from a chit the man had tossed in the harbor without a second look.

Nineteen letters that could take nineteen human beings and make them, on paper, free.

Paper freedom. Silas had arguments with himself about this at night, when the cough kept him awake. Was a man freed by paper actually free? He always came back to the same answer: paper was where their bondage lived, so paper was where it would die. You could not fight a lie with truth in your heart. You had to write the truth down, in the right hand, with the right seal, or the lie simply continued, tidy and official, forever.

Nineteen done. Eleven to go. And the last three required the hardest forgery of all — the current factor's hand, Verner's, live and vigilant, a hand Silas had only seen twice and could not risk studying again.

He looked at his own fingers, spotted brown with age, trembling at rest like leaves deciding whether to drop.

His hand could not finish them. That was the plain arithmetic of his body. He had perhaps a week of steady pen-work left in it, maybe less; already the letters he'd managed in the past month had cost him a day of shaking afterward, lying on his cot listening to the water and wondering if this was the night the shop found him in the morning cold.

So he needed hands. Younger hands. Steady ones.

And there was exactly one person in Voss Landing who had stood in this room, watched him copy a hand not his own, and shown the two things that mattered: quick eyes, and a face that knew how to be invisible.

The Quintana boy. Marguerite.

Silas turned the name over. The son of Joaquín Quintana, who had signed for five years in 1886 and drowned in 1890, and whose debt — inflated, compounding, obscene — had been transferred to a woman named Rosa Quintana, who had never held a pen in her life, and whose boy had been taken into company service at twelve to work it off, because the company had discovered that children made excellent entries in a ledger: cheap, quiet, and legally theirs.

Silas had seen the name in the old book years ago and said too much about it, once, to the boy. He had regretted it and not regretted it. Mostly he had wondered, since, whether the boy ever thought about it — whether some part of him had been counting, all these years, the way Silas counted. Whether he knew, in the wordless way of the young, that the number on his contract was a fiction maintained by ink.

If the boy could learn. If he could be taught fast enough. A week. Perhaps eight days.

Silas laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into a long minute of leaning on the desk waiting for the world to stop tilting.

*A week to teach a boy a trade I took ten years to learn.* Well. He had learned slowly because he had learned alone, stealing his craft letter by letter from documents smuggled out of offices. The boy would have a teacher. That was worth a decade, maybe.

He rose, steadier than he expected, and went to the shelf where he kept the practice sheets — blank papers ruled in faint pencil, alphabets in a dozen official hands, the anatomy of an *R* in the factor's style drawn out stroke by stroke like a lesson in surgery. He gathered them into a stack. Then, after a moment, he added the false-bound ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth.

Not tonight. Tomorrow, perhaps. But soon he would send for the boy, or arrange to have him sent, and when he came, Silas would show him everything.

It occurred to him, wrapping the ledger and tying the cord with fingers that would barely cooperate, that what he was planning was monstrous by every law that governed this coast. Forgery. Theft. Sedition, if you wanted the ugly word. If Brandt or any of the company's inspectors caught wind of it, they would hang him — well, they would not bother hanging a dying man; they would burn the letters and let the cough do its work, and they would take the boy and make an example that lasted a generation.

He tied the knot anyway.

Outside, the rain kept falling on the broken bridge, and somewhere across the narrows the ship rode at anchor with its lamps lit, patient as a creditor, waiting for a road that was no longer there.

*Let it wait,* Silas thought. *Let the whole machine wait. For once, the trap has closed on the wrong side.*

* * *

Marguerite Quintana heard the bridge go at the second bell of the night and was out of his bunk in the company dormitory before the shout came.

That was the training. Twelve years of it. When wood cracked in the night you moved first and asked questions never, because at Voss Landing the answer was always the same: cargo, or weather, or both, and neither waited for permission.

He stood in the yard with thirty other indentured men, rain running off his chin, looking at the gap in the far span where the barge had punched through. Even in the dark you could see it — the road simply stopped, a jagged end of planking sticking out over black water like a broken tooth.

"They'll ferry us out," someone said. Old Baptiste, a diver with shoulders like a door. "Company won't lose a shipment. They'll run lighters across."

"Lighters can't load at anchor in this swell," another voice said. "They'll wait for the bridge."

"They'll wait for the bridge," Marguerite said, before he could stop himself.

They all looked at him. He was the one who read, the one who carried messages between the compound offices, the one who sometimes knew things a moment early because he delivered the papers that said them. Being useful had earned him that much: when Marguerite spoke about company business, they listened.

"The factor's order went out at dusk," he said. "Ship holds at anchor until the bridge is passable. No date given. All hands board within one day of reopening." He recited it flatly, the way orders sounded, and hated how easily the words came, how much he sounded like the office when he used the office's voice.

One day of notice, then. Pack your life, say whatever goodbyes fit in a day, walk across a mended bridge, and disappear onto the water for another season. Or forever. Nobody's contract had ended in living memory; everyone knew it, nobody said it.

Baptiste spat into the rain. "Then we're sealed in with the wolves till they fix their road."

"We're sealed in," Marguerite agreed, and something moved in his chest that he refused to examine — something that felt disgracefully close to relief, and beneath the relief, worse, a small bright coal of something like hope.

Because sealed in meant the audit was sealed in too. He had heard the clerks talking that evening, low-voiced: a company inspector coming within the fortnight, unannounced, checking the books ahead of the waterfront demolitions. The whole compound would spend the next weeks in a frenzy of paper — counts and recounts, inventories, men summoned to offices to confirm signatures and accounts.

Men like Marguerite would be summoned constantly. Men like Marguerite would need places to be when they weren't wanted.

He thought of the cramped shop at the edge of company land, the smell of ink, the old man's eyes catching him mid-copy that afternoon — *that's my stroke you're stealing, boy, do it again, slower* — and the strange heat of being caught at something and told, not punished, but *instructed*.

He pushed the thought down. Hope was a thing you couldn't carry. He had a philosophy about that, a whole life built on it: measure everything by what fits in the bag, want nothing that doesn't pack flat, be small enough to slip through any door the company opens.

But the bridge was broken, the ship was anchored out of reach, and the bag was packed and going nowhere.

Back inside, wet, he lay in his bunk listening to the rain and did the dangerous arithmetic he never allowed himself.

The bridge had taken two months to repair after the last storm, seven years ago. Two months, minimum. Possibly the whole season.

Two months was enough time to learn almost anything, if you were taught by someone who knew, and if you wanted it badly enough to be ashamed of wanting it.

He didn't sleep. Toward dawn the rain thinned, and through the dormitory's high window he could see the anchorage lights across the black water, the ship riding there with its holds empty and its patience enormous, and beyond it the broken span, and beyond that — though nothing could be seen in the dark — the mainland, the world, everything that happened to people who were free.

The trap had closed. He felt it close, felt the familiar weight settle over him, the old resignation sliding into place like a well-worn yoke.

And then, for the first time in his life, he noticed the other thing about a trap:

It holds you in one place long enough to work.

At first light there was a tap at the dormitory door — a dockside runner, dripping, holding out a scrap of paper. Company business. Marguerite took it, expecting an inventory summons.

The note was unsigned, in a hand he recognized from ledgers and labels: cramped, precise, economical, each letter doing exactly its share and no more.

*Ink delivery, as usual. Come this morning. Bring nothing you cannot leave behind.*

Marguerite read it twice, then folded it small — small enough to carry, small enough to burn — and reached for his coat.
