## 5. Apprentice by Accident

The audit came on a Tuesday, the way bad things did in Voss Landing — without warning, without apology, and always when you had something to hide.

Marguerite heard it first as a sound: boots on the compound gravel, too many pairs, moving in the formation men used when they wanted to be seen coming. Then the bell at the pay office rang twice, which meant assemble, and by the time he reached the yard there were forty indentured divers standing in rows with their hands at their sides, and Inspector's clerk Pellier walking down the line with a clipboard and a face like wet slate.

"Roll audit," Pellier announced, to no one who needed telling. "Every hand will present papers. Every paper will match the ledger. Anyone whose numbers don't reconcile stays behind after."

*Stays behind after.* In Voss Landing those four words had weight the way ballast stones had weight. Men who stayed behind after audits sometimes came back with their terms extended. Sometimes they came back with their terms *clarified*, which was worse — a clerk's pen redrawing the line of your debt further out than the horizon.

Marguerite's papers were clean. He kept them clean the way he kept everything else: folded small, carried close, never a gram heavier than it needed to be. His term ran four more years. His debt stood at an amount that never changed no matter how many seasons he dived, because the interest moved faster than his wages did, but that arithmetic was written in a ledger he'd never been allowed to see, so as far as any roll audit could prove, Marguerite Quintana owed exactly what the company said he owed, and presented exactly the man the company expected.

He should have passed. He would have passed.

Except that morning, before the bell, he'd done the one thing in six years of careful smallness that couldn't be explained: he had Silas Okafor's ink on his fingers.

Not much. A smudge at the base of his thumb where he'd steadied a bottle the old man had nearly dropped, delivering it from the company store like he delivered everything — head down, quick, invisible. But Pellier was checking hands now, turning them over one by one, looking for calluses that didn't match trade records, for stains that didn't match work assignments. Someone upstream had told him to look. Marguerite didn't know who or why, but he knew what it meant when the company started checking things it had never bothered to check before: someone had made them afraid, and afraid companies went through the town like fire through dry grass.

Pellier was three men away. Two. Marguerite looked at his thumb, at the faint violet ghost of iron-gall ink, and understood that in ninety seconds he would either have to explain why a pearl diver smelled of a letter-writer's shop — a shop already whispered about, a man already watched — or not be there when the explanation was asked for.

There was nowhere to go. That was the whole architecture of Voss Landing; there was never anywhere to go. The compound walls on three sides, the quay on the fourth, the bridge closed since the storm, the ship riding at anchor out past the point like a promise the company could revoke. Forty men in rows because forty men had nowhere to be except where the bell put them.

Except.

At the far end of the yard, past the cooperage, where the company land went soft and uncertain at its edges, there was a gap in the fence that every child in the town knew and no adult admitted knowing, and beyond the gap the ground sloped down toward the water through a stand of salt-stunted pines, and at the bottom of the slope, leaning against the hillside like a thing that had grown rather than been built, sat the shop of Silas Okafor.

It wasn't a plan. It was less than a plan; it was a body deciding before a mind agreed. Pellier turned toward the next man, and Marguerite stepped backward out of the row, once, twice, into the shadow of the cooperage wall, then turned and walked — not ran, *walked*, because running was a confession — out through the gap and down through the pines with the bell still ringing behind him and his heart going like a diver's pulse at depth.

* * *

He'd delivered to the shop eleven times in six years. Ink, mostly, and once a crate of pens, and once — he remembered this with a strange, delayed shame — a sealed envelope from the pay office that Silas had taken without opening it, weighed it in his palm like a man weighing a fish he already knew the size of, and set unopened in a drawer. Marguerite had never been inside. Deliveries stopped at the threshold. The shop was on the edge of company land, which meant it was technically inside it, which meant Silas was tolerated rather than permitted, and everyone understood that the toleration lasted exactly as long as Silas remained unremarkable.

So Marguerite had never remarked on it. Not the smell of the place that reached him even outside — tannin and lamp oil and the sea-salt rot of old paper. Not the light in the single window at strange hours. Not the way certain divers' families, after visiting the shop, walked differently back up the hill.

Now he hammered on the door with the bell still fading behind him, and when nothing answered, he tried it, and it opened, and going in was the second decision his body made without him.

The shop was one room and a loft, dark, dense, organized in a system that looked like chaos until you saw it wasn't. Shelves to the ceiling. Drawers under everything. A counter scarred by decades of blades and bottles. And paper everywhere — not loose, nothing wasted, but stacked in flat bricks, tied with string, labeled in a cramped upright hand. Bundles of letters. Bundles of what looked like contracts. One bundle, set apart near the cold stove, wrapped in oilcloth, unlabeled, which somehow made it louder than all the others.

Silas Okafor sat at the worktable by the window, and he did not look surprised. He looked tired in the way stone is tired — settled into it, permanent. His skin was gray under its brown, and his breathing had a whistle in it, high and thin, like wind through a cracked reed. He was writing. Even now, interrupted, he finished his line before he looked up.

"You're early," he said. "Ink comes Thursdays."

"I'm not—" Marguerite's breath was still ragged. "They're auditing. Roll audit. They're checking hands."

Silas looked at Marguerite's hands, then at his own, which were permanently stained to the second knuckle, and something moved at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile. "And yours would say what?"

"That I've been somewhere I don't belong."

"They usually do." Silas set down his pen — a fine thing, bone-handled, worn to the shape of a grip — and nodded at the room behind Marguerite. "Door bar is on the hook. If you're hiding in my house, you'll pull your weight. Wood's by the stove."

Marguerite stood the bar across the door, feeling absurd and criminal and — underneath both, unwelcome and undeniable — safe. When had he last been somewhere with a bar on the door? The dormitory locked from the outside. Everything in Voss Landing locked from the outside.

He fed the stove while boots came down the pine slope above — two sets, then three, voices calling to each other about the fence gap, about the tide flats, about whether the letter-man would harbor a runner. The voices argued, decided the shop was company property and therefore the last place a runaway would hide — *he wouldn't shit where the company eats* — and went on along the shore toward the net sheds.

Silas hadn't stopped working through any of it. Marguerite realized, watching, that the old man hadn't even paused. Whatever was on his desk mattered more than discovery, and that frightened Marguerite more than the boots had.

"What are you writing?" he asked, before he could stop himself. Curiosity was a luxury. He knew that. Six years of being useful and invisible had taught him that curiosity was the first luxury to cut.

But Silas answered anyway, or half-answered. "A letter."

"For whom?"

"For a man named Etienne Roche." Silas turned the page so Marguerite could see, and Marguerite, who had spent his life around documents only as things to be carried and surrendered, saw for the first time what a document could be when it was made instead of received. The hand was beautiful. Not ornamental — economical, every stroke load-bearing, a hand built like a good hull. "Mr. Roche is a diver," Silas said. "His term ended fourteen months ago. The company has misplaced his discharge. I am helping them find it."

"Misplaced," Marguerite said. The word came out flat.

"You find that funny?"

"I find that I've heard it before." He hadn't meant to say it. It had come up from somewhere below the level where he kept his words. "My father drowned in 'ninety-one. My mother asked for his death certificate so she could claim the widow's release clause. She was told the record was misfiled. She asked for six years. She's still asking."

Silas was quiet a long moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the gulls worked the tide line, screaming their small hungers.

"And your name?" Silas said finally. "Quintana?"

"Yes."

Silas reached beneath the counter — not the drawer where the pay-office envelope had gone; lower, a drawer that breathed dust when it opened — and brought out a ledger, old, swollen with damp, its spine repaired with cord. He did not open it. He held it flat on his palms like something that might go off.

"Ink comes Thursdays," he said. "You come Tuesdays, apparently, running from clerks. Sit down, boy. You've ink on your hands already. Let's see if there's anything under it."

* * *

Later — much later, when the light through the window had gone from white to gold to the bruised copper of late afternoon — Marguerite could not reconstruct how it had happened. There was no moment he could point to and say *there, that's where I agreed*. There was only the fact that he was sitting at Silas Okafor's worktable with a practice sheet in front of him and a pen in his hand that was worth more than everything he owned.

It had started, he thought, with the ledger. Silas had opened it at last, and it was not a company book at all — it was a copyist's exercise, pages and pages of the same passage rendered in different hands. A merchant's fat looping script. A clerk's narrow spine. A customs officer's block capitals. A woman's slanted italic. Dozens of hands, all of them perfect, all of them false, all of them flowing off the same patient pen.

"It's how you learn," Silas said. "Any fool can imitate a signature. A signature is five seconds of a man. You have to learn the whole hand — how he rests between words, where he crowds, where he goes lazy. A hand is a way of sitting inside a body. You learn it until you can sit inside it too."

"Why?" Marguerite asked. "Why learn anyone's hand but your own?"

Silas looked at him for a long time, and Marguerite had the sense of being measured — not against what he appeared to be, but against what he might hold.

"Because in this town," Silas said, "the company owns every hand that signs. Every mark on every contract, every X beside every debt — they own the making of it. So some of us learned to make marks they don't own." He tapped the ledger. "This is a tool, boy. Like your knife. It can gut a fish or gut a man. The question is never the tool. The question is the name attached to the work."

Marguerite should have left then. The boots were gone, the audit would have rolled past his absence with a note — *Quintana, M., whereabouts unknown, see file* — and every instinct he owned, every rule of six years' survival, said leave now, apologize tomorrow, be small again, be carried.

Instead he heard himself say: "Show me."

Silas pushed a sheet of cheap stock across the table and laid the bone pen in Marguerite's fingers the way you'd hand a man a blade you weren't sure he could be trusted with.

"Copy that line," Silas said, indicating the merchant's fat loop. "Don't draw it. *Write* it. Drawing is for dead letters. Writing is for live ones."

Marguerite wrote. It was terrible. The loops lurched, the pressure stuttered, the descenders dove like panicked birds. He felt his face heat. He had been proud, once, of being quick at whatever he attempted — knots, knots were easy, and mending nets, and reading current off the water's surface — and here was a thing at which he was clumsy on the first try, publicly, in front of a dying old man who could probably write him under the table with his teeth.

Again, said Silas. Again. Again. Loosen your wrist. You're holding it like a knife — good, keep some of that, but let the knife breathe. Again.

And somewhere around the twentieth attempt, something happened that Marguerite had no language for yet, though he would spend years finding it. His wrist loosened. The loops began to round themselves. The line started to move the way water moves — not thought, but *given*, each stroke arriving out of the momentum of the last — and for a few strokes he was not Marguerite Quintana, indentured hand #114, four years remaining, debt irreducible. He was nobody. He was a hand moving across a page, and the page asked nothing about his papers.

Then he looked down at what he'd made and the fascination curdled, just slightly, into something colder. Because it was good. Not Silas-good, not close, but better than it had any right to be on a first afternoon, and he could feel how easily it had come, how readily his body had obeyed a stranger's hand, and a voice in him that sounded unpleasantly like Pellier said: *this is what forgers are made of.*

He set down the pen.

"I should get back," he said. "Before they notice how long I've been—"

"They noticed at the second bell," Silas said mildly, not looking up from his own work. "Your absence has been a note in a file since midday. Going back won't unwrite it."

"How do you know?"

"Because I know what notes in files look like. I've been writing them for other people for thirty years." Silas finally raised his head. Up close, in the last light, Marguerite could see how ill he was — the hollows, the shine of fever under the gray, the effort it cost him simply to sit upright. "Here is what will happen. Tomorrow Pellier will ask where you went. You will tell him you hid from the audit because your hands were dirty and you feared a misreading. This is true. Companies punish fear less than mystery. He may extend your reporting hours. He may dock a week's wage. He will not search further, because searching costs money and you are not worth the money."

"Not worth it," Marguerite repeated. It was supposed to sting. Instead it landed like an old coat, familiar, warm almost. He'd built a whole life on not being worth the money.

"But," Silas said, "you will come back Thursday. With the ink delivery. On time, official, invisible — and you will stay an hour after. And I will teach you properly. Not tricks. The trade. How paper is sized and why it matters. How inks age differently and how a forged document is caught by its chemistry long before its hand. How names are made on paper and how they are taken off."

"I didn't ask to learn a trade."

"No," Silas agreed. "You asked to hide. But you copied twenty lines when one would have served the lie, and your eyes changed on the nineteenth." He turned back to his work. The whistle in his breath rose and fell. "Thursday, Quintana. Bring nothing. Take nothing. Especially take nothing from the bundle in the oilcloth."

Marguerite looked at the bundle by the stove. Unlabeled. Set apart. Humming, almost, with whatever slept inside it.

He walked back up through the pines in full dark, past the fence gap, into the compound where the lamps burned low and the audit lists had been posted and his name sat at the bottom of the exceptions column in Pellier's narrow hand — *Quintana, M., whereabouts unknown, see file* — and he stood reading his own name by lamplight and found, to his horror, that he was reading it the way Silas read handwriting. Measuring the pressure. Judging the spacing. Noticing, for the first time in his life, that Pellier crowded his descenders when he was hurried, and that this particular line had been written in a hurry.

Somewhere out past the point, the ship rode at anchor, waiting to carry him away on the morning tide whenever the orders came.

Somewhere down the hill, in a shop that shouldn't have existed, a dying man waited for Thursday with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

Marguerite went to his bunk, lay down in the dark, and discovered that his right hand wouldn't stop practicing. Loop, stem, crossbar — the merchant's fat script moving against the blanket, against his thigh, against the inside of his own skull, learning itself into him whether he consented or not.

Thursday was two days away. It felt like standing at the edge of deep water, hearing something enormous come up from below.

He brought nothing, Thursday. He took nothing.

Almost.
