## 7. The Harbormaster's Daughter

The trouble with watching a quay your whole life is that you learn its rhythms so well that anything off-beat rings like a cracked bell.

Adelise Voss had been watching since she was six years old, perched on the pilings with her father's spyglass she wasn't supposed to touch, learning the grammar of the harbor the way other girls learned hymns. She knew how a diver walked when he was tired — heavy through the shoulders, feet slapping. She knew how one walked when he was afraid — quick, eyes down, hands busy. And she knew, better than anyone in Porte-Cendre except perhaps her father, what smuggling looked like.

It looked like nothing. That was the trick of it. Smuggling looked like a man carrying something too ordinary to notice: a coil of rope, a crate of nails, a bundle of rags tied with string. Her father had taught her that much without meaning to. *Nobody smuggles anything worth having, Adelise. They smuggle things worth hiding.* He'd said it over supper once, gesturing at the customs manifests with his fork, and she'd understood even then that he was describing himself.

So when Marguerite Quintana came down the quay road on a grey Thursday afternoon with a bundle under his arm that was exactly the wrong shape to be rags, she saw it before he'd taken ten steps.

She was sitting on the capstan housing with a mending basket she had no intention of mending from, because the mending basket was itself a disguise — hers, learned from him, though he didn't know it. A harbormaster's daughter with a mending basket was furniture. Nobody looked twice. Nobody looked once. In four years of sitting here she had counted eleven separate acts of petty theft, two desertions planned in plain sight, and one affair conducted entirely by means of knots tied into a mooring line, which had been genuinely clever and which she still thought about sometimes.

Marguerite walked the way indentured men walked, which was a way she hated: small, folded, apologetic about the space his own body occupied. But today there was a flaw in it. Today his left elbow pressed too close to his ribs, protecting the bundle, and men who carried rags did not protect rags.

He passed beneath her without looking up. Of course he didn't look up. Looking up was for people who existed.

*Bundle of paper,* she thought, and knew she was right the way you know weather. The letter-writer's shop stood just past the last warehouse, at the ragged edge where company land gave out, and everyone knew Silas Okafor sold ink and penmanship and not much else. Everyone also knew the company had begun asking questions about who bought paper, and why, and how much. Her father had mentioned it at dinner with the flat tone he used for things that were business and therefore none of her concern, which was how she knew they mattered most.

Marguerite would reach the end of the quay, turn toward the shop, and be gone, and she would sit here with her basket and her knowledge and do precisely nothing, the way she always did nothing, the way doing nothing was its own slow death by a thousand unremarkable afternoons—

"Quintana."

Her mouth had moved before she'd decided anything. That was happening to her more lately, words escaping custody like sailors jumping ship.

He stopped. Turned. And there it was — the flinch, quick as a fish under a stone, gone almost before it arrived. He knew her name meant trouble. Everybody's name meant trouble; hers meant more than most.

"You're carrying paper," she said.

"I'm carrying—" His hand started to shift the bundle and stopped. "Rags."

"For the shop."

"For the shop."

"Silas Okafor buys no rags. I've watched his accounts." She hadn't, actually — she'd watched *him*, which amounted to the same education. "Walk over here."

And here was the thing that surprised her: he came. Not quickly, not willingly — his feet dragged like a man wading upstream — but he came, and he set the bundle on the capstan housing beside her basket, and he unwrapped it without being asked twice, because a man with no choices has learned that pretending to have them only prolongs things.

Paper. Good paper. A dozen sheets at least, heavy stock, the kind that cost more per sheet than an indentured diver earned in a week. And tucked among the sheets, white as a bone in sand, a printed form she recognized instantly because she'd seen a stack of them on her father's desk every shipping season, locked in a drawer she'd picked open at fourteen out of pure boredom:

Passage chits. Blank ones.

The quay seemed very loud all of a sudden. Gulls, water, the creak of the *Marianne* at anchor waiting on the storm-damaged bridge, the hammering from the repair crews. All of it ordinary. None of it covering the sound of her own pulse.

"Do you know what these are?" she asked, and heard that her voice had gone strange — low, careful, the voice of someone standing at the edge of a thing.

"No," said Marguerite Quintana, with the terrible honesty of a man who knows lying is pointless.

"They're blank passage chits."

"Yes."

"A man caught with those doesn't get flogged. A man caught with those gets the audit. Gets questions about who gave them to him, and where the others are, and whether he can read." She was talking fast now and couldn't stop herself. "They'll take the letters off you if you can read, Quintana. You know that? Reading is contraband on a man like you. It proves intent."

He looked at her then — really looked, first time in four years of sharing a harbor — and what was in his face wasn't fear. That was what undid her. It was *attention*. Like she was a chart he'd never been allowed to study.

"Why aren't you reporting me?" he said quietly.

Because my father is the harbormaster and this harbor is a throat and I have lived inside it twenty years swallowing everything it carries and I cannot swallow one more season of watching men board ships they owe their lives to and women wave until the ships are dots and nothing ever changes, ever, including me.

"I don't know," she said.

They sat on the capstan housing while the light went long and copper, and she learned more about Silas Okafor's operation in an hour than her father's inspectors had learned in a year, mostly because Marguerite talked the way starved men eat — carefully at first, then helplessly, then all at once.

He told her about the lessons. About ink and salt water and how names were made and unmade on paper. He didn't say *my father's name is in a ledger somewhere, erased* — he didn't have to. She'd grown up on the ledgers' leavings. She'd seen her father reconcile accounts against the diving rolls and had noticed, at fifteen, that dead divers' debts had a way of surviving their deaths, migrating sideways onto mothers and sons like hereditary property, which was exactly what the company intended them to be.

"My mother was born on the water side," she said, when he ran out of words. "Before my grandfather sold the boat and took the company's signing bonus. Did you know that? There's a whole family of us that used to be free."

Marguerite said nothing. He had a way of saying nothing that made you keep talking, which she suspected was a skill and possibly a weapon.

"She hates the harbor," Adelise went on. "She grows flowers that would die if they smelled salt. She keeps the windows facing inland. My father thinks she's being difficult. She's being *accurate*."

The gulls wheeled. Somewhere a winch groaned.

"What will you do with the chits?" she asked finally.

"Give them back. Or burn them. Silas says—" He stopped, recalibrated. "Silas says a forged document in ignorant hands is just evidence. You need to know what you're holding before you hold it."

"And do you? Know?"

"Getting there."

She turned one of the chits over in her fingers. Heavy stock, watermark of the Voss Trading Company — a ship above three waves, arrogant even in miniature — the perforated stub, the columns for name, destination, fare paid. She had watched hundreds of these filled out. She could fill one out in her sleep. Name, destination, fare paid. Three lines between a person and gone.

"Write mine," she said.

The words landed on the quay between them and sat there, enormous.

Marguerite stared at her. "Yours."

"One chit. Filled out. In a real hand, not mine — mine's known, obviously, my father makes me sign the guest registers." She was speaking quickly again, the plan assembling itself out of parts she'd apparently been storing for years without knowing it. "A woman traveling alone raises fewer flags if she's traveling as somebody's wife or sister, so put a surname on it that exists. Destination doesn't matter. North, past the bridge towns. Anywhere the company's reach gets thin."

"That's—"

"Don't say *forgery* like it's a swear word. You've got a dozen blanks in your lap."

"That's different. Those go back to Silas. This is—" He was doing the arithmetic on his face; she could see him carry the numbers. "If they catch you with it, they won't audit you. Your father owns the audit. They'll come for whoever wrote it. They'll come for Silas."

"Then don't get caught writing it."

"It isn't that simple."

"Nothing is that simple. That's not an argument, it's an observation." She stood, brushing off her skirt, and found she was shaking slightly and hoped it read as wind. "I've spent four years sitting on this capstan counting other people's escapes, Quintana. Desertions I could've warned my father about and didn't. Knots in mooring lines I left tied. I'm not asking you to make me brave. I'm telling you I already am, and it hasn't got anywhere to go, and I am *tired*."

He looked up at her from the capstan housing, this small folded man with stolen paper in his lap, and something passed across his face that she recognized because she felt it too — the shock of seeing your own cage reflected in someone else's eyes and realizing the bars were the same bars, manufactured in the same place, installed by the same patient machinery.

"You wouldn't leave," he said at last. "Not yet. Not with the bridge closed. There's no ship going north that isn't ours."

"I know when the ships go. I've told you — I watch everything."

"Then why ask now?"

Because if I don't ask now I never will. Because the asking is the escape, even before the leaving. Because a woman with a forged chit in her drawer is a different animal from a woman with a mending basket, even if neither of them ever uses what she holds.

"Because I want to know it's possible," she said. "That's all. For now. Write it, keep it, give it to me when it's done, and we'll see whether either of us ever uses the damn thing."

He was quiet for a long moment. The tide turned below them; she heard it in the change of the water's voice against the pilings, the harbor inhaling.

"There's a price," he said finally.

"Of course there's a price. Nothing free comes off this quay."

"Not money." He gathered the paper slowly, wrapping it, and she saw his hands — ink-stained now, she noticed, the right middle finger calloused in a way it hadn't been a month ago. A working hand. A writer's hand, growing. "Silas says every document wants a twin. Every truth needs a copy walking around loose, or the original gets buried. If I write your chit—" He hesitated, and she watched him decide to trust her, watched it happen like a door opening a crack. "There are other documents. Real ones. Letters that have to move across that bridge when it opens, and they can't go by post, and they can't go by me because I'll be on a ship by then, probably, and—"

He stopped. Looked at her. Understood, visibly, what he was about to do, and did it anyway.

"—someone at the boarding might carry them. Someone nobody searches. Someone whose pockets are full of mending."

The quay held its breath. So did she.

"You want me to distribute forged manumission papers," she said, "to indentured divers' families. On boarding day. On my father's quay."

"Yes."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll write your chit anyway," he said, "because you asked, and because it's yours to use or burn. But I'd rather not do any of it alone."

Below them the water slapped the stones. Above them the gulls argued. Out past the point, the *Marianne* rode at anchor, patient as a debt, waiting for the bridge to open and carry away everything the town had promised to lose.

Adelise Voss looked down at this man — this pearl diver's son with ink on his fingers and a dead father's name in some ledger and the nerve to recruit *her* — and felt the strangest sensation of her carefully invisible life: that she had just been seen. Fully. By someone with no power at all, which somehow made it count double.

"All right," she said. "But I pick which families. And Quintana—"

"Yes?"

"When you write that chit, spell my name right. *Adelise.* People always put an extra *s* in it. Even my father."

Something happened to his mouth then — the ghost of a smile, the first she'd ever seen on him, gone almost as soon as it came but unmistakably real. "I'll practice," he said.

He shouldered the bundle of paper and walked on down the quay toward the letter-writer's shop, unhurried now, the rags nobody questioned. She watched him go, then opened her mending basket and sat with it in her lap and did not mend anything, thinking about names, and drawers, and doors.

That night she would pick her father's lock again and memorize the format of a genuine passage chit down to the clerk's abbreviation marks, and tell herself it was research.

That night, too, in a shop at the edge of company land, an old man would cough blood into a cloth and look at the boy copying at his bench and say, *Who was that on the quay?* — and Marguerite would answer, *No one,* and then, correcting himself for the first time in his life: *Someone. Her name is Adelise.*

And she would not learn until later what those eleven letters would cost, or what the fire would take, or that the chit with her name spelled correctly would sit in her drawer for months like a seed waiting on rain — but standing there on the capstan housing with the tide turning under her, she already knew the only thing that mattered:

The cage had a door. She had just watched a boy walk through it, and he had left it open behind him.
