## 10. The Inspector

Silas knew the man was coming three days before the ship brought him, because gossip traveled faster than steam in a town with nothing to talk about.

He heard it first from the fishwife who sold him his supper — a woman named Bette whose voice carried across two streets and into closed windows without effort. A company inspector, she said. Coming on the *Corvane*, the supply boat that ran ahead of the last shipment. Not the usual sort, either. The usual sort came to count barrels and argue about wharfage fees. This one was from the head office itself. This one had been sent.

Sent for what, Silas did not need to ask. He had known this day would come for eleven years, since the night he copied the first true ledger page by candlelight with shaking hands and told himself that if they ever caught him, at least the copies would already be gone. They had never caught him. But men like Voss's inspectors did not come to catch. They came to confirm what they already suspected, and to leave behind consequences large enough to be seen from the water.

His cough had worsened through the autumn, and now it arrived before he did, so to speak — announcing him in rooms, waking him at the hour before dawn with a wet rattling sound that frightened him more than any inspector could. He had begun keeping a basin by the bed. In the mornings there was sometimes color in it that he chose not to name. A letter-writer knows the weight of words; he also knows when a word is not worth writing down.

He sat up that third morning, spat into the basin, and looked out the window above his workbench at the gray water of the harbor, where the *Corvane* would appear within the day.

"Well," he said to the empty shop, because saying things aloud had become a habit of men who lived alone, "let us see what you are made of."

* * *

The shop smelled of ink and tallow and, beneath both, the salt rot of a building that had stood at the waterline longer than the company itself. Silas had kept it for thirty-one years. He had written love letters here, and bills of sale, and complaints to distant magistrates that were never answered. And for eleven years, in the back room behind the false shelf, he had written freedom.

The letters were nearly done now. Fourteen of them. Each one a manumission for a diver whose debt had been inflated past every honest measure, whose term had been extended year upon year by clerks in a head office that had never once smelled the sea. Each one signed in a hand that belonged to no living man — or rather, to men who had drowned, or been transferred, or simply erased from the rolls, their names left orphaned on company paper like unclaimed parcels. Silas had learned over decades that a signature is only ink arranged confidently. Confidence could be learned. Arrangement too.

What could not be learned, except by doing it wrong once, was the weight of what the ink carried.

He heard Marguerite before he saw him — the boy still moved like a diver's son, soft-footed even on boards that announced everyone else. The door opened and the boy came in carrying a parcel of paper he had not been asked to bring, which was how Marguerite said most of the things he could not say.

"You've heard," Silas said.

Marguerite set the paper down. "Everyone's heard. Brandt. From the head office. They say he inspected the Port Maren operation and two clerks lost their positions."

"Two clerks lost their positions," Silas repeated, "or two clerks were found to have taken the blame for something larger?"

The boy's jaw tightened. That was another thing Silas had watched grow these weeks: the tightening. When Marguerite had first hidden in his shop from the audit, he had been all smooth surfaces, a boy polished by usefulness, agreeing with everything. Now the surfaces showed grain.

"My father was blamed for something larger once," Marguerite said quietly. "A cracked valve on the air line. It was logged as diver error."

"I know," Silas said. "I wrote the entry."

The silence between them had a different quality than it used to. Not empty. Loaded, like a spring.

"He didn't make the error," Marguerite said.

"No. But the log required an error, and the log is what survives." Silas laid his hand flat on the workbench, feeling the grain of wood he had touched ten thousand times. "That is the whole secret of paper, boy. Paper outlives the truth it was meant to hold. So we must decide, every time we write, whether we are serving the paper or the truth."

"And you served the truth afterward. The letters."

"Afterward," Silas agreed. "Afterward is where old men do their work. It's the young who have to act while the truth is still happening." He coughed then, and could not stop it, and turned away toward the basin so the boy would not see what came up. When he turned back, Marguerite's face had gone carefully blank, and Silas understood that the boy had seen anyway. Divers' children learn to read bodies the way clerks read ledgers.

"The inspector will want to search the shops," Marguerite said. "All the waterfront buildings. The demolition order comes after — that's how they do it. Search first, condemn second. Condemnation is just paperwork for what they decided on the boat."

"You know their methods well."

"I grew up inside them." Marguerite looked at the back room door, at the false shelf behind it, at everything it held. "How long would it take you to move fourteen letters?"

Silas smiled, though it cost him breath. "Not long to move them. Longer to decide where they'd be safe. There is nowhere safe, Marguerite. There is only places where being found costs someone else more than it costs us."

"That sounds like something my mother would say."

"It sounds like something every mother says eventually."

* * *

The *Corvane* came in on the afternoon tide, riding high and light, and Inspector Brandt came down the gangway in a gray coat cut for a colder city, carrying nothing but a leather case and an expression of mild disappointment, as though the entire town were a document he had already read and found wanting.

Silas watched him from the doorway of the chandler's next door, where he had gone under the pretense of buying wick oil. Watching was an old trade of his. A man who forged signatures for a living learns to study faces the way other men study weather: what is coming, how hard, from which quarter.

Brandt was perhaps fifty. Broad through the shoulders in a way that spoke of desk-bound years over an athletic youth. His eyes did the thing Silas dreaded most in officials: they counted. Doorways, boats, faces. Silas watched the eyes pass over the waterfront shops — the chandler, the ropewalk shed, the net-mender's stall, and last, lingering a half-second longer than the rest, the narrow painted sign that read OKAFOR — LETTERS, DOCUMENTS, FAIR RATES.

There it was. The half-second. Silas had spent thirty-one years learning what a half-second meant.

He bought his wick oil, walked home slowly along the quay, and let himself be seen. If the inspector wanted to find the letter-writer, the letter-writer would be found. A man hiding looks like a man hiding. A man copying documents in plain sight of the harbor, in full daylight, with fair rates posted on his door, looked like exactly what he appeared to be: an aging tradesman with a cough, writing other people's correspondence for pennies.

The best forgery, Silas had told Marguerite only last week, is the one nobody thinks to check because it is too ordinary to be worth checking.

That evening Brandt took a room at the harbormaster's house — the company always lodged its inspectors with the harbormaster, which meant, Silas noted, that Adelise Voss-Quintana — no, that was not her name, that was the name the boy's heart had invented and Silas was too old to indulge such inventions — that Adelise, the harbormaster's daughter, would be serving the man his dinner. He wondered what she made of that. He wondered, with a flicker of dry amusement, whether the harbormaster's daughter understood yet what kind of storm had docked at her father's pier.

She would. She was quicker than her father. Most daughters were.

* * *

Brandt came to the shop on the second morning.

Silas heard the footsteps pause outside — that counting pause again — and then the bell above his door rang, a small tin sound he had never bothered to replace, and the inspector stepped in out of the gray morning light and stood dripping mildly on the floorboards.

"Silas Okafor," Brandt said. Not a question. He had read the manifest of Silas's life on the boat, or before it.

"Inspector. You'll forgive me if I don't rise; the sea air gets into my chest." Which was true, though Silas would have risen anyway if rising wouldn't have triggered the coughing fit he could feel gathering in his lungs like weather. He gestured with his pen toward a stool. "Sit, if you like. I'm finishing a bill of lading for the chandler. Fair rates, as the sign says, but slow rates, I'm afraid."

Brandt did not sit. He moved along the shelves instead, reading spines, letting his fingertips trail across the leather and board. A man inventorying. Silas wrote his line and blotted it and waited.

"Thirty-one years at this address," Brandt said.

"Give or take. The first two I shared with a sailmaker who drank."

"You write a great many documents."

"A town writes a great many documents. I merely put them down. Bills, letters, petitions, the occasional complaint to a magistrate who will never read it. People need their words made official, Inspector. That's the trade."

"Mm." Brandt lifted a jar of ink, uncapped it, sniffed it, set it back with the cap aligned precisely as he had found it. "You'd know, then, about the demolition order coming for this row. The waterfront improvements. Company land reclamation."

"I've heard talk."

"There'll be compensation schedules. Relocation allowances. Standard procedure." Brandt turned and looked at Silas directly for the first time, and Silas met the look with the flat pleasant patience of a man who has nothing in his shop but ink. "You serve a lot of the divers' families, I imagine. Letters home, that sort of thing."

"All sorts of people. Divers among them."

"They're a population of interest to the company just now. There was a leak, Mr. Okafor. Some pages from an internal ledger, circulated off-island. The head office takes it seriously." Brandt said it lightly, conversationally, the way a man mentions rain. "Debt figures. Contract terms. Nothing a townsman like you would ever see, of course."

"Of course," Silas said. "Though I'll tell you what I do see, Inspector, free of charge, since you're standing there. I see men who signed contracts they couldn't read. I write out the terms for them in plain speech, and I'll tell you plainly that half of them go pale when they hear the numbers spoken aloud. Whatever your leaked pages say, I'd wager they didn't surprise anyone on this island."

Something moved behind Brandt's eyes — not anger. Recalculation. He had expected either a guilty flinch or a stupid innocence, and had gotten instead an old man stating facts.

"Careful talk," Brandt said mildly.

"Cheap talk. It's the expensive kind I sell." Silas blotted his line, blew on it, and set the bill of lading aside. "Was there something specific, Inspector? My afternoon is full of other people's errands."

"Just familiarizing myself with the waterfront." Brandt put on his hat. At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and gave the shop one last slow survey — the workbench, the shelves, the curtain across the back room — with those counting eyes. "Your health, Mr. Okafor. You sound like a man who shouldn't plan too far ahead."

"None of us should," Silas said. "But I find planning is cheaper than hoping, and lasts about as long."

The bell rang. The footsteps went away along the quay, unhurried, counting.

* * *

When he was certain the man was gone, Silas drew the curtain, opened the false shelf, and sat down heavily on his stool with the fourteen letters spread before him in their oilcloth wrapping.

Fourteen names. Okon. Eze. Tamuno and his brother. Nine others whose debts had been grown like tumors in duplicate columns, year over year, until no diver could pay his way out of a hole dug deliberately beneath him. Eleven years of nights. Eleven years of borrowed hands — dead clerks, transferred clerks, a supervisor drowned off the north reef whose signature Silas had practiced until his own wrist ached.

And now a man in a gray coat was lodging forty paces away, sniffing the harbor air for the scent of true figures.

Silas coughed, long and deep, and when it passed he found himself gripping the edge of the workbench, breathing in shallow sips. The basin again, later. More color. He was running out of the only currency he had ever truly owned, which was time.

He thought about burning the letters. He had thought about it before, in the way a man thinks about drowning his own children to spare them a worse fate. Burned, they protected no one. Carried, they might free fourteen families — or get fourteen families questioned, detained, disappeared into the company's interior offices. Every path ended in risk. The only variable was whose hands bore it.

Marguerite's hands, some treacherous part of him whispered. The boy's hands were quick and exact. The boy's hand could already do four of Silas's borrowed signatures better than Silas himself could now manage, with his joints stiffening and his breath shortening. The boy had been raised to carry things — that was the whole shape of his life, measuring everything by what could be borne away when the orders came. Well. Here was a thing worth bearing.

But Marguerite was indentured. The last shipment sailed on the morning tide, and every bound hand was meant to be on it, and a boy caught delivering forbidden letters would forfeit far more than a contract. Silas had watched enough young people be broken by other people's causes to distrust his own longing to hand the burden over.

A knock at the front door — two taps, soft, the rhythm of someone who did not wish to be seen knocking.

Silas wrapped the letters, slid them back behind the false shelf, and pulled the curtain. Through the shop's front window he could see the figure waiting outside: broad-shouldered, patient, in a coat that had been mended well but had still been mended.

Marguerite. Come in the dusk, when the quay was emptying, when watchers were tired.

Silas opened the door. The boy slipped in, glancing once over his shoulder at the darkening street, at the lit window of the harbormaster's house where a gray-coated guest was taking his supper.

"He asked about you today," Marguerite said without preamble. "At the house. Over dinner. My— the harbormaster mentioned the shops along the row, and Brandt asked who kept the letter-shop, and how long, and whether I knew you." He hesitated. "I said I delivered ink sometimes. That you were an old man with a cough and fair rates. He wrote it down."

"You did well."

"I lied to an inspector's face," Marguerite said, and there was something strange in his voice — not fear. Wonder, almost. The wonder of a boy discovering a muscle he hadn't known he had.

"Yes," Silas said. "First lie is the hardest. After that they're only ink." He coughed again and waved off the boy's half-step forward. "Sit. There's work tonight. He'll search this row within days — I watched his eyes count my shelves, and I've watched eyes for thirty-one years. Before he does, we move the letters, and we move them somewhere no warrant reaches, and I need your help to decide where, because I am suddenly an old man who shouldn't plan too far ahead, and you—" he looked at the boy, at the quick careful hands, at the grain showing through the polish, "—you are going to have to start planning farther ahead than you've ever allowed yourself."

Outside, the tide was turning. Somewhere on the water, the great ship that would carry the season's divers lay at anchor, waiting on the weather and the bridge, patient as a creditor.

The clock, Silas thought, settling his pen against the sound of it, has started in earnest.
