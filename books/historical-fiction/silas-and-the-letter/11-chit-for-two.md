## 11. Chit for Two

*Marguerite*

The harbor pass was the hardest thing Silas had ever asked me to copy, and he hadn't even asked. He'd left it on the desk under a paperweight — a blank form, the Voss crest at its head, the watermark showing through when I held it to the lamp like a ghost wearing a company uniform. Harbor passes went out with the harbormaster's own signature and a countersign that changed every season. Silas had the old countersign memorized from years of watching boats leave with men who hadn't chosen to leave. But this season's was different. This season's lived in the head of one man and his daughter.

Which meant there was only one way to get it, and I hated myself for thinking of it before I'd finished thinking of anything else.

I told myself it wasn't a plan. It was paper. Paper for the letters, because the letters needed to move by human hand and human hands needed passes, and Adelise kept the key ring of the quay office and knew which drawer breathed when her father opened it. That's what I told myself as I waited for her behind the salt shed where the fishermen hung their nets, where nobody stood after dark unless they wanted something they couldn't ask for in daylight.

She came late, wrapped against the wind, and she didn't pretend surprise at seeing me. She had stopped pretending things around me somewhere between the first smuggled sheaf of paper and the second, and I still didn't know what to do with that. A person who stops pretending is either very safe or very dangerous, and I couldn't decide which she was.

"You look like a man about to ask for money," she said.

"Worse." I held out the blank pass. "I need the countersign."

The wind pushed the nets against their hooks. She looked at the paper a long time without touching it.

"Do you know what happens to my father if a forged pass goes out under his seal?" she said. "Not to you. Not to your letter-writer. To him."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway."

She took the paper then, finally, and turned it over in her fingers the way her father must have taught her, edge to edge, checking for tampering. "You've changed," she said, and I couldn't tell if it was an accusation or a discovery. "In September you wouldn't have asked. In September you'd have carried whatever they gave you and said thank you and made yourself small."

"In September I didn't know what was in the ledgers."

"No," she said softly. "In September none of us did. We just felt it, like weather coming." She tucked the pass inside her coat, against her ribs. "I'll bring it tomorrow night. Same place. And Marguerite—" She paused, and the wind filled the pause with rope-creak and water. "When I bring it, you're going to write something on it. For me. Because I'm not doing this for your letters alone."

"What do you want written?"

"My name," she said. "And a destination. On my own pass. My own chit." Her chin came up the way it always did when she was daring someone to argue. "If everyone else gets to be free on paper tonight, so do I."

* * *

*Adelise*

He stared at me like I'd spoken in a language he almost knew. Good. Let him stare.

I had thought about saying it for weeks, turning the sentence over at night the way I turned my mother's ring over, worn smooth from handling but never put on. A woman who wanted off this island had exactly two roads: marriage or forgery, and I had watched what marriage did to women here — how it traded one man's ledger for another's, how the debt followed them like gulls follow the fish carts. I would not be entered in anyone's book. If I left, it would be on paper I controlled, in ink I chose, signed by a hand that owed me nothing.

Except his hand. His hand I was asking for, and we both knew why, and neither of us said why, and the not-saying was getting heavy enough to sink us both.

"Tomorrow night," he repeated.

"Bring two blanks. Don't make me steal."

"I'll bring two blanks." He hesitated. "Adelise. Where would you even go? The ship leaves on the morning tide. It doesn't come back until—"

"I know when it comes back." Everyone knew. The whole town ran on that rhythm: the ship away, the bridge closed, the compound sealed, the men counted and recounted like crates. "I'm not going on your ship. I'm going across, when the bridge opens. There are shipping offices on the mainland. A woman who can keep a quay office's books can keep anyone's books, and half the world is drowning in books nobody wants to keep."

"And your father?"

"My father has a son-shaped hole in him where a daughter should have been and has never once noticed I'm standing in it." I heard my own voice go flat and stopped. This was the thing about Marguerite: he made you honest whether you wanted to be or not. He listened like listening cost him something. Most people listen the way they watch a tide come in — pleasant, distant, no skin in it. He listened like your words were cargo he was personally responsible for delivering.

"Your father loves you," Marguerite said.

"My father loves the harbor," I said. "I live in it. There's a difference."

He didn't answer, and I was glad, because any answer he gave would have been wrong, and I liked him too much right then to let him be wrong.

We walked the seawall together because it was on both our ways and neither of us admitted it. Below us the anchored ship rode its lights, waiting for a morning that was still weeks off, and above us the compound lamps burned in their neat rows, counting everything, measuring everything, weighing every life on scales calibrated to tonnage. I had grown up inside those scales. So had he. That was the whole story of us, really — two weights pretending to be measures.

"The inspector," he said suddenly. "Brandt. Has your father said anything?"

"He's been summoned to the mainland office twice this month. He comes back quieter each time. Last week he burned a stack of old manifests in the yard and told my mother it was spring cleaning, in November." I glanced at him. "You know something."

"Silas knows something. Brandt isn't here for the audit. He's here because a true ledger exists and someone in the mainland office has started reading numbers that don't match." Marguerite's voice stayed level, but his hand found the rail and gripped it. "If they find the copy before the letters go out—"

"Then don't let them find it."

"Easier said."

"Everything worth doing is easier said. That's why they pay people to do the saying and boys like you to do the doing." I meant it lightly. It came out heavier than I intended, and I saw him feel it — the word *boys*, and under it the truth that he was twenty-two years indentured and would be called a boy by men who owned his next breath.

But he smiled. Small, crooked, real. "Adelise Quintana would have been a terrible company clerk."

"Adelise Harrow," I corrected, "and yes. Terrible. I'd have inflated every debt in the book out of spite and freed the whole island by accident."

That got a laugh out of him, quick and startled, the kind of laugh a man makes when he's forgotten he owns one. The sound went into me and stayed. That was the trouble with him. Everything he did went into me and stayed.

* * *

*Marguerite*

Two nights later she brought the countersign written on a scrap in her own careful clerk's hand — four characters, a date code, a small flourish over the last stroke that I would never have guessed existed — and I brought two blank passes as promised, and we sat in Silas's shop with the lamp turned low and the shutters barred while the old man slept in the back room, coughing in a rhythm I had learned to hate.

She watched me work. I could feel her watching, and it did something strange to my hand — steadied it, actually, which made no sense. Alone, my letters sometimes shook. Under her eyes they went down clean.

"You hold the pen like it's a knife," she said.

"It was the first thing I learned to hold."

"A knife or a pen?"

"Same thing, on this island. Both of them cut, and both of them belong to somebody else."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "Write mine now."

I pulled the second blank toward me. And stopped.

Because here was the thing I hadn't let myself think all week: her chit, done well, was perfect. Done well, it would carry her off this rock the moment the bridge opened, into a life with no compound in it, no ledgers, no Voss crest anywhere in sight. Done well, I would never see her again except as a name on a manifest I might someday forge.

And I could ruin it. A single hesitation in the countersign, a fraction too much ink in the flourish, and the harbor office would reject it, and she'd be stuck here with me, and I could spend the rest of my life ashamed of wanting that.

My hand knew both ways. That was the curse of the craft Silas had given me. A forger's hand will serve any master, including the worst one — the one that lives in your chest.

"Marguerite." Her voice, low. "Whatever you're deciding, stop deciding it and tell me."

I looked up. The lamplight caught her face, and there was no softness in it, only attention, total and unblinking, the same attention she gave ledgers. She had seen my hesitation. Of course she had. She read figures for a living; she could read a man's pause like a column that didn't add up.

"If I write it perfectly," I said, "you'll leave."

"Yes."

"And if I write it badly—"

"Don't you dare." She said it fast and quiet and fierce, leaning across the desk. "Don't you dare make me a bad forgery, Marguerite. Do you understand what that would be? It wouldn't be love. It would be the company's trick with better handwriting. Debt disguised as devotion. I have watched this island do that to people my whole life and I will not be done to." She sat back, breathing hard. "You write it true or you don't touch it."

The silence after that was the loudest room I'd ever been in.

So I wrote it true. Every stroke clean, the countersign exact, the flourish exact, the date coded to the reopening of the bridge. When I finished I blew the page dry and slid it across, and she picked it up and read her own freedom in her hands and her eyes went bright and furious at once, and I understood that I had just done the hardest thing I would ever do, harder than the letters, harder than staying or leaving, and that it was also the first thing I had ever done that was entirely mine.

"Thank you," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated that it cracked, and I loved her for hating it.

That was when I knew. Not a decision — a fact, arriving like the tide, indifferent to my plans. I loved her. I had probably loved her since the quay, since the paper, since the first time she looked at me and saw a person instead of a pair of hands.

"Ask me," she said.

"Ask you what?"

"What you've been not asking me for three weeks. Ask me what I'll do. Ask me what happens after." She set the chit down and flattened it with both palms, deliberately, like sealing a contract. "Because I'm not leaving on a maybe, Marguerite. Say it or don't."

And I opened my mouth to say it — to say *stay, wait for me, I'll find you when the letters are delivered, I'll cross the bridge, I'll sign my own name in your book* — and what came out instead was:

"I don't know what I'll do."

Her face didn't change. That was worse than anger.

"The orders come," I said. "The ship boards on the morning tide and every indentured hand walks up that gangplank or forfeits everything — the contract, the years, the release clause, all of it forfeited, and you start the count over at zero. I've spent my whole life learning what can be carried. I know how to leave. I have never once in my life known how to stay, Adelise. Staying isn't in my inventory. Nobody ever packed it for me."

"So learn," she said. "You learned a dead man's handwriting in six weeks. Learn your own life faster than that."

From the back room came a cough, long and wet, and then a silence that lasted one breath too long, and we both went still and listened, the way you listen to the sea when you're afraid of what's under it.

When the coughing settled, Adelise gathered her chit and rose, and at the door she paused with her hand on the latch.

"For the record," she said, not turning around, "I already know my answer. I've known it since the nets behind the salt shed. Whatever you choose — the ship, the letters, the fire I can smell coming for this shop — I choose the work. You're the only variable left in the equation." She looked back at me once, over her shoulder, lamplight and sea-dark. "Do your sums quickly. Brandt posted notices today. They're taking the waterfront shops at month's end."

The latch clicked. The wind took her footsteps.

I stood alone in the low lamplight with eleven unfinished letters and one finished lie and the first true sentence I'd ever failed to say, and on the desk, under the dying lamp, Silas's list of names waited for my hand — and beside it, folded into quarters where he must have left it days ago, a letter addressed in his failing script to a name I knew better than my own:

*Mrs. Quintana.*
