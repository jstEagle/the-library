## 12. The Workshop Still Stands

The notices went up on a Tuesday, pasted to the pilings at eye height so no one could pretend they hadn't seen them. I counted eleven of them on my walk from the compound — eleven sheets of good company stock, the Voss crest stamped in black at the top, and beneath it the same sentence repeated down the waterfront like a tide chart:

*Structures herein marked are condemned for harbor improvement. Occupants shall vacate within thirty days.*

Thirty days. The season's last ship sailed in twenty-six.

I stood in front of the notice nailed to Silas's doorframe and read it twice, because the first time my eyes had done what my eyes were trained to do: slid past anything with an official seal, the way a man learns to slide past his own reflection. *Structure herein marked.* Not even a name. The shop where a man had spent forty years writing other people out of bondage was, to the company, a structure.

Inside, Silas was at the desk, which surprised me, because he had not been at the desk in three days. He'd taken to the cot behind the ink shelf, coughing in a rhythm I had learned to time without meaning to — two short, one long, a pause that lasted long enough for me to set down whatever I held and listen for the next breath. That morning he was upright, dressed, his collar buttoned wrong by one hole, copying something in a hand so small I thought at first it was a mistake.

It wasn't a mistake. It was compression. He was fitting whole letters into half the space, as if paper itself had become expensive to him.

"You saw it," he said, without turning. He knew my step. He'd known it since the second week, when he told me I walked like a man carrying water across ice — all weight held in reserve.

"I saw it."

"Then sit down, Marguerite, and don't waste my morning looking sorry for me. Sorry is a luxury. We have work."

I sat. There were papers everywhere now, more than I'd ever seen in the shop, spread across the floor in ordered drifts like tidelines. In three weeks of lessons I had learned to read the geography of his chaos: the left side of the room was finished work, the right side was waiting work, and the middle — the middle was everything he hadn't decided about yet. The middle had grown. It had grown toward the walls.

"How many?" I asked. It was the question I'd been avoiding since the night he collapsed against the shutter and I'd caught him before the town could see him fall. "How many letters are still unfinished?"

He set down his pen. That alone frightened me. Silas Okafor did not set down his pen while there was light enough to see ink.

"Eleven," he said. "Eleven names. Okon you know. After him there's Bello, and the twins Adeyemi and Adebanji — those two go together or not at all, their contracts run joint, the company wrote it that way so neither could be freed without the other, clever devils — then Farrow, who's been held nine years past term, nine years, boy, and doesn't know there's a letter with her name on it because she can't read and no one dared tell her—"

His voice caught, and he pressed his knuckles to his mouth until the cough passed through him like weather.

"Eleven," he said again, quieter. "And I have maybe ten days of hand left in me. Maybe less. You've seen it." He held up his right hand, turned it over, and I saw what I had been refusing to see for a week: a tremor, fine as a hairline crack, running from the wrist up. A letter-writer's hand shaking. It was like watching a lighthouse go dark.

"The doctor—" I began.

"The doctor charges what I owe the company, which is to say everything, which is to say nothing can be done." He said it flatly, the way he said everything true. "Sit properly. Ink your pen. Today we finish the twins."

We worked until the light failed. Or rather, he worked, and I worked beside him, and somewhere in the third hour the distinction stopped mattering. He dictated the terms of each manumission from memory — dates of indenture, dates of term served, the inflated debts struck through in his head before they reached the page — and I drafted the body text while he corrected my spacing, my slant, my habit of pressing too hard at the ends of words, as if I could hold the ink down against its will.

"You write like you carry things," he muttered once. "Like every word might be taken from you if you don't grip it. Loosen. Paper isn't going anywhere. Well." He glanced at the notice outside the window. "This particular paper isn't."

At dusk he asked me to bar the door, which meant the lesson was over, but also that it wasn't. I dropped the bar and turned around and he was standing, using the desk edge the way a drowning man uses flotsam, and he had the strangest look on his face — not grave, exactly. Almost apologetic.

"Marguerite. Come here."

I came. He opened the drawer I had never been allowed to open — the bottom one, the deep one, the one he locked every night with a key he wore on a cord under his shirt — and took out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string, and heavy the way only paper can be heavy when enough truth is stacked inside it.

"Sit," he said again, and this time it wasn't a lesson-voice.

I sat. He untied the string with fingers that fumbled the knot twice, and I wanted to reach out and do it for him and knew he would sooner die than let me. Inside the oilcloth were letters. Finished ones, sealed ones, some decades old by the look of the wax — manumissions, dozens of them, delivered and undelivered, the whole secret archive of a life.

"Every one of these went out by hand," he said. "Never post. Post has clerks, clerks have eyes, eyes report to men who count indentures like sheep. A letter travels safe only when the person carrying it knows what it weighs." He looked up at me. His eyes were too bright, fever-bright, and very tired underneath. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"You're telling me why you never use the post."

"I'm telling you what happens next." He drew a breath that whistled faintly, and pushed the packet aside, and laid flat on the desk instead a single sheet — the list. Eleven names in his cramped compressed hand, each with a date beside it, each date inside the twenty-six days remaining. "When my hand goes, these get written anyway. That's the arrangement. That's been the arrangement since the day you walked in here pretending you didn't know what forgery looked like."

"Silas—"

"Swear it."

The word landed in the shop like a dropped anchor. Outside, the harbor slapped its slow applause against the pilings. Somewhere down the quay a winch groaned, loading the last of the season's cargo onto a ship that would sail whether I swore or didn't.

"Swear what, exactly?" I heard myself ask, and hated how careful the question sounded, how much it sounded like the voice of a man measuring what can be carried. "That I'll finish them? I can barely hold your hand for a full page. My letters wobble. You say so yourself, daily, at length—"

"Your letters wobble," he agreed, "and your hand shakes when you're afraid, and you press too hard, and you still think invisibility is a virtue instead of a scar. And none of it matters, because the letters don't need to be beautiful, Marguerite. They need to exist. A freed diver who can't read doesn't know if the flourishes are right. She knows someone put her name on paper and said *she belongs to herself*. That's the whole craft. Everything else is vanity." He leaned forward, and the tremor in his hand was visible even at rest now, a small animal shivering in the cage of his bones. "Eleven names. Before the tide. Swear it."

And here is the thing I have not told anyone, the thing I will carry longer than any letter: in that moment I wanted to refuse. Not out of cowardice — or not only cowardice. Out of arithmetic. I stood there doing what I have always done, weighing the bag before the journey: eleven letters against one ship, a dead man's oath against a clean contract, the burning shop against the open water. Every instinct I owned told me to say *I'll try*, which is the word people use when they've already decided no.

Instead I heard myself say: "If I swear, you teach me the seals. All of them. The company seal, the magistrate's countersign, the harbor office mark. If I'm signing other people's freedom, I won't do it half-blind."

Something moved across his face — surprise, then something older and sadder, almost pride. He had expected a refusal or a weeping yes, and I had given him negotiation, which is apparently what inheritance looks like when it comes from someone raised on ledgers.

"Done," he said. "Kneel if you want. Or don't. Kneeling is theater."

I didn't kneel. I put my hand flat on the list of eleven names instead, on the paper, the way you'd steady something that might blow away, and I said the oath in plain words, no ceremony, because ceremony felt like something I'd be allowed to take back later:

"I'll finish them. All eleven. Before the tide."

"And you'll deliver them by hand, or into hands that know the weight—"

"—never by post. Yes."

"And Marguerite." His voice dropped, and for a moment the teacher fell away and there was just an old dying man looking at a young man he had, against all his own rules, come to trust. "When they burn this place — and they will burn it, Brandt has already decided, the notice is just the polite part — the ledger copy goes with you. Not the letters first. The ledger. The letters free eleven people. The ledger frees everyone who comes after."

I took the oilcloth packet and the list and the weight of both, and walked home through streets that smelled of tar and rain coming, and I did the thing I have done every night of my life since I was seven years old: I inventoried. Shell comb. Diver's knife. Indenture papers. Now: eleven names, one true ledger, one oath.

My bag had gotten heavier without gaining a single ounce.

* * *

Three days later, the demolition crew arrived early.

Not to demolish — to measure. Six men in company canvas with poles and chains and a surveyor's instrument on a tripod, walking the waterfront shops like appraisers at an estate sale, calling numbers to a clerk who scratched them into a book. They measured Silas's shop twice, which I noticed because I noticed everything, and the second measurement was shorter than the first, and the surveyor frowned at the discrepancy as though the building itself were cheating him.

Silas watched them from behind the shutters, and I watched him watch, and neither of us said what we were both thinking: thirty days was a fiction. The measuring crews don't come for buildings you're giving a month to live.

"They'll fire it before the ship sails," he said that evening, calm as a man reading tomorrow's weather. "Burned structures make messy paperwork. Ashes don't contest assessments." He coughed, two short, one long, and the pause afterward stretched until I started counting. "So we move faster than the fire. Okon tonight. The twins tomorrow."

He was worse. Anyone could see it. The tremor had climbed past his elbow, and twice during the Okon letter he lost the thread of a clause he himself had dictated a minute before, and sat staring at the blank space where the word should be with an expression of pure bafflement, like a man arriving in his own kitchen and finding the furniture rearranged. Each time, I fed the word back to him — I had begun keeping a silent transcript in my head, every phrase he spoke, filed away the way I used to file escape routes — and each time relief flooded his face so nakedly that I pretended not to see it.

By midnight the Okon manumission was complete. Seal, countersign, harbor mark. My hand had done the body; his hand, guided mine over the signature block for the final flourish, trembling like a branch in wind, and the result was imperfect and legal-looking and true, and I set it in the drying rack between two bricks and felt something turn over in my chest that I refused to examine.

"That's one," Silas said. He was gray around the mouth. "Ten."

"Sleep," I told him. "You're no use to anyone dead before dawn."

"Death keeps no schedule I've signed," he said, and laughed, and the laugh became the cough, and the cough became so long and so deep that I got him to the cot and propped him up and held the cup to his mouth while he drank, and when it was over he gripped my wrist with both hands — both, the strong one and the shaking one — and said, very clearly, the way people speak when they want the words to survive the night:

"The tide doesn't wait, Marguerite. Neither does the fire. Whichever comes first, the letters outrun them both. Promise me again. I won't remember asking."

"I promise. Again. Sleep."

He slept. I sat by the shuttered window with the list of eleven names and read them like a rosary: Okon, done. Bello, the twins, Farrow, and seven more whose faces I was beginning to know better than my own — divers whose contracts I had helped audit, whose inflated debts I had copied without ever once wondering who they were. Ten names left. Twenty-two days to the tide. And somewhere out on the black water, the ship at anchor, patient as a debt.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, Silas's breathing kept its broken rhythm, two short, one long, and I matched my own breathing to it without meaning to, the way I match everything, the way I was made to match everything — and then, deliberately, for the first time in my life, I breathed off-rhythm.

Just to feel what it cost.

In the morning, when I came back with ink and bread, there was a folded note wedged in the doorframe, no name, no seal, paper I recognized because I had sold the stack it came from to the chandler's daughter three weeks ago.

Adelise's handwriting. Five words:

*Brandt boarded early. Be careful.*
