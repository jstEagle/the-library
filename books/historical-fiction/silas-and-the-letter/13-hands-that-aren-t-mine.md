## 13. Hands That Aren't Mine

The letter to Okon took me four days.

Silas made me do it four times. Not because the hand was wrong — by the second attempt he said the hand was nearly perfect, better than perfect, perfect the way a coin is perfect, identical on both sides and worth nothing — but because I kept stopping.

"You stop at every name," he said, standing over my shoulder with his breath whistling through the ruined places in his chest. "You write *Okon* like it's a stone you're setting down carefully so it doesn't wake up."

"It is a name," I said.

"It is eleven names, and then it is more than eleven, and if you flinch at each one you will die of old age before the tide." He pulled the paper away from me and held it to the lamp. "Again."

I wrote it again.

By the fourth attempt my wrist had learned something my stomach hadn't agreed to: that the letters could come without permission. That was the thing nobody tells you about forgery, about any craft done long enough. The body is a traitor. It learns what you teach it whether or not your heart signs off. My father's hands learned to dive until they could find shell in black water without light. My mother's hands learned to carry until carrying was just walking. And now my hand was learning Silas Okafor's script — the narrow ascenders, the way he hooked his capitals like a man pulling a nail — and it did this while I stood apart from myself and watched.

The manumission for Okon read:

*Let it be known that Okon Adeyemi, pearl diver in service of the Voss Trading Company since the season of 1889, has satisfied in full the terms of his indenture as recorded in the true ledger of accounts, and is hereby freed of all debt and obligation, his term complete, his person his own...*

I had copied the true figures from Silas's duplicate ledger myself. Nine years of dives. The company's own book showed Okon owing four hundred and twelve pesos more than any diver could earn in nine years of black water. The real arithmetic showed him owed *them* nothing, and them owing him sixty-three pesos in back wages, which we both knew would never be paid, which was its own kind of lie, a smaller one, the kind you let stand because the alternative was drowning.

Silas read the finished letter twice. Then he set it down and looked at me with an expression I couldn't name.

"Well?" I said.

"Say the words aloud."

"What words?"

"The words you're thinking. The ones making your mouth go sour."

I almost said there were none. But lying to Silas had become difficult in a specific way — not impossible, but costly, like spending coins I needed for something else.

"I hate how easy it was," I said. "The last one. My hand just — went. It didn't ask me. It wrote a man free like I was signing for a delivery of ink."

"And?"

"And it should be harder." I heard my voice climb and hated that too. "It should cost something. A man's whole life is in those lines and my wrist didn't even ache. What does that make me?"

Silas lowered himself onto his stool — slowly, the way he did everything now, like a ship settling — and folded his ruined hands on the desk between us.

"Sit," he said.

I sat.

"When I was younger than you," he began, "in a port I won't name because the company has agents who still remember my face, I forged papers for a man who beat his wife. Passage chits, work permits, a new name entirely. She came looking for him two years later, and I recognized her at once, because she had the look women get when they have stopped expecting anything. And she asked me — not angrily, that was the terrible part, she asked me tiredly — why I had helped him run."

He paused. His cough sat in his chest like cargo shifting, and he mastered it.

"I told her the truth. I said I never asked what the papers were for. A hand for hire, a pen for sale. And she looked at me and said: *so your skill belongs to whoever walks in first.*" He tapped the desk. "That sentence rearranged me, boy. I decided that night that my hand would have opinions. That before I wrote for anyone, I would know what I was writing, and why, and for whom. Not because the writing became moral the moment I cared — the ink doesn't know, the paper doesn't know, the hand doesn't know. Because *I* would know. The knowing is the only part of this that can't be forged."

"So the ease doesn't matter."

"The ease matters nothing." He pushed Okon's letter across the desk toward me. "Ease is just practice. You've been practicing smallness your whole life — making yourself invisible, useful, portable. Do you think invisibility was hard for you? No. Your body does it without asking, same as the script. Ease proves nothing except where your hours went." He leaned forward. "What matters is whether the man holding the pen knows what the ink is buying. Read it again. Whose freedom is this?"

"Okon's."

"Whose money bought the paper? Whose risk sits in these lines? If Brandt finds this shop tomorrow, whose neck—"

"Mine. Yours."

"And whose debt does this letter erase — a debt the company invented out of thin air and salt water?" He waited. "Answer it properly. Not the answer I want."

I looked at the letter. At the narrow ascenders, the hooked capitals. My hand's work, wearing Silas's hand like a coat.

"A debt that was never owed," I said. "So it isn't erasing anything. It's just — telling the truth in a shape the truth isn't allowed to take."

"There." Silas nodded, once, and something in his face eased, though the cough came right behind it and took a long time leaving. "A borrowed hand can still carry true weight. The weight was never in the handwriting. Any inspector can verify a hand; no inspector alive can verify a conscience. That's why they fear the ledgers and not the letters. Letters are just the ledgers with the cruelty removed."

But I couldn't let it sit there, because there was another question under it, the one that had been souring my mouth all four days, and Silas had taught me too well to leave it unsaid.

"Then why the disguise at all?" I said. "If the weight is in the truth and not the hand — why must it wear your script? Why can't Okon's letter go out signed by me, in my own writing, my own name?"

Something moved through the room then, quiet as the lamp flame bending.

"Because my name opens doors yours cannot," Silas said slowly. "Twenty-two years of delivered letters. Twenty-two years of divers' families who trust the hand that freed their fathers. A letter in a stranger's writing gets read as a rumor. A letter in mine gets read as a fact." He studied me. "And because you are not ready to put your name on anything. You proved that yourself, four nights ago, when Brandt offered you a clean contract and you said yes."

The word landed where he meant it to land. I kept my face still — I'd had practice; smallness is mostly face — but inside, the shame turned over like a diver rolling in current.

"I said yes because—"

"You said yes because saying yes was easy. And now you know exactly what I think of ease." He gathered Okon's letter, squared its edges against the desk, and set it atop the growing stack — ten letters left after this one, ten names waiting in the true ledger. "Your hand obeys you. Good. One day soon it will need to obey you in your own name, and you'll want the obedience ready. Until then—" he nudged the stack toward me, "—we have ten more truths to dress in stolen clothes."

I picked up the next sheet. Blank paper has always frightened me a little; it asks so directly what you intend to be. I dipped the pen, and my wrist settled into Silas's rhythm without being told, and I felt the old grief move through me — my father erased from the company books, my mother's undelivered freedom sitting in a drawer twenty feet from where she slept — and I let it move, because Silas said grief given a language was fuel, and he had been right about everything else.

Halfway down the page, footsteps on the boardwalk outside. Heavy, unhurried, official. Silas's eyes found mine across the lamplight, and his hand closed around the true ledger, and neither of us breathed until the steps passed.

They didn't pass quickly enough.

Because through the gap in the shutters, as they faded, I caught the sound of voices — two men, low, and one of them saying words I knew the shape of before I knew their order:

*"…the Okafor shop. Brandt wants it listed first."*

* * *

We worked until the lamp burned low. Ten names became nine, then eight. Somewhere past midnight Adelise scratched softly at the back shutter with the signal we'd agreed on — three taps, pause, two — and slipped in smelling of rain and lamp oil, her eyes going straight to the stack of letters on the desk.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Eight finished."

"Brandt posted the demolition notices early." She laid a folded paper on the desk, and I saw the company seal and the date, and the date was wrong, too soon, weeks too soon. "The waterfront shops come down in eleven days. The tide comes in six." Her voice stayed level the way mine did when I was pretending, and I recognized it, and hated hearing it in her. "Whatever you're doing here — you'll be doing it faster than you planned."

Silas looked at the notice for a long moment. Then he laughed — a dry, rusted sound, half cough.

"Faster," he said. "Good. Truth keeps poorly anyway. Marguerite — fetch the next name. We'll sleep when the tide takes us or it doesn't."

I dipped the pen. Outside, the harbor lay black and patient, the ship already riding at anchor, waiting for morning with everything I owned packed in my bag aboard it.

Eight letters. Six days. And somewhere across town, an inspector drawing up a list, and my name — I was sure of it now — written on it in a hand far worse than Silas's, and far more honest.
