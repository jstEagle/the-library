## 14. Brandt's Offer

He came for me at the cooperage.

I should have seen it sooner. For three days the compound had been too quiet — no audit clerks on the quay, no tally-men asking after Silas Okafor's customers — and quiet, in Voss Town, is never peace. Quiet is the sound of someone deciding where to put their hands.

I was counting barrel staves for the shipment manifest when the yard went still around me the way water goes still around a dropped stone. The coopers kept working, but slower. The tally-boy at the gate found something urgent to study in his ledger. And Brandt walked down the row of sheds like a man touring property he already owned.

I had seen him twice before, both times from across a room: once at the season's opening address, once passing the harbor office in his grey coat that never took salt-stain no matter how long he stayed on this coast. He had the look of a man sent out from somewhere colder and better lit, a man who measured this place in ledgers returned and problems closed. He stopped three feet from me. Not closer. Three feet was deliberate; it said *I don't need to loom over you*.

"Marguerite Quintana." He didn't make it a question. "The letter-writer's errand boy."

"I carry ink," I said. "That's all."

"That's all." He turned a stave over with one finger, examining the grain as if the wood might confess something. "Do you know what I admire about this company town, Quintana? Everything in it has a function. The divers dive. The coopers coop. The tally-boys tally. Even the bridge has one job, and it does it half the year and rests the other half. Nothing here is decorative." He set the stave down. "Except, apparently, an old man writing letters nobody commissioned."

"The old man writes letters," I said. "People pay him. It's not complicated."

"Nothing is complicated." Brandt smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all week, because it was patient. "That's what people refuse to understand. Every problem I've ever been sent to close was simple underneath. Someone wanted something they weren't paid to want."

* * *

He didn't ask me anything else at the cooperage. That was the first lesson of it — he made me stand there through forty seconds of silence while he read my face like a manifest, and then he touched his hat brim and walked on, and I understood that the interview had happened whether or not any questions had been asked. He had learned what he needed: that I flinched at *errand boy*, that I lied badly about ink, that I would rather stand silent than run, which meant I could be cornered.

I told myself I was imagining the weight of it. I told myself a lot of things that day.

At dusk I went to the shop. Silas was propped in his chair with a blanket over his knees even though the evening wasn't cold, copying the last lines of a manumission for a woman named Ferren whose contract had expired four years ago and who was, at that moment, diving for pearls two hundred miles up the coast believing she owed Voss another decade. His hand shook between words now. It didn't shake during them. I had started to understand that distinction — how the disease waited politely at the edges of each stroke, letting him finish, taking its toll in the spaces.

"Your ink is low," I said, because we spoke to each other mostly in inventory. It was safer than the other language growing between us, the one about fathers and ledgers and letters signed by dead men.

"Everything is low," Silas said. "Ink. Breath. Time. You'll want to get used to rationing."

I trimmed the lamp. I ground more ink the way he'd taught me, slow circles, the consistency of blood when it pools. And I told him about the cooperage, because leaving it out felt like a kind of theft, and because some part of me — the part I liked least — wanted him to tell me there was nothing to fear.

Silas listened without looking up. When I finished he dipped his pen and wrote one more line before he spoke, giving himself time, or giving me time, I couldn't tell.

"Brandt doesn't corner men he intends to arrest," he said finally. "Arrest is cheap. He corners men he intends to buy."

"I'm not worth buying."

"Everyone is worth buying. The price is just different." He looked at me then, and his eyes had the wet brightness they got in the evenings, fever sitting just behind them. "The question isn't whether he'll make you an offer. The question is what shape your *no* takes when you hear it."

I said I would say no. I said it easily, the way you promise yourself you'll rise with the tide tomorrow.

Silas went back to his letter. After a while he said, quietly, not to me exactly: "My father sold palm oil to a company like this one. Men came to him too, near the end. He always told me the offer comes dressed as the sensible thing. That's how you know it's the wrong thing. If it were wrong and looked wrong, only fools would take it, and fools can be handled. No — it will look like prudence. It will look like keeping your mother fed and your name clean and the tide caught. Weariness will do most of its arguing for it." He sanded the page. "Prudence is the best disguise betrayal ever wore."

I carried that home through the dark streets and did not sleep.

* * *

Brandt came again on the fourth day, and this time he chose the place, and the place told me everything.

Not the shop. Not the compound yard where witnesses stood. He found me on the seawall past the drying racks, where I went sometimes to sit with my legs hanging over the drop and watch the ship at anchor — *the* ship, our ship, riding at her moorings beyond the bar with her boatswain's call carrying faintly across the water whenever the wind turned right. She'd been waiting eleven days. She would wait until the tide and the weather agreed, and not one hour longer.

He sat down beside me. Actually sat, coat and all, on the wet stone, like a man settling in for a conversation between equals. Below us the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored, and the demolition notices on the waterfront shops fluttered faintly in their frames, white rectangles that had already begun to look native, part of the architecture, the way gallows do.

"You come here to count the days," he said.

"I come here to smoke," I lied, having no pipe.

"Mm." He produced, of all things, two boiled sweets wrapped in paper, and offered me one. I took it. I have thought since about why I took it, and I think it's because refusing felt like declaring something, and I still believed — God help me, I still believed that morning — that I hadn't declared anything yet. "Eleven days the ship's been at anchor. Nine days the bridge has been shut. Do you know what that makes you, Quintana?"

"A man waiting for a boat."

"It makes you free." He unwrapped his own sweet and rolled it along his jaw. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Right now, today, you are the only man in this town with no contract binding him past the tide. Your term ends when you board. The others — the ones Okafor writes his little letters for — they're held. Paper holds them. But you? Board that ship and walk off it a free hand, wages owed, record clean. Twenty-two years old and unencumbered." He let that sit. Out past the bar, the ship's bell rang the half-hour, thin across the water. "That's rare. That's valuable. And value is wasted on men who spend it badly."

"I haven't spent anything," I said. "I carry ink."

"There it is again." He laughed softly, genuinely, and somehow that was worse than the smile. "'I carry ink.' You know what I did before this posting? Twelve years in the debt offices at Port Meridian. You learn to read a man's exposure. Yours is—" he ticked it off on gloved fingers, unhurried, a clerk closing an account— "an aging forger with no family of record, no protectors, a shop already condemned, dying of something the port physician could name from across a room. And you. An indentured diver's son with a talent for imitation that at least four people in this town have now noticed. That's the whole ledger. Two entries. One of them is about to resolve itself naturally."

"And the other?"

"The other is you." He turned and looked at me straight on, and I saw that the patience was real, that it wasn't a technique, that he had all the time in the world because the world was arranged for him. "Here is the offer, and I'll say it once, plainly, so you can weigh it honestly. You board the morning ship. Before boarding, you bring me the true ledger — the copy Okafor keeps behind his third floorboard — and the names of every hand he's written free papers for. In exchange: your contract closes clean, no questions attached to your file. A bonus of forty crowns, paid at the Purser's office on the far side. And a letter of standing under the company seal, which on the mainland opens doors that men like you otherwise die behind."

Forty crowns. My mother's whole debt was thirty-one.

I didn't say that. But he watched my face while the number landed, and I knew he knew. Of course he knew. Somewhere in the harbor office there was a page with my household on it, debts and all, and a man like Brandt had read it the way Silas read handwriting — completely, at a glance.

"And if I say nothing at all?" I asked. "If I just board?"

"Then you board, and the file closes, and none of this touches you. That option remains open. I want to be clear about that." He stood, brushed off his coat, and looked out at the ship with something almost like sympathy. "I'm not threatening you, Quintana. Threats are for men who've chosen. You haven't chosen anything yet. All I've done is price two futures." He put the sweet paper in his pocket instead of dropping it — tidy to the last. "One future is worth forty crowns and a clean name. The other is worth an old man's gratitude and a burned building. I've priced a great many men. Almost all of them, given quiet and time, choose correctly."

He walked back along the seawall toward the harbor office without hurrying. The demolition notices fluttered. The bell rang the three-quarter.

* * *

Here is the thing I could not explain to Silas, and cannot fully explain now: the offer was good.

Not the money — the money was bait and I knew it. Good, the way a current is good, the way a following wind is good. Everything in me that had spent twenty-two years learning to survive by being small and useful heard *forty crowns and a clean name* and sat up like a dog hearing its name called. Board the ship. Close the account. Carry nothing heavier than a bag. My whole life had trained me for exactly this choice, had drilled it into my hands: when the orders come, pack light, keep your head down, be aboard before the trouble starts. Brandt hadn't invented the temptation. He'd only named it. It had lived in me since I was six years old, watching my father's kit laid out on the table — comb, knife, papers, everything portable, everything ready — and learning that readiness to leave was the same thing as safety.

And against it: an oath sworn half-willingly to a dying man, eleven unfinished names, a woman I'd known a handful of weeks, a ledger behind a floorboard, a shop with weeks to live.

Weighed honestly, as he'd asked — weighed the way I'd weighed everything since childhood, by what can be carried — there was no contest. Gratitude doesn't float. Love weighs nothing you can stack against crowns. The sensible thing stood there shining, and the other thing stood there in the rain getting wet.

I told myself it was prudence. I remember using the word. I sat on the seawall until full dark rehearsing it: *prudence*, *keeping my mother fed*, *a man can't fight a company with a pen*, *he's dying anyway, the letters can burn with the shop, it changes nothing*. Each argument was true the way a single plank is true — sound in itself, and together forming a bridge over exactly the distance I needed to cross without looking down.

Then I went and did the thing that settled it, though I told myself it was only tidying.

I went to the shop, while Silas slept in his chair, and I lifted the third floorboard, and I took the true ledger out and held it. It was heavier than I expected. Not physically — it was an ordinary book, salt-swollen, its spine stitched twice — but holding it was like holding a live thing, all those names breathing in my hands, Ferren, Okon, Adaeze, my father's name in the margin where Silas had noted the drowning in a hand so small it was nearly a whisper. Eleven letters unfinished beside it in the drawer, each one a person still underwater, still paying interest on a debt that had ended years ago.

I stood there in the lamplight with the ledger in my arms, and I gave myself one honest minute. I let myself feel the whole weight of both futures at once, no arguments, just the two shapes of my life standing side by side. Forty crowns. Or this.

And then — this is the part I am ashamed of, more ashamed than the yes itself — I put it back. Floorboard down. Ledger hidden. Drawer shut. I banked the lamp, tucked the blanket higher on Silas's shoulders, and walked out into the street with my heart going hard and my face arranged into the small, useful blankness I'd worn all my life, and I went to find Brandt.

Because that was the trick of it, the thing Silas had tried to warn me about. The offer doesn't wait at the door wearing horns. It waits inside you, dressed as you, speaking in your voice, saying *this is the smart thing, this is what a survivor does,* and by the time you're walking toward it your feet are already convinced and your mouth just comes along to say yes.

I found him where I knew he'd be, in the harbor office window with its view of the anchorage. He saw me coming. He didn't smile. He simply opened the door, and that frightened me more than any threat would have — how sure he was that the door was the right thing to open.

"Well?" he said.

"The floorboard," I said. My voice came out level. I'd give myself that much. "Third one from the wall, under the press. The ledger's there tonight, and I'll show you the drawer with the unfinished letters. Names and delivery marks included."

He studied me for a long moment, checking the work, the way you check a forged signature — not for flaws, but for hesitation.

"And the ship?" he asked. "You'll board."

"I'll board," I said. "Clean contract. Forty crowns. The letter of standing."

"Done, then." He extended his hand, and I took it, and his grip was dry and cool and brief, a transaction closing. "You've made the correct choice, Quintana. Most men need more time to arrive at sense. You may be smarter than you look."

I walked home through streets gone blue with the hour before midnight, past the shuttered quay, past the notices fluttering on the condemned shops, past the dark window of a certain cramped storehouse where a lamp still burned low behind the glass because an old man slept in his chair and feared the dark. I did not look at that window directly. I looked at it the way you look at a debt — sideways, in installments.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I would bring Brandt to the floorboard. Tonight, there was one more thing to do first, one small thing, a private thing, nothing that changed anything: I turned toward my mother's house at the edge of the divers' rows, where a light was also burning, and where I suddenly, urgently needed to see her face.

She opened the door before I knocked. Mothers do. She looked at me — at the arrangement on my face, the blank useful mask worn thin — and stepped aside to let me in, and said, quietly:

"Whatever you've done, you'd best sit down and tell me while it's still small enough to carry."
