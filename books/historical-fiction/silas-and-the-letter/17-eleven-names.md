## 17. Eleven Names

Marguerite's POV

The list was eleven names long and it took Silas's whole remaining strength to write it, so Marguerite copied it twice — once in Silas's hand for the record they would burn, once in his own for the pocket over his heart.

He read the names aloud to fix them in him like a diver fixing depth marks on a line. Okon Adeyemi. Tomas Reyes. Ilsa Brandt-Voss — no relation, or a relation nobody claimed. Nine more after that. Each one had come into the shop over the years, or sent a scrap of cloth, a token, a whispered word through a third party: *my term is served, my paper says otherwise.* Each one had left a mark in the true ledger, the copy Silas had kept against the company's false one, and each mark was a small act of faith that someday a hand would carry it somewhere it could not be burned.

"Straight letters first," Silas rasped from his cot behind the counter, propped up on pillows gone gray with use. "Manumissions. Then the ledger pages. Then Adelise's pass. In that order, because if you run out of night, the manumissions matter more than anything."

"I won't run out of night."

"Everyone runs out of night." He coughed, wet and deep. "That's the whole lesson, boy. Everything else is penmanship."

Marguerite set the first sheet of paper on the blotter — good paper, smuggled from Adelise in three folds inside his boot, and he did not let himself think about her yet, because thinking about her made his hand unsteady and there was no time for an unsteady hand. He dipped the pen. He breathed the way Silas had taught him, slow out, longer out, until the breath ran out of him entirely and only the letter remained.

*Know all men by these presents.*

Okon's manumission went down line by line in a dead man's living hand. Marguerite had done this four times now — the practice sheets, then the letter he'd finished alone when Silas collapsed — and the strangeness hadn't dulled. His fingers knew Silas's cramped rightward lean better than they knew their own upright script. The borrowed hand came faster every hour, and every time it did, some quiet part of him recoiled, as if he were stealing something from a man not yet cold.

"You're slowing," Silas said. "Why are you slowing?"

"Because it isn't mine."

"It doesn't need to be yours. A boat doesn't need to own the sea to carry cargo across it." Silas pushed himself higher on the pillows. "It needs to stay afloat and know its heading. Write."

Marguerite wrote. Outside, the wind off the harbor carried the smell of the storm-damaged bridge — tar, splintered timber, salt working into wounds — and under it, fainter, the sound of the town that had watched the shop burn without saying a word. Half of Voss Landing had stood in the street two nights ago with lamps in their hands and their mouths shut while Brandt's men fed the workshop to the fire. Their silence had been a kind of shout. He was learning to hear it.

The second name. The third. Wax melted; he sealed each letter with the plain stamp Silas had pressed into his palm an hour ago, warm from the old man's grip. *They travel by human hand,* Silas had said. *Not post. Post can be bought. A hand has to be looked in the eye.*

By the fourth letter his shoulders burned. By the sixth, Adelise slipped in through the back door with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and everything in him that had been bent over the desk turned toward her like iron to a lodestone.

She put a finger to her lips. Set the lantern down. Came around behind him and stood reading the page upside down, close enough that he could feel her breath move the fine hairs at his ear.

"Fifth one's crooked," she murmured. "The descender on the *y*."

He looked. It was. In Silas's hand, a crooked descender was almost worse than a wrong name — Silas's writing never wavered even when the man himself could barely lift the pen.

"Burn it and do it again," she said.

"There's no time."

"Then make time. You have six hours and eleven names and a harbor full of people who'll hang us both if a seal looks wrong." She pulled the second stool beside him and sat, dragging her satchel onto her knees. "So we don't waste any being proud of near-enough. Again."

He burned the fifth letter over the ink-stone flame and started again, and this time she stayed beside him, and the work went faster anyway, which he decided not to examine too closely.

Adelise's POV

Her father believed she was asleep, which required believing she had been home since supper, which required a whole architecture of assumptions Adelise had spent nineteen years building and reinforcing like a seawall. Tonight the seawall held. She had left her window latched but unlocked, her boots oiled so they made no sound on the quay stones, her absence covered by a shape of blankets arranged to suggest a sleeping girl with a cold. Her father had checked on her at nine and touched her forehead and said nothing, because her father expressed love the way the tide expressed itself: regularly, powerfully, and without a single word you could argue with.

Now she sat in a burned shop's surviving half with a stolen harbor pass under her hands and a boy who wrote other people's names breathing beside her, and she had never in her life felt more awake.

The pass was the hard part. Manumission letters were just paper with authority behind them — the authority was real, buried in ledgers and law, and would outlive them both. But a harbor pass was a living document, checked tonight by men her father paid, signed in a hand her father knew like his own signature. She had studied that hand her entire childhood: on chits, on mooring orders, on the discipline notices that ended boys' contracts and sometimes, quietly, ended boys.

She practiced on scrap. Ten times. Twenty. The capital *A* of Augustin Voss-Harbor-master, the way he bore down on the final stroke like he was driving a piling. By the thirtieth attempt she could produce it drunk, blind, or furious, which were the three conditions under which her father ever signed anything urgent.

"You're better at this than I am," Marguerite said quietly, sealing the seventh manumission.

"I should be. I've had more practice watching him than he knows." She blew on the pass, folded it, unfolded it, checked the fold creases against the genuine article she'd lifted from the office waste-basket — her father discarded drafts carelessly, another thing he didn't know she knew. "Boarding starts at slack water, before dawn. The gangplank goes down at first bell. For an hour after that, the quay is chaos — families, cargo, inspectors counting heads twice because they always miscount once. That's our hour."

"And the families take the letters how?"

"We've been through this."

"We'll go through it again. If we go through it enough times, we'll find the hole in it before Brandt does."

She didn't say what she thought, which was that Brandt had already found the hole, that the hole was named Marguerite Quintana, that the inspector had bought him with a clean contract and would come collecting. She'd seen them talking behind the net sheds. She'd seen Marguerite's face afterward, and she'd seen it change over the last day — shame burning down to something harder — and she had chosen, deliberately, not to ask. There would be time for accounts later. Tonight there was only the work.

"The divers' families board separately," she said instead. "Starboard side, away from the company clerks. Wives, mothers, children going to the mainland for the season. They're searched lightly because they're poor and the company finds poverty honest." The bitterness in her own voice surprised her. "Each family gets one bundle of personal goods, wrapped, tagged with their own name. No clerk checks a tag against a woman's memory of her own husband's business."

"So the letters travel inside bundles tagged with the freed men's names."

"Tagged in your hand. Silas's hand. Whichever." She laid the finished pass flat between them like a card played. "And this gets me onto the quay past the gate watch, so I'm the one distributing. You—"

"I deliver the ledger copy."

"To the delegate. The mainland trade commissioner's man, Ferran. He boards at second bell, he owes Silas a debt from before either of us was born, and he's the one soul on that ship whose word carries weight when the bridge opens." She recited it and heard how it sounded: rehearsed, certain, final. As if she had already agreed to be on the quay and not on the ship. As if the plan had room in it for her escape chit and no room at all for using it.

Marguerite set down his pen. In the shuttered lamplight his face was all planes and shadows, and he looked at her the way he looked at a page he was copying — total attention, total care, as if getting her wrong were the one unforgivable error.

"Your chit is written," he said. "It's in my coat. It's been ready for two days."

"I know."

"You could be on that ship. Clean papers, no name in any ledger, out before anyone knows the girl existed."

"I know," she said again, and found she was angry, and examined the anger and found underneath it not fear but something worse: the recognition that she had already decided, days ago, and had let him believe otherwise because watching him try to save her was the closest thing to being loved she had ever been allowed to feel. "Ask me properly, then. Ask me why I'm staying. Because you've wanted to ask since the fire, and I'm tired of carrying it for you."

* * *

Marguerite's POV

The lamp guttered. Somewhere behind them Silas slept — or lay silent, which had lately become difficult to distinguish, and Marguerite did not turn around to check because checking would mean knowing and knowing would mean stopping.

"Why are you staying?" he asked.

"Because my father owns the harbor," Adelise said. "Every rope, every gate, every pass. And in nineteen years I have never once put my hand on any of it and made it do something he didn't order. Tonight I hold a forged pass in my own hand and eleven families get their husbands and sons back, and it is the first thing I have ever *done*, Marguerite. Not endured. Done." She said it without drama, flat and precise, which made it worse. "You measure your life by what you can carry. I measure mine by what I've been permitted to touch. It comes to nearly the same arithmetic."

He wanted to tell her to go. The words assembled themselves — *the ship, the chance, the clean break* — and fell apart, because he heard them in Brandt's voice, in the company's voice, in the voice of everyone who had ever explained to him that safety was the same thing as wisdom. He had taken Brandt's offer. That fact sat in him like swallowed ballast. He had said yes, and then he had gone to his mother and come back and unsaid it in every way except aloud, and Adelise knew — she must know — and she had not asked him to explain, and somehow that was the most forgiving thing anyone had ever done to him.

"Then we finish the letters," he said.

"Then we finish the letters."

The eighth name. The ninth. His hand moved through Silas's strokes without hesitation now, and he noticed — with a feeling he couldn't name, between grief and pride — that he was no longer borrowing the hand. He was simply writing. Whatever lived in the pen had passed to him the way a trade passes, not by permission but by necessity, master to apprentice, across the short dark gap where the master used to be.

Toward the smallest hour of the morning, Silas stirred. They both froze. But the old man only turned his face toward the light and said, without opening his eyes:

"How many?"

"Nine sealed," Marguerite said. "Two to go. Then the ledger pages."

"Good." A long breath. "The tenth is Ilsa Brandt-Voss. Careful with that one. Her manumission names her kin. When Brandt reads his own family name on a freedwoman's paper, he'll understand exactly what kind of war this is." Something that might have been a smile moved under the gray skin. "Make sure he reads it."

Adelise caught Marguerite's eye across the lamp. Neither of them smiled. Both understood.

Adelise's POV

They finished as the sky over the harbor went from black to the color of wet slate. Eleven letters, waxed and sealed. The true ledger's key pages, copied fair and folded flat. The harbor pass, perfect, bearing a signature her father would swear to on any Bible on the coast.

Adelise packed them into oilcloth, into bundles sized for a woman's personal effects, and tagged each one in a neat commercial hand — not Silas's, not Marguerite's, a third hand entirely, hers, invented that night for the purpose. Eleven tags. Eleven names. Some of those names belonged to women who would unwrap their bundles mid-channel and find their lives returned to them, and would not know whose hands had done it, and that was right. That was how Silas had worked for thirty years. Invisible ink, invisible hands, visible freedom.

She stood at the broken front window of the burned shop and looked out at the harbor. The ship rode at anchor beyond the mole, riding lights doubled in the black water, patient as a debt. On the quay below, early figures were already moving — stevedores, a clerk with a lantern, the first families arriving with their bundles and their held breath.

"Slack water in ninety minutes," she said. "First bell in two hours."

Behind her, Marguerite gathered his bag — the bag he'd packed the day the orders came, everything he could carry, packed twice over and unpacked twice over since. She watched him look at it. She watched him leave it by the door rather than sling it on, and understood that the bag had become a question he was still answering.

"When it's done," she said, "when the delegate has the ledger and the last bundle is aboard — meet me at the net sheds. Win or lose, we meet at the net sheds, and we decide the rest together. Promise me that. Not the oath Silas made you swear. This one. Ours."

He crossed to her. For a moment they stood in the wrecked doorway with the harbor wind pushing at them, and he took her hand — the first time he had initiated it, she realized, in all their nights — and pressed the folded harbor pass into her palm and closed her fingers over it.

"I promise," he said. "But listen to me. Once you step onto that quay with those bundles, there's no version of this where your father doesn't eventually learn. And when he learns—"

"He'll rage. He'll disown me or he'll lock me up or he'll marry me off to some inspector's son as punishment." She shrugged, and was startled to find the shrug was real, light, almost easy. "I've priced all of it, Marguerite. I told you. We recognize each other's cages. Mine just has a better view."

Down on the quay, a bell rang — not first bell, not yet, but the harbor clock marking the hour, the same bell that had measured her father's whole life and Silas's and maybe soon hers. The ship at anchor swung slightly on the tide, turning its bow toward open water, toward the morning, toward everything the company believed it owned.

Adelise pulled her hood up, checked the pass one last time, and stepped out into the street.

"First bell," she said over her shoulder. "Don't be late for the end of the world."

And behind her, in the ruined shop, the last letter-writer of Voss Landing began, very quietly, to cough blood.
