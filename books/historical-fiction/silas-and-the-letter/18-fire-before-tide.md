## 18. Fire Before Tide

I smelled the pitch before I understood it.

That was my first mistake — thinking in terms of things instead of intentions. Pitch has a smell like a promise kept badly: sweet, black, final. It came through the shutters of Silas's shop at an hour when the quay should have smelled only of tar and fish and the green rot of rope, and for a full ten seconds I stood at the worktable with a pen still wet in my hand and thought: someone is caulking a boat.

Then I remembered that no one caulked anything after dark in Voss Landing. The company forbade open flame near the waterfront after the fire of '91, and the rule had never once been broken, because rules in this place were not suggestions wearing uniforms; they were bones.

The second smell arrived under the first. Kerosene.

I put down the pen.

* * *

They came at the change of watch, which was the smart hour and therefore not Brandt's idea alone. He would have been told to do it then by someone who understood tides and men — do it when the harbor guard changes, when the taverns are half-full and half-blind, when anyone walking abroad can be assumed drunk or mad. Three men crossed the yard toward the shop with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and behind them, at the corner where the company land ended and Silas's lease began, stood two more with staves and the particular stillness of men paid not to see what they were about to see.

Brandt himself stayed back by the warehouse wall. I knew his silhouette the way you know a hook in a fish's mouth — by the shape of the hurt it makes. He had not come to watch the burning. He had come to watch me.

Because he knew. That was the thing I understood too late and all at once, the way ice understands water. The offer he'd made me on the warehouse steps — betray the old man, board clean, take the bonus — had not been a trap I could walk around. It had been a test whose answer he already carried sealed in his coat. When I said yes, I had told him everything he needed: that Silas had told me about the letters, that I knew where they slept at night, that the old man trusted me enough to die in front of me. My yes had been a map drawn on his palm.

And then I hadn't boarded. Hadn't reported the letters' hiding place. Hadn't come back to him with the true ledger folded inside my shirt like a stolen heart. So he had stopped waiting.

Eleven letters. That's what there were now — eleven names left of the list Silas had made over twenty years, the divers held past their terms because their debts grew faster than their wages, each one owed a paper that would say, in language no one could argue with, *this man is free.* Okon's was finished. Six others sat in the drawer under the false bottom, inked in hands that belonged to dead clerks and living liars. Four waited for tomorrow's light and whatever strength I had.

The true ledger lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboards where Silas's bed had been. Twenty years of the other arithmetic: what the company actually charged, what the men actually earned, the small elegant theft repeated ten thousand times until it looked like weather.

Everything else — the ledgers of forged hands going back decades, the practice sheets, the pens worn to nubs, Silas's own papers, his letters from home in a language I couldn't read, the chair with the shape of him pressed into its cushion — everything else was kindling now, and had been from the moment Brandt decided I wouldn't sell.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I will set it down plainly because plainness is the only honesty I have left.

I did not think. Thinking is what I had done for nineteen years — weighing, measuring, folding myself smaller — and it had bought me a bag packed for a ship and a yes spoken to a man who owned my name. What I did instead was move.

Through the back door into the yard, where the night was thick as wet wool. The three with the lantern were at the front, working methodically — pitch on the doorframe first, so the frame itself would feed. I heard the brush slap against wood. A man laughed softly, the laugh of work going well.

Under the floorboard. The oilcloth bundle was cold and heavier than any book has a right to be, the way truth is always heavier than the lie it corrects. I took it against my chest.

Then the letters. The false bottom stuck — swollen with salt air, it always stuck, Silas had meant to plane it in the spring, and there is no spring now, old man, there is no spring for either of us — and I heard myself make a sound low in my throat like an animal caught, and I wrenched it, and it gave, and the six letters came up into my hands along with a shower of pen nibs that scattered across the boards with a sound like rain on tin.

One of the men at the front said, "You hear that?"

The world went very narrow. I crouched in the dark of a dying man's shop with a ledger against my ribs and six free men in my fists, and I listened to my own blood.

"Wind," said another voice. "Get the back wall. He wants it gone to the studs."

He. Not Brandt — Brandt didn't give orders at fires, he attended them. Someone above Brandt. Someone who had read the leaked pages and understood that a ledger is not dangerous until people can stand where it was written and point at the ashes and say *here, here is where they lied to us.* Burn the evidence and burn the place it lived, and when the bridge reopens in spring, the delegates will find a clean lot and a company shrugging.

I got the four unfinished letters from the drawer by touch — Silas organized even his incompletions, each one clipped to its man's debt record, each one labeled in his cramped, patient hand — and I took the nibs too, without deciding to, a fistful of them rattling in my pocket like seeds.

At the back door I stopped one last time. The room was nearly black. I let myself look at it — the table where I had learned that a name is a thing made by hands, the floor worn pale in the path between desk and shelf, the hook where Silas hung his hat every evening for thirty years as if the hanging mattered, as if some day someone would notice the ritual and understand that this shop had been a country with laws of its own, kinder ones.

Then the kerosene hit the back wall, thrown from outside, and the cold splash of it came through the gap in the boards and struck my shoulder, and I went out into the yard with my heart going like a diver's hammer and the whole inheritance of a dead letter-writer clutched against my chest.

* * *

They lit it at the front, which told me they were amateurs at arson and professionals at obedience. Pitch takes slowly from the top; it should be laid low. The flame walked up the doorframe with a soft greedy whisper, and the men stepped back into the yard to admire it, and that was when I saw the town.

They were in the street. All along the lane, at their doors, at their windows, at the corners — the caulker's widow with her shawl over her head, the netmenders off the late shift, the chandlery boys, old Tomas who mended the company boats and hated them privately the way a dog hates a chain it cannot name. Half the waterfront, standing in the dark, watching a shop burn.

Nobody shouted. Nobody ran for buckets. The company had long ago drained the public wells near the waterfront — a safety measure, posted on a board nobody had ever been allowed to question — and everyone standing there knew it, and knew that the absence of water was not an accident of geography but an argument, and that tonight the argument was being settled.

A woman near the chandlery began, very quietly, to sing. It was a diving song, the low one they sing to steady a man before he goes under, the one about holding your breath and coming up changed. Two voices joined her. Then none — she let it go, or was elbowed quiet, I couldn't see. But the silence afterward was not the same silence as before. Before, it had been fear. After, it was agreement.

The men with the lantern felt it too. I watched them glance down the street at all those still faces, and shift their staves, and discover that there is no stance that guards you from being seen. One of them said something to the other — too low to carry — and the other shook his head slightly, the way you shake your head when a job has turned into something you'll be asked about later, in better times, by people with memories.

I was forty feet away in the shadow of the cooperage, soaked in kerosene from shoulder to wrist, holding a nation of paper, and not one face turned toward me. Not because they hadn't marked where I'd come from. Because they chose not to see, and their not-seeing was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

The town had chosen its side. It simply could not yet afford to say so out loud. So it said nothing, together, in unison, a whole street of nothing, and the nothing wrapped around me like a coat.

* * *

The fire found its appetite and stopped being polite.

Heat rolled across the yard in waves you could lean against. The pitch caught properly and the whole front of the shop went up in a single roar, and sparks lifted off the roof and turned over in the wind like a school of bright fish, and I thought — absurdly, unforgivably — of the shell comb in my mother's house, how fire is the one thing pearls and paper agree on.

Silas's chair went. I heard the window glass go, a long crystalline sigh, and then the shelves, and the heat pushed me back step by step until my shoulders met the cooperage wall, and I stood there and let it push, because a man should feel what it costs.

Twenty years of forged hands. Every signature he'd ever borrowed, every clerk he'd imitated, every practice sheet covered in alphabets that belonged to no one — ash. And I found that I did not grieve them. They had been scaffolding. The building they'd raised was folded in oilcloth against my chest, and scaffolding is meant to burn.

What I grieved was smaller and worse: a tin cup with the glaze cracked, that he drank his chicory from every morning. A blanket with a burn hole from a lamp accident years ago, that he refused to replace. The pencil stubs in a jar. The ordinary archaeology of a life. Fires don't take arguments, I thought. Arguments survive in any mouth. Fires take cups.

The harbor bell began to ring — the alarm, slow and formal, though everyone within sight of the flames already knew what was burning, and everyone beyond sight would learn by morning. From the direction of the company compound came running boots and lantern-light, the fire crew coming at their regulation pace, arriving in time to keep the flames from spreading to company property and not one minute sooner. They'd known the schedule. The whole thing had a timetable, and the timetable had a signature, and someday I intended to copy that signature too.

Brandt was gone from the warehouse wall. But as the crew strung their line and the crowd's faces went orange, I saw him at the far end of the lane, standing apart, and he was not watching the fire.

He was watching the street.

I understood then what he saw and why his stillness had a crack in it. He had ordered a shop burned and expected a lesson, and instead he was looking at forty silent people who had not screamed, not wept, not begged — who had simply stood in the dark and witnessed, which is the one act a company cannot post a rule against. You can forbid water. You cannot forbid eyes. He was learning it in real time, the inspector with his clean contract in his pocket: that the fire was supposed to end a story and had instead become one, and stories travel, and the bridge opens in spring.

He turned and saw me — I'm sure of it, across all that distance and light, the way creditors see debtors. He didn't move. Neither did I. Between us lay a burning building and a streetful of witnesses, and neither of us could afford the first step.

* * *

I went to Adelise.

Not to the harbor, not to the quay — those would be watched by morning, watched tonight probably, because Brandt would now know exactly what I carried and exactly where I had to deliver it: aboard the ship, into departing hands, before the tide. He would salt every gangplank in the harbor with his men. The letters could not go by the obvious road anymore than the truth could have gone by the company's ledgers.

Her father's house sat above the harbor steps, lamplit, respectable, furious — I could feel the fury through the walls, the harbormaster's rage at a daughter who'd been seen too often in wrong places. But there was a light in the boathouse below, the little one, the mending light.

I knocked the diver's knock — twice, pause, once — the knock my father used on my mother's door, the knock Adelise and I had made ours without ever saying we had.

She opened it with a boat knife already in her hand, and lowered it, and looked at the oilcloth bundle and the char-smell on me and the kerosene stiffening my sleeve, and she said, "How much time?"

"Tide's at dawn."

"And Brandt?"

"Watching every plank."

She nodded, already moving, pulling canvas off the mending table, clearing space, and when she spoke again her voice had the flat calm I'd heard her use on her father, the voice of a person who has decided.

"Then we don't use planks. The fishing families load their own dories before first light — they always row out to the ship rather than wait for the tender. Eleven letters, eleven families." She looked up at me, and the mending-lamp made her eyes the color of the deep water where the pearls are. "Marguerite. Tell me you saved all eleven."

I unwrapped the bundle on her table: the true ledger, black and dense as a seed. Six finished letters. Four clipped to their debts, waiting for a hand.

"Four aren't written," I said. "And my hand—" I stopped. All night the words had been queuing behind the fear, and now the first one out was a confession. "My hand is good enough to fool a clerk. I don't know if it's good enough to carry a man's freedom."

Adelise picked up one of the unfinished letters, read the name at its head — Ede Okafor, no relation to Silas, just a diver who'd been owned nine years too long — and set it down in front of me, square, facing me the way you face a man at a bench.

"Silas taught you," she said. "So write like the fire taught you. There isn't another kind of time."

Outside, the glow of the shop lit the underside of the clouds red, and the tide clock in her father's boathouse ticked toward dawn, and I sat down at the mending table, dipped the first new nib, and began.
