## 19. The Morning Tide

The bag was packed by lamplight, and it was small.

That had always been the point of a life, Marguerite had believed — that it should fold down to something a man could carry at a run. The shell comb wrapped in cloth. The diver's knife his father's hands had worn smooth. His indenture papers, folded soft as cloth from years in his breast pocket, the Voss seal pressed into them like a thumbprint on wet clay. A spare shirt. Half a loaf. The ink-stained rag he could not explain to himself and would not leave behind.

He set the bag on the cot and looked at it the way a man looks at a sum he has checked three times and still doesn't trust.

Everything I can carry.

Silas's voice came back to him across two dead days — *a borrowed hand can still carry true weight* — and beneath it, quieter, the voice Silas had used only once, near the end: *What you carry isn't the same as what owns you.*

Outside, the town was already moving toward the water. He could hear it through the shutter slats: cart wheels on the cobbles, the low herd-sound of families walking with everything they owned in baskets, gulls screaming over the quay like creditors. The *Meridian* had come up on the night tide and sat at anchor in the roadstead, black-hulled, her lamps strung along the rail like a string of patient teeth. She would take on the season's divers at first light and be gone before the sun cleared the headland.

Brandt's men had posted the boarding order on the company house door before dawn. ALL INDENTURED HANDS TO THE QUAY BY SIX BELLS. CONTRACTS VOIDED FOR ABSENCE. The word *voided* had been underlined twice, in the tidy red hand of a man who enjoyed his work.

Marguerite buttoned his coat over the papers. In the inner pocket, against his ribs, lay eleven letters.

* *

The quay at six bells was a country unto itself.

Two hundred souls crowded between the warehouse walls and the water — divers and their wives, their mothers, their children too young to remember any other shore. Company clerks moved along the line with ledgers chained open, checking names off against debts, and behind each clerk stood one of Brandt's men with a stave held not quite threateningly, which was its own kind of threat. The gangplank ran down from the *Meridian*'s waist, and at its foot a mate called names in a bored bellow, each name a hook drawing one more body out of the crowd and onto the plank.

Marguerite stood at the edge of it all with his bag at his feet, doing what he had done his whole life: making himself furniture. He watched the line move. He counted. It was easier than thinking.

Forty aboard. Sixty. A woman with twin boys gripping her skirt wept without noise, the way people weep who have learned that noise costs extra.

Then he saw Adelise.

She came along the warehouse wall in her father's gray coat, hood up, carrying a market basket on her arm like any harbor girl out early for fish. But she moved the way she always moved when she was working — unhurried, unremarkable, her eyes doing all the hurrying there was. She stopped among a knot of waiting families, spoke briefly to a grandmother in a diver's widow's weeds, and passed something small from the basket into the old woman's hand.

The old woman read it. Marguerite saw her whole body change — saw the reading travel through her like current through water. She pressed the paper to her mouth. She looked around wildly, found no one to show, and folded it away inside her bodice, next to her heart if such things went there.

Adelise had already turned to the next family.

Marguerite picked up his bag and walked toward her, and every step felt like stepping off a ledge in the dark, testing for floor that might not be there.

* *

She didn't look up when he reached her. "You're late," she said, low, eyes on the basket. "I've placed four."

"Four."

"The Okon letter went to his sister — she's sailing on this tide, thank God, she'll be in the capital by week's end. The Adeyemi letter to his mother." Her hand moved in the basket, sorting blind. "There are seven left. Seven families still in this crowd."

"Adelise." He kept his voice level, a man discussing weather. "If Brandt's men find those—"

"They won't find them because they aren't looking for them. They're looking for a ledger and an old man's papers, and they're looking in ash." She glanced at him then, just once, and he saw the sleeplessness in her face and the iron under it. "Nobody watches women carrying baskets on boarding day. My father taught me that without meaning to."

A clerk drifted near; they fell silent until he passed, his pen scratching, scratching, each stroke shortening someone's life.

"You're boarding," Adelise said. It wasn't a question. Her eyes went to the bag. "You packed."

"I packed." The words came out of him like stones from a dredge. "It's six bells. The order says—"

"I can read the order."

"Can you?" Something in him flared, sudden and hot, and he let it, because letting it was easier than looking at what was underneath. "You stand here handing out letters that will hang every one of these families if they're caught reading them wrong, and you ask me about my packing? Your father is the harbormaster. When this is done, you'll go home to his table. There'll be shouting, maybe. Maybe worse. And then it will pass, because you're his daughter, and blood forgives in this town faster than ink does." He heard himself and hated it and could not stop. "Me, they'll take off that ship at the other end and ask why my contract shows a bonus paid by Inspector Brandt and no explanation. Me, I'm carrying nothing but what fits in a bag."

"That's not true," Adelise said quietly. "And you know it's not true."

The crowd roared suddenly — a gangplank moment, some reunion or some parting, hard to tell grief from joy at this distance — and in the cover of the noise she stepped closer.

"You're carrying seven letters," she said. "You're carrying a ledger copy. You're carrying your father's name, which they erased, and your own, which nobody has ever seen written down. That's what's in the bag, Marguerite. The shirt and the bread are just ballast."

* *

He walked to the foot of the gangplank because walking anywhere else required an answer he didn't have.

The mate had a list. The clerks had their chains. The morning was gray-gold and cold, and the *Meridian* swung at her anchor with the patience of a thing that has never once cared what a man decides.

"Name," said the mate.

"Quintana. Marguerite Quintana."

The clerk found him in the ledger. Ran his finger down the column of debt. Marguerite knew what the finger was reading — he'd read it himself now, in the true ledger, in the duplicate truth: charges entered thrice, interest compounded on interest, a sum designed never to reach zero, so that no man's term ever ended and every man's term lasted forever. He had spent his childhood believing he owed that number. He had spent his childhood being *that number*, portable, small, carried.

"Step up," the mate said.

Marguerite stepped up.

The plank flexed under his boots. Below him the harbor water moved, green-dark, holding in it the reflection of the ship and the sky and himself, a thin dark figure mid-crossing, suspended between the deck and the town. His father had gone into water heavier than this and not come up. His mother had carried a comb across two oceans and given it away to keep him honest. Silas had died at dawn with ink on his fingers and a stranger's name half-finished on the page, and the last thing he'd asked — not ordered, asked — was that the letters travel by human hand.

*Not post,* he'd said. *Post can be intercepted. Post has no face. Send them by hand, boy. Make someone look in their eyes when they hold it.*

Marguerite stood on the plank, halfway between ship and shore, and understood that he had been standing on this plank his entire life. Every year the orders came. Every year he packed what he could carry and made himself small enough to fit the packing. Every year the ship took the men away and brought some of them back lighter, and the ledger at the quay office grew fat, and he told himself that survival was a kind of contentment, and he had almost — almost — made himself believe it.

The deck was eight steps away. The shore was ten steps back.

Adelise was on the shore.

He turned around.

It should have been harder, he thought later. There should have been thunder in it, some great tearing sound. Instead it was eight steps, taken calmly, past the mate who shouted after him — "Oi! Quintana! You're marked boarding!" — past the clerk whose pen paused above the page, down onto the cobbles where two hundred pairs of eyes pretended not to watch him arrive.

His legs shook only once he was on solid ground. He let them. Nobody was looking. Everybody was looking.

* *

He found Adelise at the warehouse corner, white-faced. "You turned around," she said.

"I turned around."

"Don't make me say I'm glad. Not here." But her hand found his sleeve and gripped it, brief and fierce, and then she was all business again, pressing the basket's remaining weight into his arms. "Seven letters. I've memorized the faces. The woman in the blue shawl — the tall one — that's Ngozi, Okon's wife; she sails today. Give hers first. Then the brothers Daramola, by the water barrel. Then—"

"Ngozi," Marguerite said, and stepped out of the shadow of the wall.

He had forged forty names in Silas's hand. He had written a dead man's living signature. But walking up to a stranger and putting a letter in her hands with his own face attached to it — that was the hardest penmanship he had ever done.

Ngozi looked at him with flat suspicion until she broke the seal. Then her suspicion died. She read it twice, her lips moving, and when she looked up at him her eyes were wet and terrible and grateful. "Who wrote this?"

"A friend," Marguerite said. "His term was served. The company kept him anyway. This says his term was served."

"He's been free four years," Ngozi whispered. "Four years they've held his pay against a debt that grows." She folded the letter into her bodice the way the old woman had, heart-ward. "When I get to the capital, who hears this? Who reads it?"

"When the bridge opens," Marguerite said, "it will already have been heard. Trust the timing. Go."

She went, up the plank, carrying the truest thing on the ship.

The Daramola brothers took theirs in silence, reading together, one letter between two men, and the younger one laughed — actually laughed — a short disbelieving bark, and clapped Marguerite on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, saying nothing, saying everything. A mother with a baby at her hip read hers and began to sing, very softly, a song in a language the clerks wouldn't know, and her voice shook and did not stop.

Five. Four. Three. Each name a small detonation going off quietly in the crowd, invisible to the clerks, invisible to Brandt's stave-carriers, visible only to anyone who knew how to read a human face — and Marguerite thought, with a wild lightness, *they can't stop this, it's already everywhere, it's already in bodices and bundles and memories, it's crossing the water while we stand here.*

The seventh letter was the hardest. An old man, nearly deaf, who squinted at the paper and then at Marguerite and then at the paper again, and finally said in a cracked voice: "This is my son's hand. This is my son's writing." And Marguerite, who had written it in Silas's hand, in the hand of a man the old diver had known for thirty years, could only say, "Yes. He wrote it. Before the end. He wrote it true."

The old man touched the signature with one finger, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. "Bless you, boy," he said. "Whatever you are."

* *

The bells rang seven.

The mate's bellow changed key — LAST CALL, LAST BOARDING, GATES CLOSING — and the crowd compressed toward the plank like water toward a drain. The clerks snapped their ledgers shut. Somewhere behind the warehouse, boots: Brandt's men beginning their sweep of the stragglers.

Marguerite stood at the foot of the plank with his bag in his hand and the empty basket on his arm and the ledger copy — Silas's ten-year labor, the true arithmetic of two hundred stolen lives — wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat, against his heart, next to where his indenture papers used to sit.

He had taken them out an hour ago. All three folds of them, the Voss seal, the number that was supposed to be him. They were in the harbor now. He had dropped them off the quay edge in the gray dark before dawn, and the water had taken them the way it takes everything, without comment.

The mate saw him still standing there. "Quintana! Last call! You're marked!"

"I know," Marguerite said. "Mark me something else."

And he turned from the plank — turned for good this time, the decision made in the body before the mind finished drafting it — and walked not away from the ship but alongside it, along the whole length of the quay, to where a delegate from the capital sat waiting to board with the last passengers: a thin, tired official with a document case, a man whose job was complaints and petitions, whose ears were technically public property.

Marguerite put the oilcloth bundle into the delegate's hands.

"For the bridge," he said, loud enough for the nearest fifty people to hear, loud enough for the clerks to hear, loud enough for whoever needed to hear. "The true accounts of the Voss Trading Company's indentures. Two hundred names, written true. Carry it across when the road opens, and let it be read aloud."

The quay went quiet — that particular charged quiet of a crowd deciding, all at once, whether it saw nothing.

Down the length of the waterfront, a door banged. Brandt came out from behind the warehouse at a walk that wanted to be a run, his red-underlined fury visible even at distance, two men peeling off after him.

Marguerite did not move. Adelise appeared at his shoulder, hood back now, her face bare and unmistakable, daring her father's harbor to look away.

The crowd closed around them — not dramatically, not all at once, just bodies turning, drifting, resettling, a tide of blue shawls and widows' weeds and diving coats, until the two of them stood in the deep middle of two hundred people who had all, in the same silent minute, chosen.

Brandt stopped at the edge of them, stave-men flanking him, and found nothing to seize but air.

On the water, the *Meridian* raised her anchor. The chain came up dripping, link by link, and the great black hull swung slow toward the open sea, taking with it the ship's doctor, the season's cargo, the mate's list, and every version of Marguerite Quintana who had ever boarded anything.

He watched her go with his empty hand open at his side, and Adelise's fingers found it and closed around it, and behind them, faint under the gull-cry and the creak of hawsers, an old woman's song was still going — the song in the language the clerks didn't know, sung now by a second voice, and then a third.
