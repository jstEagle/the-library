## 1. The Weight of Lead

There is a particular way a man walks into his own shop in the morning, and I have spent eleven years learning it.

Master Wendell Winterbourne — that is the name over the door, painted by my father's own hand in letters so confident they seem to dare the weather to argue — enters at seven o'clock precisely. He does not hurry; hurrying is for men with something to hide. He nods to the lamplighter on the corner, who nods back with the flat courtesy Vellham extends to everyone it has decided not to think about. He unlocks the door of Winterbourne & Sons, Type Founders, and he hangs his coat on the peg his father drove into the wall before I was born, and only then, only when the shutters are down and the kettle is on and the day's first deception is fully dressed and fed, does the performance end.

Or rather, it doesn't. That's the trick of it. It never ends. It simply changes costume.

I am Master Wendell now, and I will be Master Wendell until the candle gutters or the Provost's men come through the door, whichever arrives first. My father would be proud of the discipline, if not of the reason for it. He was a man who believed that lead could be trusted when nothing else in this city could — that a piece of type, once cut, would hold its shape forever, would say exactly what you told it to say and never one syllable more. He built this foundry on that faith. He died believing the regime would honor his contracts because they honored his invoices. The confiscation order arrived nine days after the funeral. Bureaucracy moves slowly everywhere except where it means to take things from the dead.

* * *

The morning's work was matrices. There are worse ways to begin a day. I set myself at the cutting bench with the good lamp pulled close and a blank of type-metal softening in the little stove, and I let my hands do what they know how to do while my mind performed its usual morning inventory: flour enough for three days, coal enough for two, the ledger balanced to the farthing because an unbalanced ledger is an invitation, and the back room swept, and the shutters oiled, and nothing — *nothing* — left out that a man with a warrant might read as confession.

The matrix I was cutting was a lowercase g, double-story, for the Aldermoor Press commission. A fine g. A g worth being. You'd be surprised how much of a letter's character lives in that one curve, the way the lower bowl swings out from the upper like a dancer leaning back at the top of a turn. My father taught me to see it when I was six years old, standing me on a stool beside the casting bench. "A letter is a promise," he said. "Cut it true and it'll keep your word for five hundred years." He said it the way other fathers say *God is watching*, which in our house amounted to much the same thing.

My hands, I should say, are a problem.

Not to look at — they're good hands, steady, precise, the hands of a craftsman, and that is the whole point of them. They are a problem in the sense that they are too fine for the story they're required to tell. Master Wendell Winterbourne, sole heir of a founding family, master of his trade, ought to have hands like a dockhand's. Broad palms. Knuckles with history in them. Instead there are these: slender, cool-fingered, with calluses only where the craft puts them — the pad of the right thumb, the side of the first finger where the graver rests. Hands that a careful man might look at twice.

No one in Vellham looks twice. That is not innocence; that is policy. The census asks its questions, the registry keeps its rolls, and every household head in the print district has learned the great civic art of answering without saying anything. Eleven years I have answered without saying anything. I could teach a seminar.

* * *

At half past eight came the knock I had been waiting for, because I wait for it every morning the way a sailor waits for weather: three raps, brisk, official, followed immediately by the door opening anyway, because the man behind the rap considers knocking a formality he performs for his own amusement.

"Morning, Master Winterbourne." Provost's clerk Tam Hobb, round as a cheese wheel, smelling of cheap tobacco and cheaper ambition, letting himself in with the air of a man doing me a tremendous favor merely by breathing my air. "Inventory day."

"It was inventory day last month, Mr. Hobb."

"And here we are again." He set his satchel on my clean bench — on my *clean bench*, the animal — and began unpacking his forms with the ceremony of a priest laying out relics. "New directives. The Registry wants current stock counts, wage rolls, and confirmation of dependents."

"Dependents," I said. "I employ four men. You met them last month."

"Employed men aren't dependents. Dependents are—" he consulted his form with theatrical slowness, though I knew he knew the words by heart, "—persons residing under the household head's authority whose standing may be irregular."

"Whose standing may be irregular," I repeated. "That's a phrase someone was paid to write."

"Somebody's always paid to write everything, Master Winterbourne. That's why your trade exists." He grinned at me, gap-toothed, almost friendly, which was the most dangerous thing about him. Hobb was not clever, but he was a dog that had learned to earn its keep by pointing, and dogs that point get fed regardless of whether the bird is real. "So. Any irregular persons residing?"

Four men sleep above the cooperage next door, in rooms I rent under names that are nearly their own. I sleep above the shop, alone, in the apartment my father kept, in a bed made each morning with hospital corners because a rumpled bed tells stories. "None," I said. "Unless you count the cat, and her standing is impeccable."

He wrote it down. He actually wrote down the cat. Somewhere in a Registry drawer in the Hall of Records there is a sheet of government paper attesting to the moral character of a mouser named Duchess, and somewhere behind that sheet stands a clerk who believes himself the backbone of the state. This is how the regime works, in case you've ever wondered: not with armies but with forms, an ocean of them, each one a little net, and every citizen a fish obliged to swim through all of them daily and pretend to enjoy the exercise.

He counted my type stock, which took him an hour because he counted it wrong twice and I corrected him twice, gently, the way you correct a child handling knives. He admired the new matrices. He asked whether business was good and I said it was adequate, because *good* invites questions about volume and *adequate* closes doors. He drank my coffee. And at the door, pausing with his hand on the latch, he delivered the day's actual message, the one he'd been walking toward since he entered:

"There'll be a loyalty inventory come spring. All the presses. New directive from the Committee of Civic Order." He said it lightly, a man mentioning rain. "Nothing to worry about for an honest house like yours, I'm sure."

"I'm sure," I said, and smiled, and held the smile until his footsteps faded on the cobbles, and then stood quite still in the middle of my shop with my heart going like a compositor's stick.

A loyalty inventory. For an honest house. The words sat together in the sentence the way oil sits on water, each one pretending to touch the others.

* * *

I worked through the morning the way you work when working is both the thing you love and the wall you hide behind. There is no state of mind I know — not prayer, not drink, not even grief — that matches the hours when the cutting goes well and the shop is quiet and the only voice in the world is the small silver whisper of the graver taking metal away. Cut away, cut away; a letter is not made, it is revealed; you remove everything that isn't the letter and what remains is truth in miniature. My father again. He is always saying things in my head. Dead eleven years and still the loudest man in the room.

By noon the g was done and beautiful and I hated to send it to the casting box, the way you hate to see a daughter married off to a perfectly suitable man.

Josiah came up from the casting floor for his bread and cheese, wiping his hands on his apron, and paused at the bench to look at the matrix. Josiah is sixty if he's a day, cast type for forty years, and he is the closest thing I have to a confessor, in that he knows nothing whatsoever and suspects less, and loves me anyway in the wordless way of old workmen for the young person who pays wages and cuts a finer letter than they can.

"That's a lovely g," he said.

"Aldermoor wants twelve point."

"They always want twelve point. Twelve point and cheap." He bit his cheese and studied me sideways, which old men do instead of asking questions. "You look tired, Master Wendell."

"I'm always tired, Josiah."

"No," he said comfortably. "You look *tired*-tired. Different animal." And he went back downstairs, having planted his observation like a seed in my chest where it would grow into something inconvenient, because that is what the people who love us do, generally without meaning to.

*Tired-tired.* Well. Yes. Eleven years of performing a man before breakfast, at market, at the Guild dinners where I sit among the masters with my collar high and my answers short and my whole self folded up small inside a coat of borrowed posture. Eleven years of signing receipts with a signature I practiced until it stopped being forgery and became simply my hand. Eleven years of the ledger, the shutters, the corners of the bed, the walk — the *walk*, God, the walk — shoulders square, stride long, boots placed like a man who owns the ground he covers.

And underneath it all, the fact itself, plain as a scar under a sleeve: the late Elandra Winterbourne, type founder, did not leave behind a son. She left behind a daughter, who buried her father, burned her dresses one cold night in the yard behind the shop, bound herself flat, lowered her voice into its daytime register, and walked out of the ashes wearing her father's name like a coat of mail. Because the Registry confiscates the property of households whose head dies leaving only daughters — *irregular standing*, they'd have called me; a dependent to be reassigned, dispersed, married off to some cousin of the Civic Order or shipped to the mill towns — and because the foundry was not property to me. It was the last living body of my father's faith. Lead can be trusted. Letters keep their promises. Somebody had to stay and keep the promises.

So Master Wendell was born in the smoke, and has not once, in eleven years, been allowed to die.

* * *

Afternoon brought the ordinary traffic: a bookseller wanting ornaments, a lawyer wanting legal type with legal dignity, a boy from the Gazette with a rush order and the Gazette's usual insulting price, which I accepted with the Gazette's usual gracious lie about being happy to oblige. Business is adequate. Adequate pays for coal and flour and the rent on four sleeping men, and puts a modest sum aside each quarter into a strongbox beneath the floor of the casting room, which is either savings or escape money depending on how the spring's loyalty inventories go.

It was near dusk, light going amber through the front windows, when the second knock of the day came — and this one was different, and I knew it was different before I crossed the room, the way you know weather has changed by the smell of it.

Two raps. Then a pause, long enough to be deliberate. Then nothing. Whoever stood outside had knocked, considered the act sufficient, and waited to be admitted like a civilized creature.

I opened the door on a tall, spare man in a gray coat, holding a leather satchel and a slate board such as the Registry issues to its census takers. Fortyish, perhaps. Eyes the color of uninked paper. He looked at me the way I look at a fresh matrix — not with suspicion, exactly, but with the total, unnerving attention of a man whose trade is noticing.

"Master Wendell Winterbourne?" His voice was quiet and level and carried oddly, as though it had been designed for reading aloud.

"The same. If you've come about the inventory, the clerk was here this morning—"

"Census," he said. "Household returns. I'm covering the print district this week." He produced a credential card, and I performed the ritual glance at it — name: S. Ash, office stamp, seal — performing it badly, I'm afraid, because I was busy performing something else entirely, namely a man with nothing in his house worth a second glance. "Ten minutes of your time, Master Winterbourne. Fewer, if your household is simple."

"It's very simple," I said, and stepped aside to let him in, and watched his eyes do a slow circuit of my shop as he entered — the benches, the racks of type, the ledger open on its stand, the coat on its peg — reading the room the way I read a page.

He sat where I indicated, arranged his slate on his knee, and began. Name of household head. Trade. Years resident. Persons employed. And then, pen poised, in the same mild voice with which he had asked my father's death date:

"Does the household contain any dependents of irregular standing?"

There it was. Twice in one day. The phrase arriving like a bill collector, patient, certain of eventual payment.

"None," I said. "As I told the Provost's clerk this morning."

"Mm." He did not write. That was the first strange thing. Hobb had written down the cat, and this man, offered the standard answer, wrote nothing at all — only looked at me, with those pale eyes, in a silence that lasted three seconds too long to be bureaucratic and one second too short to be accusation. "Your father ran the foundry before you," he said. "Elandra Winterbourne. There's a commemorative broadsheet in the Registry files, from the Guild, twenty years ago. Fine portrait engraving. He had remarkable hands, according to the caption."

"He did," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"You have his hands," said Silas Ash, census taker, looking directly at mine where they rested on the bench — at the slender, cool, fine-cutting, entirely too-eloquent hands of Master Wendell Winterbourne — and then he looked up at my face, and inclined his head slightly, as a man does when concluding a transaction, or acknowledging an opponent across a chessboard. "Remarkable hands," he repeated mildly. "The trade keeps them, I suppose."

"The trade keeps everything," I said. "It's a jealous master."

"So I've heard." He rose, tucked his slate under his arm, gathered his satchel, and moved to the door. On the threshold he paused, half-turned, backlit by the last of the daylight, and said — conversationally, the way a man mentions that it might rain:

"I'll be in the district the rest of the week. The orchard behind the old Corvane foundry, off Tanner's Lane — do you know it? Good light in the evenings, this time of year. I find I do my writing there." A beat. "Some kinds of writing aren't welcome at the Registry."

And then he was gone, down the steps and along the street, his gray coat merging with the dusk, leaving me standing in my own shop with the lamp unlit and my heart behaving like something I did not own.

He knew. Or he suspected, which in Vellham amounts to the same grave. Or — the thought arrived slowly, turning over like type dropping wrong-foot into the stick — or he wanted me to believe he knew, for reasons of his own, and had just invited me to follow him into an abandoned orchard after dark, which was either a trap, a blackmail, a madness, or the single most interesting thing anyone had said to me in eleven years.

Duchess wound around my ankles, insistent about supper. Outside, the lamplighter made his rounds, and the gas came up gold along Tanner's Lane, and somewhere past the cooperage the derelict orchard stood in the dark with its dead trees and its locked gate, keeping whatever it kept.

I fed the cat. I banked the stove. I stood a long while at the window, watching the corner where the census taker had vanished, and told myself — firmly, in my father's voice, in the voice of a man with a ledger balanced to the farthing and nothing irregular in his house — that I had absolutely no intention of going anywhere near that orchard.

I am a foundryman. I know when a mold is waiting to be poured.

Tomorrow evening, then. Tomorrow evening I would walk past Tanner's Lane, purely to clear my head, purely as any honest tradesman might, and I would not so much as glance at a locked gate.

I put out the lamp. In the dark, my hands were shaking — fine hands, remarkable hands, my father's hands — and for the life of me I could not tell whether it was fear, or the beginning of hope.
