## 2. A Question of Households

The Ministry of Records issued its census takers three things: a leather satchel, a stub of indelible pencil, and a lecture on the sanctity of accurate returns. What it did not issue was a conscience, which is perhaps why I had to bring my own.

I had been walking the printers' quarter since eight that morning, and by noon I had counted forty-one chimneys, thirty-nine of them smoking, two hundred and six souls of varying honesty, and one door I had been circling for the better part of an hour like a moth with an appointment it dreaded.

The Winterbourne Type Foundry stood at the end of Quill Lane — no relation, as far as I know, to any binder named Quill; Vellham is a city of small ironies — with its windows fogged by decades of lead dust and its sign repainted so many times the letters had gone soft at the edges, like a word repeated until it loses meaning. WINTERBOVRNE, said the sign, because the U had been cut from the same stock as everything else in this city: cheaply, and without much regard for who would have to read it.

I knew the household register by heart before I knocked. One entry. *Winterbourne, Master Wendell, typefounder, sole occupant.* The hand that had written it belonged to a clerk named Ostrow, who had visited four years ago and recorded what he was told, because Ostrow believed that the world was exactly as large as his forms allowed. Four years ago there had been a father, Aldous Winterbourne, listed above the son. Somewhere between one census and the next, the elder line had been struck through and the younger promoted to head of household. That happens. Fathers die. It is the one demographic event no regime has yet managed to make suspicious, though ours was working on it.

What made me circle the block was not the death. It was the arithmetic. In my satchel I carried the returns for every household on Quill Lane, and I had developed, over six years of this trade, a habit that the Ministry would have called irregularity and that I called paying attention: I compared. Grocer Hask's ledger against his larder. The widow Tern's declared income against her coal. And the foundry's chimney — smoking, I noted, at seven in the morning when I passed on my way to the Tern house — against a workforce of, per the register, one.

A foundry does not run on one pair of hands. Casting matrices, dressing type, keeping a furnace fed — these are jobs, plural. Either Master Wendell Winterbourne employed workers he had failed to declare, which was a fineable offense, or the foundry was dying and the chimney smoked out of stubbornness, or —

Or something else, said the small voice I tried not to listen to, the one that had gotten me into this profession's particular kind of trouble. Or the register was wrong, and registers were never wrong; they were only incomplete, which is the same thing wearing a better coat.

I knocked.

* * *

The door opened on the second knock, and I met Master Wendell Winterbourne for the first time.

Let me describe what I saw, because seeing is the whole of my trade, whatever the form says. I saw a person of perhaps five-and-twenty, dressed in the apron of the trade, ink at the cuticle of the left thumb and graphite at the right forefinger — a compositor's marks, though the register said founder, and founders carry their dirt differently, in the whorls of the palm rather than the nails. I saw hands that had been described to me once, by a magistrate's clerk I shall not name, as *too fine for the quarter* — and I will come back to those hands, because they mattered more than anything else in the room. I saw eyes the grey-green of canal water, doing a thing I recognized with a lurch of professional kinship: measuring me while pretending to be measured.

"Master Winterbourne? Silas Ash, Ministry of Records. Census enumeration, District Nine." I produced the satchel and held it slightly forward, the way you present credentials to a dog you hope is friendly. "I'll need perhaps a quarter hour."

"A quarter hour." The voice was low and even, pitched — I noted — just below where a stranger's ear expects it, so that listeners lean in without knowing why. "We're mid-cast today. But the Ministry waits for no man's crucible, I suppose."

"No, it doesn't. It barely waits for its own clerks." I smiled. He didn't. Good. People who smile back at census takers are hiding nothing, and people hiding nothing are rarely interesting.

He stood aside and let me in, and I stepped across the threshold of the Winterbourne foundry into air that tasted of metal and hot resin and, underneath it, something faintly sweet that took me a moment to place. Lamp oil? No. Apples. Old apples, the ghost of them, as if the building remembered an orchard it no longer had. I filed this away in the drawer of unexplained things, which by then was nearly full.

The workshop was clean. That was my second note. Workshops in the printers' quarter ran to grime — it is a gritty trade, literally, lead and ink and paper dust settling into every seam — but this floor had been swept that morning, the type cases aligned with a precision bordering on devotion, the casting bench wiped down though the crucible was cold. Clean shops mean either pride or performance, and I have learned to distrust both equally.

"You work alone," I said. Not a question. Questions invite answers; statements invite corrections, and corrections tell you what a person wants you to believe.

"I do." He moved to the far side of the casting bench, putting it between us, which was sensible of him and told me plenty. "Since my father died. The work doesn't want crowding."

"And business?"

"Survives." A shrug that cost him something. "Vellham always needs type. Especially lately. Everyone wants to print something."

"Everyone does," I agreed, and opened the satchel.

Here I should explain the nature of my errand, because the Ministry had dressed it in new clothes that season and the clothes were ugly. The Fourth Enumeration, unlike its predecessors, came with an annex: Form R-7, *Household Composition — Irregular Standing.* The phrase was the Registrar-General's own, and it covered, in the ministry's elastic grammar, anyone whose presence in a household could not be cleanly accounted for. Unregistered lodgers. Kin of unstated relation. Persons whose papers did not match their persons. Servants without contracts. And — this was the clause that had emptied half the boarding houses on Tanner Street in a single week — dependents whose *documented identity diverged from observed presentation.*

You may translate that last one yourself. The Registrar-General certainly had, in a circular that used the word *corrective* eleven times.

I had spent the morning asking Form R-7's questions in a voice I kept flat as a compositor's stone, and I had spent the morning watching what the questions did to faces. The widow Tern had gone white and lied about a nephew. The Hasks had laughed too loudly. A dyer's assistant had simply closed his door. Fear has many dialects; I was fluent in all of them.

So I watched Master Wendell Winterbourne's face when I unfolded R-7 on his immaculate bench, and I confess I expected the usual weather.

What I got instead was stillness. Total, deliberate stillness, the stillness of a person who has practiced being looked at and has decided, somewhere deep and long ago, exactly how much of themselves to let be seen. It was like watching someone hold a pose for a portrait that never ends.

"The form asks," I read aloud, because reading aloud gives liars time, and I wanted to give this one time, though even then I could not have told you why, "whether the head of household maintains any dependents of irregular standing. Lodgers, kin, employees, or persons otherwise resident, whose particulars differ from those on file."

The silence stretched. Outside, a cart went by, and somewhere a printer's devil was beating a plate, the rhythmic iron-on-stone sound that the whole quarter breathes to. Winterbourne's hands rested on the bench edge, and I looked at them again — those fine hands, the nails trimmed close, a burn scar old and silvered along the left wrist — and thought: *you have been someone else, or you are being someone else, and I cannot yet tell which.*

"No," he said.

Just that. No elaboration, no tremor, no excess. The perfect answer, delivered perfectly. And that — forgive me, I am getting ahead of myself, but this moment matters, it is the hinge the rest of my life swung on — that perfection was the first wrong thing. Because everyone lies to the census. Everyone. The honest lie about money, the dishonest lie about people, the frightened lie about everything. Lies have textures. This one had none. It was smooth as poured lead, and I knew poured lead when I heard it.

"No lodgers, no kin, no employees?" I pressed gently. "Your chimney smokes early, Master Winterbourne. Foundries are hungry things."

"My father built her to run lean." A flicker now, quick as a misprint — grief or guilt or amusement, gone before I could set it. "She runs leaner still these days. Paper costs what it costs, and I cast less than I once did. But she smokes. She'd smoke through the end of the world, that furnace. Stubborn old thing."

He said it with such dry affection that I almost believed him, and then I caught it — the pronoun. *She'd smoke through the end of the world.* Not *it*. And I noticed, because noticing is my vice and my vocation, that his eyes had gone, for half a heartbeat, toward the stair at the back of the shop. Toward the rooms above.

There were no rooms registered above the shop. The upper floor was listed as storage.

"Stubbornness runs in families," I said mildly, and wrote, in the box provided: *None.* 

He blinked. It was the smallest crack in the stillness, and I have thought about it every day since. Surprise. Genuine surprise — the look of a person who had rehearsed resistance and been handed surrender instead.

"That's it?" he asked. "No further particulars?"

"The Ministry requires particulars only where particulars exist." I tapped the form. "You've given me none. I'm obliged to believe you. It says so in my instructions, in smaller print than God ever used."

Something happened to his mouth then. Not a smile — I want to be precise about this, because later I would have given a great deal to see him truly smile and could not, for years, separate memory from invention — but a loosening at one corner, involuntary, quickly arrested. As if humor had tried to escape and been detained at the border.

"You find the Ministry funny, Mr. Ash."

"I find most institutions funny. It's cheaper than weeping at them." I closed the satchel. "One more thing and I'll leave you to your furnace. Standard enumeration. Names and ages of all persons sleeping under this roof. Even storerooms, this year. The Registrar-General worries about overcrowding."

The pause lasted two seconds. I counted them; I count everything; it is a disease and I recommend it. Two seconds during which Master Wendell Winterbourne stood in a pool of window light with lead dust motes drifting around him like slow snow, and decided something behind those canal-water eyes, and arrived — I would swear this on any book you like, though books were soon to become dangerous currency between us — arrived at a decision that was itself a kind of lie:

"One," he said. "Myself. Thirty-one years of age." 

He was twenty-five at the outside. He gave the number flatly, daring me with sheer blandness to dispute it, and I understood suddenly that I was not looking at a man hiding a lodger, or hoarding undeclared apprentices, or running contraband type out the back — though I suspected all three and would learn I was wrong in instructive ways. I was looking at someone performing a self the way a compositor sets a page: letter by letter, justified to the margin, no gap where a gap shouldn't be.

And here is the thing I must own, the thing that made me a bad servant of the Fourth Enumeration and, I maintain to this day, a decent servant of something else: I wrote it down.

*Winterbourne, Wendell. 31. Sole occupant.* My pencil moved, and I felt the strangest sensation, as though I were setting type myself — choosing each character, knowing exactly what the line would print, and printing it anyway.

"Thank you, Master Winterbourne. You've been very helpful."

"Have I?" he said.

It was not insolence. It was a genuine question, quietly put, with a weight underneath it that I felt land somewhere below my ribs. *Have I?* As if he knew precisely how unhelpful he had been, and was curious whether I knew it too, and was — this was the part that kept me awake that night — testing whether I might be the kind of man who could be trusted with knowing it.

"Yes," I said. "Immensely."

* * *

I did not go straight to my next household. I stood in Quill Lane outside the foundry for a full ten minutes, pretending to consult my route sheet, and watched the windows.

At the third-floor casement — the storage floor, the unregistered floor — a shadow crossed the glass. Brief. Small, or hunched, or careful. Then gone.

I looked down at my form. *Sole occupant,* my neat clerk's hand declared to the Ministry of Records, in ink that could not be erased. Somewhere in a government office, a man named Ostrow's successor would total my column, and the number of persons in Vellham would grow by one fewer than existed, and no one would ever know that I had chosen it — that a census taker, keeper of the state's great ledger of who counts, had looked at a human being and privately ruled them uncountable, which is to say: safe.

I had done it twice before, in small ways. A grandmother listed as a servant. Two brothers entered as one. Each time I told myself it was rounding error, mercy in the margins, nothing. Each time the margins got wider.

But this was different, and I stood there in the cold trying to name the difference, and finally I could: the others I had shielded without looking at them. This time I had looked. This time I had seen a person build themselves brick by brick in front of me — *thirty-one, sole occupant, no dependents of irregular standing* — and I had seen the craft in it, and Lord forgive me, I had admired the craft. Six years of recording other people's lives had taught me to spot a false return at forty paces, and this was the finest false return I had ever witnessed, and some traitorous corner of me wanted to shake the artist's hand.

Who was up there, behind that glass? A sister? A lover? Someone the Registrar-General would have filed under *corrective*? I didn't know, and I discovered I desperately wanted to, and wanting-to-know is a dangerous appetite in a man whose job is to stop wondering.

I walked on to the Hask house, where the grocer's wife was waiting to lie to me about her sister-in-law, and I collected her lie with professional courtesy, and all the while my pencil kept returning, unbidden, to the same marginal doodle: a little tree. An apple tree, if you must know, crooked-trunked, the kind that grows wild against a wall where someone long ago spat out a seed.

I had no idea why. That should worry you, reader, as it worried me. But the truth of my life is that I have always followed the crooked trees, and every catastrophe and grace I ever fell into began exactly this way: with a door, a question, a lie told beautifully, and my wretched pencil declining to record it.

By evening the wind had turned, and it carried down Quill Lane that same ghost-sweetness I'd caught inside the shop — apples, unmistakably, from the direction of the derelict orchard behind the old foundry wall. Trees I'd assumed were dead, fruiting after all these years.

I decided, in the way of men deciding things that are actually decisions already made, that tomorrow's route would take me past the orchard.

Not in. Past. I am a careful man.

Reader, I went in.
