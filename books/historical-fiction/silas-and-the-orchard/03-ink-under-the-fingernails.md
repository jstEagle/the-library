## 3. Ink Under the Fingernails

I told myself I was not following him.

I told myself this while putting on my second-best coat — the one that makes a man look respectable but forgettable, which is the whole trick of good tailoring in Vellham — and telling myself it was a coincidence that the census taker's route out of Cooper's Lane ran the same direction as mine. I told myself a great many things that morning, and the remarkable thing is how little of it I believed and how completely I did it anyway.

A man who lives a lie develops a professional interest in other people's.

Silas Ash had been in my shop for perhaps twenty minutes on Tuesday, asking his questions in that flat, unhurried voice of his, and he had left behind no paper trail whatsoever — which was itself the anomaly. A census taker who writes nothing down is like a printer with clean hands: technically possible, deeply suspicious. He'd asked his questions about household composition and dependents of irregular standing, and I had given him answers so smooth they could have been set as display type, and then he'd looked at me — just once, at the door, over his shoulder — with an expression I have been turning over ever since like a coin I can't identify the mint of.

It wasn't triumph. That's what bothered me. If he'd known, truly known, there would have been something of the cat about it, some flicker of *caught you*. Instead he'd looked at me the way I look at a page I suspect has been altered — checking the kerning of my life against some private proof copy.

So on Thursday morning, when I saw the grey coat crossing the far end of Inkmarket Square, I followed him. For the trade's sake, I told myself. A foundry master ought to know what's moving through the quarter. The lie was thin enough to see daylight through, and I wore it anyway.

* * *

The printers' quarter has a geography all its own, and I know it the way a fish knows currents. There is the respectable current: Bindery Row, where the guild houses stand with their gilded signboards and their windows full of Bibles and almanacs and histories of Vellham written by men who were paid to find nothing interesting in it. And beneath that, the undercurrent — the back lanes where jobbing printers take work at night, where a broadsheet mocking the Provost changes hands folded inside a hymnal, where ink gets under your skin so deep that scrubbing only moves it around.

Silas moved through both currents without seeming to choose either. That was the first thing I noted. A government man walks the Row; a spy walks the lanes; Silas walked them alternately, in a rhythm that looked aimless until you watched long enough to see it wasn't. He paused at the corner of Quillsmith's Yard to let a cart pass — and used those three seconds to read every placard in the window of the radical bookseller there. He stopped to buy a meat pie he didn't eat. He gave half of it to a beggar boy and asked the boy something, and whatever the boy said made him nod and change direction.

I kept a ledger in my head. It's what I do instead of praying. *Subject: one Ash, S. Occupation: counting us. Habit: suspiciously observant. Disposition: unknown.*

He turned up Tanner's Stair — a lane I know well, because it runs behind three shops whose owners owe my foundry money — and stopped at a door I knew even better. Number nine. The widow Corvane.

My stomach did something complicated.

Hetta Corvane's husband had been a cooper, a good one, barrel-staves by the thousand, until two winters ago when the Provost's men came for him over some petition — I never learned exactly what; in Vellham the reason matters less than the knock at the door. They took him in for questioning and returned him in a crate marked *agricultural implements*, and the coroner's report said *misadventure*, and Hetta Corvane had spent every day since being a widow in a city that insisted her husband had died of clumsiness.

If Silas Ash was going to her door, he was doing one of two things. Either he was verifying census records — dull, official, harmless — or he was doing something else entirely.

I told myself I would simply walk past. Number nine has a window that looks onto the stair, and I am a man of regular habits, and regular habits are armor. But the door was open a hand's width against the spring damp, and through the gap came Hetta's voice — low, cracked, the voice of a woman reading aloud from a wound — and underneath it, the scratch of a nib.

Not a pencil. A nib.

Census takers carry pencils. Pencils are cheap, portable, erasable. A nib means ink, and ink means permanence, and permanence means someone intended what was being written to survive. Every instinct I own stood up at once like type jumping the forme.

I did not walk past.

* * *

There is a woodstore behind number seven, Tanner's Stair, belonging to a chandler who owes me for two fonts of long primer and therefore could hardly object if I inspected his stacked cordwood at length. From there I could hear the conversation in number nine the way you hear a sermon through a chapel wall — not every word, but the shape of it, the rises and falls, and enough particular stones to build the whole edifice.

"…the fourth of Frostmonth," Hetta was saying. "Or near it. They don't tell you the date. You learn to count backward from the crate."

"And the men who came," Silas said. "How many?"

"Four. Two wore the Provost's grey. Two wore nothing official, which is worse, because the unofficial ones do the parts the greys want deniable." A pause. "You're writing all this down."

"I'm writing all of it down."

"Who reads it?" Her voice sharpened, and I flinched in the woodstore like she'd caught me by the collar. "Because I've written letters. To the magistrate, to the guild, to the Provost himself, God forgive me. Every one came back stamped *received*, and every one went into the same drawer, and that drawer has never once opened in the direction of justice. So before I say another word — who reads what you're writing?"

And Silas Ash was quiet for long enough that I heard the fire tick in her grate, and then he said, in that flat unhurried voice that suddenly sounded like something else entirely:

"No one. Yet. That is the honest answer, Mistress Corvane, and you deserve honesty more than comfort. No court will hear this. No office will file it. What I am making has no reader today. I'm building a record for whoever comes after — for a time when someone asks *what happened to them*, and there is an answer that isn't the official one. Your husband's name, the date, the four men, the crate. All of it. Set down plainly, so it can't be unwritten later."

Another silence. Longer. When Hetta spoke again her voice had changed — not softened exactly, but shifted, the way metal shifts when it goes from cold to warm enough to work.

"You think there'll be such a time."

"I think regimes end. It's the one law they can't repeal."

"And until then? What happens to your book if they find it?"

"They won't find it," Silas said, "because I intend to be very careful, and because people like you will help me hide it. Which is why I ask rather than demand. This is yours to give or refuse. Refusing costs you nothing — I keep nothing of what you've already told me unless you say otherwise."

I stood among the chandler's cordwood with my heart going like a press at full tilt.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I could not stop turning over all the way home and all that night and into the grey of Friday morning: everything Silas Ash described, he described in the language of my trade. *Set down so it can't be unwritten.* A record against erasure. An archive for after. He was talking about type without knowing the word for it — or knowing it perfectly well — and he was standing in a cooper's widow's parlor offering the one thing Vellham's dead had been denied, which was to exist on paper in their own right.

I had spent six years keeping my father's foundry alive by being no one. Master Wendell Winterbourne, a solid, unremarkable tradesman with a solid, unremarkable household — and behind that mask, in the locked case upstairs, the proofs of who I actually was, waiting for a safer age that showed no signs of arriving. I knew exactly what a record for after was worth. I knew because I was one.

* * *

Friday, I followed him again. At this point the pretense of coincidence had worn through entirely, and I followed him the way you follow any thread you've decided to pull — knowing the garment may come apart, unable to stop.

He made five calls that day. I counted. A ropemaker's widow off Salt Lane. A mother whose son had been struck from the university rolls so thoroughly that the registry office claimed no such student had ever matriculated — I watched her show Silas the boy's acceptance letter, kept these two years in a Bible, and watched him copy out the name and the date and the exact phrasing of the registry clerk's denial. A ferryman's sister. A tailor. A girl — no older than nineteen — whose father had vanished from a debtors' cell, leaving a ledger of debts the family still couldn't legally prove were his.

At each door the same architecture: the flat questions, the nib scratching, the offer of refusal, the slow terrible relief of being believed. By the third house I stopped pretending to inspect cordwood and simply walked the quarter behind him, hood up, hands in pockets, a ghost with excellent posture.

By the fifth house I was doing arithmetic.

Five testimonies in one afternoon. If he'd been at this since the spring — and his shoes, his worn cuffs, and his encyclopedic way of navigating the poor lanes all suggested he had — then there were dozens. Perhaps hundreds. Names, dates, methods, witnesses, all gathered in whatever notebook rode in that satchel of his, in ink, in a hand I'd seen and could now recognize anywhere.

One satchel. One man. One copy.

I thought about what happens to one copy in Vellham. I thought about fires — accidental and otherwise. I thought about drawers that never open toward justice, and about how a thing that exists once can be made to have never existed at all, and how well I knew that art, having practiced its mirror image daily for six years. Erasure, in this city, was not an event. It was an industry. And Silas Ash was a single unarmed clerk trying to hold a ledger against the entire supply chain.

It should have frightened me. It did frighten me. But under the fear, somewhere below the waterline, something else was stirring that I recognized reluctantly, the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else's letter.

Curiosity. Worse than curiosity. Recognition.

* * *

Saturday it rained, the fine grey Vellham rain that soaks a man while insulting him quietly, and I nearly stayed home. I had type to cut — always type to cut; the foundry doesn't stay alive on sentiment — and a performance to maintain, and a dozen sound reasons to let the census taker walk his lonely rounds alone.

Instead I found myself on Salt Lane at dusk, watching him come out of the ropemaker's widow's door with his satchel wet through and his collar dark, and I thought: *he hasn't eaten.* It was not a deduction. It was the observation of a man who has gone long stretches forgetting to eat himself, recognizing the symptoms — the slight drift in the walk, the pause at the top of steps. Six years of managing my own disguise had made me fluent in the small economies of people carrying too much.

He turned down toward the river, and I made my decision the way most of my decisions actually get made: not with courage, but with momentum, walking faster than my caution could vote.

I fell into step beside him under the eaves of the rope-walk.

"Mistress Corvane keeps her window open a hand's width," I said. "Even in the rain. Bad habit, in a city like this."

He didn't startle — I'll grant him that, and I noticed it, filed it. He turned his head, took me in — the hood, the second-best coat, the face beneath — and something moved behind his eyes that was almost, almost a smile.

"Master Winterbourne." Not *sir*. Not the careful neutral address of a census man to a tradesman. My name, weighed and set. "Out taking the air?"

"In Tanner's Stair on Thursday, and Salt Lane today, and Quillsmith's Yard before that." I kept my voice low and level, a compositor's voice, each word spaced evenly. "You have a distinctive route, Mr. Ash, for a man counting chimneys."

We walked perhaps ten paces in silence, rain ticking on the eaves above us, the river smell rising with the evening. Then he said, "Are you going to report me?"

The question landed harder than he probably intended — or exactly as hard as he intended; I was beginning to suspect very little about Silas Ash was unintentional. Because *are you going to report me* was a question with a trap built into its hinges. A loyal subject reports a man collecting testimony against the regime. But I was not, strictly speaking, a loyal subject, and we were both standing in the rain aware of it, and he had just asked me the question in a way that made answering honestly cost less than lying.

That, I would learn, was his method entire.

"That depends," I said, "on whether you intend to keep writing with nibs. Nibs make permanent marks, Mr. Ash. In my experience, permanent marks attract attention. And attention, in this city, is the one commodity more dangerous than lead."

"Lead can be dangerous," he said mildly, "in the right hands. So I'm told. Foundries being what they are."

And there it was. Not an accusation — nothing so crude. Just a sentence laid down on the stone between us, type set and locked in place, waiting for me to either print over it or pull a proof.

The rain came down. Somewhere across the river the foundry bell rang the hour, and I stood in it — in the hour, in the rain, in the whole accumulating weight of what was happening — and understood that the coin I'd been turning over since Tuesday had finally shown me its face. He hadn't known, that day in my shop. He'd suspected. And he had spent three days letting me follow him, letting me listen at woodstores, letting me arrive at the truth by my own road, because a truth you're led to believe is a truth you'll defend.

"Tomorrow," I heard myself say, "you'll be canvassing the east lanes. There's an old orchard behind the disused foundry on Cordwainer's Rise — derelict land, no census value, no neighbors within earshot. If a man wanted to talk to someone about permanent marks away from open windows, that would be the place."

Silas Ash looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off the brim of his hat between us like a curtain half-drawn.

"Sunrise is late this time of year," he said finally. "But the light holds until six."

He touched his hat brim and walked on into the wet evening, satchel heavy against his hip, and I stood under the eaves of the rope-walk with my heart hammering like a press out of register, thinking:

*I have just invited the regime's own clerk to a secret meeting. Father, wherever you are, I hope you're proud, and I hope you're also prepared to explain to Mother why I've inherited your talent for finding trouble dressed as duty.*

Six o'clock tomorrow. The orchard at the wall.

I would bring the proofs.
