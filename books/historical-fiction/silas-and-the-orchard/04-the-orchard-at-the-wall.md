## 4. The Orchard at the Wall

*Silas*

There are two ways to follow a man through Vellham. The first is to be a professional, which I am, and the second is to be honest with yourself about why you're doing it, which I was trying very hard not to be.

I had been honest enough by the third evening.

Master Wendell Winterbourne — proprietor, typefounder, pillar of the Printers' Quarter, a man whose handshake had the exact temperature of a ledger — left the shop each night at half past seven. On Monday he walked home like a man going home. On Tuesday he took the long way past the binders' row for no reason I could find in his face. On Wednesday he went over the wall behind the old foundry like a man who had done it four hundred times and resented every one of them.

I gave him a decent interval, because a census taker learns patience the way other men learn drinking: early, thoroughly, and with consequences that last decades. Then I followed.

The orchard behind the Winterbourne foundry had belonged to somebody once — some ancestor who planted apples against the city wall and then died, presumably, of optimism. Now it was a ruin of trees gone wild and leggy, rows strangled by bramble, the ground soft with twenty years of fallen fruit nobody bothered to gather. The regime didn't care about it. The regime cared about grain, guns, and names, in that order, and an orchard offered none. Which made it the most valuable piece of land in Vellham.

He stood among the trees with his collar up, doing nothing suspicious whatsoever, which is itself suspicious when performed at dusk behind a wall. He held a lantern unlit in one hand and a pruning knife in the other, and he was examining a tree as though the tree owed him money.

I stepped on a branch. Deliberately? I'd like to tell you yes. Honesty being tonight's theme.

He turned fast — faster than a man carrying only a pruning knife ought to turn — and the knife came up level with my chest before either of us had decided anything.

"Master Winterbourne," I said. "Lovely evening for arboriculture."

"Silas Ash." He didn't lower the knife. His voice did something interesting, though — it went flat, the way water goes still right before it goes over the edge. "You're a long way from your ledgers."

"So are you."

"I own this land. I can stand on it."

"You can," I agreed. "You can also explain why the master of a prosperous foundry climbs his own wall instead of using the gate, prunes trees he has no intention of selling fruit from, and visits three evenings running at the precise hour when no respectable witness is abroad." I let that sit among the apple trees a moment, where it could ripen. "I'm a numbers man, Master Winterbourne. Patterns are my trade."

"You've been following me."

"For three days. You never once looked back. That worries me more than anything else I have to say tonight."

The knife stayed where it was, but something behind his eyes shifted — recalculation, I'd have called it in my reports, if I ever wrote reports that were true. "Then say it," Wendell said. "And say it quickly, because if you're here to sell me to the Provost's office, you should know I haven't got anything worth the paper they'd write the warrant on."

"That," I said, "is the single least true thing anyone has said to me all year, and this year I've interviewed four hundred households."

I reached into my coat. He flinched — a small flinch, mastered quickly, but I saw it, because noticing things is my profession and my punishment — and what I brought out was not a warrant or a bribe but a notebook. Cheap paper. My own hand. Six months of nights pressed between covers.

"What is that?" he said.

"The reason you're going to put down the knife."

* * *

Here is what I told him among the trees, and I will set it down now as plainly as I set it down there, because I have learned that plainness is the only armor I own.

"In the spring of '39, the registry struck eleven names from the Third Ward rolls. Not moved. Struck. As though eleven people had never drawn breath in Vellham." I heard my own voice go careful, the way it does when the alternative is shaking. "I know because I copied them out the week before. It's my job to copy them out. And then one morning the book simply disagreed with my memory, and memory, Master Winterbourne, is not evidence. Memory is what they're counting on us to have instead of evidence."

Wendell hadn't lowered the knife. But he had stopped pointing it at me, which in the grammar of knives is nearly a sentence.

"There was a cooper on Tanner's Lane named Aldous Ferren. Taken in the winter. His wife asked me, during the census, whether her husband would still be counted, because she wanted him counted — she said if the state wouldn't say he existed, then in ten years even she would start to doubt it, and she refused. She refused." My thumb found the edge of the notebook. "I wrote her name down too. There was nowhere to write her question, so I invented somewhere. This."

"You're keeping a private register," Wendell said slowly. "Of the disappeared."

"I'm keeping the truth in duplicate. Because the official copy keeps getting amended." I turned pages so he could see: dates, wards, descriptions, testimony taken down in the shorthand I'd developed for exactly this, a shorthand that looks like crop accounts if anyone seizes it. "Forty-one names. Forty-one people the Provost's office says were never born. Widows. Mothers. A printer's apprentice from Gilders' Row. A boy who owes me nine coppers and I intend to collect, wherever he is."

"And you're telling me this why?" His voice was very quiet. "Why tell the man you've spent three days catching?"

"Because you already knew."

That landed. I watched it land. I have delivered a great many difficult sentences in doorways across this city, and I can tell you the difference between a lie landing and a truth landing: a lie bounces. A truth sinks, and the whole face has to rearrange itself around the weight.

"I don't know what you mean," Wendell said.

"Your hands," I said. "When you cut type on Tuesday, you used the third finger and the ring together, which is how a compositor holds a body sort, not how a founder does. Your shop smells of ink under the oil. Your order books list lead purchases forty percent beyond what founding requires, and the surplus isn't in stock, because I checked the storeroom while you measured my hat size. And you visit an orchard at dusk carrying a lantern you don't light." I finally let myself smile, and it wasn't a kind smile; it was the smile of a man holding out the only thing he has. "I think there's a press under those brambles, Master Winterbourne. Or the makings of one. And I think you are the best-kept secret in Vellham, and secrets keep better in company."

For a long moment the only sound was the wall wind moving through the dead rows, and I confess I stood there composing my own disappearance in advance — where I'd run, what I'd burn, whose door I'd darken — because I had just handed a stranger everything I am, and strangers have hands.

Then Wendell Winterbourne laughed.

It was a short, bitter, wonderful sound, a laugh like a door slamming on a house full of furniture. "Three days," he said. "Three days of watching, and you open with the lead weights in my order books."

"Patterns are my trade."

"My father built this shop," Wendell said, and now the voice had changed entirely, stripped down to joists. "Built it with his hands, which were finer than any founder's hands have a right to be, and he kept it by being careful for thirty years, and when he died the Provost's men came within the month to inventory it, because a shop without a son is confiscation with paperwork. So I became the son." The lantern hand flexed. "You want to know what I do in this orchard? Nothing. I stand where no one can see me and I am no one for ten minutes a day. That's all. That's the whole crime."

"It isn't," I said gently. "But I don't care if it is."

"No?"

"No. Because I didn't come here to report you. I came here to hire you." I held up the notebook again. "Forty-one names, and I can't print them. I can barely read my own shorthand aloud without my voice going wrong. Testimony needs setting, Master Winterbourne. It needs lead. It needs someone who knows how to make words heavy enough that they can't be struck out by morning. The regime erases people with ink. I propose we answer in kind."

"You're mad," Wendell said, with feeling.

"Almost certainly. It runs in families."

"If they find this — if they find *us* —"

"They won't find us here. Nobody looks at an orchard. That's its whole virtue." I stepped closer, close enough to see the candle-flame shiver of fear in him and the thing underneath the fear, which was not courage exactly but something older and more stubborn, the thing that keeps a person standing watch for years over a grave that isn't a grave. "Look — you needn't answer tonight. But understand what I'm offering, because it isn't blackmail, whatever your face is doing. Blackmail is when I profit from your secret. What I'm proposing costs me everything. If you refuse, I'll fold this notebook away and never trouble you again, and I'll go on writing names in pencil until they come for me, and the record will die in one cheap notebook in one cheap coat. That's the alternative, Master Winterbourne. That's what you'd be refusing."

Wind again. Somewhere beyond the wall, a patrol bell rang the half-hour, and we both went still as rabbits until it passed, and in that shared stillness something passed between us that I did not have a word for yet. I would find the word later. Later cost us both a great deal.

"Say the offer plainly," Wendell said at last. "All of it. I've survived this long by making men say things plainly."

"All of it, then." I took a breath. "You help me set and print a true register of the vanished — names, dates, testimony, everything I can gather and verify. We hide the work here, or wherever you judge safest; your craft, your call. In exchange, your name appears nowhere. Not in the record, not in my notes, not in my mouth. If I'm taken, they'll find a mad census taker and a notebook, and you'll be a man who prunes trees at odd hours. I ask nothing else. No payment, no oath, no friendship — I'm told I'm difficult to be friends with."

"And what do *you* get?"

I looked down at the notebook in my hand, at the forty-one names written in a hand that had gotten smaller over six months, as though the page itself were closing over them.

"A brother," I said, before I could choose a safer word. "His name was Tomas. It isn't anymore. It isn't anywhere. I copy the living for a living, and I couldn't keep one name of the dead alive in the only book that matters." I made myself look up. "I get to stop being the man who writes down what the state tells him exists. That's the wage. Take it or leave it."

Wendell looked at me for a long time. Then down at the notebook. Then, absurdly, at the tree beside us, as if consulting it.

"You realize," he said, "that if I say no, you know too much to walk away from, and I know it, and you know I know it."

"I know you won't," I said. "I watched you measure a widow's window for shutters you weren't selling, just so she'd have a reason to open the door. People who do that don't feed witnesses to the Provost."

"That," said Wendell Winterbourne, "was one time."

"It was Tuesday."

Another silence, but a different animal now. He slipped the pruning knife into his belt — a small surrender, or a small threat, possibly both; with him I would learn it was always both — and held out his hand.

"Not yes," he said. "Not yet. But I'll hear terms. Proper ones. Tomorrow. Bring the notebook."

"Tomorrow," I said, and shook the hand of the most careful man in Vellham, and felt, in the grip, the fine strong fingers that were going to make forty-one people impossible to erase.

I climbed back over the wall into the honest dark, and I did not look back either.

* * *

*Wendell*

Let me tell you what Silas Ash cannot have known, standing there in my orchard with his terrible little notebook and his eyes like audit lamps.

He cannot have known that I had seen him coming for three days, and had spent those three days deciding, with great care, exactly which of my secrets to let him catch.

No. That's a lie, and Father taught me lying badly done is worse than none. I hadn't decided anything. I had walked to the orchard every night because it was the only place in Vellham where I breathed, and I had failed to look back because looking back is a skill and I have never had it. When he spoke my name among the trees, every plan I owned evaporated, and what remained was a woman with a pruning knife pointed at the first person in eleven years who had looked at her hands and seen the truth.

So: caught. Caught by a census taker, of all God's dull instruments, the one man licensed by the state to count me and file me. And instead of the warrant I had rehearsed surrendering to for a decade, he opened a notebook full of ghosts and asked for my help.

I shook his hand. God help me, I shook his hand, and my heart was going like a galley drum, and the moment our palms touched I understood I had just made the second reckless decision of my life. The first was becoming Master Wendell. At least that one had a corpse's wishes behind it.

I stayed in the orchard after he left. I sat down in the wet grass between the second and third rows — Father's rows, the ones he grafted himself the summer I was nine, teaching me which scions would take — and I laughed until it turned into the other thing, the thing I do perhaps twice a year, quietly, facing the wall.

Because here is what the census taker had seen in three days that the Provost's office had missed in eleven years: everything. The lead weights. The ink under my fingernails. And worse — he'd seen the orchard for what it actually was. Not a hiding place. A wound. Ten minutes a day of being no one. He had stood in my only country and offered to build something in it.

A register of the vanished. Set in lead. Names made heavy enough that morning couldn't strike them out.

I thought of Aldous Ferren's wife — no, I didn't know her; I knew of her, the way the Quarter knows things, the cooper taken in the winter, the wife who stood at the registry for six hours asking them to say his name aloud and was escorted out. I had set wedding announcements for half this city. Births, deaths, marriages, guild promotions — the whole bright ledger of the living, cast in my type, printed on my presses. And underneath all of it, for eleven years, I had kept a different book. A mental one. Every name I'd heard whispered as vanished, kept nowhere, set in nothing, as safe as a breath and as perishable.

He was offering to take that book out of my head and put it into the world. Lead and ink and paper. Evidence. The thing memory isn't.

It was the most dangerous proposal I had ever heard, and it was also the only one I'd ever wanted to accept, and those two facts sat in my chest like a font case that wouldn't lock.

Tomorrow, then. Terms. I would bring terms, and they would be iron, because if I was going to hang, I would hang for work done properly — that much Father's ghost demanded, and Father's ghost was a demanding creditor. No names. Never my name, never in any record, never in his mouth. The work hidden where I judged safest. And one condition more, which I hadn't said aloud yet and would tomorrow, the condition that mattered most:

If the Provost's men come for him, he gives them nothing, and if they come for me, I give them nothing, and neither of us is permitted to be noble about it. Nobility gets foundries confiscated and census takers buried. Careful keeps people alive. I had built a life on careful. I intended to survive this one on it too.

I picked myself up out of the grass, brushed off my coat, and lit the lantern at last, walking the rows slowly, my rows, checking the trees the way I checked the shop each night — a ritual, a rosary, a lie I told myself about ownership. At the far end, behind the worst of the bramble, under a sheet of rusted tin and forty years of leaf mold, lay the reason a census taker's clever eyes had almost — *almost* — found more than he knew.

My father's second press. Dismantled, wrapped in wool and waxcloth, sleeping under the roots of the oldest tree like a coffin we'd never had the heart to bury properly.

*Terms,* I thought. *He wants terms. Wait until he learns what's actually for sale.*

I pinched out the lantern and went home over my own wall, a man with a secret heavier than lead and, for the first time in eleven years, somewhere to set it down.
