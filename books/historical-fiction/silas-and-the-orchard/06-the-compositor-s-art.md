## 6. The Compositor's Art

I have kept ledgers for eleven years, and I will tell you what a ledger is: it is a machine for making people small. Every column wants you shorter than you are. Every line reduces a household — a kettle on the stove, a cough in the night, a grief nobody has named yet — to a number of souls, a trade, a mark in the irregular column if you're unlucky enough to be counted that way.

So understand that when I say I learned more about Wendell Winterbourne in one night at their type case than I had in three weeks of census work, I am not being romantic. I am being precise.

They had agreed to teach me the shop after midnight, and they had agreed grudgingly, the way a cat agrees to be held — technically compliant, entirely uncommitted to the experience. The terms had been settled among the apple trees two nights before: I would bring my pages, they would cut the type, no name of theirs would ever appear in anything I wrote. In exchange I would learn enough of the craft to be useful instead of merely underfoot. That was the phrase they used. *Useful instead of merely underfoot.* Wendell Winterbourne could make an insult sound like a syllabus.

"Rules first," they said, unlocking the shop door with the key worn smooth as a river stone. "You touch nothing without asking. You breathe nothing outside this room. And if anyone knocks, you are a client who couldn't sleep and wanted to discuss a funeral pamphlet."

"Do people do that?"

"People do everything at three in the morning," Wendell said, "that they'd be ashamed of at noon."

The shop smelled the way I imagine churches smell to people who actually believe — tallow and ink and something mineral underneath, the cold breath of metal. Moonlight came through the front window in a long gray parallelogram and lay across the composing stone like a body under a sheet. Wendell lit two candles, cupping the flames with fingers that moved before thought, the way mine move toward a dropped ledger. Then they stood in the middle of their own shop and looked at me with an expression I would come to know well: the assessment of a compositor measuring a stranger's worth in ems.

"Sit there. Don't sit there." They pointed at a stool by the stone, then at a spot clearly not by the stone. "Now. What did you see when you walked in?"

"A print shop."

"What did you *see*."

I looked again, properly, the way I'd look at a room where someone might be lying to me. Racks of type along the left wall, each case labeled. A press under canvas like furniture in a house of mourning. Reams of paper stacked and wrapped against damp. A galley tray. A quoin key hanging from a nail. A second door, half-hidden behind the paper stock, that I pretended not to notice because noticing it aloud would have ended the evening.

"Order," I said finally. "Everything arranged so the hands can find it in the dark."

Something shifted in their face — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a person discovering the interrogator can read.

"That's the whole art," they said. "Chaos is easy. Anyone can make chaos; the regime makes it by the ton. Order is the expensive thing. Now watch your hands, because your eyes are going to lie to you all night."

* * *

Here is what I knew of printing before that night: ink goes on paper, paper goes into the world, sometimes the world burns afterward. Here is what I learned between midnight and the birds: that everything I had ever read had been assembled letter by letter, upside down and backward, by someone whose fingers knew four hundred small metal faces the way a shepherd knows sheep.

Wendell took down a job case and set it on the stone. The compartments were shallow wooden wells, each holding its little population of sorts — lead alloyed with antimony and tin, each letter cast with a body, a face, a nick, a groove. To anyone else, a gray field of noise. To Wendell, a city with streets.

"California case," they said. "Lowercase here, capitals up top. You'll learn the lower first, because lowercase does the honest work and capitals only show off." They held out their hand, palm up, eyes on the candle. "Give me an *e*."

"How would I know which—"

"You wouldn't. That's the point. Watch."

And then they did the thing I will be trying to explain for the rest of my life. Without looking down — eyes still on me, still on the flame, conversational, almost bored — their right hand went into the case and came back with a sort. They turned it so the face caught the light. It was an *e*. They did it again. *A.* Again. *T.* The hand moved through those wooden streets in the dark as if it had grown up there, and I suppose in every way that matters, it had.

"The eye reads forward," Wendell said. "The compositor works backward. Mirror-wise. You're not setting words; you're setting their reflection, and the press will turn them right way round. Your mind must hold the sentence whole while your hand builds it broken. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That it's maddening."

"That it's *faith*," they said, with a sudden fierceness that startled us both. "You never see the thing you're making. Not once, not until it's finished. You assemble meaning blind, piece by piece, trusting that the reversal will resolve. Trusting that what comes out the other side says what you meant." They set the sorts down in their stick — the little steel channel where lines gather — and looked at them a moment. "Everyone in Vellham trusts something dumber than that every day."

I should write down, because this record is supposed to be honest even where it isn't safe: I watched Wendell Winterbourne compose a line of type that night, and it was the most intimate thing I had seen since my brother was alive.

Not because of anything tender in it. Because of the attention. Because they bent over that stick the way other men bend over prayer or drink or a woman's neck, total, unembarrassed, and the candlelight made a small gold country of their hands and nothing else existed in it. I sat on my forbidden stool and felt tenderness arrive in me the way weather arrives — uninvited, unannounced, changing everything it touched and asking no permission — and I did what any sensible census man does with a feeling he cannot classify.

I put it in the wrong column and kept working.

* * *

Near two o'clock they let me try.

"Your name," Wendell said. "Set your name."

I picked up the composing stick the way you pick up a snake you've been assured is tame. My first *S* went in upside down. My first *i* I set as a capital because I couldn't find the lowercase and refused to ask. My *l* I dropped, and it rang off the stone and rolled somewhere into the dark like a small fleeing soul, and Wendell retrieved it from beneath the paper cabinet without appearing to look for it.

"You hunt letters the way you hunt facts," they observed.

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation. Compliments cost extra." But the corner of their mouth had done something unauthorized, and I logged it.

My name, when I finished, was a disaster — uneven spacing, an *a* that faced the wrong direction like a man who'd entered a procession from the wrong end. Wendell took the stick, held it to the candle, and read it fluently, backward, without blinking.

"Silas Ash," they said. "Two words, nine characters, and not one of them placed with conviction." They slid the stick back to me. "Again."

"Why? It's just my name. Nobody's printing my name."

"Exactly," said Wendell Winterbourne, and there was something in it — a blade shown briefly, not drawn. "Nobody's printing your name. So it's the safest thing in this room to ruin. Set it until your hands believe it."

I set it eleven times. By the seventh my spacing improved. By the eleventh my name stood in steel in reverse, waiting for its reversal, and I understood — with the specific humiliation of a man who prides himself on seeing through floors and finding trapdoors — that I had spent three weeks believing I understood Wendell Winterbourne, and I had understood nothing. I had catalogued them: master of the foundry, head of household, hands too fine, secret too dangerous, ally too useful. Four columns. A number of souls. One mark in the irregular standing.

But the truth was that Wendell was not a man concealing a woman, nor a woman concealed in a man, nor any simple coin with two faces and an edge. Wendell was both at once and neither alone, the way a line of type is neither the mirror-image nor the reading — it is the process of becoming legible, and it is true in both states, and the world simply hasn't invented the word for a person who lives in the space between impression and page.

I had spent my career counting people. That night I met someone the census had no box for, and the failure was not Wendell's.

* * *

Somewhere past three, the candles burned low and the talk loosened, the way talk does when hands are busy and eyes are occupied elsewhere. Busy hands make honest mouths. I have used this fact in interviews; I had forgotten it worked on me.

"Your father taught you?" I asked.

"He taught everyone who came through that door. Half the printers' quarter set their first *e* at this stone. He believed type should be common as bread." Wendell distributed fresh sorts into the case, each falling home with a click. "Then the Charter came, and bread got regulated, and so did everything else. He died the year they closed the guild schools. Convenient timing. Or inconvenient. Depends which side of the confiscation papers you're reading."

"And your mother?"

"Went with him within the season. People said grief." Wendell's hands never paused. "It was the registry. They struck her name off the household roll because a widow heading a foundry was, quote, administratively irregular, and she used to stand in this room reading the correction notice over and over like it might apologize. She wasn't grieving him. She was grieving herself. The state told her she hadn't existed and she believed it for eleven months, and then she stopped believing anything, including that she needed to eat."

The candle hissed. I thought of my brother's name struck from the rolls, and of how the clerks had done it in red ink, casually, between two other entries, and how the casualness was the whole horror — erasure performed with the ceremony of a grocery list.

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded, the verbal equivalent of a farthing pressed into a beggar's palm.

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." Wendell looked up then, directly, and the candlelight did that thing it does, making everyone briefly beautiful and brief. "That's why I'm doing this with you. Not sentiment. Accuracy. Your book of names — if it's real, if it's exact, if every date checks and every witness holds — then it's the one document in Vellham the registry can't argue with. They can burn it. They can't correct it." A pause, and the blade again, sheathed but visible. "Which is why it had better be exact. If I find one embellished testimony in my galleys, Silas Ash, I'll melt the lot for slug metal and you can go count houses until the revolution or the grave takes you. Whichever's punctual."

"Noted."

"Good." They rose to trim the wicks, and in the act of trimming them became, seamlessly, someone else — shoulders settling, voice dropping half a register into the public instrument, the shopkeeper assembling itself around the person like a coat. I watched it happen and thought: they don't put the mask on. They take the true face off, carefully, and hang it by the door for morning.

"Who are you," I said, "when the shutters are open?"

"Master Wendell Winterbourne, head of household, proprietor, loyal subject of the Provost's charter, and a tremendous bore, God willing." They smiled with the public face, and it fit perfectly, and that was somehow worse. "And who are you when the ledger closes?"

The question sat in the room between us like a sort set on its face. I opened my mouth to give the official answer — Silas Ash, census taker, third district, diligent, unmarried, no irregularities — and heard, in advance, how it would sound in this room, at this hour, to these ears.

"A clerk," I said. "Mostly. With a locked drawer."

"Mostly," Wendell repeated, tasting it. They nodded slowly, and began putting the case away, and I understood that I had passed some examination whose questions I never saw. "Lock your drawer well, clerk. Tomorrow the provost's office sends out new inventory forms. Loyalty inventories, they're calling them. Every printer in the quarter gets a visit." Their hands paused on the case, just a moment — a hitch in the mechanism, a nick in a sort. "You'll be handing over your official books. Make sure your unofficial ones aren't wearing the same coat."

I hadn't told them about the forms. There had been no time; the notice had reached me that afternoon, and I had come straight here from the district office.

"You already knew," I said.

"I set type for the office stationery, Silas. I printed the form." Wendell blew out the first candle, and half the gold country of their hands went dark. "I've known for six days. The question I've been setting all week — backwards, blind, trusting the reversal — is whether you'd tell me yourself."

I sat in the failing light with my ruined name in a composing stick and gave the only answer that had ever been available to me.

"I'm telling you now."

"Mm," said Wendell, blowing out the second candle, and in the dark I heard them smile, or heard something that functions as a smile in the dark. "Then tomorrow you'll learn to distribute type. Bring your pages. And Silas —"

"Yes?"

"When they come for your books, look bored. Boredom is the best armor ever forged. Nobody searches a boring man twice."

I walked home through the blue hour with ink under my fingernails — real ink, my first, acquired honestly — and wrote nothing down, because for the first time in eleven years the most important record of my day was stored somewhere no clerk could seize.

In my hands, which remembered everything now.
