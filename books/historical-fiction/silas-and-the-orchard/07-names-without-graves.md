## 7. Names Without Graves

The first testimony came to me folded inside a delivery of rags.

This requires explanation. A type foundry runs on paper the way a furnace runs on coal, and paper comes from rag merchants who sell by the hundredweight — linen, cotton, whatever the city's laundry sheds off. The rag trade in Vellham is conducted entirely among people who do not ask questions, because asking questions about other people's linens is a fast way to learn things you cannot unlearn. So when Herr Brandt's cart rattled up to the alley door on a grey Thursday and handed me a bale with an odd weight to it — too dense at one corner, as if something were sewn into its heart — I understood that a message had arrived, and that Silas Ash had chosen his couriers with the same care he chose everything: quietly, and through channels no official would think to inspect.

I cut the stitching with my composing knife. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, was four pages of close, upright handwriting. Not Silas's hand — I knew his by then, small and precise as ledger entries, a hand that apologized for itself even in ink. This was rounder, slower, the letters of someone who wrote rarely and meant every stroke.

*My name is Greta Hollen. My husband was Anselm Hollen, cooper, of Tanner's Row. On the ninth of March the provost's men took him from his shop. They said he was wanted for questioning regarding unlicensed gatherings. He has not come home.*

It went on. She had gone to the registry office eleven times. Eleven times she had been told there was no record of any Anselm Hollen being taken, and moreover that no such man appeared in the rolls for Tanner's Row at all, which was strange, since she had lived beside him for nineteen years and could describe the exact sound his mallet made against staves at dawn. The eleventh clerk had suggested, gently, in the way clerks are gentle when they mean *stop*, that perhaps grief had confused her. Perhaps her husband had run off with another woman. Perhaps she should consider whether she wished to be listed herself as a woman of disordered mind.

*I am not disordered,* she had written. *I am a woman whose husband has been subtracted. Someone must keep the sum.*

Someone must keep the sum. I sat in the back room with the pages in my lap and read it three times, and each time the sentence landed somewhere below my ribs, in the place where I kept everything I did not say aloud.

Silas had appended a note in his own cramped hand: *She gave this to me willingly. She wants it set. She said if it exists in lead, it can't be argued out of existence. Set what you judge wise; I trust your judgment more than mine. But set the name.*

But set the name.

I looked at the case before me — the jobbing case I'd cleared for our work, the one that lived behind a false panel in the cabinet marked DAMAGED SORTS, HOLD FOR REPAIR — and I thought about what I was about to do that I had not done in all the years of performing Master Wendell Winterbourne, proprietor, respectable, dull as a proofing blank.

I was about to set a dead man's name in metal.

Not a customer's name. Not a title page, not a wedding notice, not the safe commerce of ink for coin. A name that the state had decided did not exist, cast letter by letter in an alloy of lead, antimony, and tin, at a foundry the state believed belonged to a man who had never existed either.

There is a particular silence in a print shop at night. People imagine it would be like any other workshop's silence — tools cooling, dust settling. It isn't. Type speaks. Lead has a voice; anyone who has spent years with it knows the small dry click of sorts settling into a stick, the whisper of a quoin key tightening, the faint ring when a good piece of brass rule is laid true. I have always found the noise companionable. That night it sounded like witnesses murmuring in a courtroom where no court would ever convene.

I set ANSELM HOLLEN first. Cooper, Tanner's Row. Taken the ninth of March. Then the details she'd given — his age, fifty-one; the scar on his left thumb from a slipped draw-knife; the fact that he sang badly and constantly and had taught their daughter every song wrong. Small facts. Useless facts, by any official measure. Exactly the kind of facts that vanish first and are never missed by anyone except the one person they were the whole world to.

The type was cold under my fingers. Of course it was; it had cooled hours ago in the case. But setting those letters, I felt the weight of them anyway — the way you feel the weight of a stone you've picked up to throw and haven't thrown yet. Every sort I slid into the composing stick added its fraction of an ounce, and by the time the line was full, the stick had that honest heft of finished work, and I stood there holding a dead man's identity in one hand and realized my hands were shaking.

Fine hands. That's what the trade called them, when the trade still complimented me — *Winterbourne's got a woman's touch with kerning*, some old customer would joke, and Father would laugh along while I smiled the smile that fit over my face like a lockplate over a mechanism. They never knew how right they were, nor how much it cost to be right in a way that could get me killed twice over — once for printing sedition, once simply for being what I was in a city that had opinions about such things. The regime didn't need two reasons to erase a person. One sufficed. I had spent my whole adult life making sure nobody ever found the second.

And here I was, manufacturing evidence against myself nightly.

You will want to know why. Everyone asks eventually — well, no one asks, actually, because no one knows, which is rather the point — but you, reading this where no one was ever meant to find it: you deserve the truth, since truth is what we buried.

I did it because of the singing.

That sounds mad. Let me finish. Greta Hollen had written that her husband sang badly and constantly. And I thought: somewhere in Vellham tonight there is a daughter who learned every song wrong from her father, and in twenty years she will not remember his face clearly — faces go, they always go, I've watched my own mother's face dissolve until only the idea of it remained — but she will remember that he sang off-key at dawn, if someone writes it down. If someone cuts it in lead and presses it into paper, she will be able to hold it in her hands. Weight is memory. That is the whole secret of my trade. Everything else burns or rots or is revised. Lead remembers.

The clerk at the registry office told Greta Hollen her husband had never existed. Very well. By morning, forty copies of a broadsheet that had never officially been printed would say otherwise, and paper argues better than widows do.

* * *

The second testimony arrived nine days later, and this one came through Maren Quill's bindery, which surprised me, because at that point I hadn't yet met Maren Quill and didn't know she was part of anything. I learned later that Silas had recruited her the way water recruits cracks — patiently, finding every weakness in a wall and filling it. At the time, I only knew that a parcel of bookbinder's board came to the shop with a sheet sewn into the lining, and that unwrapping it felt like committing a crime, which it was.

The hand this time was young. You can tell. Young writers press hard, as if the paper might dispute them.

*Tomas Ellery, student, enrolled at the Collegium in rhetoric. Removed from the registry on the fourth of April without notice to family. His mother went to inquire and was told no Tomas Ellery had ever matriculated. She has his acceptance letter. She has his fees receipt. She has his winter coat, which he wore out the door and never wore back.*

His mother, a widow named Adela Ellery, had done what mothers do: she had gathered documents. Receipts. Letters. The coat. She had carried them to the registry office like a lawyer presenting a case, and the registry office had looked at her evidence with mild interest, the interest one gives a child's drawing, and informed her that documents could be forged, mothers could be mistaken, and students could transfer to institutions abroad without informing unreliable relatives. Then they had asked her, again, whether she wished to make an appointment regarding her own fitness.

*They are not erasing him,* she had written, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. *They are pretending he was never written. There is a difference, though I could not explain it to them. Erasure leaves marks. This leaves nothing, and nothing is what they want, and nothing is what I refuse.*

Nothing is what I refuse.

I set TOMAS ELLERY at the candle-end of the evening, after the shop was locked and shuttered and the lamp turned low enough that a passerby would see only the dull glow of a diligent tradesman finishing accounts. Student, rhetoric, taken the fourth of April. Age nineteen. Left-handed. Had argued his way into a free place at the Collegium by writing an essay the examiners called impertinent and brilliant, and had celebrated by buying his mother a ribbon she never wore because she was saving it.

Nineteen. I had been nineteen once. I had been nineteen and terrified and certain the world would end if anyone discovered what I was, and I had survived by becoming smaller and quieter and very, very good at a craft. Tomas Ellery had apparently not known how to become smaller. The essay had been called impertinent. Impertinence, in Vellham, was a disease with a short course and a poor prognosis.

Setting his name, I caught myself doing something I despise in others: talking to the type. *Who were you?* I asked the sorts. *What was the essay about?* The types said nothing. Types never do; that is their virtue. They hold exactly what you give them and not one syllable more, and they will hold it for a century if the metal is good, waiting for someone to pull a proof.

Somewhere between the third line and the fifth, I stopped composing type and started composing a letter I would never send — to the mother, telling her that her son's name now weighed six ounces of lead alloy and could survive fire, flood, and committee, and that I was sorry it wasn't more, and that more was coming, because Silas Ash had shown me his ledger of the disappeared and it had names in it the way a river has fish.

That was the other thing I should confess. By the second week, Silas had begun coming to the orchard — our orchard, the derelict rows behind the old foundry where we'd shaken hands among the apple trees like conspirators in bad melodrama — and he had brought the master list. Not the whole of it; he kept the whole of it elsewhere, and I respected him enough not to ask, and feared him slightly too much. But excerpts. Pages copied in his apologetic hand. Names with dates and districts and the thin, clinical annotations of a census taker: *last seen*, *household notified*, *registry response*.

Registry response. That column was nearly uniform. *No record.* No record, no record, no record, until the words stopped looking like words and started looking like a wall.

"You understand," Silas had said to me that night among the trees, hunched in his coat against the autumn damp, breath fogging, "that this is not journalism. Journalists want readers. I want—" he'd paused, searching, and Silas was not a man who paused often; his sentences usually marched out of him like returns from the field, "—witnesses. Future ones. I want someone in fifty years to open this and know these people breathed."

"And you want me," I'd said, "because I'm the only fool in Vellham who can turn breathing into lead."

"I want you," he'd said, "because you're the best compositor I've ever seen, and because you looked at the list for a long time before you touched it, and most people look away immediately." He'd said it without flattery, which is the only kind of observation I trust. "Looking is the hard part."

He was right, damn him. Looking was the hard part. Anyone could feel noble outrage at a distance; the trick, the terrible trick, was reading each name slowly enough to let it become a person, and then going to bed, and then getting up the next morning and opening the shop and selling a wedding order to a couple who chattered about flowers while your fingers still remembered the shape of a vanished man's name.

* * *

By the time the third testimony reached me — a fishmonger named Oda Brenner, taken for the crime of selling at prices the market board called seditious, meaning cheap, meaning the poor could eat — I had developed rituals.

Rituals are what keep craft from becoming madness. I will teach them someday, if the world permits, to whoever inherits my cases. Here they are, in order:

First, wash your hands before beginning. Not for cleanliness. For ceremony. You wash off the day — the customers, the ledgers, the performance of Master Wendell — so that the hands that touch these letters belong to whoever it is I actually am underneath, the one who gets no name in any record, and that person deserves clean hands for the work.

Second, read the testimony aloud, once, alone, at full voice. Silas never asked me to do this. I began it the night of the cooper's wife, when I lost my place mid-page because my eyes were moving faster than my heart could follow, and I discovered that speaking the words aloud slowed me down to the speed of grief, which is the correct speed. The shop heard Anselm Hollen's life recited to its rafters. The shop keeps secrets.

Third, set the name first. Always the name, before dates, before details. A person is their name before they are their chronology. And set it large — larger than sense dictates for a clandestine broadsheet, larger than economy allows. Waste of space, waste of lead, says the efficient printer. Yes, I say. Exactly. They were made small by force. We make them large by choice.

Fourth — and this is the ritual I've told no one, the one that would confirm every suspicion about my sentimentality — I leave the composed form standing in the chase for a full minute before locking it up, and I put my palm flat on the face of the type. All the names at once, under one hand. It is the closest thing to a funeral rite our profession offers. No priest would recognize it. But lead holds warmth briefly after contact, and for one minute, the disappeared are warm.

Then you print, distribute, wash the forme, and distribute the type back into its case, because leaving a clandestine forme standing is how printers hang, and I had no intention of hanging. Dissolution is the printer's hardest lesson: you spend an hour building a thing, word by word, and then you break it apart yourself, letter by letter, scattering the pieces so the sentence exists nowhere except in its printed ghosts. People outside the trade think it's wasteful. People inside the trade know it's the oldest discipline we have. Nothing belongs to us. We are only the passage the words move through.

Some nights that discipline saved me. Some nights it failed utterly.

The night of Tomas Ellery's broadsheet, it failed. I had distributed his name back into the case — E-L-L-E-R-Y, each sort returning to its compartment with its small dry click — and I stood there with the empty composing stick in my hand and found I couldn't put it down. Couldn't move. The candle had burned to a stub; the shop around me was all shadow and the smell of ink and cold metal; and the thought arrived with the force of a physical blow:

*Nineteen years old. Registry response: no record. Someday, if this works, a stranger will run a finger over his name on a surviving copy and feel only the slight relief of ink on paper, and will never know that a frightened person in a locked shop wept over the setting of it, and that is exactly as it should be, and God help me, I envy the stranger.*

I envied the stranger. I wanted so badly to be the person who could read these pages as history instead of producing them as contraband. History is kind; it happens to other people. Production is not kind; it happens with your own hands, at your own risk, at two in the morning, while somewhere across the city a mother sleeps clutching a winter coat.

I blew out the candle and stood in the dark until my eyes adjusted, and then I banked the hearth, checked the false panel twice, and went up to the rooms above the shop where I slept alone and had slept alone for eleven years, and lay awake listening to the river traffic, thinking about sums.

Greta Hollen had written: *someone must keep the sum.* Subtraction was the regime's entire mathematics. Take a husband, take a student, take a fishwife, and declare that the numbers were always thus. Their arithmetic depended on everyone agreeing to forget the originals. Well. I had a different kind of counting house. Mine dealt in addition. Every name set was a number restored to the column. The provost could seize presses — they were already making their lists of printers, I'd heard whispers from the paper merchants, something about loyalty inventories, gentlemen going shop to shop — but they could not seize what we'd already sent out into forty kitchens and binderies and tavern drawers, and they could not, no matter how thorough their inventories, count what we kept in lead behind a panel marked DAMAGED.

Or so I believed, that night, lying in the dark with my heart hammering over nothing, over everything.

The knock came three days later. Not the alley door — the street door, the front, the respectable entrance, in daylight, with the shop open and customers' work on the stone. Two men in the Provost's livery, and between them, pale as unsized paper, Silas Ash, saying in a voice carefully drained of everything: "Master Winterbourne? These gentlemen wish to ask you some questions regarding irregularities in the census returns."

I set down my burnisher, smiled the smile that fits over everything, and said, "Of course. Gentlemen. May I offer you some wine?"

Behind my ribs, the sum kept counting.
