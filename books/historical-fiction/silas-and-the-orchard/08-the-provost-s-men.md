## 8. The Provost's Men

The notice arrived on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the regime's favorite day for bad news. Mondays are too raw; by Wednesday people have braced themselves. Tuesday catches you in the middle of something ordinary — mending a boot, arguing with a baker — and the blow lands before you've finished the sentence.

Mine came pinned to the door of my lodgings on Candlewick Lane, printed, naturally, on good stock. Someone at the Provost's office had excellent taste in paper and no taste whatsoever in prose.

*By order of the Lord Provost of Vellham: all keepers of public records shall present their registers, rolls, and returns for Loyalty Inventory within the fortnight. Failure to comply will be construed as an act against the peace of the city.*

Loyalty Inventory. The phrase had the particular smell of a committee that had met four times to choose it. Not an *inspection*. Not an *audit*. An *inventory* — as though loyalty were barrels of salt pork, countable, stackable, subject to spoilage.

I read it twice, then folded it into quarters and put it in my coat pocket, where it sat against my ribs all day like a second heartbeat, arrhythmic and accusatory.

Here is what nobody tells you about keeping two sets of books: the official ones become the lie you maintain out of habit, and the true ones become the life you live out of fear that someone will ask to see them. For three years I had carried both everywhere — the census ledgers with their neat columns of households, hearths, dependents of irregular standing; and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the false bottom of a case built for me by a cooper's widow named Halvard, the other record. The one with names the registry had struck. Dates the registry had never admitted. Testimonies no court would hear, taken from widows in kitchens that smelled of tallow and grief.

Wendell Winterbourne had cut the type for those pages. Wendell had printed them, hands steady as a surgeon's, in a shop that smelled of lead and lampblack. And every week or so, Wendell looked at me across the composing stick and asked, in the flat voice of a man who already knows the answer, whether anyone had come asking questions.

On Tuesday night, I went to tell him the answer was about to change.

* * *

The shop was closed when I knocked — the good knock, two and then one, which we had agreed upon back in the orchard days when we still believed agreements could protect us. Wendell opened the door with the bar still half-drawn, which told me they'd been expecting trouble of some flavor.

"You've heard," I said.

"I've heard." Wendell stepped back to let me in and shot the bolt behind me. "Master Pellowe at the chandlery got his notice this morning. He showed it around the quarter like a medal. 'Loyalty Inventory,' he kept saying, delighted, as though the Provost had personally praised his candles."

"Pellowe would frame a summons if it were handsomely printed."

"It is handsomely printed." Wendell said it with the pained precision of a craftsman forced to admire enemy work. "Caslon, or something near enough. Decent leading. Whoever set that notice knew his trade." A pause, and then, quieter: "That's what frightens me, Silas. It wasn't set by clerks."

We stood in the workroom among the cases and the galley trays, the candle turned low out of old habit. Outside, the printers' quarter was doing its best impression of a district with nothing to hide — shutters drawn, signs creaking, the river muttering to itself below the wall. Somewhere a dog was rehearsing its grievances.

"They'll canvass every press in Vellham," I said. "Every bindery, every type foundry, every stationer with more than two apprentices. They'll take inventories of stock, note who owns what presses, match output against declared work. And then—" I tapped the floorboards, meaning everything beneath them, meaning the whole submerged city of paper we'd been building, "—they'll start comparing."

"Comparing what?"

"Paper trails. That's what an inventory is for. Not to find sedition — sedition hides too easily. To find *arithmetic*. A printer who declares twelve reams sold but bought twenty. A census taker whose returns mention households that don't appear anywhere else." I watched Wendell's face do the thing it did instead of blanching — a small tightening around the eyes, like a knot pulled snug. "They won't read our pages, Wendell. They'll weigh them."

"And your books?" Wendell asked. "Your official ones?"

"Are immaculate. I've spent three years making them so. Every household accounted for, every dependent classified, every irregularity noted in the margins in my own hand with the appropriate bureaucratic sighs." I allowed myself the ghost of a smile. "I once wrote *declines further inquiry* beside a widow's name in such mournful italics that any inspector reading it would assume she'd wept on me."

"And the others? The true ones?"

"Halvard's false bottom. And after tomorrow, not even there."

Wendell crossed the workroom and stood over the font case where we hid the archive plates — the case of broken sorts, the one place in Vellham a search would be brief and bored. "You're moving them tonight."

"I'm moving mine. Yours stay. Moving yours is the one thing we mustn't do — plates leaving a print shop the week before an inventory is exactly the kind of arithmetic I just described." I took off my satchel, the heavy one, and set it on the compositor's stone between us like a patient on a table. "But there's a problem, and I want you to hear it from me before you hear it from a notice."

Wendell waited. Wendell was very good at waiting. I sometimes thought it was the truest thing about them — that under all the performance of Master Winterbourne, proprietor, householder, man of respectable ink-stained solitude, there was a person of enormous patience watching the world with the level gaze of someone setting type: measuring, kerning, deciding what deserved space and what should be crowded off the line entirely.

"The Provost's men aren't waiting for the fortnight," I said. "Pellowe was summoned early because he's harmless. The dangerous shops get visited first — quietly, informally, before any inventory can be prepared. A clerk comes in, friendly, buys some paper, asks after business. Then the formal visit follows, with the clerk's little report in hand." I unlatched the satchel. "A clerk has been asking after me since Saturday."

Silence, except for the dog outside, which had moved on to theological objections.

"Describe him," Wendell said.

"Grey coat. Soft voice. Writes left-handed, smudges the ink because he holds the pen wrong. Calls everyone *friend*, which is how you know he isn't one."

"And you know this how?"

"Because he introduced himself to me on Saturday as Master Ellery of the Office of Returns, and asked me, with great warmth, whether I found the census work *fulfilling*." I drew out the ledger — my real ledger, the archive itself, three years of the erased dead in my cramped hand — and laid it on the stone. "Nobody in the Office of Returns asks about fulfillment, Wendell. They ask about arrears."

* * *

We spent that night doing the most unglamorous work of the entire enterprise, and I record it here because history loves the barricades and forgets the bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping is where revolutions are actually survived.

Wendell made tea without asking, the way Wendell did everything essential — silently, competently, as though the kettle had simply decided. We spread my official ledgers across the workroom bench and I walked Wendell through them page by page, because if the Provost's men ever questioned us separately, our accounts of my accounts had to match down to the watermarks.

"This household here — the Vessons, Rope Walk, six souls. You'll say I interviewed the mother twice because the first return had an illegible entry. It did. Here, see the blot."

"I see it."

"You'll say I'm meticulous to the point of tedium, which requires no acting whatsoever."

"None," Wendell agreed gravely, and for a moment — one candle-lit, exhausted moment — we grinned at each other like conspirators, which is precisely what we were, and I felt the old strange pull in my chest that I had stopped pretending, even privately, was anything other than what it was.

Then back to work. Because the true ledger had to leave my possession entirely, and there was only one hiding place in Vellham that even I believed in.

"The orchard," Wendell said, when I said it aloud. "Under the floor of the lean-to?"

"Beneath the third apple tree. The one that leans against the wall. Bram showed me a hollow between the roots last month — he found it chasing a beetle and immediately understood its commercial value. I've promised him a penny for its use."

"He'll hold you to two."

"He can have three." I wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, then in waxed canvas, then in a layer of tarred sailcloth that Maren Quill had provided with the instruction to bury it deep and stop fussing. Three layers. Because paper is mortal, and memory written in water-soluble ink is a prayer said into rain, and I had promised thirty-one families — thirty-one *known* families; there were more, there were always more — that their dead would outlast the men who deleted them.

We went out at the hour when the watch changes and the streets belong to cats and insomniacs. Wendell carried a sack of legitimate type specimens as cover; I carried nothing but my coat and my nerves. Through the print district, past the shuttered chandlery where Master Pellowe's medal-glowing face had been, along the wall where the city's old ambitions crumbled into the orchard's older patience.

The apple trees stood black against a sky the color of dirty pewter. No moon. Good. The third tree leaned against the wall exactly as it always had, as it had leaned through the founding of the dynasty that now taxed its fruit, and Bram's hollow waited between its roots like a held breath.

I knelt and dug. The earth came away cold and smelled of apples rotting sweet somewhere in the dark rows. Wendell stood watch at the gap in the wall, and I heard — not saw, heard — Wendell's breathing change when footsteps passed on the far side: two men, unhurried, talking in the low fluent murmur of officials after hours. They passed. The breathing resumed.

I laid the ledger in the earth the way you lay anything you love in the ground: with more care than efficiency, and no expectation of getting it back unchanged.

"There," I said, when the soil was stamped and leaf litter scattered and the third tree none the wiser. "If they take me tomorrow, the record survives. If they take the record, I survive to rebuild it. Either way—"

"—the names stay counted," Wendell finished. We had said it so many times it had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket.

* * *

They came on Thursday, and of course it was Master Ellery of the Office of Returns, grey coat, soft voice, pen held wrong.

He arrived at my lodgings with a junior clerk carrying an empty portfolio, which is the most menacing object a bureaucrat can bring — a container explicitly designed for things currently in your possession. He admired the view from my window, which overlooked a wall and a gutter pipe, with complete sincerity. He called me friend twice.

"Silas Ash," he said, settling into the room's one decent chair as though it had been reserved. "Census taker, Ward Seven, nine years' service. That's a long time, friend. Long service builds habits. And habits—" he smiled with great gentleness, "—are what an inventory is for."

"I'd have thought inventories were for stock," I said.

"Stock. Habits. Deviations." He produced my file — my actual file, thick, ribboned, annotated in at least three hands — and let it sit unopened on his knee, which was a performance aimed at me specifically, and I applauded it internally. "Yours is an interesting record, Master Ash. Diligent. Almost *too* diligent, some might say. Returns filed early. Marginalia thorough to the point of... devotion." He let the word hang. "And yet your ward shows fewer irregular households than any ward in the city. Fewer disappearances. Fewer amendments. As though Ward Seven alone escaped the century."

"As though," I agreed pleasantly, "the people of Ward Seven are remarkably stable."

"Mm." He opened the file at last, delicately, the way a man opens a letter he already knows the contents of. "You interviewed the widow Vesson twice."

"The first return had an illegible entry."

"You interviewed the cooper Halvard's widow three times."

"She had additions. Grief is not a tidy process, Master Ellery. Households change shape after a loss. People move rooms. Dependents appear who were never declared. My work is to count what *is*, not what the registry wishes remained."

Something flickered behind his softness — appreciation, possibly, or the professional pleasure of a fisherman feeling weight on the line. He turned pages. I watched his left-smudged hand and thought about the third apple tree and the forty pounds of earth above three years of testimony.

"Here's what will happen now," Ellery said conversationally. "Over the next fortnight, you'll bring your current registers to the Office of Returns. All of them. Every ward book, every scratch pad, every private memorandum a diligent man inevitably keeps. We'll collate them against the master registry. Discrepancies will be noted." He closed the file. "Most discrepancies are nothing. Ink. Error. Grief, as you say." He rose, smoothed his grey coat, and gave me the smile of a man who has already written the ending and is merely waiting for the middle to arrive. "But some discrepancies are *arithmetic*, friend. And arithmetic is the one language the Provost reads fluently."

At the door, he paused. "Oh — one more thing. Your name appears in the visitor book of a certain print shop on Foundry Lane. Winterbourne's. Type foundry, formerly. Quiet establishment." His eyes were mild as dishwater. "A friend of yours?"

"A supplier," I said. "I buy my ink where the ink is honest."

"Of course you do," said Master Ellery, and wrote something in his little book, holding the pen wrong, and went out into the Tuesday-colored afternoon.

I stood in my lodging with the door shut and my heart going like a press run, and I did the sums myself, honestly, for the first time. Two visits logged. A pattern forming. A clerk asking after Wendell's shop by name.

The net was not tightening around me, I realized. It had been around us for weeks, drawn slow and courteous, and we had been admiring the view.

I took out my coat to go warn Wendell — and that was when I saw, wedged in the doorframe where it had been slipped sometime during Ellery's visit, a single printed slip. Fine stock. Caslon, or something near enough. Four words:

*WE KNOW ABOUT THE ORCHARD.*

And beneath them, smaller, in type I knew better than my own heartbeat — type cut at midnight by careful hands in a workshop on Foundry Lane — the letters *W.W.*, set as a printer's device, the way a forger signs his forgery.

Someone was learning our grammar. And they were fluent.
