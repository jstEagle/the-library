## 9. Maren of the Bindery

I have been followed before. It is an occupational hazard of hauling forty pounds of rag paper through the printers' quarter at dusk while wearing the face of a respectable tradesman who has no reason whatsoever to be doing that.

So when I heard the footsteps fall into rhythm behind mine — not matching, just *accompanying*, the way a cat accompanies a mouse it hasn't decided about yet — I did what any sensible person does. I stopped at Ostrey's window and pretended to admire a display of mourning stationery.

The footsteps stopped too.

"Black edging's gone out of fashion," said a voice behind me, low and dry as a bone left in the sun. "Since everyone we know keeps dying, they can't make it fast enough."

I turned.

She was perhaps my own age, broad-shouldered, with ink to the second knuckle and a leather apron that had clearly survived more arguments than its owner had lost. Her hair was pulled back with the violence of someone who considered vanity a scheduling problem. She looked at me the way I look at a misregistered page: assessing where the error lay, and whether it could be corrected or would need to be destroyed.

"You've been following me," I said, because denying it seemed beneath us both.

"Two streets," she said. "You cut through Cooper's Lane instead of taking Tallow Street, which is wider and better lit, because you didn't want to pass the provost's waystation. You carry paper like a man carrying paper, which sounds like nothing until you notice that nobody carries paper like that unless they're afraid of being seen with it." She tilted her head. "Also your collar's buttoned wrong."

I looked down. It was. One button off, the whole placket skewed, the sort of detail I would have caught on anyone else and missed entirely on myself, because for eleven years I had dressed a stranger every morning and eventually the stranger's hands had become my hands and stopped reporting their mistakes to me.

"It's been a long week," I said.

"They usually are, lately." She stuck out an ink-stained hand. "Maren Quill. Quill & Sons, Bindery, Fetterman Row."

"Quill and sons," I repeated.

"My late husband's name stays over the door. The guild trusts a dead man's name more than a living woman's, and I find that arrangement suits everyone." Her grip was brief and crushing, a handshake like a signature on a contract you hadn't read. "And you're Winterbourne. The type foundry."

"The foundry, yes."

"Mm." She let the word sit there, unbothered, the way a cat lets a bird sit there.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Maren Quill before that evening, which was nearly everything the quarter knew, which is to say everything except the things that mattered.

Everyone knew Quill & Sons bound the finest ledgers in Vellham. Everyone knew old Josiah Quill had died four years back of the lung-rot that binderies breed, all that dust and paste, and that his widow had kept the shop running without missing a delivery. Everyone knew she'd taken in apprentices after the Registry Purges orphaned half the guild children — first Pip, then Sefa, then little Bram, collected the way some women collect stray cats, except her strays could read by three and mend a spine by eight.

What the quarter did not know — what I only learned later, from Silas, whose business was knowing — was that Maren Quill had buried a brother in the same year as her husband. Not under a stone. Under an absence. He had been a compositor at the *Vellham Argus*, and one morning his case was reassigned and his lodging relet and his name, in the space of a fortnight, simply stopped appearing anywhere it had appeared before. No trial. No body. No record.

Maren had gone to every office that existed to be gone to. She had filed petitions with handwriting so beautiful that clerks kept copies of them for practice. And at the end, she had walked home, washed her hands, opened the bindery, and taught three orphans to sew signatures — and if her needle went through the leather harder than the work required, well. Leather forgives.

But that evening I knew none of this. That evening I knew only that a woman with ink to her knuckles had spent two streets confirming what I hauled in a sack, and was now standing outside Ostrey's looking at me with the patience of someone holding all the cards and finding them dull.

"Well, Master Winterbourne," she said. "Shall we walk? Tallow Street's fine. The waystation clerks don't look at women carrying bookbinder's canvas. They never do. It's the great privilege of my sex — invisibility, finally, after thirty-nine years of the other thing."

* * *

We walked. She talked, or rather she deployed talk the way sappers deploy charges, each sentence placed with intent.

"Paper's dear," she observed, as we passed the tannery. "Rag stock especially. A man buying forty pounds of it a month, cash, no jobber's invoice — I'd wonder where he prints it. Except there's no press big enough at the foundry anymore, is there? Sold the big one after your father died. Kept the small hand press, the proofing one. I know because my husband bought brass furniture off you in '61 and complained about the price for a decade."

"You have an excellent memory for other people's purchases."

"I have an excellent memory, full stop. It's the burden of the trade. You spend your life making books that last two hundred years, you start thinking everything ought to." She glanced sidelong at me. "Where's it going, the paper?"

"The orchard," I said. Because lying to her seemed suddenly, exhausting, beside the point — she had done the arithmetic already and I was merely delaying her sum. "Behind the old foundry wall."

"The derelict one. With the trees." Something moved across her face — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. "My Bram picks apples off those trees sometimes. Over the wall. I've thrashed him for it twice." A pause. "He keeps going back."

"Bram is how old?"

"Seven. Old enough to climb, young enough to think he's invisible." She smiled briefly and it transformed her face entirely and then vanished again like a lamp shuttered. "Children always think that. Then they grow up and learn the world has excellent eyesight."

* * *

At the mouth of Cooper's Lane she stopped and turned to face me fully, and I understood that the walk had been preamble and this was the document itself, the moment the seal came down.

"I'm going to say something," she said, "and you're going to want to deny it, and I'm asking you not to waste both our evenings. There's a census man been coming round the binders' row. Ash, his name is. Quiet fellow, asks odd questions, writes less than he asks. Last week he asked me — off the book, no form open, pen capped — whether I'd ever bound anything I wasn't paid for." She watched me. "Now, a census man doesn't ask that. A spy asks that. But spies ask it smiling, and this one asked it like a man confessing. So either I'm wrong about him, or he's wrong about himself, and either way—"

"Either way you've been watching who else he watches," I finished.

"He watches you, Master Winterbourne. Twice a week, regular as church, down toward the orchard wall. And you come back with empty arms and ink on your cuffs that isn't printing ink — it's got walnut oil in it, someone's cutting it with something to stretch it, wartime trick." She crossed her arms. "So. Deny it or don't. But know that if you deny it, I'll keep following you anyway, and I follow better than he does. I learned on my brother's trail. Nobody taught him to disappear properly either."

The lane was quiet around us. Somewhere a dog was complaining about the cold. I stood there weighing eleven years of careful nothing against this woman's flat grey eyes, and I will tell you honestly what decided me, because it wasn't courage and it wasn't trust.

It was Bram. Seven years old, climbing a wall for apples from trees nobody tended, in a city where children were counted and catalogued and occasionally subtracted. If the net ever closed — and nets close; it is what nets are for — I wanted it recorded somewhere that Maren Quill had known and had chosen, with full knowledge, to stand inside the circle rather than outside it. People who choose are different from people who are caught. I needed more choosers.

"There's a press in the orchard," I said. "Under the root cellar of the old foreman's cottage. We print pages that no court will hear and no registry will hold. Names. Testimonies. Of the disappeared." I watched her face as I said it, and saw the fish-under-ice thing move again, and this time stay. "Silas Ash knows. He started it. He compiles; we set and print. Four hundred pages so far, sewn, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for a world that might want them."

Maren Quill was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its dryness, the voice underneath the armor.

"My brother's name was Aldous," she said. "Aldous Quill. Compositor. He had a habit of humming while he worked, drove Josiah mad. There's no line anywhere in this city that says he lived. I've looked. I've looked in every ledger, every roll, every parish book." She looked down the lane, toward the river, toward the offices that had all failed her in their orderly ways. "You're telling me there's a book where lines like that get put back."

"I'm telling you we're writing one."

* * *

She did not cry. I want that on the record, since records are our trade now. Maren Quill did not cry in Cooper's Lane. What she did was worse to watch: she nodded, once, slowly, the nod of a woman closing a ledger she has audited and found accurate, and grief went into her the way water goes into ground, silently and all the way down.

"Terms," she said.

"You're demanding terms?"

"I'm demanding terms. First: I don't take charity and I don't give it. If I'm in, I'm in as a worker. You'll need binding — four hundred loose pages is a fire hazard and a confession. Sewn and cased, they're a book, and a book can be hidden, sold, smuggled, or burned with dignity. I bind them."

"We can't pay—"

"I didn't ask for pay. I asked for work. Second:" she held up two fingers, "my apprentices come with me. Before you refuse — hear me. Pip's twelve and reads Latin. Sefa's nine and has hands like a surgeon, she folds cleaner than I do. Bram's seven and can't be spared from minding, but he can mind anywhere, and the orchard's as good a place as any, and safer than most." Her chin came up. "They're mine, Winterbourne. Their parents trusted me with them and the state took the parents and left me the children, and I will not raise them in a shop where we pretend the world is honest. They'll learn the truth of it the way I learned it — working."

"Bringing children into this—"

"Is exactly what you're already doing," she said, without heat. "You bring yourself. You bring your secret and your risk and your name-that-isn't-quite-your-name, and every soul in that orchard is endangered by your presence and protected by your craft. Children aren't a different category. They're just smaller and quicker and they believe things, which makes them dangerous and precious in equal measure." She paused. "Besides. Bram already climbs your wall. You want him climbing it blind, or climbing it knowing why?"

I thought of the boy, then — though I had never met him — a small shape over the wall among the untended apple rows, and I thought of my father, who used to say that a press room should smell of bread and children or it would soon smell of nothing at all.

"Third term?" I asked.

"No third term." She almost smiled. "Two's plenty. Most contracts only need two parties and one lie between them, and we haven't even got the lie."

* * *

We went that night. Not to announce ourselves — Maren did not believe in announcements, she informed me, having once announced a price increase to the guild and received a delegation of angry masters within the hour — but to see, to count, to begin.

Silas was at the root cellar when we came down through the trees, lantern hooded, papers spread on a plank across two barrels. He rose when he saw me, then saw Maren, and I watched him do his census-taker's arithmetic — height, bearing, apron, ink — and arrive at the answer before I could offer introductions.

"Quill," he said. "Bindery. Fetterman Row."

"You know me?"

"I know every binding house in the district. It's my work." He hesitated, and then, with the honesty that was becoming his signature: "I also asked about you. Last week. Whether you'd ever bound anything you weren't paid for."

"Yes, I know. She told me. She also told me you asked it like a man confessing." I looked between them. "She's in. On her terms. Which include three apprentices and a complete refusal to be charmed by either of us."

"I wouldn't dream of it," Silas said gravely.

"See that you don't." Maren had already moved past us both to the plank table, and I watched her pick up a printed page — one of ours, a cooper's wife's testimony, set in my smallest text type — and hold it to the hooded light. Her lips moved slightly, reading. Her thumb, I noticed, stayed carefully at the edge of the sheet, touching only the margin, the way you handle something you understand to be alive.

When she finished, she set it down very gently and said, to the page as much as to us:

"Aldous Quill. My brother. Born 1819. Disappeared March of '58. He hummed when he worked." She straightened. "That goes in. I'll dictate the entry myself, and you'll set it clean, and when this book is bound I want to sew the signature it sits in. With my own hands."

Silas had his notebook out before she finished. I saw him write the name, the date, the humming, in that small relentless clerk's hand of his, and I saw his face while he wrote it, and I understood something then that I have carried ever since: that what we were building was not a weapon and not even, properly, an archive. It was a room. A room where the erased could be brought and set down and described — the humming, the hands, the habits, the whole human freight that registries never bother to carry — and someone would write it down and someone would set it in lead and someone would sew it into cloth boards, and for as long as the book existed, the room stayed open.

Maren Quill rolled up her sleeves, surveyed the cellar, the press, the barrels, the candle-stubs, the two men who had been managing alone out of stubbornness and secrecy, and delivered her verdict with the satisfaction of a woman finally permitted to fix something:

"This place is a disgrace. Where do you keep the paste? Never mind — I'll bring my own. And the paper stock is stacked against the north wall, which floods, which means half your run will cockle by spring. We start moving it tomorrow. After dark." She looked up, fierce and certain, ink still on her knuckles, and the orchard above us creaked in the wind like a ship coming about. "Well? Don't just stand there holding the lantern, Master Winterbourne. Show me your press. If I'm to die for this enterprise, I intend to know its specifications first."

* * *

It was near midnight when I walked her back as far as Fetterman Row — protocol, I insisted; she allowed it with the air of a queen permitting an umbrella — and at her door she paused with her hand on the latch.

"One more thing," she said. "Your collar's still wrong."

I reached up and fixed it.

"And Winterbourne." The streetlamp caught her face, and for a moment the fierceness settled into something older and quieter, something that had waited four years for a door to knock on. "Thank you isn't the word. There isn't a word. So I'll say this instead: whatever happens — raids, informers, the rising everyone smells coming — you will not carry this alone again. That's not gratitude. That's a threat."

I believed her completely. I walked home through the sleeping quarter lighter than I had walked in eleven years, and if I smiled at the waystation sentry and he found it unsettling, that was his misfortune.

Three days later, a cart of bindery canvas arrived at the orchard wall, and over it climbed Pip, then Sefa, then Bram, who took one look at the apple rows he had been raiding all autumn and informed his guardian, loudly, that he had always suspected this place was important.
