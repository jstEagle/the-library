## 10. What the Census Counts

People assume a census taker counts houses. They imagine me trudging door to door with a leather ledger, ticking off chimneys and goats and souls, dull as a rain barrel. And I let them imagine it. Dullness is a fine coat. It has pockets.

The truth is that a census taker counts *people* — and the Directorate trusts no one else to do it, because counting people is the first act of owning them. Every name I write into the official rolls becomes a fact the state can retrieve, tax, conscript, or erase. I learned early what it means when a name stops being retrievable. I was nine years old, and the name was my brother's.

But we will come to that. First, the orchard, because everything now comes back to the orchard, the way all rivers come back to the sea whether they intend to or not.

It was evening, one of those grey Vellham evenings where the river breathes fog up over the walls and the whole city sounds like it's speaking through wool. I sat on an upturned crate between the apple rows with my true ledger open on my knees — not the Directorate's ledger, mine, the other one, the one that would get me hanged if any clerk with clean cuffs ever read it — and I watched Wendell Winterbourne across the clearing, setting type by lantern light with hands that moved like they'd been doing it since before they had opinions.

Maren Quill was there too, mending the spine of a testimony book with thread pulled from her own hem, and Pip was supposed to be keeping watch at the wall and was instead asleep in the crook of Sefa's arm, and Bram was asking me, in the relentless way of eight-year-olds, why I always wrote so small.

"So it fits," I said.

"Fits what?"

"Everything."

He considered this and found it insufficient, which is the correct response to most of my answers. "But if you write small, doesn't that mean there's too much?"

I looked down at the page. Forty-one names so far. Forty-one people who had walked out of their doors in Vellham and never walked back in, whose families had been told nothing, fined sometimes for the asking, and whose names had been struck from the rolls with the same administrative tenderness one uses to strike a dead horse from the tax books.

"Yes," I told Bram. "There's too much. There's always too much. That's rather the point of me."

He went off satisfied, having extracted more honesty from me in four questions than the Provost's Office had managed in six years of employment. Children are excellent interrogators. They haven't yet learned which questions are impolite, and politeness is where the regime lives.

That night, after the lanterns were shuttered and the others had gone, I stayed a while among the trees with my coat collar up, and I did what I do perhaps once a season: I let myself remember properly. Not in fragments, not sideways, but whole. It costs something, remembering whole. But a man who builds an archive out of guilt ought to at least know his own entry by heart.

* * *

My brother's name was Tomas Ash, and he was twenty-two years old the autumn it happened, which made him eleven years older than me and approximately a hundred feet taller. Everyone's brother is a hundred feet tall at nine. Mine actually had been, once — he'd carried me on his shoulders through the Lamplight Fair until I could see clear over the crowd to the puppet show, and I had believed for years afterward that this was simply what older brothers were for: elevation.

Tomas was a compositor. An apprentice at Hallow & Vane, two streets over from the Winterbourne shop, though I didn't know then that I would one day come to know that shop better than my own childhood home. He came home smelling of ink and lamp oil and once, memorably, of a printer's devil's worth of gunpowder, which he swore was an accident involving a pipe and I chose to believe because believing him was easier than being afraid.

The protest — they called it a riot afterward, riots being cheaper to prosecute than grievances — was about bread. It is almost always about bread, whatever banner they raise. The millers' cartel had fixed prices three harvests running, and the Dock Ward had decided, with the simple arithmetic of empty bellies, that the granary warehouses owed the city an explanation. Four hundred people marched on the Granary Row on a Tuesday evening in October. My brother marched because his fellow apprentices marched, and because he was twenty-two, and because at twenty-two you believe that showing up is the same as mattering.

They were wrong about the soldiers' orders. That was the whole tragedy in a sentence. The garrison commander had instructions to disperse, and someone further up the chain — a name that appears, I now understand, in certain testimonies I have set in lead with my own hands — had quietly amended them to *disperse by any means*. Eleven dead. My brother was the fourth body carried out, though the official return listed seven, because two of the dead were children and children made the paperwork awkward.

Here is what I remember, in the order my memory insists on it, though none of it is orderly:

The sound. Not the shots — I wasn't there, thank whatever gods keep accounts — but the sound of our mother when the neighbor woman came to the door. A sound like a door being taken off its hinges from the inside.

The shoes. Someone brought Tomas's shoes home before they brought Tomas. I don't know who. I have spent thirty years not knowing who, and lately, in the orchard, writing down the names of people who wait years for a single fact about their vanished sons and daughters, I have understood that whoever it was thought they were being kind, delivering something, anything, ahead of the unbearable thing. Kindness arrives in terrible packaging.

And then, six weeks later, the paper. Not a letter — a form. A printed slip from the Office of Returns, addressed to my mother in that beautiful, indifferent copperplate the Directorate favors:

*Mrs. Ash: Be advised that upon review, the household record of Ash, Tallow Lane, has been amended. The dependent previously listed as ASH, TOMAS (b. 1791) has been struck pursuant to Ordinance 44. No further action is required.*

No further action is required.

I have set type. I know how much care goes into a well-made line — the spacing, the leading, the way a good compositor worries a sentence like a bone. Somewhere, some clerk or under-printer set that notice with genuine craft. *No further action is required.* Six words, kerned to perfection, informing a grieving woman that her son had been retroactively unmade. Not killed — killed would have required a verdict, an inquiry, a name on a martyrs' list, however short that list was permitted to run. Struck. As though he had been an error in the ledger. As though the error were hers.

My mother kept the slip. She kept it in the biscuit tin with the birth records, and I used to think she kept it out of grief. I was wrong. Years later, when she was dying and I was the one sorting the tin, I found that she had written on the back of it, in pencil, small: *They think a line through a name is a line through a man. Keep it to prove them fools.*

She was a laundress with cracked knuckles and no schooling past twelve, and she had understood in one sentence the entire theory of what I have spent my life doing. The Directorate does not merely kill. Killing leaves evidence. It *amends*. It maintains the fiction of a continuous, accurate record — a city in which nothing has ever gone wrong, because anything that goes wrong is edited out — and it does so with print, with type, with the very art that could just as easily be used to tell the truth. The press built the erasure. I intended, with whatever years I was given, to make the press build the repair.

* * *

You will want to know about the guilt. People who build monuments to their dead are assumed to be grieving cleanly, and I have never done anything cleanly in my life.

Here is the guilt: the night of the march, Tomas stopped at our door. I remember it exactly — I was supposed to be asleep, and I was hanging halfway over the banister the way children do, and he was pulling on his coat while our mother stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded, saying, *Don't be a fool, Tom, there'll be another way,* and he laughed — I can still hear it, that easy upward laugh, a boy laughing at the idea that anything could go badly for him, because nothing ever had — and he said, "Somebody has to count us, Mam, or they'll say nobody came."

*Somebody has to count us.*

I was nine years old, and I heard it, and it went into me like a splinter that works itself deeper every year instead of out. Because he said it as a joke, as a young man's throwaway line, and because the universe — or the Office of Returns, which may be the same thing — took it literally. Somebody counted. The count came back: nobody came. Nobody marched, nobody died, nobody named Tomas Ash had ever drawn bread from that table or ink from that shop or breath from that October air.

And I became, at twenty-six, after six years of clerking and two years of scraping through the civil examinations with handwriting bland enough to pass, a census taker. The lowest office in the Directorate's bureaucracy. Men laughed at it. My examiner wrote *adequate; unimaginative* on my papers, which remains the finest review I have ever received, because unimaginative is precisely what a man must appear to be when he intends treason.

They gave me a route. They gave me a ledger with pre-printed columns and a seal and a salary of forty crowns a year, and they sent me door to door through the very districts where the disappeared had lived, and every household I entered was a small museum of absence. A chair turned to face the wall. A coat still on its peg after two winters. Women who answered my standard questions — names, ages, trades, dependents of irregular standing — in voices worn smooth as river stones, and who watched my pen with the eyes of people who had learned that pens do damage.

For the first months I was only a coward with a salary. I wrote what the forms demanded and I left, and I hated myself in the specific, efficient manner of a man who knows exactly what he is failing to do.

Then came Mrs. Aldery, the cooper's widow — you will meet her testimony later, in Wendell's careful leaden type, and I hope whoever digs these pages up reads it twice. She was on my route, a grey house on Ropewalk with barrels stacked like fortifications, and when I asked the question about persons absent from the household she didn't cry and she didn't turn me out. She looked at my ledger for a long moment and said, "You're the third census man they've sent. The other two wrote 'no irregularities.' Will you?"

And I said — God help me, I don't know where it came from, except that everything comes from somewhere and mine comes from a boy hanging over a banister — I said, "Madam, if you tell me your son's full name and the date he was last seen, I will write it somewhere they cannot strike it."

She stared at me. Then she went inside and came back with a bundle of letters tied in butcher's string, and she talked for two hours, and I filled six pages in my second ledger — the true one, bought with my own money, unruled, unnumbered, unsealed. Her son Jory, taken from the cooperage at dawn, no warrant shown, no charge filed, eighteen months gone.

I did it again the next week. And the week after. It grew on me the way lichen grows on a wall — slowly, silently, and with a grip you cannot pull free without taking the wall with it.

* * *

Which brings me back around, the long way, to the orchard and to Wendell Winterbourne, because a secret archive is a useless thing unless it can be made permanent, and permanence, in this century, is manufactured out of lead.

When I confronted Wendell behind the old foundry wall — and I use the word *confronted* generously, since what I actually did was corner a terrified, magnificent liar among crabapple suckers and lay my knowledge on the ground between us like cards — I understood something immediately that it took Wendell longer to believe. We were not blackmailing each other. We were two halves of the same machine. Wendell had the craft, the type, the press access, the daily camouflage of respectability. I had the names, the testimony, the route, the cover of the dullest office in the Directorate. Alone, each of us was a candle cupped in a fist. Together we were — well. We were getting there. There was a bindery to recruit, and apprentices to corrupt into courier service, and a great deal of fear to be metabolized on all sides. But the shape of it was right, and both of us knew it before either of us said it aloud.

What I did not tell Wendell, that first day among the trees, and have not fully told anyone since, is why the work has the particular flavor it has for me. Why I write the names small. Why I check each spelling twice against the family's own lips rather than the registry, because the registry lies. Why I cannot pass a biscuit tin in a market stall without my chest going tight.

Bram asked me once — children again, they go straight through the armor — why I cared so much about people I never met. I told him about counting. I said, "Everyone gets counted, little man. The only question is who holds the pencil." He nodded very seriously and then asked if I'd ever counted a ghost, which is a question I'm still answering, and will be answering for the rest of my life, one page at a time.

Because here is the engine of it, laid bare, as the plan of this account requires: I take the census now for the dead. I am the counter my brother asked for. Somebody has to count us, he said, laughing, on his way out the door — and the Directorate counted, and struck, and amended, and required no further action. So I act. Further. Daily. In small handwriting, in stolen hours, in an orchard that smells of rotting apples and gun oil, I perform the only census that cannot be falsified by ordinance: the one taken witness by witness, name by name, grief by grief, and set in metal by the finest hands I have ever watched work.

Forty-one names. Mrs. Aldery's Jory among them. And tonight, walking home along the wall with the fog coming up off the river and my satchel heavy with the day's honest-dishonest labor, I added a forty-second — a student, vanished from the university registry, whose sister had waited three years to say his name aloud to anyone with a pen.

Forty-two. The number should have comforted me. Each name was a small victory over Ordinance 44 and everyone who ever wielded it.

Instead, standing at the mouth of Tanner's Lane, I noticed the clerk. Grey coat, Directorate-issue boots, pretending to read a playbill with the absorbed intensity of a man who cannot read. He had been at the far end of Quay Street an hour ago. Now he was here. Now he tipped his hat to me, pleasant as Sunday, and strolled away into the fog.

I stood in the dark a long moment, doing the arithmetic men in my position learn to do in their sleep: what I carried, whom it would burn, how fast I could walk to the orchard before the lamplighters finished their rounds.

Then I turned, and I walked — unhurried, adequate, unimaginative — toward the trees, and toward telling Wendell that our counting had finally been noticed.
