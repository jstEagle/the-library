## 11. Apprentices of the Orchard

There is a particular kind of silence a child makes when they are trying very hard to be useful and have no idea how. I had heard it for years in my bindery — three small bodies arranging themselves around my worktable like cutlery around an empty plate — and I heard it again the morning Pip stood in front of me with his chin up and announced that he was going to be a soldier.

"You're eleven," I said.

"Soldiers are younger than that in Vellham."

"Soldiers are *dead* younger than that in Vellham, which is a different thing entirely." I set down my bone folder. "What's brought this on?"

He shifted his weight. Pip never could stand still; he was built of leftover sparks. "I heard you and Master Winterbourne talking behind the shop. About messages. And I can run faster than anyone."

"And Sefa can climb better than anyone," said Sefa from the doorway, where she had been eavesdropping with the shameless thoroughness of her age. She was thirteen and had the eyes of a customs inspector. "And Bram can fit through things nobody else fits through."

Bram, who was seven and had been quietly eating an apple in the corner, held up the apple as if presenting credentials.

I looked at the three of them and felt the specific dread of a woman who has spent nine years keeping children alive by keeping them *small* — small voices, small footsteps, small ambitions — watching that strategy walk out the door wearing its best coat.

"You were listening," I said.

"We always listen," Sefa said. "You taught us to."

That was true, God help me. In the bindery, listening is a trade skill: you learn to hear the difference between paper that will fold clean and paper that will crack, between glue that's ready and glue that's past it, between the street sounds of an ordinary afternoon and the street sounds of trouble coming up the lane. I'd trained them without meaning to. A woman teaches what she knows even when she doesn't know she knows it.

"Pip," I said carefully, "what exactly did you hear?"

"That there are papers that need moving. That they can't go through the streets the normal way. That Wendell—" he stumbled on the name; we all still stumbled on it, though not for the reason he thought, "—that Master Winterbourne is frightened of something."

"Wendell," I corrected, mostly out of habit.

"Wendell is frightened of something," Pip repeated solemnly, and then, because he was Pip, "and I'm not."

* * *

Here is what I had learned about fear since the Rising began brewing in earnest, since bread lines turned into bread riots and printers' windows started getting chalked with marks I didn't want to read too closely: fear is not the enemy. Fear is a craftsman's tool. Used properly it keeps your fingers attached to your hands. The trick is not to let it do the *designing*.

So instead of saying no, which would have sent all three of them straight over the wall into worse ideas, I did what any sensible bookbinder does with valuable material that keeps trying to tear itself: I changed the format.

"Sit," I said, and they sat, Bram still chewing, which made him look like a squirrel attending a funeral. "If you're going to be part of this — and I haven't agreed yet, so don't celebrate — then you'll be part of it properly. Not soldiers. Soldiers get shot. You'll be couriers, and couriers have rules, and the first rule is that a courier who gets caught knows nothing."

"I don't know anything anyway," Bram offered.

"Bram, love, that is the most encouraging thing you've ever said to me."

Sefa leaned forward. "Messages where? To who?"

I weighed it. There is a moment in every conspiracy when you decide how much truth the young can carry, and everyone decides differently. My mother decided none, and I grew up a fool until I was twenty. So I gave them the shape of it without the contents.

"There are people in this city whose names have been taken off things," I said. "Off registers, off rolls, off gravestones that should exist and don't. Some folk are writing those names back down. The pages need to travel. They need to travel in ways that look like nothing at all."

"Like book deliveries," Sefa said immediately.

I confess I felt a flicker of pride so fierce it was nearly painful. "Like book deliveries."

* * *

We drilled for two weeks before anything real moved through their hands, and I make no apology for the drilling. If you think children can't learn tradecraft, you've never watched a child learn to lie about being somewhere they shouldn't. It comes to them naturally; the difficulty is teaching them to lie *badly*, on purpose, with the right details, so the lie looks finished rather than polished. A child caught carrying contraband who says "nothing, sir, nothing at all" while sweating like a cheese in summer is a child under suspicion. A child caught carrying contraband who complains bitterly about the price of tallow candles and the state of the cobbles and her sister's snoring is a child who has already been forgotten.

Sefa invented half the techniques herself, which I intend to tell her someday when it won't make her insufferable. She worked out that a girl carrying books is invisible if the books are boring — ledgers, hymnals, a primer with water damage — and that the same girl carrying one interesting book might as well be ringing a bell. She worked out that checkpoints search hands and satchels but rarely bother with the inside of a coat lining if the coat is patched, because patched coats read as poor, and poor reads as harmless, and harmlessness is the whole architecture of our disguise. She worked out that if a guard stops you, you stop *first*, eagerly, before he can decide to, because a person who volunteers is a person with nothing to hide, in the arithmetic of men who count things for a living.

Pip's gift was different. Pip could look at a street and tell you who didn't belong in it. Not by clothes — clothes can be borrowed — but by *attention*. Everyone in a street is looking at something: a window, a sweetheart, a pie stall, their own boots. A man watching people instead of things is either lonely or working, and Pip had a genius for telling the difference. He'd perch on the bindery steps with a broom he never actually used and catalogue the quarter like a little gray clerk of the ordinary, and once a week he'd hand me a list of faces that had appeared twice where they should have appeared once.

The third time his list matched something Silas had noticed independently, Silas looked at the boy for a long moment and then at me, and said, "Maren. Where did you *get* these children?"

"The same place everyone gets children," I said. "They arrived and refused to leave."

* * *

The orchard changed them, is the thing I keep circling back to. I'd been hauling paper out there myself for a month by then, and I knew what the place did to adults — Wendell went loose-shouldered the moment the wall closed behind us, like a corset unlaced; even Silas, that long gray ruler of a man, walked softer among the trees. But I hadn't reckoned on what it did to the young.

City children live vertically. Stairs, rooftops, ladder-backs, the vertical world of walls and windows. The orchard handed them horizontal ground and told them it was theirs, and they expanded into it like dough proving. Pip claimed the crooked apple tree by the south wall as a lookout post within a day and defended the claim against all comers, including me, including gravity, which eventually took a payment of skin off his elbow and taught him nothing whatsoever. Sefa mapped the place — actually mapped it, scratched onto a slate — marking which wall stones were loose, which drain grate lifted, which tree had a hollow big enough for a package the size of a psalter. Bram appointed himself keeper of the root cellar, which meant he sat at the top of its steps and narrated to whoever passed below, a duty of his own invention that nobody had the heart to revoke.

They stopped asking whether they could come. They simply appeared, the way weather appears, and after a while the meetings adjusted themselves around them the way a river adjusts around stones.

Wendell worried. I'll say that plainly, because Wendell's worrying is quiet and constant and easy to miss under all that composed surface — worried the way a compositor worries about a line of type that's a hair too tight. One evening, watching Sefa shin up the old tree with a wrapped bundle of printed pages tucked in her shirt like a second heartbeat, Wendell said to me, low, "How do you bear it? Sending them out?"

"How do you bear sending the pages?" I said. "It's the same question."

"It isn't. Pages don't bleed."

"No," I agreed. "But they can burn, and everything they carry burns with them. I've made my peace with the arithmetic. Three children who know how to move quietly and lie well and believe they're *for* something — against three children who grow up learning that the safe thing is to see nothing and say nothing and let other people's mothers disappear unrecorded." I watched Bram solemnly hand Silas an apple he had definitely stolen from the wrong tree. "I know which three I'd rather raise. The danger's real. But so is the alternative, and nobody ever died of the alternative, they just died of it slowly, inside, where nobody counts."

Wendell was silent a moment. Then, wry as ever, in that voice that does the work of ten sentences with two words: "You should write pamphlets."

"I bind them. It's more honest work."

* * *

I want to record, because this chapter of my life deserves its small glories alongside its dangers, the Great Cabbage Incident.

Silas had established a signal system — innocuous enough: a chalk mark on the third fencepost of Copper Lane meant *meeting tonight*, a mark on the bindery shutter meant *come to the orchard, bring nothing*. What he hadn't established was who maintained the marks, and one wet Tuesday the mark went up on Copper Lane and stayed up four days because Silas had been called away on census business and nobody erased it. By day four the neighborhood had opinions. Mrs. Halloway from the chandlery was convinced it was smugglers. Two boys from the ropewalk were convinced it was a treasure sign and dug up a respectable portion of the verge looking for it. And Pip, arriving to erase the mark, found an elderly man standing over it with a notebook, taking notes.

What Pip did — and I got this account from three sources, each more embellished than the last, so I'll give you the least impossible version — was walk directly up to the man and say, "Grandad! You said you'd wait by the chandler's!" and then berate him loudly, in front of witnesses, for wandering off and confusing himself again, poor lamb, he does this since his fall, and steer him away down the lane while apologizing to the general air for old men who mean well and remember nothing.

By the time the man thought to wonder why a stranger's grandchild was manhandling him toward the fish market, Pip had delivered him to the fish market, vanished into the crowd, doubled back, and erased the chalk.

The elderly man, it turned out, was a retired excise officer with a hobby of noting oddities, and his notebook entry for that Tuesday reads — Silas obtained a copy months later, at some risk and considerable expense — *"Chalk mark, origin unknown. Local boy claims relation. No further significance."*

No further significance. Four words that have done more work protecting us than any lock or bolt in Vellham.

When I heard the story I laughed until I had to sit down among the apple rows, and then I stopped laughing quite suddenly, because underneath the comedy of it was the cold fact that my eleven-year-old had lied fluently and beautifully to a former officer of the excise, alone, without rehearsal, and I was proud of him, and what did that make me?

It made me a woman raising children for a world I wasn't sure I wanted them to have. But it also, I decided, made me a woman raising children who might *build* a better one, and I have made peace with less defensible positions than that.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the way bad news does — all at once, after promising otherwise. The orchard turned rust-colored and bare, the trees holding their branches up like arms surrendering, and the meetings moved closer to the root cellar's mouth, where a brazier of poor coal threw light that we rationed as strictly as the coal itself.

It was on one of those nights, huddled with the smell of apples rotting sweetly underfoot and the distant sound of the city doing its anxious nighttime muttering, that I understood what we had actually built out here. Not a network, though it was that. Not a courier system, though Pip's routes ran cleaner every week, and Sefa's dead-drop in the drainage culvert by the tannery wall had yet to fail, and even Bram had graduated from narrator to actual carrier of things too small to matter and therefore perfect for carrying — a pencil stub here, a scrap of paper rolled tight as a wick there.

What we'd built was a household with a conspiracy attached, or possibly the reverse; by then I genuinely couldn't tell which.

Sefa was teaching Bram letters by scratching them in the dirt with a stick, using the archive's own pages as her copybook — *A is for Aldous, who kept the toll bridge; B is for Brenna, who mended sails.* The disappeared, teaching the living to spell. Pip had talked Wendell into letting him sort broken type, and came home — came to the bindery, I mean; I'm careful with that word now — came home speaking of em quads and ligatures like a man discussing old friends. And Wendell, that careful, armored creature, had begun to laugh. Actually laugh, short and surprised, as if the sound had escaped custody.

I sat there on an upturned crate, warming my hands over coals worth less than the tin they burned in, and let myself feel it — the warmth, the ridiculous, dangerous warmth — threading through the danger like gold wire through dark leather. This is what I know about binding: the strongest seams aren't the ones where nothing presses against them. They're the ones stitched through strain, doubled, waxed, pulled tight against the very forces meant to tear them apart.

I looked at my strange congregation — the counterfeited census-taker, the counterfeit master printer, three orphans pretending to be nobody in particular, and me, a widow with strong hands and a growing criminal portfolio — and I thought: whatever comes for us, it will find the seam holds.

I was almost right about that.

Because it was the following week, on a bright cold morning with frost on the bindery shutters, that a knock came at the door and I opened it to a government inspector with a clipboard and a smile like a filed-down tooth, informing me that all premises in the print district were subject to loyalty inventory, beginning Thursday, and that the Winterbourne establishment was first on his list.

Thursday. Which gave us two days, and one very full font case, and a printer across town who needed warning before the net came down on all of us at once.
