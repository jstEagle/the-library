## 12. The Loyalty Inventory

The knock came at seven in the morning, which told me everything before I opened the door.

No honest visitor calls at seven in the morning. Honest visitors want something — a wedding invitation cut in copperplate, a funeral broadside, a hundred handbills for a lost dog with an expression of profound moral grievance. They come at civilized hours and they bring their wants wrapped in apology. A knock at seven is a man who has been sent, not a man who has come.

I put down my coffee, checked myself in the glass by the door — Master Wendell Winterbourne, proprietor, grey at the temples, hands of a craftsman, face of a man with nothing to declare — and let them in.

There were three of them. The front one wore the slate-grey coat of the Provost's Office and the smile of a man who had learned it from a manual. Behind him stood two clerks, one carrying a leather ledger and the other carrying nothing at all, which frightened me more than any instrument could have. An empty-handed clerk is a clerk whose hands are kept free for other work.

"Master Winterbourne." The grey coat produced a folded paper with a flourish that suggested he'd rehearsed it on the walk over. "A loyalty inventory. By order of the Provost's Office. All printing establishments within the walls are to be surveyed for seditious stock."

"Of course," I said, and stepped aside, because there was nothing else to say and a dozen worse things not to say. "Will you take coffee?"

He blinked. I have found that courtesy disorients these men faster than obstruction ever could. Obstruction they are trained for; it confirms their suspicions and justifies their salaries. But a man who offers them coffee while they rummage through his life gives them nothing to push against.

"I don't— yes," he said. "Thank you."

"Black or with milk?"

"With milk."

I poured three cups with steady hands. I want to be honest about those hands, because later — when the moment came, and it did come, as it always comes — I would need to remember that steadiness was a thing I had practiced, not a thing I possessed. My father used to say a compositor's hands lie for him better than his mouth ever will. He meant that a man who can set type all day without a tremor can hold a cup under any circumstances whatsoever. He never imagined how thoroughly I would test the theory.

* * *

They began in the front room, among the display faces, and I will confess that for the first quarter hour I was almost bored. The grey coat — whose name, according to the ledger clerk's murmured address, was Inspector Voss — walked the shelves running his finger along the font cases like a bishop inspecting pews. He asked questions designed to sound dangerous: how long had I held this establishment, who were my regular customers, had I printed anything for the strike committees on Tanner's Row.

"Handbills for a funeral society," I said. "Twice. Funeral societies pay promptly, which in this district makes them nearly unique."

Voss smiled his manual smile. "And what do you print for yourself?"

"Bills of lading. Invoices. Last month, four hundred invitations to the Weavers' Benevolent Midwinter Supper." I gestured toward the wastepaper bin, where I had indeed placed a few spoiled copies, misprints with a crooked rule and one where the word *Benevolent* had been set as *Beloved*, which Pip had declared an improvement. "You're welcome to read every one."

He didn't, quite. That's the thing about inspectors — they want to find something, but they also want to be finished by lunch, and these impulses war within them constantly. Voss wanted to find sedition in my shop the way a fisherman wants to catch a fish: sincerely, but not so much that he'd stay out past dark.

But the second clerk — the empty-handed one — was another animal entirely. He hadn't spoken since entering. He moved through the shop slowly, touching nothing, reading everything. He read the specimen book page by page. He read the framed apprenticeship certificates on the wall, including the one bearing my father's name, dead eleven years now and still vouching for me, which is more than most fathers get to do. He stood for a long time before the composing stone where I'd left yesterday's work locked up in the chase: a job lot of trade labels for a vinegar merchant, forty-eight to the sheet, the merchant's name in fat bracketed serifs around a picture of an urn that looked less like an urn than like a very dignified pickle jar.

I watched him watch it and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear itself but from knowing precisely what he might find if he looked one shelf lower.

Because here is what he might find. On the third shelf of the case by the window — the old casement, the one with the warped door that sticks unless you lift the latch *and* press the wood, a defect I had complained about to no one for years — sat a font case of broken sorts. Every print shop has one. Sorts crack in the casting, letters come back from jobs with their shoulders knocked off, faces wear down until an *e* prints like a smudge with ambitions. You keep them because lead is lead and you never know when you'll need half a letter. Mine held perhaps thirty pounds of ruined type: mangled capitals, clipped numerals, sorts so battered they were barely more than slugs of metal with opinions.

And beneath them, laid flat in rows like corpses under floorboards, the archive plates.

Eleven weeks of Silas's testimonies, cast in stereotype plates so the pages could be reprinted whole: the cooper's wife describing her husband's last morning; the student vanished from the registry, set in the same size as the vinegar labels; the ferryman; the schoolmistress; the boy from the tannery whose only crime had been standing where a bullet needed somewhere to go. Fourteen names. Fifteen. Sixteen. I had stopped counting after twenty because counting made it real in a way that setting the type somehow didn't, and I needed, some mornings, to pretend I was only doing craft.

If Voss lifted that warped door, pressed the wood, slid out the case, and tipped it, he would hear the shift of metal and see, between the broken sorts, the clean edges of plates that had no business existing in a shop that printed pickles and supper invitations.

So I did what any sensible counterfeiter of respectability does. I made him tea-adjacent conversation and I prayed, in the flat commercial way of men who haven't prayed properly since childhood, that the latch would stick.

* * *

It took them an hour to reach the back room, and I spent that hour learning things about fear I had never wanted to know.

Fear, it turns out, is not one sensation but a committee. There is the cold sweat, which arrives without consultation. There is the voice in the ear that says *you should have moved the plates last night, you fool, you had all night* — which is true and useless, because moving them last night would have meant carrying them through streets where the Provost's men were already stopping carts, and the voice in the ear never offers alternatives, only verdicts. There is the strange hyperclarity, where the grain of the table wood becomes fascinating and the inspector's left boot acquires a scuff you will remember for the rest of your life. And underneath it all, humming like a press at speed, the arithmetic: sixteen names, one family, three children across town who call Maren *auntie* and think the orchard is a game.

I answered questions. I showed papers — my license, my guild card, my tax receipts, the small bureaucratic reliquary by which a citizen proves he is furniture rather than a man. Voss leafed through them with the reverence of a man handling relics he privately suspected were bones of a horse.

"You knew Aldous Renwick," he said suddenly, tapping a receipt. "The binder on Cordwain Lane."

"He bound for me. Ledgers, account books. Good work."

"He was taken in the spring. Irregular associations." Voss watched me over the paper. "Did he seem irregular to you?"

"He seemed punctual," I said. "He charged fairly and returned borrowed tools. In my experience irregularity shows elsewhere."

Silence. The empty-handed clerk, in the doorway behind us, wrote something in a small notebook he had apparently produced from nowhere, and I understood that I had just been recorded, though whether as loyal, evasive, or amusingly insolent remained to be determined.

Then they went into the back room, and the arithmetic got louder.

* * *

Here is a thing about the back room of Winterbourne & Son — which is called Winterbourne & Son because repainting the sign costs money and because my father, who had no son, thought the ampersand gave the place dignity — it is where the old cases live. The cases nobody uses daily. The 14-point Egyptian nobody orders anymore, the long primer with the chipped *Q*, and the casement by the window with the sticking latch, containing its congregation of broken sorts, keeping its secret the way a deaf aunt keeps family scandal: quietly, and in full view of everyone.

Voss went to the shelves first. The clerks followed. And I stood in the doorway holding my coffee cup like a man attending a wake for his own composure, and I ran through options the way a compositor runs through a galley looking for the right piece.

Option one: speak. Say *not that case, it's rubbish, broken sorts, you'll waste your time.* Which is exactly what a guilty man says, and both clerks would write it down.

Option two: silence, and trust the latch.

Option three — and this arrived unbidden, the way the best and worst ideas always do — option three was to give them something else to find.

"Gentlemen," I said, as Voss's hand drifted along the shelf toward the casement, "if you're going to open cases, mind the third one down on your left. The lock's sprung and the sorts are loose. If you spill them we'll spend the afternoon sorting, and neither of us wants that."

Two lies braided together: that I minded, and that the afternoon mattered more than whatever they might find. It worked the way such things usually work, which is imperfectly. Voss paused. Looked at me. Looked at the case I'd pointed to — the innocent one, the Egyptian — and then, deliberately, turned back to the casement by the window, because I had taught him in one sentence that the cases mattered to me.

That's the trap of protecting everything: you reveal that something needs protecting.

He lifted the latch. He pressed the wood. The door stuck.

For a moment — I could measure it in heartbeats, and I did, three of them, each arriving like a debt collector — nothing happened. Voss pulled again, frowning, and the door stayed shut, and inside that warped wooden box sixteen people who had been erased from every official record in Vellham continued to exist in lead, unknown, unreported, alive in the only way left to them.

"Sticks," Voss said.

"It does that. Lift and pull."

"Lift and pull," he repeated, in the tone of a man noting that even the furniture in this shop had something to hide.

He lifted and pulled. The door came open with a groan that I felt in my teeth. And then — God help me, and I mean that with none of the irony I usually carry — he reached in and began, casually, idly, the way a man tests a pocketful of coins, to stir the broken sorts with two fingers.

Metal whispered against metal. The sound of it went through me like a file.

"Rubbish," said the empty-handed clerk, from over Voss's shoulder.

"Mostly," Voss agreed. His fingers moved deeper. I watched the tendons in his wrist and calculated, with horrible clarity, the exact depth at which fingertip would meet plate edge. Sixteen names. One family. Bram's gap-toothed grin when he'd carried his first message and demanded payment in apples.

And then the bell above the front door rang.

Not dramatically — bells don't understand drama — but with the ordinary jangle of commerce, and a voice came through from the front room, high and aggrieved and blessed:

"Master Wendell! Master Wendell, the vinegar labels, you said today, and Mrs. Ostrevant will have my head if—" Pip. Eleven years old, freckled, incapable of entering a room at less than a run, skidding to a halt at the sight of three grey coats. To his eternal credit, he only froze for a heartbeat. Then his eyes went wide and round with the purest terror, and I realized he thought he'd walked in on my arrest, and that he was prepared, at eleven, to lie or run or bite whichever came first.

"Ah," said Voss, turning, fingers still in the case. "An apprentice?"

"My errand boy," I said. "Pip. The vinegar merchant's daughter sent him. Pip — the gentlemen are conducting an inventory. Wait outside. Tell Mrs. Ostrevant her labels will be ready tomorrow."

"But you said—"

"*Tomorrow,*" I said, in the voice I saved for the boy and for anyone who touched my type without asking, and Pip fled, and I heard his boots hammering up Cordwain Lane, and I thought: *run, love, run to Maren, tell her the grey coats are here, count the minutes.*

Because that was the true purpose of my interruption, whether heaven intended it or the boy simply blundered into glory: every minute Voss spent deciding whether to chase an errand boy was a minute his fingers weren't in my case.

He didn't chase. He looked at the open door instead, annoyed, distracted, a man whose morning was getting long. He stirred the sorts once more — once, twice, his knuckles grazing the case's inner wall, a finger-width, perhaps two, from the topmost plate — and then withdrew his hand, wiped it on his handkerchief, and stepped back.

"Broken sorts," he announced to the ledger clerk, as though pronouncing on a disease. "Note it. Font case, damaged material, consistent with ordinary wear." A pause. He looked at me, and for a moment the manual smile dropped away and something older and tireder looked out. "You keep a tidy shop, Master Winterbourne. Tidier than most. Tidiness can be its own sort of statement."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"Don't." He closed the casement door himself, and I noted — filed away, engraved — that he did not press the wood, and the latch caught wrong, and the door did not fully seat. From across the room it looked shut. From across the room, everything looks shut. "Sign here. The inventory states you were cooperative and that no seditious stock was discovered."

*I discovered it for you,* I did not say. I signed. My hand did not shake. My father would have been proud; my father, who never knew what his shop would become, what his sign would shelter, what his name — *Winterbourne & Son*, ampersand and all — would come to mean.

They left at ten minutes past nine. Voss first, then the ledger clerk, then the empty-handed one, who paused in the doorway and looked back at me for a long, unreadable moment, and touched his hat brim with one finger, and went.

I stood in my own shop listening to their footsteps fade, and then I crossed the room on legs that had waited, patiently, through the entire performance, and I lifted the latch and pressed the wood and opened the case, and the plates lay untouched, cool under my fingertips, sixteen lives sleeping in lead.

Then I sat down on the composing stool and shook.

I shook for a full minute, hands, arms, jaw, the whole disgraceful ensemble, alone in the smell of ink and oil, and I am not ashamed of it. Fear deferred is still fear paid; I had simply arranged installments. When the shaking spent itself, I cleaned my spectacles, straightened my waistcoat, and did the only sensible thing a craftsman can do after surviving an inspection.

I went to the casement, took out the plates, and began building a better hiding place — because there would be a next time, and the next inspector might not be interrupted by an errand boy, and might not be satisfied with broken sorts.

And because I am who I am, I hid them well, and then I sat down at the stone and set, in spare moments between customers, the newest testimony Silas had brought me the night before — a cooper's widow, a date, a gate, a graveless name — and I printed it clean.

That evening, a scrap of paper reached me by ways I won't describe, in handwriting I'd know anywhere, all angles and economy:

*They visited me too. Books accounted for. Are you intact? The orchard, tonight. Bring the new pages. — S.*

And beneath it, smaller, added in different ink, in a rounder, younger hand that had clearly gotten hold of the note before Silas could stop it:

*Maren says well done. Pip says he's sorry he ran. Bram drew you a picture of an apple. Come safe. — all of us*

I read it three times by candlelight, and then I banked the fire, and gathered the pages, and went out into the cold to count my family.
