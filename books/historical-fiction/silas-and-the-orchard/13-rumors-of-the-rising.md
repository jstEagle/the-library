## 13. Rumors of the Rising

The trouble with counting a city, I have learned, is that eventually you count it accurately.

I had been a census taker for eleven years before I understood this. The Bureau trained us to see Vellham as columns: households per ward, souls per household, dependents of irregular standing per soul. Columns are comforting. Columns do not look at you. But a census is only arithmetic wearing a coat of officialdom, and underneath the coat it is just one man walking from door to door asking questions, and if that man has eyes — and mine, whatever their other failings, still function — he begins to notice what the columns are built to hide.

By the second week of Frostmonth, I could not stop noticing.

I noticed it first in the bread queues. A queue is a wonderful thing to count. You can stand at the end of one with your ledger open and appear to be doing the Bureau's work while actually doing your own arithmetic: how many women, how many children, how long since anyone in this line had eaten meat. The queue outside Hobb's bakery on Cordwainer Street stretched two hundred and forty souls by seven in the morning, which was forty more than the week prior, and the baker's boy had chalked SOLD OUT on his slate before the sun cleared the granary roofs. Two hundred and forty people stood looking at a slate. That is not a queue anymore. That is a lesson.

And then there was the flour dust. The Bureau does not teach you about flour dust. It hangs in the air of the milling quarter like weather, and when the wind came off the river that morning it carried it across into the print district, where every surface — sills, shutters, the hats of respectable men — wore a fine grey bloom, as though the whole city were aging fifty years in a single season.

"You'll want to log the mill closures," said Doss, my fellow taker, falling into step beside me on the bridge. Doss is the sort of man who believes everything can be logged, including grief, given sufficient categories. "Three mills dark on the east bank. Provost's office says mechanical fault."

"Mechanical fault," I repeated.

"Faults of mechanics," Doss said comfortably, "that occur whenever workers discuss wages." He chuckled at his own joke, then saw my face, and stopped chuckling. Silas Ash laughing at sedition: even Doss knows the Bureau watches its own. "You're in a mood."

"I'm in a ledger," I said. "There's a difference."

* * *

Here is what my ledger — my true ledger, the one that lived wrapped in oilcloth beneath the floorboard of my room, the one whose pages Wendell Winterbourne was setting in lead a few streets away — here is what it had been telling me all autumn, if I'd had the wit to read it.

A city does not rise the way a loaf rises, evenly, all at once. It rises the way bread dough does in a cold room: slowly, invisibly, from a hundred separate pockets of warmth, until one morning the cloth over the bowl is stretched taut and something underneath is pushing up against it, and everyone acts surprised.

The strike committees had gone from five to nineteen since Harvestmonth. I knew because widows kept mentioning them in their testimonies — not because they were political, most of them, but because a vanished husband's union card was sometimes the last thing he'd touched that wasn't his own skin. The cooper's wife had held her husband's card the way other women hold letters from the dead. The committees met in ropewalks and chapel basements and the back room of the Anchor and Chain, and they passed resolutions nobody read, and they collected dues in copper, and they believed, with the touching faith of men who have never seen a committee win anything, that they were preparing for something.

The broadsheets told the rest. Underground presses had woken up all over Vellham — I could track them by their errors. The press behind the Anchor printed its *g*s upside down; the chapel press ran out of ink halfway through every fourth page; a third, working somewhere near the tanneries, set its type so tightly that the ink filled the counters and every *e* was a black square. Three presses, minimum, maybe five. A year ago there had been one, and it had lasted six weeks before the Provost's men found it in a chandler's cellar along with the chandler.

The chandler's name was in my ledger now. Row 41. I had interviewed his sister in September. She had talked for two hours and cried for none of them, which frightened me more than weeping would have.

So: hunger, organization, and ink. Any student of history could tell you what those three make when you bake them together. The trouble was that I was no longer merely a student of history. I was, in however small and leaden a way, an ingredient.

* * *

I went to the orchard on Thursday, ostensibly to deliver testimony — the widow Anselm's account of her son, taken off a ferry two years ago, never registered anywhere, never existing officially enough to have been missed. Officially I was also checking on "the property," since the derelict orchard behind the old Winterbourne foundry fell within my enumeration rounds and no one at the Bureau cared enough to verify that I walked through dead trees instead of knocking on doors.

Wendell was at the worktable under the leaning wall, setting type by the grey daylight that came through the broken roof. They did not look up when I came in. They never looked up right away; I think it was a discipline, letting the hands finish the line before letting the face react. I had watched that discipline enough times now to find it beautiful, though I would have bitten my tongue off before saying so.

"Anselm," I said. "Fourteen pages. The ferry crossing is corroborated by a ticket clerk who's since left for Corvane."

"Corroborated how?"

"The clerk kept the manifest copy. Ferries log every passenger. Except ones the Harbor Office removes."

Wendell's hands paused above the case. "They remove passengers from manifests."

"They remove the row," I said. "Not the passenger. The passenger goes in the river or a wagon or a cell in the Saltgate. The row goes nowhere. That's the elegance of it. You can't miss someone who was never counted."

"That," said Wendell quietly, "is why you started the ledger."

"It's why I started it. It isn't why I'm telling you the rest."

Now they looked up. Grey eyes, ink to the second knuckle, that expression of wary attention I had learned to read as Wendell's version of alarm. I laid it out for them the way I'd been turning it over all week, walking the wards with my official ledger under my arm and my unofficial dread under everything else.

"Bread riots on Cordwainer Tuesday night. Not a riot yet — a crowd that shoved a grain cart over and stood around looking astonished at themselves. Strike committees coordinating across wards for the first time; the ropewalk men sent delegates to the mill men. And the presses. Five underground presses running, maybe more. Every one of them printing the same thing: lists. Names of the taken. The regime made these people disappear, and now the opposition wants to make them reappear, as martyrs, as banners, as proof of grievance." I took a breath. "Our archive is a list of names of the taken, Wendell. Four hundred names, nearly finished, each one testified, each one true. If the rising comes — and it's coming, the whole city smells it the way you smell rain — then our archive stops being a memorial. It becomes evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"Of whatever the winner wants it to be evidence of. That's the nature of archives. They don't contain meaning; they contain material. The Provost finds it, it's proof of a seditious network — testimony gathered illegally, distributed by an unlicensed press, implicating dozens of witnesses who will hang alongside us. The rebels find it, it's propaganda — four hundred grievances to be waved at rallies, names shouted until they mean nothing but noise, witnesses dragged before tribunals to perform their grief on schedule." I heard my own voice going flat and bureaucratic, which is what it does when I am most afraid. "Either way, the archive gets seized. Either way, the dead get used. Either way, the living get destroyed. I spent three years building the one honest record in Vellham and I've built a weapon that both armies will want to pick up."

Silence, except for the wind moving through the dead apple rows, and somewhere beyond the wall the hammers of the ropewalk, which had not stopped striking all month.

Wendell set down the composing stick with great care, the way they did everything, as if the world were an object that might bruise.

"You've thought about burning it," they said.

It was not a question. I hated them a little for it. "Every night this week."

"And?"

"And I can't. Can you?"

"No." Wendell picked the stick back up, almost absently, thumb finding the groove worn into the wood. "But I keep arriving at the same problem you have. It isn't enough to hide it better. Hiding works against the Provost. It doesn't work against the mob, or a tribunal, or a committee with a warrant and righteousness. Anything findable is seizable, and anything seizable will be seized, eventually, by somebody. We've been building a vault. What we need—" they stopped, and I watched the thought arrive in their face like weather, "—is a grave."

"A grave."

"Something nobody digs up because nobody wants what's in it. Or doesn't know it exists. Or—" Wendell frowned at the type case. "I don't know yet. Something that isn't a hiding place. There's a difference between hiding a thing and burying it. Hiding assumes the search continues forever. Burying assumes it ends, and plans for after."

"After what?"

"After whoever wins finishes being certain they're right."

We looked at each other across the worktable, two conspirators in a dead orchard, and I felt the full strange weight of what we had become: a census taker who falsified his returns and a printer who falsified their life, custodians of four hundred names that belonged to no faction, no party, no side — which meant they belonged to no protection either. Truth, I was beginning to understand, is the only commodity in a civil conflict that both sides confiscate.

"There's something else," I said, because it needed saying and because saying frightening things aloud is the only tax I know how to pay. "If the rising comes while the archive is still here, still active — witnesses coming, pages printing — then we're not archivists anymore, we're combatants. Every testimony we take after the barricades go up could be read as recruitment. Every page we print could be read as ammunition. Maren's apprentices run messages for us. Pip is twelve. If any of this unravels in public—"

"Don't." One word, quiet, and it landed harder than shouting would have. Wendell's hand had closed around the composing stick hard enough that I could see the tendons. "Don't enumerate the ways my people can be hurt. I do that myself, nightly, free of charge."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." They exhaled, set the stick down, and looked out through the broken roof at the pale sky. "Bram asked me yesterday what a magistrate was. He'd heard the word from Sefa, who heard it from a dockhand. I gave him the nursery answer — a judge, a lawman — and he said, 'Do they count people?' And I didn't know what he meant, and then I did. Somebody's been telling children that the disappearances happen because someone is counting them wrong." Wendell's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Children are inventing folklore about your profession, Silas. Congratulations."

"My profession deserves worse."

"Your profession is about to get interesting." They turned back to the type case, and the discipline returned, hands steady, voice level. "Anselm's pages. Give them here. If the world means to come apart, it can do it after we've set this line properly. I refuse to let fourteen pages of a dead boy's truth go into the world with a bad justification."

I handed over the manuscript. Our fingers did not touch, and I noted that I had noted this, which is the sort of thing I try not to note.

* * *

I walked back through the print district as the lamps were lit, and the city told me the rest of what I already knew.

A crowd had gathered outside the granary offices — not rioting, not yet, just standing, two hundred strong, silent, which is infinitely worse than shouting because silence is a decision. Someone had pasted a broadsheet to the granary door: THE PROVOST COUNTS GRAIN. WHO COUNTS THE HUNGRY? The *g*s were upside down. The Anchor press. I stood in the lamplight reading it and understood that I was reading my own indictment, or my own epitaph, depending on the fortnight.

Because here was the thing I had finally counted correctly, the sum at the bottom of the column: we were no longer spectators with a secret. The rising would come — days, weeks, a month at the outside — and when it came, it would come for records. Revolutions are, among their other qualities, audits. The old regime's first act had been to seize the guild rolls and the parish registers; I had read enough history to know the new power's first act would be the same, whichever new power it was. Every paper in Vellham would change hands. Every cellar, every floorboard, every buried box would be fair spoil.

And somewhere in that general confiscation, if we did nothing, would go four hundred names — seized as evidence, burned as sedition, or shouted into slogans. The dead conscripted posthumously into somebody's army. Everything I had promised the cooper's wife, everything I had promised myself at my brother's grave: betrayed not by cruelty but by timing.

Unless we became unseizable. Unless the archive could be made into a thing that waits rather than a thing that hides — buried deep, deniable, patient, planted like seed against a spring none of us might see.

I did not yet know how. But standing in the flour-dusted lamplight, watching hungry people read about themselves in upside-down letters, I understood that we had perhaps one month, possibly less, to solve the only problem that mattered:

How do you bury the truth so thoroughly that even victory cannot dig it up — and so carefully that someday, kinder hands might?
