## 14. A Testimony Too Far

The knock came at the hour when honest people are asleep and I was doing neither, which is how most of my life has been arranged.

I knew before I opened the door that it was trouble, because nobody knocks at that hour for pleasant reasons. Pleasant reasons keep daylight hours. Trouble keeps no hours at all; trouble is a printer's best customer, always wanting something set in lead by morning.

I put out the candle first. Old habit. Then I stood in the dark of my own shop like a fool listening to my own heart conduct its indecent percussion, and asked, "Who's there?"

"A friend of the orchard," said a voice, low and cracked as old binding glue. "Maren sent me. She said you'd leave the bolt off."

Maren had not told me she'd leave the bolt off. Maren had simply decided it, informed me after the fact with one of her looks — the ones that could set type without hands — and here I was, living inside her decision, unbolting my door at midnight because she had looked at me in a particular way three days ago. This is what comes of letting other people into your life. They start using it.

I let him in.

* * *

He was a small man, the kind the world steps on without noticing it has done so, with ink-stained cuffs that told me he'd been a clerk of some kind and hands that shook in a way that told me he'd been one badly, lately, under strain. He would not give his name. He sat where I pointed him, in the chair by the cold stove, and he held a oilcloth parcel against his chest the way a man holds a child, or a bomb.

"You're the compositor," he said. It wasn't quite a question. "Maren Quill says you're the best in Vellham. Says you can set a page blind drunk and no proofreader alive would catch it."

"Maren Quill flatters me when she wants something."

"She wants this." He patted the parcel. "And I want it printed. And I'm told those two wants meet at your door."

"Nothing meets at my door at midnight except regret." I lit a lamp anyway, low, turned to the wall. Curiosity has always been my worst vice, worse even than pride, and pride has kept me alive through some very stupid years. "Show me."

He unwrapped the oilcloth like a man defusing something, which, in fairness, he was.

Inside were papers. Not many — six sheets, written close in a clerk's hand, plus a folded document that made my stomach drop before I'd read a word of it, because I recognized the paper stock. Heavy rag, watermarked with the Provost's seal. That was requisition stock. That was paper that did not leave government offices except in files, or in the pockets of men who took things they shouldn't have.

"This came out of the Hall of Records," I said.

"It came out of a fire," he corrected. "Three years ago they burned a crate of correspondence. Routine destruction, they called it. I was detailed to feed the fire. I fed it everything they gave me." His eyes came up and met mine, and there was something underneath all that fear, something banked and patient, like coals under ash. "Everything they gave me, Master Winterbourne. But I can read upside down faster than most men can burn right side up."

I looked down at the sheets. Testimony — his own testimony, sworn to himself, dated, signed with a name I will not set down even now, though my hands know every letter of it. And beneath it, the requisition orders. Transport manifests. Dates. Numbers of prisoners moved from the Hall cells to the river garrisons, and then — nothing. No arrival records. The numbers went into the manifest and did not come out again anywhere, and between the going-in and the not-coming-out sat a single signature authorizing each transfer.

I knew the signature. Everyone in Vellham knows the signature. It appears on civic proclamations, on festival programs, on the dedication plaque of the new public baths that everyone agrees are very fine.

Magistrate Corvane Aldous.

"Say nothing," the little man said, watching my face. "I've watched four faces read these pages. Yours is the fifth. The others —" he paused — "the others looked away from the signature first. You looked at it last. Like you wanted to be sure."

"I wanted to be sure," I agreed, "that I was about to become a dead man."

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about Corvane Aldous, since apparently I know it better than most.

I have set his name perhaps two hundred times. Festival proclamations. The address to the Guild of Compositors, which he delivered in person and which I set as a broadsheet, and which contained the sentence "A city that keeps faithful records keeps faith with its citizens" — I set that sentence, letter by letter, and if irony could kill, I would have died at my own composing stone three years ago and been spared all this. He is beloved. He is the reasonable face of the regime, the one they send to funerals and ribbon-cuttings, the one whose portrait makes him look like a kindly uncle rather than what he is, whatever precisely that is, which is the question these six sheets answer in a clerk's careful hand.

The disappeared of Vellham — Silas counts them. That is his whole vocation and his wound. He has names, dates, last-known-addresses, testimony gathered widow by widow in rooms that smell of grief and cheap tallow. What he has never had, not once in all our nights among the apple trees, is proof. Pattern, yes. Probability, certainly. A magistrate's name spoken in whispers by people too frightened to say it above a whisper, endlessly.

What we have never had is paper with the Provost's own watermark bearing the man's actual signature on orders that account for forty-one human beings who got on a barge and were never seen again.

Forty-one. I counted twice. I am a compositor; counting is my religion. Forty-one names' worth of transfers, authorized, signed, sealed, and burned — except that fires only destroy what you put entirely into them, and one clerk's hands had been quicker than his conscience, or slower than it, I've never decided which.

"They'll hang us," I said. "Not arrest. Arrest is for sedition, and sedition is survivable if you recant. This is different. This isn't criticizing the regime. This is naming a sitting magistrate as a murderer in his own hand. They won't bother with trial. They'll burn the print shop with us in it and call it an accident, and the coroner's report will be more truthful than anything else they publish that year."

"Yes," said the little man, with terrible calm.

"And knowing that, you walked to my door."

"Where else?" He spread his ruined hands. "I tried the underground presses — the strike committees, the students. They want it. Oh, they want it. They want to run ten thousand copies and nail them to the Hall doors and light the city on fire with them. And they're right to want that, and if I give it to them, every copy gets seized within a week, the magistrate declares martial law, and the archive dies in a bonfire with everything else. Maren understands this. She says —" he recited it, so I knew he'd heard it more than once — "she says a truth shouted once and silenced proves nothing. A truth set in lead and hidden survives to accuse. So print it small. Print it few. Print it to *last*."

That was Maren all over. Maren, who thinks in decades the way other people think in sentences.

"There's a third option," I said. "We don't print it at all. We bury the papers somewhere and wait. Regimes fall. This one will too, someday; they all do. When it falls, the papers speak for themselves. Nobody hangs tonight."

"And until it falls?" He leaned forward. "How long? Ten years? Twenty? I'm fifty-three years old, Master Winterbourne, and the widows are older than me, and every year we wait is another year those forty-one are officially nobody. Officially, they never existed. Their names aren't in any record the state keeps. When their mothers die, the last people who remember them die, and then Corvane Aldous's signature sits in whatever vault we hide it in accusing a dead man of nothing at all." He tapped the manifesto. "You print this, and somewhere — someday — someone reads it while he's still alive to blush. Or while his sons are alive to be ashamed. That's not nothing. Shame outlives the shameless. It's the only thing that does."

I looked at the pages a long time.

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, not Silas, not Maren, not the trees themselves: part of me wanted to refuse. Not from fear — well, substantially from fear, I am not a liar even in my own head — but also from something meaner and more honest, which is this: printing that page changes everything. Everything we have built in the orchard has been survivable so far. Testimonies of the vanished, accounts of erasure — tragic, illegal, dangerous, but deniable. We are archivists, not assassins. The regime can almost afford not to notice us, because grief is not treason and memory is not a weapon unless someone picks it up.

This page is someone picking it up. The moment that signature goes into lead, we stop being mourners and become accusers, and accusers get killed on schedule, and worse — everyone near them gets killed on schedule too. Maren's apprentices. Bram, who is nine years old and thinks our secret meetings are a grand adventure. Pip. Sefa. Every name in Silas's ledger becomes a name on someone's list, because we will have shown them exactly what we are and exactly what we hold.

And yet.

I thought of the cooper's wife, from the first testimonies — chapter and verse of my own private calendar now, the way other people mark saints' days. I thought of her hands around the cup she never drank from. She didn't want vengeance; she wanted her husband *counted*. She wanted the record to say he existed. And the record can say he existed, and the record can say who erased him, and those are not actually two different statements, are they? That's the trap. That's the fulcrum, and I was standing on it, and both sides of it were made of the same wood: a page with names on it.

If we print the testimonies and omit the signature, we are safe and we are liars by omission. If we print the signature, we are honest and possibly brief. If we print nothing —

If we print nothing, then why did we start?

* * *

The little man left before dawn, the parcel staying behind, wrapped again in its oilcloth and sitting on my workbench like a sleeping animal I was afraid to touch. I watched him go down the lane toward the river fog and I thought: he has been carrying this for three years. Three years of feeding fires and memorizing manifests and waiting for someone brave enough or foolish enough to set it in metal. And now it was in my shop, and by morning it would need to be somewhere safer than my shop, and the question of whether it ever became lead at all belonged — I realized with a sensation like a stair missing in the dark — not to me alone.

Because that was the covenant. That was the handshake among the apple trees. I cut the type; Silas judged the words; Maren bound the pages and guarded the gate; the children carried and watched. None of us had ever put it in exactly those terms, but the shape of it was true: this thing we were building had stopped being mine somewhere back along the way, and I could no longer make a decision this size alone without committing a kind of theft against the others.

So I did what I always do when I cannot face a thing directly. I worked.

I cleared the broken-sort case — the font of damaged letters we keep for hiding plates, the one that saved us during the loyalty inventory — and I did not hide anything in it, just touched each compartment like a man checking locks. I sorted type. I cleaned the press bed that didn't need cleaning. And around four in the morning I caught myself doing the unforgivable thing: standing at the composing stone with the magistrate's name half-set in my hand, testing the fit of it, seeing how the letters fell.

C-O-R-V-A-N-E. It sets easily. That's the obscenity of it. No name is harder to set than another; lead doesn't care what it spells. Forty-one disappearances and one signature, and the type takes it all with the same dumb willingness, and the only weight involved is the weight I bring to it.

I broke the line apart. Not yet. Not alone.

At dawn I went to the orchard. The frost had come early that week and the apple trees were going gold at the edges, dropping fruit nobody would harvest, sweet rot in the grass. Silas was already there — he is always already there, as if he sleeps standing up in the rows — with his ledger open on his knee and that look on his face, the census-taker's look, the one that counts everything including me.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"Neither did you."

"I slept." Which from Silas means he sat still with his eyes closed for an hour. "Something happened."

I set the oilcloth parcel on the tree stump between us, and I watched him unwrap it, and I watched the census taker of Vellham — the man who has spent years recording the disappeared name by name, alone, unpaid, unheard — read the signature that answered the question he has carried since his brother's name was struck from the rolls.

His hands did not shake. That frightened me more than shaking would have. He read it through twice, closed the folder, and was quiet for so long that a wren came down and inspected us both and left, disappointed.

"Corvane Aldous," Silas said at last, tasting it. Saying aloud the name we had only ever breathed. "Do you know how many times I've written 'a high official, unnamed' in that ledger? Unnamed. As if naming were the one power I didn't have." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Wendell. If we print this—"

"If we print this, they hunt us to the last child. If we don't, we've lied to every widow who trusted us. I've been turning it over all night and it won't come right either way, and I am done pretending it's my decision alone." I nodded toward the lanes beyond the wall, where Maren's bindery smoke would soon rise, where Pip and Sefa and Bram would come trotting with their satchels and their lookout routes and their terrible, necessary trust in us. "Call them. All of them. Tonight, among the trees."

Silas stood, tucking the parcel inside his coat, against his heart, where the little man had carried it.

"Bram will get a vote?" he asked quietly.

"Bram will get a vote. He carries what we make; he should help choose it."

Silas nodded slowly, and for a moment he looked less like a man holding dynamite and more like a man finally setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine.

"He'll say print it," Silas said.

"I know," I said. "That's what frightens me. Children still believe names have power. At some point we have to decide whether they're right — or whether that's just one more lie we haven't grown out of yet."

By evening the whole orchard would be full of us, and the parcel would sit on the stump, and I would have to say out loud the thing I had rehearsed all night at the stone: that I could set the page, that I would set it if they chose it, and that whichever way they chose, none of us would ever be only archivists again.

I heard the bell of the bindery across the quarter marking the hour, and beneath it, faint, the river barges loading at the garrison docks, and I thought of forty-one people on a barge, unnamed in every official book, waiting — however absurdly, however long — to be counted.
