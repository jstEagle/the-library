## 15. The Vote Among the Trees

I've bound books for thirty years, and I'll tell you what nobody tells you about binding: it is the art of holding a thing together past the point where it wants to fall apart. Glue, thread, board, and stubbornness. A book is just a rebellion against entropy with a cover on it. So when I say I knew how that night in the orchard would go before anyone said a word — five people and three children standing among apple trees gone wild as orphans themselves, arguing over whether to put a magistrate's name in lead — understand that I knew because binding is my trade. Things held together past the breaking point. That's all we were. That's all we ever were.

It was the third Thursday of October, if you care, and cold enough that our breath hung in the rows like small ghosts looking for their trees. Silas had brought the testimony at dusk, carried flat against his chest under his coat the way he carried everything that mattered, which is to say like a man smuggling contraband into his own heart.

Her name was Ilse Vann. She'd been a laundress before her hands gave out, and she was the widow of nothing — her husband alive, thank the saints — but her brother had been a customs clerk who asked one question too many about a shipment of confiscated Bibles, and he'd been taken from his own doorstep on a Tuesday while the bread was still rising on his sill. Ilse had waited four years. And then she'd done something no one else had dared: she'd gone to the house of Magistrate Corven Hale himself, taken a position as a scrubwoman through a cousin's recommendation, and spent eleven months emptying his chambers' slops and listening at doors until she could recite what she'd heard — dates, orders, the names of men paid to make other names vanish. Including her brother. Including, by her telling, fourteen others whose families still lit candles in windows that never got answered.

She'd written it all down in a hand gone shaky, on paper she'd stolen from the magistrate's own desk, which was either poetry or suicide, and given it to Silas in the market with a bunch of turnip greens wrapped around it.

And now it lay on an upturned crate between us, weighted down with a stone so the wind wouldn't take it, and none of us would look at it directly, the way you don't look at a debt collector.

* * *

Wendell spoke first, which surprised me. Wendell usually let silence do the heavy lifting and stepped in only when the silence started asking for money.

"You understand what this page is," Wendell said. Not to Silas particularly. To all of us, in that level foundry voice that made even the trees stand straighter. "A name in the registry is an administrative error. A name in print, naming a sitting magistrate as the author of disappearances — that is not a record anymore. That is an accusation. If they catch us with it, they will not hang us for sedition. Sedition they can forgive; it's almost fashionable. They will hang us for *insolence*. There's no statute for insolence, so they'll invent one, and we'll be its first entry."

"Then we should be flattered," said Pip. Pip was twelve and had opinions the way the river has water, constantly and without much regard for obstacles. "First entry in something. Better than last."

"Pip," I said, because someone has to be Maren in these situations, though privately I thought the boy had the shape of it right.

Wendell went on as if the child hadn't spoken, which was a kindness, since acknowledging Pip generally encouraged him. "There's another consideration. It isn't only our necks. Every name already in the archive — the cooper's wife's testimony, the student from the registry, all forty-one pages of them — those are safe *because* they are deniable. Grief makes accusations; everyone knows grief lies. But a documented chain of orders, witnessed inside the magistrate's own household? That converts the whole archive. Suddenly we're not keeping a memorial. We're keeping evidence. Evidence gets seized. Memorials just get burned, and burned is survivable." Wendell's hands were doing their work while Wendell talked, turning over a stray piece of type that lived in Wendell's pocket the way other people carry worry-stones. "I can set anything. That's never been the question. The question is whether setting this one thing destroys everything else we're carrying."

Silas stood apart, near the wall, arms crossed, wearing the face he wore at doorsteps when households lied to him. Patient. Terribly patient. The patience of a man counting.

"Ilse Vann waited eleven months," Silas said finally. "She listened to that man eat dinner. She heard him give the order for her brother in the same tone he used to complain about the wine. She wrote it down knowing that if she was caught writing it, she'd follow her brother, and no one would write *her* name anywhere at all. And then she handed it to a stranger in a vegetable market because I was the only person she'd ever met who wrote things down and kept them." He looked around at us. "So the question isn't whether printing it endangers the archive. The question is what the archive is *for*. If it's a memorial, Wendell's right. Keep it clean, keep it deniable, bury it soft. If it's a record — if it's meant to actually say what happened here, someday, to somebody — then it's incomplete without him. You can't record the crime and omit the criminal. That's not an archive. That's embroidery."

"It might be both," Sefa said.

We all turned. Sefa didn't talk often — she was thirteen going on forty and had learned early that words cost — but when she did, the rest of us had learned to shut up and listen, mostly because she was usually right and always annoyed about it.

"It might be a memorial *and* evidence," she said, picking at a loose thread on her sleeve. "The dead don't need the magistrate's name. They're dead. The living need it. The ones who come after need it. Otherwise whoever finds this archive fifty years from now reads it and thinks, how terrible, people simply *vanished*, weather happened, fate happened. No. He happened. Put him in it or don't call it true."

Well. There it was, from a girl who couldn't legally inherit a spoon.

* * *

Bram had fallen asleep against my side somewhere around the second argument, the way small children do at exactly the moments adults most wish they wouldn't, and I'd shifted him onto my knee without thinking, because that's the kind of thing your body does after two years of an orphan sleeping in your bindery loft. He woke during Sefa's speech — children wake for arguments the way dogs wake for thunder — and instead of doing the sensible thing and going back to sleep, he sat up, rubbed his eyes, and listened.

I want to be honest about my own position, since history may read this. I wanted to burn it. Not because I'm careful — ask anyone in the binders' guild, I once took a commission for a heretical commentary on scripture because the pay was good and the censor was stupid — but because I've buried enough people. My husband. Two apprentices taken in the cholera year. I know what names weigh. I know that a page with a dangerous name on it doesn't just endanger the person who printed it; it endangers every hand that touches it afterward. Couriers. Binders. Children carrying hymnals through checkpoints, though I didn't know yet how literally that would come to pass.

But I also knew Ilse Vann. Not personally — I knew her *type*. I'd bound account books for women like her, widows and sisters and mothers who came to the guild hall with ledgers of debts nobody would honor, and watched them carry those books home again unopened because there was no one to open them to. Eleven months in that man's house, breathing his air, waiting. You don't spend eleven months like that to have your testimony filed under *deniable grief*.

So I sat on my hands, figuratively, and let the argument run, because I've learned that a committee needs to hear itself break before it can hear itself decide.

Wendell laid out the case for caution with the precision of a man setting a difficult line of type — each argument kerned tight against the next. The provosts were already canvassing printers. The loyalty inventories had started. Two underground presses in the Tanners' Row had been seized that month, their operators "detained pending review," which everyone understood was the regime's way of saying *disappeared* without dirtying the word. Adding the magistrate's name now, at the worst possible moment, was like pouring oil on a fire you were trying to carry across a wooden bridge.

"And yet," Silas said, "the moment will never improve. That's the nature of regimes like this one. There is no good season for truth. You wait for the thaw and you get the flood; you wait for the flood to pass and you get the frost. Ilse gave it to me now because now is when she had it. We print it now because now is when we can."

"The rebels might win," Pip offered. "Everyone says the Rising's coming. Then the magistrate hangs anyway and we didn't need to risk it."

"And if the rebels win," Silas said, "they'll hang the magistrate and write a history in which they were always righteous and the dead died conveniently. You think the new masters want a document proving that ordinary clerks and washwomen did the work the committees claim for themselves?" He shook his head. "Revolutions forgive their enemies faster than their witnesses. I've seen it. Everyone who's lived through one has seen it."

That silenced even Pip, which I noted as historically significant.

Wendell stood very still for a long time, looking at the crate, at the stone, at the paper underneath it that none of us had touched. I watched Wendell's hands, because I've known Wendell Winterbourne for two years now and the hands tell the truth before the mouth does. They were steady. Steadier than they had any business being. Whatever storm was going on behind that composed foundry face, the hands had already voted: *give me the type, I'll do the work.* Craftsmen are like that. We argue with our heads and then our hands betray us into honesty.

"If we print it," Wendell said slowly, "we print all of it. Not excerpts, not the safe parts. Dates, sums, the payments to the men who did the taking. If Corven Hale goes into this archive, he goes in complete. A half-truth about a man like that is worse than silence — it gives the apologists a seam to pick at. Whole cloth or nothing."

"Whole cloth," Silas agreed. "Every word she wrote. I'll verify the dates against the registry rolls tonight."

"It adds six pages," Wendell said. "Six pages of the most dangerous composition I've ever done. And I want it understood—" and here Wendell's voice changed, dropped into something I hadn't heard before, quieter and harder, like metal cooled too fast, "—that I'm not agreeing because I'm persuaded by the argument. I'm persuaded by the woman. Anyone who spends eleven months in the lion's den counting his sins deserves a scribe. That's the whole of my reasoning. Let no one in this orchard mistake me for brave."

"Nobody would," said Pip.

"Pip," said Sefa.

"What? Nobody would. Master Wendell hides in a font case when the inspector comes. I've seen it."

"That was tactical positioning," Wendell said with dignity, and even Silas cracked, just at the corner of his mouth, which for Silas constituted open mirth.

* * *

But it wasn't decided yet. That's the thing about our little family that outsiders never understood — we didn't decide things the way committees decide, by weight and weariness, until the loudest voice wins. Maren's rule, I told them, though really it was the guild's rule first: a vote means everyone votes. Everyone.

Sefa voted yes, obviously; she'd made her argument and stood by it with her chin up. Silas voted yes, having been born voting yes on precisely this question, if his brother's struck-from-the-rolls ghost had anything to say about it. Pip voted yes twice, and was informed that enthusiasm did not constitute a second ballot.

I voted yes. I'll admit I surprised myself. I said, "Ilse Vann emptied that man's chamber pots for eleven months. The least I can do is sew her pages into a book that outlives us all," and I felt the rightness of it land in my chest like a stitch pulled tight.

Wendell voted yes — had already voted yes with those steady hands — and then looked down at Bram, who was sitting on my knee with his legs dangling, following the grown-ups' faces back and forth like a spectator at a tennis match he only half understood.

"Bram," Wendell said. "You're part of this household. You get a word too."

Now, Bram was seven. Bram's previous contributions to household governance included deciding that the bindery cat should be named Turnip and that Thursdays were superior to other days. I opened my mouth to wave him off — the vote was already four to nothing, the matter settled — but Silas caught my eye and shook his head, barely. *Let him.* Because Silas, I think, understood something the rest of us were slower about: that we weren't just deciding a page. We were deciding what these children would believe about the adults who raised them. Whether we were people who weighed danger and chose comfort with excellent justifications, or people who weighed danger and chose the dead.

Bram thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything, forehead furrowed, one thumb hooked in his mouth and then removed on grounds of dignity. He looked at the crate. He looked at Silas.

"Is it the man who makes people gone?" he asked.

"Yes," Silas said quietly.

Bram nodded, satisfied, as if this confirmed something he'd long suspected about magistrates. And then he said it — plain as bread, plain as a boy saying grace, no drama in him at all, which is why it went through all of us like a needle through cloth:

"Print it. Names are all they have left."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere out beyond the wall a night bird was making its idiot complaint, and the wind moved through the wild rows, and I felt Bram's small warm weight on my knee and understood that I had just heard the truest sentence spoken in that orchard in its entire neglected life.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the entire terrible arithmetic of it. The dead had nothing — not graves, most of them, not trials, not last words, not even the dignity of being missed on official paper. Their households had been struck off, their rents reassigned, their coats sold. Everything portable about a human being had been confiscated except the sequence of letters that had spelled them, and we held the only trade in the city that could make those letters heavy and permanent. Lead doesn't burn easy. Ask any founder.

"All right," Wendell said at last, and the word came out roughened, like type that had been handled too long. "All right. Six pages. Whole cloth. We start tomorrow night — no, tonight. If we've decided, we decide all the way." Wendell picked up the stone, lifted the papers from the crate, and held them a moment, weighing them, though I suspect it wasn't the paper's weight being measured. "Master Ash. Your verification of the dates?"

"By midnight," Silas said.

"Composing begins after. Bram — you're lookout at the fence line until moonset, then straight to bed."

"Yes, Master Wendell." Bram slid off my knee, suddenly very important, and marched toward the wall with the gravity of a general reviewing troops, and Pip trailed after him loudly offering unsolicited strategic advice.

I gathered the lantern and the cold-end of the evening's bread and stood a moment watching Wendell and Silas bend together over Ilse Vann's shaky hand in the lantern light, two heads close, reading the dead aloud to each other in whispers. And I thought: *well, Maren Quill, look what you've joined.* Not a conspiracy — conspiracies have meetings and passwords and men in hats. This was more like a bindery. Thread, board, glue, and stubbornness, holding a thing together past every reasonable breaking point.

The wind came through the rows again, and the oldest tree at the orchard's heart creaked like a ship, and far off across the river, faint as a lie, the city bells began to ring the hour — nine strokes, then, oddly, a tenth, ragged, out of order, which shouldn't happen, which had never happened, which I chose that night not to think about.

I should have thought about it. By morning, the whole quarter would be talking about nothing else.
