## 16. Setting the Dangerous Page

There is a superstition among compositors that the type remembers your hands. Set a page in fear and it will hold the fear; set it in haste and it will crumble at the press like a lie told quickly. My father believed it. He used to say you could read a man's soul in his justification — whether he spaced his words evenly or squeezed them together like a coward cramming for a door.

I had never entirely believed him. That night, I did.

The magistrate's name sat in my case like a coal. Aldous Verrick. Eleven letters, if you counted the one nobody ever said aloud — *Provost's Own Justice*, they called him, though justice had nothing to do with what the testimony described. I had set ten thousand names in twenty years of living as a Winterbourne. Names of brides on wedding invitations. Names of the dead on memorial cards. Names of aldermen on ballots that were never really ballots. Every one of them had been a kind of armor: harmless lead, respectable work, a shop so dull with legitimacy that no inspector ever looked twice.

This name was not armor. This name was a knife, and I was about to forge it letter by letter, and every letter would be evidence.

* * *

Maren had left an hour past midnight, taking Pip home across the wall with Sefa walking point and Bram half-asleep against her shoulder. She had paused at the orchard gate and looked back at me the way she does when she is deciding whether to say something fierce or merely think it.

"You'll want candles," she'd said finally. "Two. One for the case and one for your face, so you can see yourself lying to yourself. It helps."

"That's not how candlelight works."

"Neither is courage," she said, "but we make do," and she was gone down the dark row with her boy on her hip like a sack of flour that occasionally asked questions.

So there were two candles. And there was Silas, sitting where I'd told him not to sit — on my father's stool, at my father's stone bench, with the original testimony open before him in its careful disguised hand. We had copied it out three times in different scripts, because a document that could be traced to a witness was a document that got witnesses killed.

"Read me the line again," I said.

"Wendell, I've read it four times."

"And I'll ask a fifth, because the fifth time you won't flinch, and I need to know which reading is the true one." I held out my hand without looking up from the case. "The line. Please."

He sighed — that particular Silas sigh, the census-taker's sigh, patient as paperwork — and read: "*On the morning of the ninth of March, Magistrate Aldous Verrick did personally order the removal of eleven persons from the holding cells beneath the Custom House, and did instruct the guards thusly:*"

"Stop."

"*—and did instruct the guards*—"

"No, stop, I mean I have it." I pulled the sorts. A-l-d-o-u-s. Then V-e-r-r-i-c-k. The letters came to my fingers the way letters always do, without thought, without mercy. Twenty years of muscle memory serving a purpose my muscles had never signed on for.

Here is something no one tells you about setting type. You do it upside down. Backwards. Every character reversed, mirror-true, so that when the paper meets the ink the world comes out right-side round. Compositors learn to read the world inverted until inversion feels native. I had sometimes thought it was a metaphor for my whole existence — everything backwards in the case, forward only under pressure.

Tonight the metaphor was less comforting than usual.

* * *

Silas watched me work for a long while. He has this habit of watching that should be intrusive and isn't, mostly because he watches everything the same way — the way a man counts coins he doesn't own. Finally he said, "Your hands are shaking."

"My hands are *cold*. It's October."

"It's not cold enough in here to shake a compositor's hands. You've told me the shop stays warm off the stove because warm metal keeps its measure."

Damn the man and his notebooks. Everything anyone ever said near Silas Ash went into some internal ledger, cross-indexed, available upon request, subpoena-proof. I flexed my fingers and lied with dignity.

"I'm thinking about kerning."

"You're thinking about the scaffold."

"I am *kerning*," I said, and set the word *eleven* with perhaps more force than eleven deserved.

The truth — since this chapter of my life appears to be devoted to truths I once swore I'd never tell — is that I kept seeing the gallows behind my eyes, and worse than the gallows, I kept seeing the shop after me. Empty. Or worse than empty: occupied. Some loyal functionary sweeping out the font cases, melting my father's matrices down for cannon fittings, hanging a new sign over the door that read something dutiful and dead. The Vellham regime didn't just kill people; it killed *shops*, it killed trades, it killed the little continuities by which a city knows itself. Confiscation first, then erasure. I had spent fifteen years being Master Wendell precisely so that the foundry would go on having a master.

And now I was going to hand them a reason. One page. Four hundred impressions. A name in lead that any fool could trace back to the only foundry in the print district with a hand press small enough to hide in a root cellar.

"There's still time to reconsider," Silas said quietly. He says it every time. He is constitutionally incapable of coercing anyone, which makes him the most dangerous blackmailer alive, because he simply stands there radiating permission until you coerce yourself.

"The vote was taken under an apple tree by a nine-year-old," I said. "The matter is settled by law."

"Bram would take it back if you asked him to."

"Bram," I said, selecting a period with great care, "is the only person in this city who understands anything. Print it. Names are all they have left. I'm not going to explain to a child that we agreed with him right up until it cost something."

Silas was quiet. Then, because he is Silas: "That's the most honest thing you've said all year, and you've said almost nothing honest all year."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly lies, but multitudes."

* * *

Here is what the page required, technically speaking, and technicalities are the only place I have ever felt safe.

It was to be the fourth leaf of the archive's second signature — sixteen pages, gathered and sewn, unbound, untrimmed, meant to be buried rather than sold. No title page. No printer's device. No imprint, colophon, or flourish that a bookbinder's eye could read as provenance. Maren had drilled this into us: *a book that can't testify to its own making can't testify against yours.* But a page still has a voice even when it has no name. Paper stock speaks. Ink density speaks. The width of the margins, the height of the chase, the very rhythm of the justification — these are fingerprints. Any competent examiner in the Provost's office knew the printers' quarter the way I know my own cases, and if they laid our leaf beside a sheet from the Winterbourne foundry—

Well. They would find the fingerprints matched.

Which is why I spent the first two hours of that night committing small acts of treason against my own craft. I changed my leading from the foundry standard. I widened my margins by a hair that would look like carelessness to anyone who knew my work, because my work is never careless. I deliberately misjustified three lines — actually *loosened* word spaces so the rivers ran visible down the paragraph, an ugliness that physically hurt me, that made my skin crawl the way bad grammar makes Silas wince. I mixed in a handful of worn sorts from the reject drawer, letters with chipped feet that would print with tiny haloes of extra ink, blurring the edges of my precision.

It felt like mutilating myself. It felt wonderful. It felt like the truest thing I'd made in years.

"Do you know," I said somewhere past the second hour, "that this is the best composition of my life?"

Silas looked up. "It's full of errors."

"Deliberate ones. Do you understand how hard it is to err *on purpose*? To space a line badly, knowingly? Every flaw on this page is a decision, and every decision is me choosing to survive long enough to bury the truth properly." I wiped my palms on my apron. "My father thought type remembered the hands that set it. I used to think that was sentiment. Now I think it's physics. There are four hundred copies of tonight going into the world, and every one of them will carry my fear in the spacing, and I *made* the fear legible on purpose so it couldn't be traced to me. Fear as forgery. He'd have hated it. He'd have been proud of it. Both."

Silas turned back to his transcript. "You should write that down someday."

"I write things down for a living. It's nearly killed me twice."

* * *

The trouble came at the magistrate's title.

Not the name — the name I had set and reset until it sat square as a headstone. The trouble was the honorific. The testimony read *Magistrate Aldous Verrick*, and to print those words adjacent was to commit a specific crime with a specific statute number; I knew the statute; I had typeset pamphlets citing it ironically in braver years. If I broke the words apart — *Aldous Verrick, Magistrate* — the crime became vaguer, deniable, a mere juxtaposition. Coward's typography. But coward's typography might keep the archive out of a bonfire, and an archive in a bonfire testifies to no one.

I stood over the composing stick with the sorts balanced in their groove and could not move.

"Wendell." Silas's voice, low.

"Don't talk to me. I'm kerning."

"You've been kerning that comma for four minutes."

"It's a very important comma."

He rose from the stool — my father's stool, and I resented him for the warmth of it — and came around the bench and stood beside me, and did not touch the stick, and did not touch me. He understood, as few people do, that a compositor at the stone is a surgeon mid-incision and that laying a hand on either is how people bleed.

"Set it straight," he said. "*Magistrate Aldous Verrick.* In order. As the witness spoke it."

"If they catch it—"

"They will not catch it, because it will be underground before they know it exists. And if they do catch it—" he paused, and I heard him choose honesty, which he always does at the worst moments, "—then the ordering of two words will not save you, and scrambling them will not save the dead. Set it as truth sets it. Straight. In order."

The candles burned. Somewhere beyond the wall, the river muttered against its stones, and farther off a patrol bell rang the quarter-hour with the smugness of institutions. My hands, cold or afraid or both, hovered over the case.

Then I set it. M-a-g-i-s-t-r-a-t-e. Space. A-l-d-o-u-s. Space. V-e-r-r-i-c-k. Period. New line.

And I will confess something that I have confessed to no one but the apple trees: when the last k locked into the stick, I laughed. A short, awful, relieved sound, the laugh of a man stepping off a ledge and finding the air holds. Because it was done — that word was done, that name was fixed in lead, and there is a finality to composed type that no manuscript possesses. Ink can be washed. Paper can be burned. But a line of type, locked in its stick, is *committed*. It cannot take itself back. Neither, now, could I.

"What?" Silas asked.

"Nothing. My father's superstition. The type remembers your hands." I lifted the stick, checked the line against the copy — upside-down letters marching in their groove, gibberish to any eye but a compositor's, and underneath the gibberish the most dangerous sentence in Vellham. "I hope these remember someone kinder than me."

* * *

We distributed the page through the forme in silence after that. This part is craft, pure craft, and craft is a country where I am not afraid, and I fled into it gratefully. Furniture in the margins. Quoins tightened — not too tight, a locked chase cracks; too loose, it pies. I have pied a chase exactly twice in my life, both times as a girl, both times while my father pretended not to watch, and both times the scattered letters sounded like the end of the world. Type on a stone floor has a voice like teeth.

At some point Silas began reading the testimonies aloud. Softly. Not for rehearsal — he had them by heart, all forty-one names and the circumstances of their vanishing, which is a thing I try not to think about too closely, the sheer interior room a man must devote to grief. He read them, I think, so that the room wouldn't be silent. So that the work would happen inside a liturgy instead of inside a terror.

"...Tobias Renner, cooper, taken from his yard on the sixth of February..."

I set Tobias Renner's name weeks ago, in the first safe nights, and it had been heavy then. Tonight it was heavier, because the magistrate's page gave it context — gave it a *who* — and a name with a who is a different weight entirely.

"...Liesel Marchetti, student of languages, struck from the university registry on..."

Struck from the registry. I glanced at Silas. He read on steadily, but I saw his thumb press flat against the edge of the transcript, whitening at the nail, and I knew whose chapter of the ledger this was, and I set the next line of type a little truer for him, because that is the only language I have for tenderness: better kerning.

Somewhere in the third hour the fear left. Not all at once — it drained, the way adrenaline drains, leaving behind the peculiar clean exhaustion of a body that has done what it feared. And in the space it left, the old love arrived, the love of the work itself, which is the oldest and strangest thing about me. The candlelight on the fresh-cast sorts. The smell of ink and apple smoke from the stove. The forme coming together leaf by leaf until it lay complete on the stone: sixteen pages, forty-one names, one magistrate, all of it waiting for the press like a held breath.

"Maren says you can read a man's soul in his justification," Silas said.

"My father says. Maren says I can read a man's fear in it."

"What does mine say?"

I looked at the forme. At the lines he had dictated and I had built. Even, generous word spaces. Nothing cramped. Nothing crowded. Whatever terror lived in Silas Ash, it had gone into the words themselves, not the spaces between them.

"It says a man who has already lost everything once," I said, "and has decided not to perform the losing again."

He considered that. "Yours says a woman who has spent fifteen years making her handwriting lie and has just stopped."

"We should both take up less revealing hobbies."

"Tomorrow," he said, and for the first time all night, smiled.

* * *

I locked the chase at half past four. The sky beyond the dirty window had gone the color of pewter, and the bells of Saint Ferren's would ring the dawn shift soon, and somewhere out there the city was waking to bread queues and rumor and the low electric hum of a place deciding whether to burn.

The forme went into the press bed. I oiled the platen screws, checked the tympan, dampened the first sheets. Silas fetched the stack of good rag paper Maren had acquired through channels she declined to describe — "a widow paid in kind," was all she'd said, with a look that closed the subject like a door.

Four hundred impressions. At my speed, with the hand press, six hours if nothing went wrong. Longer if the worn sorts threw haloes where I hadn't planned them. Longer still if my hands remembered how to shake.

I pulled the first proof and hung it on the drying line, and Silas and I stood looking at it — the magistrate's name dark and upright in the gray light, surrounded by the vanished, every error deliberate, every deliberate thing true.

"Well," I said. "Ink under the fingernails."

But when I looked down at my hands, ready to make the joke land, I saw that my nails were clean. Twenty years of printing, and I had learned to scrub myself spotless every night, because a foundry master with ink under his nails invites questions, and questions invite inspectors, and inspectors invite ruin.

Except tonight. Tonight I looked at my clean hands holding the dirtiest page in Vellham, and I thought: when this is over — whatever *over* means, if it ever comes — I am going to leave the ink on. Let them ask. Let them wonder. Let them look at Master Wendell's tidy cuffs and ink-stained fingers and draw whatever conclusions keep them awake.

I inked the plate. Outside, the first bell of morning rang thin and clear over the river, and beneath the orchard, in the root cellar, the secondhand press waited in the dark like a promise we had made to strangers not yet born.

"First pull," I said.

Silas took the other side of the bar. Together, we pressed the most dangerous sentence in the city onto ordinary paper, and the paper took it gladly, the way paper takes everything, keeping all our secrets and none of our excuses.

We were twelve sheets into the run when the knock came at the shop door below — three slow blows, official cadence, the knock of men who expect doors to open for them.

Silas and I looked at each other across the press, ink still wet between us.

"Provost's men don't knock politely," he whispered.

"No," I said, wiping my hands, reaching for my Master Wendell voice like a coat. "They kick. Which means whoever's down there wants something."

I started down the stairs to find out what — and whether the world had already read our page before we finished printing it.
