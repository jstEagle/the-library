## 17. Silas Is Followed

There is a particular art to being unremarkable, and I had thought myself its master. A census taker learns it early: you must be the sort of man a door opens for and then forgets. Grey coat, grey ledger, grey opinions offered in a grey voice. For six years I had walked the streets of Vellham as furniture with legs, and no one — provosts, priests, or wives of magistrates — had ever once looked at me twice.

So when the young man in the brown jacket turned left at the cooper's yard for the third time without any reason on earth to do so, I felt less like a hunted man than like a craftsman watching an apprentice botch a join he ought to have mastered years ago.

*Amateur*, I thought, with something dangerously close to professional contempt. *You're counting your steps. Never count your steps.*

It was the fourth of Frostmonth, and the light was going out of the day the way it always did that time of year — grudgingly, like a miser closing his hand. I had spent the afternoon in Salt Row taking testimony from a rope-maker's widow whose husband had been "transferred" fourteen months ago to a work detail that existed on no manifest anywhere in the province. She had wept twice and laughed once, all three times at things that were not funny, and I had written down every word she said in my true book — the one that lived inside my census satchel behind a false panel, beneath the official returns that were so much tedious fiction about chimney counts and grain stores.

I was carrying eleven finished testimonies. Eleven names, eleven last-seen dates, eleven descriptions of men and women who had been subtracted from Vellham as neatly as figures from a column. And somewhere between the widow's door and the corner of Tanners' Walk, I had acquired a shadow.

He was maybe twenty-three. Soft hands. Boots too good for his walk — clerk's boots, not street boots. He had the particular posture of a man who has been told he is clever and believes it: chin up, eyes everywhere, absolutely certain that his face was doing nothing at all while it did everything.

I did not look at him directly. You never do. You use windows.

The glazier's shop on Tanners' Walk has the finest mirrors in the print district, though the glazier would be offended to hear his wares described so prosaically. I stopped to examine a display of cut glass — I have learned over these months to develop sudden, consuming interests in window displays, in bootlaces, in the architectural merits of door lintels — and there he was in the long pane, twenty paces back, pretending to read a broadsheet about the price of coal.

The same broadsheet, I noted, that had been pasted to that wall since before the frost. He had been reading about the price of coal for some time now. Either he found it riveting or he was waiting for me to move.

I moved.

Here is what nobody tells you about being followed: it makes the whole city feel like it's conspiring against you personally. Every alley becomes a question. Every crowd, a trap with people in it. The lamplighter making his rounds — is he one of them? The woman selling roasted chestnuts who's stood on that corner for years — does she report foot traffic? Your own heartbeat becomes suspicious, loud, a traitor drumming in your chest saying *they know they know they know*.

And beneath all of it, running like a current under ice, the arithmetic. Eleven testimonies. Four hundred pages of type already set at the orchard, most of it still drying in Maren's bindery loft or stacked in Wendell's font cases disguised as broken sorts. Three children who carried messages through checkpoint lines for pennies and sweets and the pure pleasure of being trusted. One bookbinder who would fight the Provost's Guard single-handed if it came to that, and lose, but make them remember her. One compositor whose entire existence was a forgery that could be undone by a single line in a single ledger.

One of us caught meant all of us caught. That had been the covenant from the beginning, spoken among apple trees by a man who shook my hand like he was signing something in blood.

I turned down Cooper's Lane because it was narrow and I knew it, then thought better of it — narrow lanes are where tails like to close the distance — and came back out onto Weavers' Street with my heart conducting itself disgracefully.

Think, Silas. I made myself think the way I imagined Wendell thought, which is to say coldly, in straight lines, like setting type. What does this boy want? If they knew everything, there would be no boy. There would be six men and a wagon. A tail means suspicion, not certainty. Suspicion means they want to know where I go, whom I see, where the records live. Which meant the worst thing I could do was go anywhere near the orchard, near the shop, near Maren's bindery, near any of the eleven places that were my life.

Which meant, in turn, that I had to stop being a man with destinations.

* * *

There is a bridge over the Corren where it runs slow and industrial behind the tanneries, and on the far bank there is nothing much: mudflats, a derelict rope-walk, the backs of warehouses that no longer warehouse anything. Nobody goes there unless they are poor, criminal, or courting, and often all three. It was the only direction in which my shadow seemed reluctant to follow, and that reluctance told me he had orders — stay in sight, don't spook him, don't get your good boots muddy — orders written by someone who assumed a census taker would eventually go home.

So I gave him a home to watch.

I crossed the bridge at a stroll, pausing at the rail like a man enjoying the view of tannery effluent, and heard — not saw, heard — the hesitation behind me, the soft scuff of boots stopping, starting again. On the far side I turned up along the embankment to a row of condemned cottages awaiting demolition, chose the one with the least collapsed roof, and let myself in through a door that had not locked since the Corren flooded in my grandfather's day.

Inside it smelled of wet plaster and mouse. I stood in the dark of the front room with my heart going like a press at full run, and I listened to the young man in the brown jacket settle himself outside to wait, because of course he settled himself outside to wait; a stake-out is the laziest form of surveillance ever devised, and lazy men trust their patience more than their legs.

I waited until full dark. I counted to four hundred, twice, the way Wendell taught me you wait for ink to tack — never rush the pull, never rush the pull. Then I opened the false panel of my satchel by feel alone, in the blackness, the way I'd practiced a hundred nights with my eyes shut, and I took out the true book.

Eleven testimonies. The rope-maker's widow with her terrible laugh. The cooper's wife from the autumn, whose husband had been gone since the spring and who had said, in the flat voice grief gets when it goes on too long, *"They can strike him off their lists. He's on mine."* The student vanished from the registry, whose mother had pressed a lock of hair into my hand and pretended afterward she hadn't. Page after page after page, in my careful hand, each name a small leaden thing.

I could not carry them home. Home was burned — not literally, but in the sense that matters; any route I took led through streets where I might be seen, and the satchel itself might be seized tomorrow by anyone with authority and a bad mood. The Loyalty Inventory canvasses were moving through the print district like a comb through wet wool. If they took the book from me on the street, they would take it from me gently, bureaucratically, with a receipt, and by morning every name in it would be a confession, and everyone connected to it would be a case file.

I sat down on the floor of a dead man's cottage — whoever he had been, he had left a chair, and I declined to sit in it; superstition, perhaps, or respect — and I held the book and I thought about what it cost.

Here is the thing I have never confessed to anyone, not even in the pages of that book, especially not there: part of me wanted to be rid of it. Not the mission. The weight. There is a difference between carrying a thing and being carried by it, and lately I could no longer tell which of us was holding which. Every testimony I collected bought another night of not sleeping. Every name I wrote down was a debt taken on that no printing could discharge. And here came a boy in a brown jacket offering me, unknowingly, the one exit that came with clean hands: *it was taken from me, I had no choice.* The perfect excuse, gift-wrapped by the Provost's own office.

I am not a good man. I want that recorded somewhere, since recording things is apparently what I do. Good men burn with righteous fire and die for principles. I am a petty bureaucrat who started keeping a forbidden ledger because his brother's name was struck off a list, and half my courage is just stubbornness wearing its good coat.

But I looked down at the page in my hands — the rope-maker's widow, her last line: *"Tell it plain. Don't make him noble. He just wanted his wages and to come home."* Tell it plain. That was the whole archive, really. Not heroism. Plainness. The regime dealt in grandeur — grand destiny, grand order, grand erasures — and our entire counter-arsenal was four hundred pages of plainness, set in lead by a person who wasn't supposed to exist, bound by a widow, carried by children.

You don't surrender plainness to a boy in a brown jacket because your feet are tired.

So I did the arithmetic one final time, and then I did the unforgivable thing, which was also the only thing.

There was a stove in the corner of the cottage, rusted through, its pipe long fallen. Under the floorboards beside it — I had scouted this ruin months ago, back when caution was still a habit rather than a religion — there was a cavity where the hearthstone had cracked away from the joists, dry above the flood line, big enough for a book wrapped in oilcloth. I had told myself when I found it that it was for exactly this contingency, and I had told myself further that I was being prudent, and both statements were lies covering the third one, which is that some part of me had known since the day I began this work how it would end for me, and had quietly begun building the places I would need to leave things behind.

I wrapped the book. I knelt — knees cracking like green wood; I was thirty-four years old and felt sixty — and pried up the board, and laid the eleven testimonies into the dark, and put the board back, and scattered the dust and plaster crumbs across it with a house-painter's brush I'd left there in the summer for exactly this purpose. Then I sat back on my heels in a ruined cottage by the river and grieved.

Because that is what it was. I understand people think grief is only for the dead, but I had just buried eleven living voices — the only copies of them left on earth — under a floorboard in a house slated for demolition, and I did not know when or whether I would dig them up again. The demolition order could come next week. Rats could find the oilcloth. A child playing where children shouldn't could pull up a board and sell a treasure of paper for bread. Every hour those pages lay there, they were dying by inches, and I had killed them to keep the rest of us alive, and that trade — that specific, filthy, necessary trade — is one I will be settling accounts on for the rest of my life.

I left through the back, over the collapsed garden wall, along the mudflats where the tannery smell was strong enough to strip paint, and I did not go home. I went instead to the one place I could think of where a man might stand in the dark and look at a lit window and feel less alone, which is a foolish sentiment and I disown it entirely, but the feet insisted.

* * *

Wendell's window was lit past midnight, which meant the compositor was working, which meant the compositor was anxious, because Wendell Winterbourne worked late for pleasure exactly never; the craft was armor, not joy, whatever else got said among the apple trees.

I didn't knock. Knocking was for people whose visits were innocent. Instead I stood in the alley beside the print shop, in the cold, looking up at the amber square of glass with the silhouette moving inside it — that precise, economical silhouette, arms doing their patient work — and I composed the sentence I would have to say tomorrow, and the one after that, and the harder one underneath both.

*I was followed.* Three words, and everything changes. Because once you say *I was followed*, you cannot unsay it. Once you say it, Wendell stops sleeping, Maren starts sharpening things, Pip and Sefa and little Bram stop being couriers and start being risks, and every route, every drop, every friendly face gets reexamined as a possible trap. The whole architecture we'd built — so careful, so patient, so beautifully invisible — becomes a different structure overnight: not a secret anymore, but a target that knows it's a target.

And underneath that sentence, the worse one. Because standing in that alley, watching the candle-shadow move across the glass, I finally understood what the boy in the brown jacket actually meant. Not that they suspected me. Not yet. But that someone, somewhere in the Provost's offices, had done the same arithmetic I did every night — counted the disappearances, weighed the silences, noticed that in a city where printers kept getting inventoried, no new sedition appeared and yet none quite died either — and had concluded that the silence itself had a shape. That the erasures were being *countered*. That somewhere in Vellham, someone was keeping score.

They didn't know it was me. They didn't know about the orchard, the press, the pages, the children. They knew only that the dead were not staying properly dead, and that this could not be allowed, and that a census taker who visited too many widows was as good a thread as any to pull.

We were hunted. Not caught — hunted. There is a difference, and the difference is time, and I had just spent my evening burying mine under a floorboard.

Above me, the candle in the print shop guttered, steadied, and went on burning. Wendell would work until the small hours because that was what Wendell did with fear: set it in lead, one character at a time, until it was something you could hold. Tomorrow I would climb those stairs and say my three words and watch the color leave that careful face. Tomorrow Maren would call me a fool in three dialects and be right in all of them. Tomorrow Bram would ask why the grown-ups looked frightened, and Sefa would lie to him badly, and we would all pretend not to notice.

Tomorrow, tomorrow. But tonight I stood in the cold in a dead man's alley with empty hands, having traded eleven voices for one warning, and I made myself look at the truth plainly, the way the rope-maker's widow would have wanted:

Whatever we built in that orchard, they were coming for it now. And we had perhaps one season left to finish it, hide it deeper than hope, and teach the ground itself to keep our secrets.

The candle above me went out at last. Somewhere across the city, a bell marked the hour, indifferent as arithmetic. I pulled my collar up and walked home the long way, by routes I invented as I went, a grey man in a grey coat carrying nothing at all —

— which was, I understood by then, precisely what they wanted me to be.
