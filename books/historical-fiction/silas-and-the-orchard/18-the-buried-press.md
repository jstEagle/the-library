## 18. The Buried Press

### Wendell

The press arrived in the back of a coffin cart.

I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere — that I did not choose the theatrics. Maren chose them. She stood in the print shop doorway at dusk with her arms crossed and announced that Old Hobb at the undertaker's owed her a favor, that a coffin cart could pass any checkpoint in Vellham without a second glance, because grief was the one cargo this regime never inspected, and that if I had a better idea I was welcome to say so.

I did not have a better idea. I never have a better idea. That is, I suspect, why she keeps me around: as an audience for hers.

So on a gray Thursday when the river fog came up thick enough to chew, I stood in my own yard and watched a coffin cart rattle to a stop, and I helped lift out not a body but four hundredweight of cast iron wrapped in sacking, which we arranged in the coffin-shaped gap of the cart's bed with something like reverence. Hobb himself drove, a stooped man with the face of a bloodhound who had long ago stopped being surprised by anything Maren Quill asked of him.

"Mind the corner brackets," he said, as though instructing us on handling a widow rather than a machine. "They'll mark."

"They'll be buried by nightfall," Maren said.

Hobb looked at me then — a long look, the look of a man doing arithmetic with other people's secrets — and said only, "The Quill woman paid her husband's funeral bill in full, twenty years back. I don't forget accounts." And he climbed onto his seat and left us standing in the fog with a disassembled press and no further questions asked, which in Vellham these days was its own kind of eulogy.

The press itself was a secondhand Columbian, or what passed for one in a city where the good machines had been requisitioned years ago. It had printed hymnals in Corvaster for thirty years, then broadsheets, then nothing, because its last owner had been a man named Tetch who sold it to me through three intermediaries and refused, at every stage, to tell me why he was selling.

I knew why he was selling. Everyone knew why Tetch was selling. He had been visited by the Provost's men for a loyalty inventory — Silas had warned me they were coming for every printer in the quarter — and whatever they said to him in his own shop had drained the trade out of him the way you'd drain ink from a reservoir. He wasn't afraid of printing sedition. He was afraid of printing *anything*, because anything could become sedition retroactively. The regime had perfected a kind of time-traveling guilt: paper printed innocently in March could be declared criminal in June, and the crime dated from the printing, not the decree.

So he sold. And I bought. And now the thing sat in pieces in my cellar while I waited for full dark, thinking about the fact that I owned two presses — the honest one above, which printed ledgers and playbills and the occasional wedding announcement, all squeaky-clean and loyal as a lapdog — and this other one, which would never see daylight again if I had any sense.

Which raised the question of why I was buying lamp oil instead of sense.

### Maren

People think iron is heavy. Iron is nothing. Iron is honest — it weighs exactly what it weighs, and if you drop it on your foot, it does so without malice or apology. What's heavy is the *reason*. Four hundredweight of press and eight hundredweight of reason, and only three adults and three children to carry both.

We moved it in stages, over two nights, because Wendell insisted on caution and I insisted on speed, and what we actually did was neither: we moved it carefully, which is the worst of both worlds, like walking slowly toward a thing you dread.

First night: the frame, the platen, the bed. Down the alley behind the shop on a handcart muffled with felt, Pip walking ten paces ahead as lookout with instructions to cough twice if anyone came, once if a cat, and nothing at all if he simply wanted attention, which was a real possibility with Pip. Second night: the screw, the tympan, the type bed, and the box of spare parts wrapped as "machine oil and sundries." Bram came along on the second night because he refused to stay behind and because Sefa pointed out, with the cold pragmatism she gets from nobody knows where, that a seven-year-old carrying a bundle looks like a seven-year-old carrying a bundle, whereas a grown man carrying a bundle at midnight looks like exactly what he is.

Bram carried the bundle of screws. He held it against his chest the whole way like it was a kitten.

The orchard behind the old foundry wall has gone properly wild in the years since the Winterbournes stopped tending it — which is precisely why we love it. The city forgot it exists. Tax rolls list it as unproductive land; the Provost's maps show a blank green square labeled only ORCHD (DISUSED). Rows of apple trees, most of them half-feral, fruiting small and sour and free for anyone desperate enough to climb, which last winter was half the printers' quarter. There's a root cellar dug into the slope beneath the oldest tree — a great gnarled thing, older than the foundry, older probably than Vellham's current idea of itself — with a stone lintel and a door that hasn't opened honestly in thirty years.

We opened it honestly.

Wendell went first with the lamp, and I confess I watched him more than I watched the stairs. He'd dressed for the work in his oldest coat, hair tied back, no wig, no performance, just Wendell — and there are moments, fewer than I'd like, when the whole elaborate architecture of Master Winterbourne falls away and what's left standing is someone quicker and funnier and more frightened than the person the city sees. In the lamplight at the top of those cellar steps, that was who I saw: a person about to do something irreversible and doing it anyway.

The cellar smelled of earth and old apples — the sweet-rot smell of decades of fallen fruit composting into the floor. Dry, though. Drier than it had any right to be. The old foundry workers had built it well, back when building things well was still permitted.

"We'll need to dig," Wendell said, crouching, pressing a palm flat against the earthen floor like a physician taking a pulse. "A pit. Deep enough for the frame to sit below frost line, with the lead lining around it."

"And the lead?"

"I've been buying broken type." A pause. The particular pause Wendell does when admitting to a crime. "For six months. From every scrap dealer in the district. Broken sorts, worn plates, damaged rules. Anyone who asks, I'm recasting damaged stock." Another pause. "No one has asked."

Six months. While I was trading paper and arguing with guild masters, Wendell had been quietly buying up lead a few pounds at a time, and I had noticed nothing. I decided to be impressed rather than unsettled. With Wendell it's a daily negotiation.

* * *

### Wendell

Digging a grave for a press is an education in humility.

We began the third evening, Maren and I, with the children stationed above as lookouts — Pip in the crook of the low wall, Sefa walking the lane with a basket as cover, Bram officially forbidden to help and therefore helping constantly in small deniable ways, such as holding the lamp, handing up stones, and asking questions no one could answer.

"Why bury it at all?" he asked, on the first night of digging. "Why not hide it in a shed?"

"Because sheds get searched," Maren said, driving the spade.

"Then why keep it? If we can't use it, why not melt it down and forget it?"

Ah, Bram. Seven years old and already asking the questions that keep grown people awake.

Wendell answered — answered carefully, the way you answer a child when the true answer is a confession. I said: "Because there may come a day when printing a true thing is worth dying for, and on that day we will need a press, and there will be no shops open to buy one. Because everything else in this city can be confiscated, but no one digs up dirt looking for ideas." And then, because Bram deserved better than a speech: "Also, your sister buried her doll under the kitchen floor for a month and it made her feel very powerful. This is the same principle at civic scale."

Sefa, above, called down that she had heard that, and that the doll had deserved it.

But Bram's question stayed with me after he'd forgotten it, the way children's questions do — they hand you a stone and walk away, and you're left carrying it. *Why keep it?* Because I have begun to understand how this ends. Not how the Rising ends — nobody knows that, not even the Rising; the strike committees meet in cellars and emerge with resolutions and no plans — but how *we* end, if it goes wrong in either direction. If the Provost wins, printers hang. If the committees win, printers hang slightly differently, to different music. Either government will want the story told their way, and the Winterbourne shop, with its loyal little ledger press, might survive either — might survive as a tool. But the archive we're building, Silas's pages and my type and Maren's bindings, cannot survive either. Truth is contraband in every currency.

So we plant a press the way you'd plant an orchard: not because you'll eat the fruit, but because someone will, and you want them to have it.

Maren worked beside me in the pit, and I will note for the record that she digs like a woman settling a personal score with the earth. I measured; she excavated. We got the dimensions off the frame drawings I'd made — I had spent an afternoon drafting the burial plan with the same care I'd give a title page, plumb lines and drainage gravel and all, because if we must do an absurd thing we may as well do it beautifully — and by midnight the pit was chest-deep and square and true.

It was in the third foot of earth that my spade struck something soft.

Not stone. Cloth. I knelt and brushed the soil away with my fingers, and the smell rose up before the shape did: apples. Dozens of them, shriveled to brown leather, wrapped in a rotted sack, laid deliberately in a hollow in the clay. Placed. Someone had put these here on purpose, years ago — fifty years, a hundred — fruit saved in the dark of the ground by hands I would never know.

"Wendell?" Maren said, seeing my face.

"Someone stored apples here." I lifted one. It weighed almost nothing, light as a promise. "In a cache. Under the oldest tree." I sat back on my heels in the pit and laughed, I'm ashamed to say — a short, unsteady laugh that had grief in it somewhere, or its cousin. "We're not the first people to decide this orchard was the safest place in Vellham."

Maren took the apple from me, turned it over, and set it gently aside on the ledge of the pit, apart from the spoil heap. Not thrown back. Kept.

"Right," she said. "Carry on digging. We'll put yours deeper."

### Maren

Here is what nobody tells you about burying a press: the lead is worse than the iron.

Iron you can wrestle — ropes, levers, blasphemy, done. But Wendell's six months of hoarded type came to us in crates of broken sorts, misstruck letters, worn plates, hundreds of pounds of lead that had to be melted and poured into a lined box before the frame went in, because lead doesn't rot, lead doesn't rust, lead is the one metal in this city that keeps its word. So we built a fire in the cellar — vented through the old coal chute, thank God for foundry men and their thoroughness — and melted type in a crucible like two witches running a very literate coven.

Pip tended the fire. He's fourteen and has the patience of a saint with a gambling problem, and he kept the pour temperature steady by eye, the way he'd learned watching Wendell cast. Sefa skimmed dross. Bram was sent to bed and appeared again within twenty minutes wearing the expression of a boy who intends to be present at history whether history likes it or not, so we gave him the tongs and the solemn duty of not dropping them.

The lead poured golden and went gray as it cooled, and we filled the box in layers, and each layer had to cool before the next, and so we sat in a root cellar at two in the morning watching metal become patient, and somewhere in the third hour Silas came down the ladder with bread and cheese and the news.

He didn't lead with the news. That's Silas all over — he handed round the food first, commented on our progress, crouched to examine the cooling lead with genuine interest, and only then, in the mild voice he uses for terrible things, said: "Tetch hanged himself yesterday. Left a note saying he couldn't guarantee what he might have printed."

Nobody spoke. Pip fed the fire. The lead ticked softly as it cooled, the way metal does, contracting toward its final shape.

"He's the fourth printer this season," Silas went on. "The Provost's office calls them accidents. The guild calls them accidents. Everyone agrees very quickly and loudly that they're accidents." He looked around the cellar — at the pit, the lead box, the dismantled press waiting under sacking — and something in his face settled, like sediment finding the bottom of a glass. "This is the right thing you're doing. I want that said plainly, while I'm here to say it. Whatever happens next."

"It's a hole in the ground, Silas," I said.

"It's a hole in the ground with a printing press in it," he said. "That's a different animal entirely."

* * *

### Wendell

We lowered the frame on the fifth night, using a tripod and block and tackle salvaged from the foundry's dead days, and it went into the earth as smoothly as anything has ever gone anywhere in my life, which should have frightened me more than it did. The platen followed. The screw, greased and wrapped in waxed cloth. The bed, the tympan, the chase, every bolt in a sealed lead tube with an inventory written in my smallest, plainest hand — the hand I use for legal documents, the one that says *nothing here but honesty*.

Then the gravel, for drainage. Then the sand. Then the earth, shovelful by shovelful, until the floor of the root cellar was a floor again and the only evidence was a faint difference in the color of the soil that no one would ever notice unless they were looking, and if they were looking, we were dead anyway.

Maren tamped the last of it down and stood there a moment with the shovel across her shoulders like a yoke, breathing hard, sweat cutting clean lines through the cellar dust on her face.

"Well," she said. "Planted."

And that was the word for it, I realized. Not hidden, not buried — planted. A press under an orchard, beneath the roots of the oldest tree, in ground that had already given up one secret harvest of somebody's saved apples. Things go into the ground in Vellham all the time — bodies, bribes, inconvenient documents. Very few things go into the ground alive.

Silas climbed down to see the finished work, and stood over the spot with his hat in his hands, uncharacteristically silent. Then he took from his coat a folded page — one of his testimony pages, a widow's account in his careful clerk's hand — and crouched, and pressed it flat against the earth over the buried press, and left it there.

"What's that, then?" Maren asked.

"A seed," Silas said, and had the grace to look embarrassed immediately after, as though the sentence had escaped custody.

Above us, faint through the cellar door, I heard Bram reporting to Pip and Sefa in a stage whisper that the press was *definitely* dead now, *definitely*, cross his heart, and Sefa telling him that things weren't dead just because they were underground, and Pip saying nothing, because Pip at fourteen already understood that some things you keep in the ground on purpose, like bulbs, like teeth, like grudges.

We banked the fire. We climbed the ladder. Maren locked the cellar door and pocketed the key, which she informed me she would wear on a cord around her neck, and which she informed the rest of us she would lose before surrendering, and which she informed Bram he would never touch, in that order of increasing emphasis.

Walking home through the lanes in the blue hour before dawn, past shuttered shops and the first carts coming in from the country farms, I felt lighter than I had in months, and I mistrusted the feeling thoroughly. We had done a wise thing, and wisdom rarely feels like this. Wisdom feels like Tetch's face must have felt, at the end. Lightness felt like something else — like hope, which in my experience is just fear that hasn't read the newspapers yet.

At the mouth of Printers' Row we stopped, the four of us, and looked down the street at the shop windows, dark and ordinary and loyal. Somewhere east across the river, the shipyard bells were ringing change-of-shift, and under that sound, if you knew how to listen, the city had a second sound: the sound of a crowd gathering its breath.

"There'll be trouble before spring," Silas said quietly.

"There's always trouble before spring," Maren said. "That's what spring's *for*."

But she said it looking at the orchard road behind us, not the row ahead, and her hand had gone to the cord at her neck where the key lay against her skin, and I understood that none of us believed our own calm, and that we had planted the press not for the spring we expected but for the one we dreaded —

— and that was when Sefa caught my sleeve and nodded down the lane, where a figure stood under the foundry wall lamp reading a broadsheet by matchlight, and dropped the match, and did not leave.
