## 19. Winter Rations

There is a particular arithmetic that belongs to winter in Vellham, and it is not taught in any countinghouse. It goes like this: one loaf divided by five mouths equals hunger by Thursday. One coat divided among three children who take turns standing near the stove equals a mother's guilt measured in shivers. One secret kept by five people equals a weight that does not divide at all but multiplies.

I had been a widow for six years before I learned that arithmetic, and I have been learning it ever since.

The bindery was cold enough that the glue went sullen and refused to set properly. I sat at my bench with my breath fogging over the covers I was meant to be finishing for a client — a merchant's daughter's prayer book, gilt edges, more money than sense — and listened to Bram count the coal in the scuttle behind me. He counted out loud, because he was seven and had not yet learned that some numbers are better left uncounted.

"Nine," he announced. "And two of those are only half."

"Then we are rich," I told him. "Half-coals are twice as precious. Everyone knows that."

He looked at me with Sefa's skepticism delivered through a younger face. The children had developed a collective expression over the autumn, a single look shared among them like a coat, and it said: *You are lying to us kindly, and we will allow it because you are also feeding us.* Children are not fools. They are merely patient, which is worse.

Sefa came down from the loft with her arms full of the quilts we rotated nightly — whoever coughed got the thickest one, an honor no child wants — and said nothing, which from Sefa was a paragraph. She set the quilts on the bench and looked at Pip.

Pip was asleep sitting up, which is never a good sign in a fourteen-year-old. She had come in from a courier run with snow in her hair and a lie on her lips — "I'm fine, Maren, just tired" — and had gone to sleep upright against the wall like a puppet with its strings cut halfway. Her color was wrong. Not pale exactly; Vellham winters made everyone pale. Wrong. A grayish undertone, the color of old pewter, and when she breathed there was a small wet catch at the top of each inhale that I pretended not to hear and heard perfectly.

I am a practical woman. My late husband used to say I could find the useful side of a funeral, and he did not mean it unkindly; he meant that grief, in my hands, becomes organization. So I did what a practical woman does. I put my palm against Pip's forehead, found it baking, took off my own boots, wrapped her in the thickest quilt, carried her upstairs myself — she weighed no more than a sack of rags, and I noted that too, filed it away where I file things I cannot yet afford to feel — and sent Sefa for the physician on Cordwainer Lane.

"He'll want paying," Sefa said.

"He'll want paying," I agreed. "Go anyway."

* * *

Physician Hobb was a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the bedside manner of a tax collector, which I confess is why I liked him. He did not tell me everything would be well. He looked down his nose at Pip's chest, listened with a stethoscope older than she was, and said, "Inflammation of the lungs. She has been running messages in this weather?"

"She has been delivering books," I said, which was true in the way most of our truths were true — technically, structurally, load-bearing if you didn't lean on them.

"Hm." He straightened up. "She needs rest, warmth, proper food — meat if you can get it, broth at minimum — and this." He wrote on a pad, tore the page off, and handed it to me the way a man hands you a bill, because it was one. "The apothecary on Tallow Street mixes it. Tell him I sent you. And Mistress Quill—" he paused at the door, and something almost human crossed his face, "—don't wait until they're gray next time. Gray costs more than pink."

Everything costs more than pink. That is the whole of medicine, if you ask me.

The tincture cost eleven marks. I will record that number here because numbers are how I keep from screaming: eleven marks, for a brown bottle holding perhaps eight doses. I had nine marks in the house, and those were earmarked for coal, rent, and the flour that stood between us and actual starvation, in descending order of negotiability.

Which left me standing in my own bedroom, staring at the cedar box under the floorboard.

* * *

Every widow owns a box, and every box contains a life reduced to metal. Aldous's silver: a christening cup from his grandmother, six teaspoons, a small dish with a chased rim that he'd won at cards and was absurdly proud of, and a watch chain he never wore because he said gold chains were for men who wanted to be looked at. He had wanted, always, to leave them to our children someday — though we never had children of our own, and then the apprentices arrived like weather, three of them in one bad year, and "someday" quietly became *them* without either of us saying so aloud.

Aldous died of a fever caught binding ledgers for a shipping concern that didn't believe in heating its offices. Practical man, my husband. Believed in warm children and cold warehouses, and the warehouse won.

I knelt on the floor with the box open on my knees for a long time. This is the part no one tells you about poverty: it isn't the going without that grinds you down. Going without, I can do. I have done it so long it fits me like a glove. It is the selling — the conversion of love into currency, the moment a spoon becomes four marks and your husband's pride in winning it becomes nothing at all — that is the true tax on the poor. The rich lose money. We lose objects, and objects are where we keep our dead.

"You'd laugh at me," I told him, "for hesitating over spoons while the girl can't breathe."

He would have laughed. He'd have said, *Maren, love, it's metal. The girl is flesh. Even I can do that sum,* and then he'd have kissed my hair and gone back to pretending he wasn't worried about the rent.

I wrapped the silver in a flour sack and went out into the snow before I could develop any further opinions on the matter.

* * *

Tallow Street smelled of camphor and boiled sugar. The apothecary was a fat, melancholy man named Grell who weighed everything twice and trusted no one, qualities I respect professionally even when they're aimed at me. He examined Hobb's script, examined me, and said, "Eleven marks."

"I know what it says. I can read."

"Can you pay it?"

"I can pay eight now and the rest within the fortnight."

He sniffed. Behind him, on the shelf, sat a row of identical bottles, and I understood with the clarity that comes to women who have bargained all their lives that the price was soft and the man was testing whether I was desperate enough to haggle or honest enough to admit need, and that the correct answer was both.

"My late husband was Aldous Quill, of the Binders' Guild," I said. "You'll know the name. He bound half the guild's registers himself. I'm bringing you eight marks today and you have my word — my word, Master Grell, which every binder on this street knows is worth more than paper — for the other three by the new moon."

Something shifted in his face. Guild names still opened doors in Vellham, even doors belonging to fat melancholy men. "Eight now," he said. "Three by the new moon. And if the girl worsens, bring her to me before you bring her to Hobb. His fees are criminal and his leeches are unnecessary."

"They're always unnecessary," I said.

"They're always billed," he said, and we understood each other completely, which is the closest thing to friendship commerce allows.

I walked home through the snow with the bottle inside my mitten, against my palm, where I could feel it like a second heartbeat, and I did not think about the christening cup until I was safe indoors, and then only briefly, and then I went upstairs and made Pip drink the foulest medicine ever decanted and called it cherry, and she was too ill to call me a liar, and Sefa called me a liar on her behalf, and Bram asked if he could have some of the cherry too, and I said certainly, when he was dying, and he seemed satisfied with the arrangement.

That is a family, if you want to know. Not the blood kind. The kind where everyone knows the medicine isn't cherry and drinks it anyway.

* * *

Wendell came that evening, which was itself a risk — the shopkeeper's rounds don't naturally include my bindery after dark — carrying, of all improbable things, a goose. Not a whole goose. Half a goose, wrapped in cloth, plus a bag of barley and a look of profound embarrassment, as though someone had caught him committing kindness and he needed it known that circumstances had forced his hand.

"There was a misprint," Wendell said. "A client's order. Unsalable stock. It would have been wasteful to destroy it."

"A goose," I said.

"Poultry is frequently misprinted." They set the parcel on my table with the rigid dignity of a person refusing to acknowledge the joke they have just made. Wendell Winterbourne is many things — careful, brilliant, brave in the specific way of people who have been frightened every day for years and have simply outlasted the fear — but a liar they are not, except constantly, and the effort shows.

"Sit down," I said. "Sefa, put water on. If we're eating misprinted poultry, we'll do it properly."

They stayed. That was the thing I noticed that night, watching them across my table, this composed stranger who had walked into my life through a paper delivery and refused, with tremendous politeness, to leave: Wendell watched Pip eat. Watched her the way I imagine sailors watch lighthouses. And when Pip, flushed with fever and soup, said, "Master Winterbourne, is it true you set type faster than anyone in the district?" Wendell said, "It is not true, but it is flattering, and I accept flattery in lieu of wages," and Pip laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and Wendell's hands — those extraordinary hands, made for lead and beauty — curled once around the edge of the table and went still.

I thought, then: *This one would burn down the world for these children and apologize to the world afterward for the mess.*

I had already decided to demand entrance into the archive business weeks ago, hauling paper through the snow in the dark like a smuggler of scripture. But decisions are cheap. Watching Wendell's hands on my tabletop, I understood I hadn't joined a conspiracy. I had adopted a family member. There is no undoing that. Believe me, I've tried with several relatives.

"You'll stay until the storm passes," I said. It wasn't a question. My questions sound like statements. Ask anyone.

"The snow—"

"The snow is an hour old and four inches deep and nobody official is walking in it. You'll sleep in the loft. Bram snores, so you'll have company."

Bram, delighted beyond measure, immediately began planning the snoring.

* * *

Winter tightened its grip the way winter does — not all at once but in increments, each morning discovering a new economy to practice. We ate the goose over three days, broth first, then the meat, then a soup made from the bones that Sefa declared tasted like "ghost goose," and ate anyway. The coal went to nine halves, then seven. Pip recovered by degrees, gray to pink, pink to loud, loud to insufferable, which is the natural progression of adolescent health, and Hobb pronounced her fit and charged me a follow-up fee for the privilege of being told what I already knew.

The archive work did not pause for the cold. Testimonies kept arriving — slower now, frozen roads being poor conductors of secrets — and Silas Ash came and went with his satchels and his careful, mournful face, and I fed him whenever he appeared, because the man had the self-feeding instincts of a candle flame in wind. He tried to refuse. He tried to pay. I let him pay once, took his coin, and spent it on bread for the household, thereby completing a circle of money so pointless it achieved poetry.

One night, working late — I was sewing signatures for the archive copies while Silas read testimony aloud in a low voice, partly to check the text and partly, I think, because reading those names alone in a room is unbearable — Pip crept halfway down the ladder to listen. I let her. Some lessons you cannot teach; they can only be overheared.

"—and the cooper's wife asks that the date be corrected," Silas read. "She says the registry has him taken on the ninth, but it was the eleventh. She says the ninth matters to them, because of the registry, and she wants it known the registry was wrong about the day too."

Silas stopped. Cleared his throat. Went on.

I looked at my needle, my thread, the little white stitches marching down the fold like a fence around something living. Six years a widow and I had thought I knew the shape of loss. Then I met a woman whose grief included a calendar correction, and I understood that the regime had stolen not just husbands and sons but the *true days*, and that somewhere in Vellham a woman lay awake grieving on the wrong night because a clerk had been careless or cruel, and no one had thought it worth fixing.

We fixed it. I stitched the corrected page into forty copies. It is not much. It is everything. Both things are true, and I have stopped expecting the world to choose between them.

* * *

Here is what I did not tell them, gathered in my bindery among the ghost-goose bones: what the silver had bought, and what the buying had cost. Sefa noticed the empty space in my room where the cedar box lived — Sefa notices everything, God help her, she will grow up to be either a magistrate or a saint and I dread both — but she said nothing, and her silence was so precisely my own silence given back to me that I nearly wept over the glue pots.

Love without romance. That is what I know about. I loved my husband with the ordinary ferocity of twenty years, and I love these children with a ferocity that frightens me more, because there is no vow underneath it, no document, no ring. Just choice, renewed daily like a lease. Just: *these ones, mine, whatever it costs.* And it costs. It costs eleven marks and a christening cup and the last of the coal and sleep and the comfortable fiction that a bookbinder in Vellham can keep her head down and her hands clean.

My hands have ink in them permanently. Under the nails, in the cracks, a stain no pumice touches anymore. Aldous used to say I'd die inked. Good, I told him. Let them know what killed me — work.

I did not know then how soon I'd think of that again, nor in what tone.

Because it was the last week of the month, the new moon, the night I owed Grell his three marks, that a woman came to the bindery door whom I had never seen before — pleasant-faced, shawl-drawn, snow melting on her shoulders — and said she had a testimony for the archive, a cousin taken in the September arrests, and could she speak with whoever kept the records? And her eyes moved past me, quick and inventorying, cataloguing my presses, my apprentices, my sleeping loft, the way a buyer eyes livestock, and I smiled at her the way you smile at anything with teeth, and said of course, come in from the cold, sit down, tell me everything.

And while she talked — fluently, plausibly, with details that rang slightly false the way church bells ring slightly flat — I poured tea with steady hands and thought, with a calm that surprised me:

*Aldous, love, I may need more than spoons before this winter's done.*
