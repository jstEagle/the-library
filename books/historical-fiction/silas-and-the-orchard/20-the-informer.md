## 20. The Informer

There is a particular kind of cold that lives in the space between trusting someone and learning you shouldn't have. I had felt it once before, standing in a registry office at nineteen years old, watching a clerk draw a single line through my brother's name. I felt it again on a grey Thursday in November, in Maren Quill's bindery, holding a sheet of paper that smelled faintly of another man's cologne.

But let me begin where all good disasters begin: with a kindness.

His name was Aldous Fenwick. He was a chandler by trade — candles for the churches of Vellham, mostly, which meant he moved through parishes freely and was waved through checkpoints with a nod. He had come to me three months earlier, after I'd recorded his household for the census, and he'd lingered on my doorstep with the awkward courage of a man about to confess something. His nephew, he said. Taken from the Candlemakers' Guild hall in August. No arrest record, no body, no explanation. Just an empty bed and a mother who set two plates at table out of habit.

I did what I always do now. I took down every detail he could give me. The date. The clothes the boy wore. The names of the men who came, as best Aldous could describe them. And then I told him, in the careful half-language we'd developed among witnesses, that there were people who would remember his nephew properly.

Aldous became useful. Better than useful — he became enthusiastic. Chandlers see everyone; nobody guards their tongue around a man selling tallow. Over the autumn he brought me four more testimonies, each one detailed, each one plausible, each one delivered with that same trembling sincerity. A dyer's apprentice. A ferryman. Two sisters who ran a pie stall near the Provost's barracks and had seen carts go in at night and not come out.

Wendell set each name in lead. Maren stitched each page into the growing archive. Pip memorized the delivery routes. Sefa drew maps of the streets in pencil so fine it looked like breath on glass. We had a system. We had, God help us, a rhythm.

And rhythm is exactly what a clever man waits for you to fall into before he cuts your throat.

* * *

It started, as these things do, with something small enough to dismiss. Bram — who is nine years old and misses nothing, being small enough to live below the sightline of adults — came to the orchard one evening and said, "The candle man walks different when he leaves your street."

"Walks different," I repeated.

"Like this." Bram demonstrated: shoulders back, chin up, boots striking the cobbles with the crisp authority of a man reporting to a superior rather than leaving a friend's house. Then he slouched again into his natural boy-shape and shrugged. "When he knocks, he's sad. When he goes, he's glad. I don't like it."

Children are supposed to be innocent, and everyone says so, and everyone is wrong. Children are simply honest, which is far more dangerous.

I told myself it meant nothing. Grief does strange things to a man's spine; perhaps Aldous straightened up on leaving because carrying testimony lightened him. I told myself this on Tuesday. On Wednesday, Sefa reported that she'd seen Aldous speaking with a provost's clerk outside the Customs House — just speaking, just two men passing the time of day, except that the clerk had written something down afterward, and men don't write down the time of day.

On Thursday, Wendell sent word via Pip that I should come to the shop after close, and the tone of the note — Wendell's notes are models of compression, being written by a woman who has spent her life fitting words into spaces too small for them — said everything the words did not.

I went.

* * *

The print shop after dark is a different country. By day it performs respectability: orders posted, apprentices' brooms leaning by the door, the smell of ink and oil announcing itself honestly to any inspector who might poke his head in. By night the shutters close and the candle comes down to the composing stone, and Wendell Winterbourne stands over the type in her shirtsleeves with her hair tied back, and Master Wendell ceases to exist entirely.

She didn't look up when I entered. She held a sheet of paper between two fingers, angled toward the candlelight.

"Do you know this hand?" she asked.

I took it. It was one of Aldous's testimonies — the ferryman, I thought, though the details swam. "Of course. It's—"

"Look at the paper."

I looked. I am a census taker; paper is my native element; I catalogued its weights and watermarks the way other men catalogue horses or wine. And the moment I actually looked, I wanted to put it down and never touch it again.

"It's Provost's stock," I said. "This weight, this finish — it's issued through the Office of Returns. I've filled out ten thousand forms on this exact paper." My mouth had gone dry. "Aldous is a chandler. Why would a chandler be writing on government stationery?"

"Because," Wendell said, setting the candle down with the deliberate care of a woman who wants very much to throw something instead, "he isn't a chandler."

She laid out the rest while I stood there feeling the floor tilt gently beneath my professional feet. Three of his four testimonies, examined closely, contained details no genuine witness would know — the interior arrangement of the Provost's barracks courtyard, the specific hours of the night watch rotation, a description of a holding cell that matched no building on the street where the sisters' pie stall supposedly stood. The fourth — his own nephew — was the only one that rang true, and Wendell suspected it was true precisely because it was the bait: a real grief, offered first, to purchase our belief in everything that followed.

"He's been feeding us fabrications," Wendell said. "Names that lead nowhere, or worse — names that lead somewhere they want us to go. And every page you've taken from him, every route Pip has walked, every contact he's watched us use—" She stopped. I watched her do the same arithmetic I was doing, and I watched it land in her face the way it landed in mine.

"The cooper's wife," I said. "He introduced us to the cooper's wife."

"Yes."

"She's real. Her husband is really gone. I've sat in her kitchen."

"And how many people saw you sitting in it?"

I thought of the street. The neighbors' windows. The way word travels in Vellham faster than any courier we employ, because gossip needs no payment and fears no checkpoint. "Anyone," I said. "Everyone. If they wanted a map of our network—"

"They've had one since September."

* * *

We woke Maren at half past midnight, which in her world constitutes an act of war, but she took one look at our faces and put the kettle on instead of shouting, which is her version of mercy.

By candlelight in the back room of the bindery, with the apprentices mercifully asleep behind the curtain, we took inventory of the damage. It was worse than I'd let myself believe in the print shop, and better than it might have been, and both of those truths arrived together like twins who hate each other.

Better first: the archive itself was clean. Every page Aldous had given us was fabricated, and fabricated pages had gone into a section Wendell kept separate — provisional entries, pending corroboration, a habit born of her craftsman's pride that had accidentally saved us. Nothing false had been set in final type. Nothing false had been sewn into the true binding. The archive's spine was still honest.

Worse: everything else was soaked in him. The cooper's wife. The parish contacts he'd suggested. The two occasions he'd walked partway home with Pip, chatting amiably about candles, asking — casually, oh so casually — whether the boy carried papers for "your master Ash," and what sort of things a growing lad might earn carrying them. Pip had answered honestly, because Pip is eleven and honest, and because Aldous had bought him honey cakes and asked like a friend.

"One courier's already burned," Maren said flatly. She wasn't guessing. She'd spent the evening making inquiries of her own, through the binders' guild, through the quiet web of tradeswomen who know everything and charge nothing for it. "Tam Rouse. The ferryman's boy — the one who ran pages between the cooper's wife and the bindery. Didn't come home last night. His mother's been to the watch house and they told her no one by that name was held, which is what they tell everyone, which means yes."

The silence in that room had texture. It had weight. Somewhere beyond the curtain, Bram turned over in his sleep and murmured something to a dream, and I found I couldn't look at the children's side of the room at all.

"Then we move," Wendell said. "Tonight. The cooper's wife, the parish contacts, anyone Aldous touched — warnings, new routes, new signals. And the archive sections he contaminated get pulled and re-cut."

"You can't re-cut four weeks of type in a night," Maren said.

"No," Wendell agreed. "But I can start."

They began to argue logistics — who went where, in what order, with what cover story — and I stood by the window with my hand on the cold glass and did the thing I had been avoiding since the print shop. I counted.

Not the pages. Not the routes. The people. Tam Rouse, sixteen years old, in a cell somewhere because he carried truth for us. The cooper's wife, whose name I will not set here even now, whose kitchen I warmed myself in while she grieved, marked and measured by men who used her grief as hook-bait. Every witness who'd ever spoken to me within eyeshot of a window Aldous knew about.

I had built a record of the disappeared. And I had handed the disappearing machine a fresh ledger of names, written in my own careful census-taker's hand.

"There's a question," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "that we need to ask ourselves. Not tonight — tomorrow, when we've done the urgent work. But soon."

Maren paused mid-sentence. Wendell looked up from the list she was drafting.

"Aldous gave us four testimonies. Three were lies dressed as grief, and one was real grief dressed as trust. We caught the paper. Good. Fine. But here's what keeps me at this window: he was good. He was better than good. If we hadn't noticed the watermark — if Bram hadn't noticed the walk — how long would we have gone on? And the next one won't make the same mistakes. So the question is: how do we ever take another stranger's testimony again? How do we weigh a widow's tears against the possibility that the tears are rehearsed?" I turned from the window. "Because if the answer is that we can't — if we have to suspect every grieving person who finds us — then the Provost doesn't need to catch us. He only needs to make us cruel, and we'll destroy ourselves."

Nobody answered. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Behind the curtain, little Bram slept on, dreaming whatever nine-year-olds dream in a city that eats names.

It was Maren, finally, who spoke, and she spoke the way she does everything: without ornament.

"We take the testimony," she said. "We verify the testimony. And if a liar costs us a boy like Tam Rouse, then the liar pays for it. That's not cruelty, Silas Ash. That's arithmetic." She looked at me with those flat grey eyes that miss nothing and forgive less. "You want to grieve nicely. Grieve later. Right now there's a cooper's wife who doesn't know she's a target, and every hour we spend being philosophical, she sleeps sounder and dies sooner."

So we worked. Until nearly dawn, we worked. Wendell drafted warning notes disguised as trade circulars; Maren sewed them into the spines of account books bound for the right addresses; and at four in the morning I walked the long way to the cooper's street, past the Customs House, past the barracks, past the pie stall where two sisters sold their lies fresh every morning, and I slipped a book through the cooper's widow's door with a message sewn inside that said, in the plainest words I could compose: *Burn anything you gave us. Speak to no one who mentions candles. Trust no kindness you cannot trace.*

Whether she read it in time, whether she understood, whether she was already being watched — none of that was mine to know. That is the particular torment of this work: you send your warnings into the dark and the dark keeps its receipts.

* * *

We learned what happened to Tam Rouse nine days later, and I wish to God we hadn't.

Aldous Fenwick was arrested publicly — that was the point of it, the performance of it — marched from his candle shop in handcuffs while the Provost's criers announced that a dangerous agitator had infiltrated honest commerce to spread sedition. The crowd gathered, as crowds do. And Aldous, playing his final scene, confessed beautifully. He named the underground archive. He described it accurately — too accurately — the orchard, the press, the bindery connection, the child couriers. Everything he'd learned walking our streets with his friendly face and his government paper.

Everything except anything that mattered. Because by then there was nothing left to matter. We had burned the contaminated sections, rerouted every soul he'd touched, emptied the safe houses, and moved the archive twice. When the Provost's men raided the orchard on the eleventh day, they found apple trees, frost, and the ghosts of a root cellar swept cleaner than a registry office. They arrested a tramp sleeping under the north wall, interrogated him for two days, and released him baffled.

It was, by any measure, a victory. Maren called it one. Wendell permitted herself one glass of the rough plum brandy she keeps for catastrophes, and said the craft had held.

But I kept thinking about Tam Rouse. Because here is the arithmetic Maren didn't say aloud, the sum underneath the sum: Aldous's confession, accurate as it was, named the couriers. And the couriers were the one part of our network we couldn't fully dissolve, because children still had to carry pages, and children still had to sleep in their mothers' houses. We changed the routes. We changed the signals. We couldn't change the fact that Tam Rouse had been taken before we knew, and that everything Aldous confessed afterward merely confirmed what his handlers already had: a boy, a name, a family, a cell.

Nine days after the arrest, a fishwife who owed Maren a favor came to the bindery with news from the river gate. A body had been pulled from the water below the Custom House steps — a boy, sixteen or so, no papers, hands showing rope burns. The watch logged it as an accident. Drunk, probably. Nobody's son in particular.

Maren went herself, alone, to look. She came back gray-faced and said only: "It's him. It's Tam." And then she went upstairs and told his mother herself, because she said a lie from a stranger was unbearable but the truth deserved a human voice, and she sat with that woman until midnight while the rest of us stood in the bindery among the unfinished books and could not work and could not leave.

That night I wrote in my private record — the one I keep in cipher, the one that will outlive all of us if we succeed, and mean nothing if we fail — a new kind of entry. Not a testimony of the disappeared. Something else.

*Tam Rouse, sixteen, ferryman's son. Did not disappear. Was taken because of us. Carried truth for eight months without once dropping a page or giving up a name under whatever they did to him. The record exists because of him. The guilt is ours.*

I have made my peace with many things in this work, or told myself I have. I have not made my peace with that entry, and I do not intend to. Some debts are meant to stay open. They keep you honest.

But peace, as it turned out, was not going to be on offer. Because three days after we buried Tam Rouse in the cheap ground where the watch dumps its unclaimed, a girl came looking for me at the census office — a stranger, maybe seventeen, with a burn scar on her wrist and a story about a brother taken from the tanneries. And she told it well. Too well. And I sat across from her with my pen poised over government paper, and I heard Bram's voice in my head saying *when he knocks he's sad, when he goes he's glad*, and I looked at her eyes, which were wet, and wetness proves nothing, wetness can be learned —

And I realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, that I no longer knew how to tell the difference.
