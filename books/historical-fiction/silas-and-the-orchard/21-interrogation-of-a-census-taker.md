## 21. Interrogation of a Census Taker

They came for me on a Tuesday, which I have always considered an insult. Mondays are for arrests — everyone knows that. A Monday arrest says *you are important enough to disrupt the week's beginning*. A Tuesday arrest says *you were fourth on the list and the clerk had a dentist appointment*. There is nothing quite so demoralizing as being a bureaucratic afterthought even in your own destruction.

The men who arrived at my lodging house were not provosts. Provosts wear their authority like a uniform; it is the whole point of uniforms. These two wore the gray coats of the Registry Office, which meant someone upstairs had decided that whatever was wrong with Silas Ash could be handled with paperwork rather than irons. I have never decided whether this was good luck or the most elegant trap I ever walked into.

"Census Taker Ash?" said the taller one. He had a face like an unpaid invoice.

"That's me," I said, and had the presence of mind to pick up my hat. "Am I wanted somewhere, or is this a collection call? I'm current on my rates."

Neither of them laughed. Bureaucrats never do; humor is a form of unrecorded income.

* * *

The Registry Office on Candlewick Street is a building designed to make you feel like a misfiled document. The corridors are long and dim and lined with shelves of ledgers going back a century — births, deaths, marriages, property, the whole machinery of who counts and who doesn't. I had walked those corridors a hundred times as a functionary. Walking them as a subject is an entirely different education in architecture. The ceiling gets lower with every turn. I am fairly sure they built it that way on purpose.

They put me in a room with a table, three chairs, a lamp, and a man named Examiner Voss, whom I knew by reputation. Voss had spent eleven years auditing grain merchants and had once bankrupted a family of millers over a discrepancy of four sacks. He was famous for never raising his voice. His reputation preceded him into the room like a bad smell.

"Taker Ash." He did not ask me to sit, so I sat anyway. It's important to establish early in an interrogation that you will be mildly disobedient in small ways. It confuses them about what else you might dare.

"Examiner Voss," I said. "I'd offer to make this easy, but I suspect easy isn't in your job description."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger. Interest. That frightened me more than anger would have. Anger is predictable. Interest means he thinks there's something here worth his time.

"Your returns," he said, opening a folder, "for the districts of Tanner's Row, Cooper's Lane, and the Weavers' Quarter, covering the past fourteen months, show certain irregularities."

"Do they." I folded my hands on the table like a man settling in for a tedious sermon. "Irregular how? Undercounting? I'll admit the Weavers' Quarter has more doors than maps. Half those alleys don't appear on any survey drawn since the flood."

"Overcounting, in some cases." He turned a page with one dry finger. "Households recorded with dependents whose names do not appear on any other registry. Persons listed as resident who cannot be located. And in eleven instances" — he looked up — "households marked as containing individuals *of irregular standing*, where your annotations suggest exemptions you were not authorized to grant."

Here it was, then. Not the orchard, not Maren, not the archive. Something older and smaller: Wendell Winterbourne's household return, and the careful fictions I had written around it, and the dozen smaller mercies I had extended to others along the way because I had learned from the best liar in Vellham how a lie is constructed and thought I might as well build some honest ones.

"Irregular standing," I repeated. "You mean people without papers."

"People without *proper* papers."

"In my experience, Examiner, paper is paper. It either exists or it doesn't, and the ones who need it most are always the ones holding the least of it. But if you're asking whether I falsified returns—" I spread my hands. "You're asking a census taker if he counted things. Yes. Counting is the entire profession. The question is whether I counted correctly, and for that you'd want my field books, which I assume you've already seized."

"We have your official books." He said *official* with a small weight on it, the way a man sets down a stone he intends to drop later.

"Then let's look at them together," I said pleasantly. "I find errors go faster with two pairs of eyes."

What I did not say — what I held very still inside myself, like a full cup carried across a crowded room — was that the books on his table were, in every particular that mattered, Wendell's work. Three nights before, when the summons had come down through the guild channels that certain takers would be "reviewed," I had gone to the print shop after closing and laid my true records on Wendell's bench like a confession. Wendell had looked at them for a long moment — fourteen months of testimony, of names gathered from widows and runaways and the vanished-adjacent, written in my hand, cross-referenced in my private cipher — and then had said only, "How many pages does the official version need?"

And then my printer had sat down and forged me a history.

Not blank pages. Blank pages are the first thing an examiner looks for. Wendell had produced a complete, plausible, boring set of field books — the same households, the same dates, the same ink stains in the same corners, but scrubbed clean of every irregular dependent, every exempted soul, every name that might lead back to the shop or the orchard or the dead. And because Wendell understood paper the way a priest understands scripture, the forgery was *better* than my originals. My real books had smudges where I'd written in the rain. The forgeries had smudges too — placed, deliberate, artful. My real books had a water stain from the night Pip spilled tea on me. So did theirs, in the same place, aged with something Wendell mixed from tea and iron gall until it looked six months old.

"You can't forge wear," Wendell had told me, blowing on the last page. "Wear is just time doing the printing for you. You can only *arrange* to have had some." Then Wendell had pressed the finished books into my hands and said, with a steadiness I did not feel capable of borrowing, "Voss will look at the bindings first. Binders tell the truth even when liars don't. Maren checked them against guild stock. They're old covers, real spines, rebacked properly. If he knows books at all, he'll believe the object before he doubts the contents."

So now I watched Voss open my false life, and I made myself think about bindings.

* * *

He went slowly. Good examiners do, and Voss was excellent, which meant that for two hours I sat in a chair under a lamp while a man read my handwriting — my handwriting, or close enough that even I had done a double-take — and searched it for the seam.

"The Halloway household," he said eventually. "Cooper's Lane. You record five persons. The parish rolls show four."

"The parish rolls show whoever the parish likes," I said. "There's a grandmother in that house who hasn't left the upper floor since the winter her son was taken. She doesn't attend church. She doesn't buy bread — the neighbors bring it. As far as the parish is concerned she died years ago. As far as I'm concerned, she eats supper every night, and I counted her."

"Why?"

It was the question under all the questions, and we both knew it. Why does a census taker count a woman the state prefers forgotten?

"Because she's there," I said. "That's the job. You count what's there. If the Registry wants to count something else — countable citizens, loyal subjects, persons of proper standing — it should issue different forms. I work with the forms I'm given, and the form says *persons residing in household*. Grandmother Halloway resides. Therefore grandmother Halloway goes in the book." I shrugged. "If that's irregular, the irregularity is in the form."

Voss studied me for a long moment. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the corridor a cart of ledgers squeaked past on bad wheels, and I thought, absurdly, of Bram's hand-cart in the orchard rows, and made my face do nothing at all.

"You have a reputation," Voss said, "for thoroughness."

"I have a reputation for walking every street I'm assigned. Most takers sample. Sampling is faster and it's cheating."

"And yet." He turned another page — my forged page, Wendell's forged page, and I felt the strangest vertigo, watching my own invented honesty examined for fraud. "In fourteen months of thoroughness, Taker Ash, your returns contain no patterns. No clustering. Every anomaly stands alone, explicable, local, dull. Do you know what that tells an examiner?"

I had a suspicion it told him that someone with a compositor's eye for detail had edited out every thread a pull might catch. But aloud I said, "That my district is dull?"

"It tells me that either your district is remarkably free of the disorder affecting every other quarter of this city — which the Provost's office strongly doubts — or that you are the most consistently unremarkable recorder this office has ever employed." He closed the folder. "Consistency, Mr. Ash, is its own kind of signature. Perfectly regular handwriting is a sign of forgery in banknotes. Did you know that?"

My heart performed a maneuver I chose not to acknowledge. "I know banknotes," I said. "I don't write them. And I don't write my returns twice, if that's the next question, because no one pays me enough to do anything once, let alone twice."

For the first time, Voss almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, like frost forming on a window you're trying to see through.

"Let us speak plainly," he said. "The city is becoming difficult. Bread, printers, committees, fires in the night. The Registry has been asked to identify takers whose returns might prove... useful. To the coming inquiries. Useful either way, if you take my meaning." He laced his fingers. "A taker who notices grandmothers the parish misses is a taker who notices other things. I am offering you the chance to demonstrate that what you notice is loyalty."

"There it is," I said.

"Pardon?"

"The actual charge. It took you two hours. Most examiners lead with it and pad the rest. I respect the craftsmanship, honestly."

"Mr. Ash—"

"Here is what I notice," I said, and leaned forward, and gave him the performance of my life, which is saying something, because I had recently watched a printer lie with typeface and nearly believed it myself. "I notice that this city has more doors than registers, and that the difference between the two grows every month, and that when the reckoning comes — whichever reckoning — the Registry will want takers who can produce accurate books quickly, because accurate books are the only thing anyone in this building will still trust. You don't want my loyalty, Examiner. Loyalty is cheap and everyone's selling. You want my *returns*. So keep me employed, pay me the same insulting wage, and I'll keep counting what's actually there instead of what the parish wishes were there. When the inquiries come, you'll have the only honest ledger in Vellham, and you can decide what it proves."

Silence. The lamp buzzed. My pulse was a drum I kept behind a door.

"You realize," Voss said quietly, "that a man who talks like that could as easily be describing sedition as service."

"A man who talks like that," I said, "is describing a census. Everything else is your reading."

* * *

He dismissed me at dusk — "We may require further sessions, Taker Ash" — and I walked out through those low corridors with my hat in my hand and my knees conducting negotiations of their own. At the outer desk a clerk returned to me the satchel they had taken at the door, and I signed a receipt for my own belongings, because that is the world we lived in: even your pockets required paperwork.

Outside, Candlewick Street was going lamplit and blue. I made myself walk at a normal pace past three corners before I allowed my legs to shake, and then I walked another mile in the wrong direction entirely, doubled back through the fish market, and came to the orchard wall as the last light failed.

Maren was waiting inside the gap in the hedge, arms crossed, which meant Wendell had sent her, which meant Wendell had spent the entire day pretending to cut type and cutting nothing.

"Well?" she said.

"They kept my satchel for six hours and returned it with the buckles re-threaded wrong. Left over right. Someone opened it and wasn't careful putting it back." I handed it through the hedge to her. "Which means they'll do it again, and next time they'll be careful, so everything travels loose from now on and nothing travels at all that we can't afford to lose."

"Wendell asked what you told them."

I climbed through after the satchel. The apple trees stood black and patient around us, and from the direction of the root cellar came the small sounds of children who should have been asleep and were not.

"I told them the truth," I said, "with such conviction that it passed for lying."

Maren snorted. From somewhere among the trees, Wendell stepped out of the dark, and I saw by the shape of the shoulders that the day had been long there too.

"He wants to know about the books," Maren translated.

"The books held." I looked at Wendell directly. "He checked the bindings, exactly as you said. He believed the object. Your wear fooled him — the tea stain, the rain smudges, all of it. Eleven years auditing grain merchants, and he never doubted a page." I paused. "But he said something at the end. About consistency. About perfect regularity being a signature of its own. He didn't press it. He filed it. Men like Voss don't lose thoughts; they shelve them."

Wendell was quiet a moment. "Then we vary the hand," Wendell said at last, in that flat craftsman's voice that meant the fear had already been converted into technique. "Next batch, the entries get tired. Mistakes that get corrected. A page rewritten in different ink. Paper should look *lived in*, Silas. I can age anything except innocence."

"Then age everything," I said. "Because there won't be a next session. There'll be a next arrest, and it won't come with paperwork."

The wind moved through the orchard rows, and far off across the river, faint as a struck match, something glowed and guttered that was not a lamp. Sefa appeared at the edge of the trees, breathless, holding a scrap of paper she'd taken off a dead drop by the tannery, and I read it by Maren's shielded candle:

*Bread riot at Granary Square. Two presses seized by the strikers tonight. The city is starting.*

I looked up at Wendell across the candle flame, and I said the thing we had all been refusing to say:

"Whatever comes next, it comes fast now. We print what's left to print — and then we dig."
