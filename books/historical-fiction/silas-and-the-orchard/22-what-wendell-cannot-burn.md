## 22. What Wendell Cannot Burn

The summons came on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the most insulting day for catastrophe. Monday at least has the decency to announce itself; Wednesday is already halfway to forgiveness. But Tuesday arrives wearing an ordinary face, and it was a Tuesday when Corporal Vess of the Provost's Office stood in my doorway holding a document sealed in red wax, as though the wax itself might intimidate me.

"Master Winterbourne." He had a voice like a drawer of unsorted type — all the pieces there, none of them in order. "By order of the Magistracy, you are instructed to conduct an inventory of your stock and to destroy all materials deemed seditious."

"Seditious," I said, tasting the word the way one tastes milk gone faintly off.

"Broadsheets. Pamphlets. Any matter critical of the lawful government." He handed me the paper. It was signed by a magistrate whose name I knew very well indeed — knew it in lead, fourteen-point, set twice in the archive sleeping beneath a false bottom in my font cabinet not four feet from where we stood.

I read the order with the expression of a man mildly inconvenienced by weather. This is a skill. I have spent years perfecting it, and I will tell you now that it is harder than setting a page of Greek by candlelight. Anyone can look frightened. Looking bored while your heart attempts to exit through your ribs — that is craftsmanship.

"Naturally," I said. "One does one's civic duty."

"One does," said Corporal Vess, and looked past me into the shop with the particular gaze of a man who has been told to look and told what he will find, and resents both instructions equally. "You'll be supervised."

* * *

The supervision was a man named Dree, who arrived the following morning smelling of onions and official importance, and who installed himself in my shop like furniture nobody wanted. He was perhaps twenty-five, with the complexion of a man who took his meals standing up and his orders lying down. The Provost's Office had chosen him, I suspected, for two qualities: his inability to read quickly, and his inability to imagine that anyone else could.

He sat by the window with a list of forbidden titles and watched me work, and I will grant him this: he watched honestly. He simply did not know what he was looking at.

That is the secret of my trade, if you want it. A compositor's bench is a landscape, and to the untrained eye it is only chaos — trays within cases within frames, hundreds of little metal letters in compartments no larger than a thumb, arranged according to a logic older than the regime, older than the city, possibly older than God's patience. Dree saw clutter. I saw a language, and buried inside that language, inside a case of broken sorts that had not printed so much as a wedding invitation in three years, lay the plates of forty-one names that the government of Vellham insisted had never existed.

Forty-one. We had passed forty the week before, when Silas brought the testimony of a ferryman's daughter whose father had been taken off his own boat between one bank and the other, as though the river itself had swallowed him on the regime's behalf.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and Tuesday's fire has not yet been lit.

Dree consulted his list. "You'll begin with the pamphlet stock."

"There is no pamphlet stock," I said truthfully. There hadn't been since the Loyalty Inventory, since Inspector Hask's gloved fingers had come within the width of a hair of the false bottom and been distracted — God forgive me, I still think of it with tenderness — by Pip falling audibly off a barrel outside the window. That boy can fall off anything when required. It may be his true vocation.

"You printed last month." Dree said it like an accusation, which, technically, it was. Printing being legal, this confused even him, and he frowned at his own sentence as if it had misbehaved.

"I print every month. Funeral notices. Price lists. A run of hymnals for the Chapel of the Ninth Hour." I let my hands do a small, eloquent gesture of commerce. "Sedition pays poorly, Master Dree. Ask any printer."

I should not have said *any printer*. Something moved behind his eyes — not intelligence, exactly, but the shadow it casts. He wrote something down. I have wondered since what it was. Possibly my name. Possibly *sedition pays poorly*, transcribed phonetically and underlined three times. With men like Dree you never know whether you are planting doubt or watering it.

* * *

The burning took place in the yard behind the shop, in the iron brazier I use for melting spoiled castings, because the Provost's Office required that destruction be witnessed, documented, and performed with a solemnity appropriate to the occasion. Dree stood with his list. I stood with my arms full of paper.

Here is what I carried out, item by item, over the course of an afternoon:

First, the decoy stock Maren and I had prepared weeks earlier, back when the net began tightening and Silas, with his census-taker's genius for anticipating what officials will demand, said the words that have kept me alive more than once: *"They'll ask you to burn something. Make sure you have something worth burning."*

So we had made sedition. Real-looking, plausible, gloriously incriminating sedition — half-printed broadsheets calling for the abolition of the grain levy, pamphlets with deliberately smudged type and a fake imprint from a press in Kettering that had closed two years before anyone would bother checking. Maren aged them with tea and ash. Sefa crumpled and re-flattened them until they looked loved and feared in equal measure. Pip contributed a coffee stain he described as "artistic," and I did not argue, because the boy has a better instinct for evidence than half the magistracy.

Second, genuine old papers of mine that were merely foolish — student broadsheets from my youth, arguments about typography dressed up as arguments about liberty, the kind of thing every young printer produces and every old printer prays stays in the attic. These went onto the fire with a sincerity that cost me nothing and lent the whole performance its credibility.

Third — and here is where I must be honest, because this account is for whoever finds it, and honesty is the entire point of the enterprise — third were two pages that were not decoys at all. Proof sheets of testimonies we had already printed and safely distributed, copies of copies, redundant. They burned, and their names went up in smoke over the yard of the Winterbourne Foundry, and I watched each of them go, and I said each name silently as the flame took it. The cooper's wife. The student from the registry. The ferryman. It felt less like destroying evidence than like a funeral conducted backwards — the body going up instead of down, the soul remaining firmly in my keeping.

Dree checked items against his list. His lips moved. The fire crackled with what I can only describe as civic enthusiasm.

"You're very thorough," he said at one point, watching me feed the flames sheet by sheet rather than dumping the lot, because a guilty man dumps, and an innocent craftsman is methodical, and methodical was the role and I have never broken character yet.

"My father taught me that paper is debt," I said. "Every sheet you print, you owe the truth of it to somebody."

He considered this. "That sounds almost seditious."

"It sounds like bookkeeping," I said, and he laughed, and wrote something down.

* * *

Now. Here is the part I have circled around for two pages, the way you circle a hot stove knowing you must eventually touch it.

While the decoys burned — while I stood in my own yard performing loyalty with onion-scented supervision and a straight face — what I felt was not fear. Fear was there, yes, humming along underneath like the press itself, familiar, almost companionable. But what rose in me, what I had to keep pressing down lest it surface on my face where Dree could see it, was something I did not have a word for until the fire had burned down to embers and Dree had stamped his inventory form and left, and I was alone in the yard with the ash.

It was anger. No — anger implies heat, and this was cold. It was clarity.

Because here is what I understood, standing over that brazier: I had just committed treason. Not the paper treason of the decoys — the real kind, the capital kind. Every sheet I fed the flames while keeping the true archive intact was a lie told to the state, and the state kills people for smaller lies than mine. Forty-one families' worth of smaller lies. If they found the false bottom in the font case — if Dree had pulled one tray six inches further, if Hask had been less distractible, if any of a hundred small mercies had failed to arrive — then everything I am ends on a scaffold or in a cell, and the ledger of my life closes marked *seditious printer, executed*.

And do you know what I felt about that?

Pride.

Not the trembling, hopeful pride of a man who might get away with it. The settled kind. The kind that sits in the chest like well-set type sits in its chase — locked, tight, immovable. Somewhere between the Loyalty Inventory and this Tuesday's fire, without my noticing the exact moment, the performance had stopped being a performance. I had spent my whole adult life being two people: the Master Wendell who cut type and paid taxes and bowed correctly, and the person I actually was, living in the space between candlelight and dawn, cutting the names of the erased into lead so they would outlast the men who erased them.

I had thought of those as separate lives, one armor, one flesh. Standing over the ashes of my own decoys, I understood they were the same life. The armor was flesh. There had never been two of us. There was only ever one, and his name — all of his names — was on the record, in the archive, in lead, where no committee of public anything could strike them.

My father built this foundry to make letters. Letters that say things. He used to hold a sort up to the light and say, "Wendell, this is the smallest machine ever invented, and it has toppled more governments than artillery." I thought he meant it as poetry. He meant it as instruction.

I swept the ashes myself. That too is part of the craft — you never leave your fire for someone else to read.

* * *

Silas came after dark, as he always does, entering through the paper store with the furtive grace of a man who has spent years making himself administratively invisible. He read my face before I said anything. He is maddeningly good at that. Census takers learn to see households whole, he once told me — not the rooms but what the rooms are hiding.

"Well?" he asked. "How did it burn?"

"Beautifully. Pip's coffee stain drew particular scrutiny. Dree examined it for the better part of a minute. I believe he suspects the Kettering press of revolutionary sympathies and poor hygiene."

Something loosened around his eyes, which for Silas constitutes open celebration. Then, because he is Silas, he went immediately to the font case and checked the false bottom, and then the plates, counting them by touch in the dark the way a miser counts coin, and then, satisfied, he sat down on my casting bench as if it were an ordinary evening and nothing in the world were on fire anywhere.

"You're different tonight," he said.

"I committed treason today."

"We committed treason in the autumn. You've barely mentioned it since."

"That was treason by correspondence. This was treason in person, in daylight, under supervision, with a signature." I turned my hands over, looked at them — my father's hands, ink in every crease that soap has long since given up on, fine enough once to draw comment and now calloused enough to pass. "Do you know what I thought, while the decoys went up? I thought: let them catch me. Let them find it. Because whatever they do to me, the pages exist. The names exist. And I am not — I am not the man they think they'd be arresting. I'm not a printer who strayed. I'm the record keeper of forty-one people the state murdered, and I have never in my life been more certain of what I am."

Silas was quiet a moment. In the dark I heard him breathe once, slowly, the way he does when something matters too much for his usual economy of words to carry it.

"When they took Tomas," he said finally — his brother, whose name started all of this, whom I have never met and feel I know the way I know a well-set page — "the hardest part wasn't the grief. It was that officially, nothing had happened. There was nothing to grieve. Grief needs a record, Wendell. That's what I learned. You can bury a body, but you cannot mourn a name that never existed." He looked toward the font case, toward the lead sleeping inside it. "You gave them back their names. All of them. Whatever else happens —"

Whatever else happens. He didn't finish it, and I didn't ask him to, because we both heard the same sound at the same moment and neither of us needed the sentence completed.

Boots. More than one pair, moving up Copper Lane with the unhurried confidence of men who intend to knock, and who have never once knocked without meaning it.

Silas was through the paper store before the second boot fell. I put out the candle, and stood in the dark of my own shop, listening, my heart doing its old familiar attempt at escape and my hands — my steady, traitorous, honest hands — already drifting toward the press lever, as though a press could stop what was coming up the lane.

The knocking, when it came, was not Corporal Vess's knock.
