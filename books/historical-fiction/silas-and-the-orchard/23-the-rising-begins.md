## 23. The Rising Begins

I had set down exactly forty-one words of the cooper's widow's testimony — the forty-second word was "husband," and I remember this because the bell interrupted me before I could reach it — when the church bells of Vellham began to ring out of order.

Every city has a grammar of bells. The morning bells speak in threes; the funeral bells in ones, spaced like a man walking to his own grave; the fire bells tumble over each other like children down a stair. What came across the river that evening was none of these. It was every bell at once, all eleven parishes, no pattern at all — which is not chaos, if you know how to listen. It is agreement. Eleven parishes that cannot abide one another had somehow agreed on something, and there is only one thing in Vellham that has ever managed that.

I put down my pen.

Then, being the sort of man who finishes what he starts even as the world ends, I wrote *husband*, and put a period after it, and blotted the page, because whatever else happened tonight, Marta Kellin's husband was going to be in the record with his name spelled correctly and his sentence complete.

The smoke came next. Not the grey domestic smoke of supper fires but a black, greasy column rising from the direction of the Custom House, leaning eastward on the river wind like a drunk being walked home. And under the bells, fainter, a sound I had only read about in foreign dispatches and had privately suspected of exaggeration: a great many people shouting the same thing at the same time.

I gathered my pages into their oilcloth, tied it, and went out into a city that had stopped pretending.

* * *

Vellham by night is usually a city of shutters. That night it was a city of doorways, and every doorway held people — not watching from behind glass but standing in the open, which in nine years of the Provost's rule was its own kind of riot. A fishwife stood on her step with her arms folded, surveying the street like an admiral. Two clerks from the Customs Hall walked past arm in arm, still wearing their badges, having apparently decided that the fastest way to be on the right side of history was to remove the badge later. An old man was sweeping his doorstep with tremendous dignity, as though the revolution were a bit of mud tracked in and he would have it out before it stained.

"Evening, sir," said the old man, without looking up.

"Is it?" I said.

He considered. "It might be."

That, I thought, was the most honest assessment of the situation anyone would offer all night.

I went the long way, through Tanner's Row, because the direct route ran past the guard post on Weir Street and I had no interest in explaining my satchel to anyone, loyalist or otherwise. From the bridge I could see them: barricades going up along the quay like crocuses after a hard winter — here a cart overturned, there a stack of herring barrels, there a wagon dragged sideways across the road by twenty pairs of hands, and everywhere the small industrious figures of people tearing up cobblestones. There is a sound paving stones make when they come loose, a wet click and grind, and I will hear it in my sleep for years. They were building fortifications out of their own streets. It occurred to me, watching, that a city is only ever borrowed from itself, and tonight Vellham was calling in the loan.

A boy of perhaps ten ran past me toward the bridge carrying an armload of kindling, breathless with importance.

"Is it started, then?" I asked him.

"It's been started," he said, scandalized at my ignorance, "since Tuesday," and vanished into the dark.

Since Tuesday. All those weeks of bread riots and strike committees and broadsheets, which I had catalogued in my head as symptoms, were in fact a pulse. I had been taking the temperature of a fever and calling it weather.

* * *

The print shop was lit.

That was the second wrong thing, after the bells. Wendell Winterbourne did not light the shop after dark unless working, and working meant the archive, and the archive meant that if any of tonight's enthusiastic patriots happened past the windows, they would see exactly what the Committee of Public Record would spend the next decade shooting people for.

I let myself in with the key Maren had cut for me back when trust between us was still a formal arrangement rather than a settled fact. Inside, Wendell stood at the stone in shirt-sleeves, sleeves actually rolled, hair escaping its pins in a way that would have caused a sensation among certain of my colleagues at the Census Office, inking a form with one hand while Bram — perched on a stool far too tall for him, officially forbidden from touching anything, touching everything — handed up letters one at a time with the gravity of a squire arming a knight.

"You're printing," I said. "During the revolution."

"I'm finishing a page during the revolution." Wendell did not look up. "There's a difference. The page doesn't care what's happening outside. That's rather the point of it." A pause; the brayer rolled; ink went down smooth and black as a night sky. "Also, if I stop now, I'll lose my place, and if I lose my place I'll say something unforgivable, so do come in and close the door."

"The bells—"

"I heard the bells."

"And the smoke—"

"I can see the smoke from the window, Silas, I'm not blind, I'm *composing*." But Wendell looked up then, and what was in that face wasn't fear or indifference. It was the expression I had learned to read over months of candlelit nights: the look of a person holding two true things at once and refusing to drop either. Joy and dread, in equal measure, balanced like a sheet of paper on the tympan.

Sefa appeared in the doorway from the back room, where she'd been sorting quires, a rag over her shoulder and her chin set at the angle that meant she had opinions. "Pip says there are crowds on Cordwainer Street and someone's already taken the powder store."

"Someone's *what*?"

"Took it. Opened it. Handed it out." She shrugged, thirteen years old and unimpressed by armed insurrection, or determined to be seen to be. "Maren says we're to keep printing till she's back, and lock the door after her, and if anyone knocks claiming to be rebels we're to ask them which ward they're enrolled in, because real ones will know."

Maren Quill: running a bindery, three apprentices, and now, apparently, civil defense, with the same iron efficiency she applied to sewing signatures.

"She went *out*?" I said.

"Maren went to get the cart," Sefa said, as though this explained everything, and in fairness it did.

* * *

It is strange, the things one notices in the middle of history. While the city outside rang and burned and shouted itself hoarse, what I noticed was the sound of the press. Wendell pulled the bar; the screw groaned; the platen came down with a soft decisive clap, and a page existed that had not existed before. Pull. Groan. Clap. It was the most orderly sound in Vellham. Against eleven parishes of unruly bells, against the crackle off the Custom House, that steady rhythm said: *something can still be finished.*

Bram fetched the fresh sheets and laid them on the drying line, tongue between his teeth, handling each page like a bird that might startle. He was eight. When this began he was seven. I try not to think about what that means, and fail regularly.

"That's the student," Bram said, pointing to a name midway down the form. "The one whose mother came. Tomas."

"Tomas Ellery," Wendell confirmed quietly.

"He's dead, isn't he."

"Yes."

"But he's in the book now."

"Yes," Wendell said again, and pulled the bar. Groan. Clap. Tomas Ellery, printed in four hundred impressions, more copies of his existence than the Provost's men had ever allowed him while he breathed.

I stood in the doorway with my oilcloth satchel against my chest like a breastplate and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, why I had spent nine years walking door to door asking questions no one wanted answered. Not for the census. The census was never the point. The census was just the only legal way a man could go about asking a whole city *who is missing from your table*, and writing it down.

"Silas." Wendell had paused, inked fingers hovering. "You've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one you make when you're about to say something you think is very clever and we'll all pretend is comforting."

"The bells mean the garrison's split," I said. "Eleven parishes don't agree on God, let alone rebellion. If the river regiments have joined, the Provost holds maybe the Citadel and the Custom House and nothing between. By morning either it's over or it's truly begun."

"Which is it?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. That's what the bells are *for* — everyone ringing until somebody wins."

Bram looked up at that, stricken. "Nobody told me there was a *winning*."

"There always is," Sefa said grimly, from her quires. "That's the part you have to watch."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I have rarely heard the political science put better.

* * *

Maren returned at half past ten with the cart, a lantern, and a bleeding lip, in that order of importance to her narrative.

"Dockworkers took the Weir Street post without firing a shot," she reported, setting the lantern on the composing stone — Wendell made a noise like a kettle — "because the sergeant opened the doors himself and asked could he join. Half the watch has gone over. The other half has gone home and hung their coats on the hedge, which amounts to the same thing." She wiped her lip with the heel of her hand, examined the blood without interest. "A lieutenant didn't care for my tone near the bridge. His loss. I got the paper."

"You were seen?" Wendell asked. Always the question. Always the right question.

"I was seen buying paper, from Ostler's, with money, like a customer. If the new government wants to arrest me for that they'll have to form a queue behind the old one." She began unloading reams with Pip, who had materialized from somewhere and moved boxes with the silent competence of a child who has learned that usefulness is safety. "Now. Somebody tell me what the bells mean, because Pip's given me three versions and none of them agree with each other or with physics."

I gave them my reading — garrison split, Provost reduced to the Citadel, the city ours or nobody's by dawn. Maren listened with her arms crossed, nodding at intervals like a woman pricing goods.

"So," she said. "Either we wake up free tomorrow, or we wake up in the middle of a war with our names in a box under an apple tree." A beat. "Same as yesterday, really, except louder."

Wendell laughed. I want to record that properly, because Wendell's laughter was rare enough that each instance deserved documentation, and this one was startled out of them, brief and genuine, there among the drying sheets and the ink and the smoke-smell drifting through the shutters. "Maren, you are a comfort and a terror."

"I've been called worse by better."

* * *

We worked until nearly two.

That was the decision, made quickly and without ceremony, the way all our real decisions were made: the press run would continue tonight because tomorrow might offer no press, no quiet, no certainty that the shop still stood. Four hundred pages of testimony, the dead of nine years set in lead — the cooper's husband, Tomas Ellery, the women from the tannery, the boy from the registry whose name I had copied out in a fair hand three winters ago while his mother watched me with eyes like closed doors. Every pull of the bar was an argument against erasure, and we pulled it in shifts, and outside the world rearranged itself.

I took a turn at the tympan, badly; Wendell corrected my grip twice and gave up and assigned me to folding, which even I could manage. Pip and Sefa collated. Bram, ordered repeatedly to sleep, slept sitting upright on his stool with his cheek against a bundle of spoil sheets, and no one moved him, because moving him would have required admitting we noticed, and noticing felt too tender for a night like this.

Somewhere around one o'clock, between pulls, Wendell spoke without stopping work.

"They'll seize the presses, you know. Whoever wins."

I kept folding. "What makes you say that?"

"Because it's the first thing I'd do." Clap. Another page. "The old regime burned pamphlets. The new one will want to *license* them. It's kinder, and it works better. You don't need to silence printers if you can employ them." Wendell looked at me across the stone, ink to the knuckles, lamplight making the exhaustion visible at last. "Silas, they're going to come to *you*. You know that, yes? A census taker who kept records. The Committee of Public Record or whatever they christen themselves by lunchtime — they'll want a man who understands archives. A man who knows how to count a population and leave things out."

The word *leave things out* sat in the room like a third person.

"And you'll say yes," Wendell went on, quieter. "You'll have to say yes, or they'll wonder why not, and a man who wonders why not starts looking at why. So you'll sit in their office and help build their beautiful official history, and every name we've set in lead tonight will be left out of it, tidily, professionally, with a citation."

Groan. Clap. The platen rose.

"So I suggest," Wendell said, laying the next forme, "that we finish."

And that is how the revolution arrived for us: not with trumpets but with a work schedule. The bells rang on. The smoke leaned east. Somewhere downriver a crowd was singing something tuneless and enormous, and a city that had spent nine years holding its breath finally exhaled — into fire, into hope, into both at once, which is the only way revolutions ever come.

At half past two we printed the last sheet. Wendell pulled it, held it to the lamp, checked the impression with a craftsman's squint, and passed it around so that each of us — Maren, Pip, Sefa, sleeping-Bram-by-proxy, me — touched it once. Four hundred copies of nine years of the disappeared, finished at last, complete but for one thing.

Because Maren, counting the stack with her quick binder's eye, frowned and said:

"This is the master copy. Which means the moment the sun's up, every faction in Vellham is going to be hunting every press in the print district." She looked at Wendell, then at me, and her voice dropped into the register she used for invoices and ultimatums. "So before that happens, somebody tell me exactly where we're keeping it — and pray it isn't anywhere obvious."
