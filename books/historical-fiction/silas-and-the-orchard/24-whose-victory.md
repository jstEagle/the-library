## 24. Whose Victory

The bells started before dawn, and for the first time in my life I heard them without counting.

That is what fear does to a person, in case you've never had the pleasure. For eleven years I have heard the bells of Vellham as arithmetic — how long since the patrol passed, how long until the shop must open, how long I have been standing at this window pretending to admire the weather. On the morning the Rising came to the print district, the bells rang wild and unscheduled and glorious, all of them at once, and I simply stood there and listened like a man hearing music after a long illness.

"Up," I said to the empty shop, because Maren wasn't there to say it to me. "They've done it."

I should note, for honesty's sake, that I did not do anything. The Rising happened around me the way weather happens around a stone. I set type; the city caught fire; somewhere between the third forme and the fourth, the old regime stopped being the government of Vellham and became merely the men who used to be. I would like to report that I marched, that I threw a cobblestone, that I stood on a barricade with my shirt open and my heart full. What I actually did was finish setting a page of testimonies — the cooper's wife's sister, two names I hadn't yet cast — because the page was due and because, whatever else changed that day, the page was still due.

Maren arrived at mid-morning smelling of woodsmoke and triumph, Pip and Sefa behind her like ducklings who had recently discovered they were wolves.

"It's over," she said. "The Provost's house is burning. They're calling it Liberation Square already." She said the word *liberation* the way one tastes a strange fruit — pleased, cautious, ready to spit.

"And the Committee?"

"Forming. There are broadsheets everywhere — every underground press in the city running flat out. Half of them can't spell 'liberation,' but nobody's quibbling." She grinned, and for one whole moment we were simply people grinning in a print shop while history went by outside like a parade.

Then Sefa said, from the doorway, in her flat, careful way: "There are men at the corner taking down the names of every printer on the street."

And there it was. The fruit turned in the mouth.

* * *

I will say this for the rebels: they moved fast. Within two days of the Provost's fall they had done more organizing than the old regime had managed in a decade, which tells you something about both parties. There was a Committee of Public Safety, a Committee of Supply, a Committee of Bells (I am not certain about the last one, but given the racket, someone was being paid). And on the third morning, a printed notice appeared pasted to every door in the print district, set in — I could tell at ten paces, and it hurt me physically — badly kerned Casconian.

*TO ALL KEEPERS OF PRESSES IN THE DISTRICT OF VELLHAM: In the new order, the press belongs to the people. All masters shall register their works, declare their stock, and swear the Oath of the Free Word. Unregistered presses are by definition instruments of reaction.*

Silas read it aloud to us in the orchard that evening, holding the paper at arm's length as though it might bite. "'By definition,'" he repeated. "You see what they've done? It's the census trick. You don't outlaw a thing — you define it into illegality. The old regime made testimony irregular. The new one makes an unregistered press reactionary. Same sentence, different ink."

"They're printing oaths," Wendell said, which was me, saying something obvious, which is a habit of mine under stress.

"They're printing everything," Silas said. "That's the trouble. A revolution is a press run, Wendell. Someone has to set the forme. And whoever sets it decides what the page says."

Maren took the notice, folded it with great precision, and put it in the stove. Nobody commented. Some arguments are settled best by combustion.

* * *

The seizure of presses began, as these things always begin, politely.

A delegation of three came to Winterbourne's on the fourth morning — a young woman with a red armband and a ledger, a broad-shouldered man whose hands had clearly never touched a case in his life, and a boy of perhaps sixteen whose job appeared to be carrying things and looking earnest. The woman introduced herself as Citizen Verel, of the Committee of Public Record, and asked to inspect our works.

"Certainly," I said, and showed them a shop containing exactly one working hand press, one font of worn Casconian, one font of broken sorts I described as "rubbish awaiting recasting," and absolutely nothing else. Eleven years of hiding things gives a person a tidy shop. My mother's sampler hung crookedly on the wall; my father's portrait — stern, respectable, entirely fictional in its respectability — presided over the composing stone. I had spent a decade building a room that lied fluently, and I confess I felt a flicker of pride watching it lie for the new administration as smoothly as it had for the old.

"You're Master Winterbourne," Citizen Verel said, checking her list. "Registered household head, Winterbourne Foundry and Press."

"I am."

"No dependents of irregular standing?" She said it lightly, administratively, a box to tick. Beside her, the broad man examined my press with the expression of a horse studying a watch.

"None," I said, and my voice did not shake, and I hated that I'd had to practice that.

She wrote it down. Then she explained the new arrangement: the press would remain nominally mine, but its output belonged to the people. I would be issued daily copy — proclamations, notices, the Oath itself — and required to print it promptly and faithfully. In exchange, the district would protect me, feed me, and consider me a friend of the Revolution.

"And if I decline?"

"Oh, no one declines," she said pleasantly. "Declining is how a press becomes reactionary. We'd hate that. You seem like a careful man." She glanced around the shop once more, and her eyes snagged — just for a heartbeat — on my hands. On fingers too fine, some would say too slender, for a master printer's story, if you believed such stories needed fingers of an approved shape.

I have been read by experts: provosts, inspectors, a census taker with eyes like awls. Citizen Verel was not an expert. She saw only a curiosity, filed it nowhere, and left with her committee and her boy and her ledger full of loyal printers.

But I stood in the doorway afterward feeling cold in a warm street, because I understood, finally and completely, what we had built the archive against. Not the old regime. Regimes generally. The human talent for deciding whose words count — and doing it with a form and a pleasant voice.

* * *

That night the district printed the Oath of the Free Word four hundred times over, and I set part of the type myself.

I want that recorded plainly, because I am not proud of it and I refuse to pretend otherwise: my hands set the oath, and my hands set it well. *I swear fealty to the sovereign people and to none beside, and hold my press in trust for the common truth.* Lovely sentiment. Rotten leading. I tightened it, corrected a turned letter in "sovereign," and pulled thirty proofs before midnight, each one crisp enough to frame.

Silas found me at the wash-up, sleeves rolled, arms black to the elbow.

"You're good at that," he said quietly.

"At printing lies? Yes. It's my trade." I wiped a roller harder than the roller deserved. "Eleven years, Silas. I told myself the performance was temporary — keep the foundry, wait out the storm. Then the storm changed direction and I'm still performing, same lines, new audience." I looked at him. "Do you know what the worst part is? When they read out the oath, part of me meant it. I *do* want the common truth. That's rather the whole point of my last year. And so I stand there swearing to a committee that will burn the very thing I swore it to protect, and I mean it and I don't mean it, and both are true."

"That's not hypocrisy," Silas said. He sat down on the paper stool across from me, the way he does when a thought has been following him all day and he's finally letting it catch up. "My brother swore an oath to the old regime. Guild loyalty, when he was seventeen. Everyone did; it meant nothing; it was weather. Then the registry struck his name and the oath everyone swore suddenly counted against everyone who'd sworn it carelessly. Words don't betray you, Wendell. Definitions do." He tapped the damp proof of the Oath drying on the line. "This paper isn't the danger. The ledger behind it is. Verel's book of registered presses is going to become, in about a month, exactly what the census rolls were — a list of who exists, kept by people who get to decide."

"So we're back where we started."

"We're never back where we started. But yes — the wheel goes round." He was quiet a moment. "There's something else. The Committee has announced a Bureau of Official History. They're inviting clerks. Men who kept records under the old regime, provided their returns were regular."

I felt the floor tilt slightly, though it was only my stomach. "Silas. Your returns were nothing of the kind. You falsified half a decade of household counts. If anyone looks—"

"Verel's list has my name on it. Census takers who served faithfully." His mouth did the dry almost-smile that serves him instead of laughing. "They think I'm exactly the sort of careful, obedient record-keeper a new state needs. Which, in fairness, I am. I'm just not theirs."

"What will you do?"

He looked at the drying proof of the Oath, then at the hidden drawer where the archive's master pages lived behind a false back I'd built from a broken font case, and I watched him arrive, visibly, at the answer.

"I'll go," he said. "I'll listen. A record office is just an orchard with better lighting, Wendell — a place where names go in and come out changed. I want to know what comes out changed, and in whose handwriting." He stood, and straightened his coat, and for a moment he looked every inch the bureaucrat he had spent years pretending to be, which I suppose means he'd stopped pretending somewhere along the way and simply become fluent.

"Silas," I said. "If they ask you to write the official account of the disappearances —"

"Then I'll learn what they want written," he said. "And we'll know precisely which names are in danger of being lost twice." At the door he paused. "Get the archive finished. Whatever happens next, the pages matter more than any of us. Especially now. *Especially* now."

* * *

The trees were bare when we buried nothing that week, and heavy with green when the summons came, but that is getting ahead of myself. What matters is the night after Silas left for the Bureau, when Maren, the children, and I met among the apples to take stock, the way farmers do after hail.

Pip counted our assets on his fingers: one hidden press under the root cellar, disassembled and greased and waiting; four hundred pages of testimony printed and sewn; a second, unsewn stack; the plates in the broken font case; and "Master Wendell's face, which fools everybody." Sefa added, gravely, "And Citizen Verel thinks the shop is clean." Bram, who was seven and had appointed himself our moral faculty, said only: "We still have the names. Names are the whole thing. Everything else is furniture."

Out of the mouths of orphans. Though I suppose we were all orphans of something by then — of a country, of a cause, of the pleasant fiction that a rising was the same thing as a rescue.

Because here is what I learned in those first weeks of the new order, watching wagons carry seized presses through streets hung with red bunting, watching printers I'd known for twenty years sign oaths with shaking hands and relieved ones alike, watching the word *reactionary* acquire the exact weight the word *irregular* used to have:

The dead do not care who wins. The dead cared only that someone, someday, writes their names down truly. And every government we will ever have — red armband or gold braid, committee or crown — will contain a Citizen Verel with a ledger and a pleasant voice, explaining that the record must be regular, that the page must serve, that some names are simply not convenient this season.

We had won the war against the men who erased people. We had not yet begun the war against the habit.

So when, three mornings later, Silas came down Copper Lane at a pace far too quick for a bureaucrat, coat buttoned wrong, and said only, "Wendell. The Bureau has the old disappearance files. All of them. And tomorrow they start deciding which ones were ever real" — I put down my composing stick, closed the shop, and went with him.

Some pages cannot wait for either side's permission.
