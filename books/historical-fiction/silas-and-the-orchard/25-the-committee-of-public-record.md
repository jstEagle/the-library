## 25. The Committee of Public Record

The invitation came on cream paper with a gilt border, which was how I knew before opening it that it was a lie. The regime we'd just buried had never bothered with gilt. They'd summoned you with a stamp and a threat, and at least you knew where you stood — usually against a wall.

*The Committee of Public Record requests the presence of Silas Ash, formerly Census Taker Second Class, to advise upon the establishment of the Official History Office of the Free City of Vellham. Your service to the people's memory is noted and valued.*

Noted and valued. I read it three times by the window of Maren's bindery, while Bram practiced his letters at the table behind me, tongue between his teeth, laboring over a B that kept coming out backwards.

"They want *you*," Maren said, when I showed her. She said it the way she said everything, flat as a pressing board, but her eyebrows had opinions.

"It appears so."

"You spent eleven years counting heads for the old bastards. Now the new bastards want counting too."

"That," I said, "is more or less the shape of my entire life."

Bram looked up. "What's a bastard?"

"A person who works for a committee," Maren said, and went back to her glue pot.

I should explain what the Committee of Public Record actually was, because the name was doing more work than a dray horse. It had been announced from the steps of the Custom House nine days after the barricades came down, in one of those proclamations written in the grammar of liberty and the vocabulary of ledgers. *The people shall no longer have their history written by their masters.* Good line. I might have believed it if the second sentence hadn't been about standardization.

There was to be an office. There were to be clerks. There would be *a single authoritative account* of the Rising and of the years of the Provost's rule, compiled under committee supervision, to be taught in schools, quoted in newspapers, and engraved — this was the part that made me laugh until Maren threatened me with the bone folder — engraved upon a commemorative tablet in Founder's Square.

A single authoritative account. In my trade, we had another word for that. We called it a first edition with all the errata suppressed.

* * *

They received me in the Custom House itself, which the rebels had taken over without changing so much as the wallpaper. That was the first thing I noticed: they had moved in like tenants who liked the furniture. The same marble floor that had echoed with the boots of provosts' men now echoed with the boots of citizens' deputies, and the echo sounded identical. Marble doesn't care whose feet it flatters.

The man waiting for me was named Deputy Oren Vasse. I knew him slightly, or knew *of* him — he'd been a typesetter at the *Vellham Courier* before the war, one of the underground men, the ones who printed broadsheets in cellars with ink still wet on their cuffs. A hero, by the current reckoning. He was perhaps forty, quick-eyed, with the permanent faint stoop of a man who'd spent decades bent over a composing stone, and when he shook my hand his grip was a compositor's grip: precise, brief, assessing weight.

"Silas Ash." He smiled. "The census man. You know, half the strike committees used your returns. Best maps of the city anyone ever drew — you counted every tenement, every widow, every hungry mouth. Precise work." He said *precise* the way some men say *loyal*.

"I counted what was there."

"Exactly the man for us, then." He led me down a corridor lined with fresh paint drying over older paint, and into a room that had been, judging by the smell, a customs assay office. Now it held long tables, stacks of blank ledger books — good paper, wartime paper being scarce elsewhere, which told you where the priorities lay — and six clerks, young, earnest, dipping pens with the fervor of converts.

On the wall hung a great printed placard, freshly struck, and I confess my eye went to it professionally before it went patriotically. Decent setting. Ragged right, sensible leading, a bold face for the heading:

**THE PEOPLE'S HISTORY SHALL BE WRITTEN BY THE PEOPLE.**

"Fine piece of type," I said.

"We pulled it ourselves, night of the ninth." Vasse touched the corner of it with something like tenderness. "You can see where the E dropped. We didn't correct it. Felt honest, somehow."

It was a nice detail. It was exactly the kind of detail I would have recorded. And that, I thought, is how they'll get you — not with lies but with true details arranged into a false room.

He sat me down and explained the work. The Office would compile the definitive account. Testimonies would be gathered — properly gathered this time, none of the old regime's erasures — witnesses interviewed, documents collected, the whole terrible archive of the Provost's years opened at last and set in order.

"And here," Vasse said, sliding a folder across the table, "is the framework. Chapter headings. Chronology. We've drafted the structure so contributors know where their material fits."

I opened the folder.

Now. I have spent my adult life reading official documents the way a physician reads a face — looking past what is presented to what is withheld. So let me be exact about what I saw, because exactness is the only honesty I've got left.

The chronology began with bread riots in Miller's Ward. Correct. It proceeded through the printer's strikes, the massacre at Tannery Gate — listed, good, listed by date and casualty count, though the count was forty-three and I had personally recorded fifty-one names, five of them gathered in an orchard by lamplight — and then the Rising, and then the victory, and then the Committee.

What it did not contain was anything afterward. Nothing about the loyalty oaths the rebel committees had begun issuing in the third week. Nothing about the presses seized "for coordination of effort" — including two I knew of, printers who had run illegal work for years and now found their type confiscated by the very cause they'd printed for. Nothing about the disappearances since the barricades fell, which were fewer than the old regime's, I'll grant, but were not zero, and a number that is not zero does not stop being a number because the arithmetic has changed sides.

And there was a section, near the back, titled *Contributions of Irregular Character*, which instructed contributors to exclude "material of factional tendency, personal grievance without civic dimension, or accounts unverifiable by committee review."

Personal grievance without civic dimension.

I set the folder down. My hands were steady. Eleven years of handing satchels to men who could ruin me will teach your hands a great deal.

"Deputy Vasse," I said. "May I ask a question about procedure?"

"Of course."

"When a witness comes to you with testimony — say, a woman whose husband was taken by the Provost's men four years ago, no trial, no body, no record anywhere — and she tells you his name, and what he did, and what he was — where does that go in your structure?"

"Why, chapter three. *The Cost of Tyranny.* Every such account belongs there. That's precisely why the office exists — so no name is lost again." His sincerity was genuine. I want to be fair to him. He meant it. He had bent over cellar presses for that sentence.

"And if the woman adds that the warrant for her husband was signed by a man who now sits on the Committee?"

The room did not go silent — rooms in busy offices don't oblige you like that — but the pen-scratching three desks away faltered for half a beat, and Vasse's face performed a small, swift maneuver, like a man catching a plate before it hits the ground.

"Then," he said carefully, "the claim would go forward for verification. Committee review. We can't simply print accusations, Mr. Ash. Anyone can allege anything. We owe the people rigor, not rumor."

"Of course," I said. "Rigor."

"The difference between us and them," Vasse went on, warming now, gesturing at the placard, "is that they erased by decree. We verify. When the record is clean — verified, sourced, agreed — then it can never be used against the people again. A clean record is armor."

I have thought about that sentence since, more than I'd like. Because there's something in it. A lie with a true spine is still mostly lie, but it moves better than a truth with none. And I felt the temptation — I want to write this down honestly, since honesty is supposedly our whole business here. I felt it, sitting in that warm room with its good paper and its earnest clerks: the temptation of the almost-true. To take everything Wendell and Maren and I had bled for, every name in the orchard archive, and pour it into their chapters, trimmed to fit, the sharp edges planed off, the magistrate's name filed under *pending verification* forever. To be inside the room where the deciding happens instead of outside it. To have done it *officially.*

They would even have let me save most of the names. That was the poison of it. Fifty-one minus five. Forty-six restored, five deferred, and the deferral called prudence. History as an installment plan, and the interest paid by the dead.

* * *

I walked back across the city slowly, the way I do when I need my head to settle, taking the long route along the river even though the bridges were crowded with demobilized men selling odds and ends. The light was doing that autumn thing it does over Vellham, going bronze at the edges, and the smoke from the chimneys stood up straight in the still air like a forest of grey trees.

At Tanner's Row I stopped, because there was a new broadsheet pasted to the wall, still wet enough to shine. Rebel printing — one of the free presses, or what was left of free. It was a list. Just a list. Forty-three names in three columns, headed **THE DEAD OF TANNERY GATE — REMEMBER THEM**, and beneath it, smaller: *Printed without permission of any committee.*

Someone had corrected the count. In pencil, in a careful hand, eight additional names had been added below the printed columns, with a note: *and these, whom the count missed.* One of the penciled names was Tomas Ferrow. Cooper. Father of three. His widow had sat in our orchard two winters ago with her hands folded like a woman in prayer and given us every date, every detail, down to the color of the wagon they took him in.

I stood there until my eyes stung, and I won't pretend it was the wind off the river.

Because here is what I understood, standing in front of a wall with wet paste and pencil marks: the pencil was the whole argument. Not the press — the press could be seized, and had been, and would be again. The pencil was a citizen refusing to let the official count stand, correcting it in the margin where no committee could reach without admitting the margin existed. Our orchard archive was a pencil mark. A very elaborate, very dangerous pencil mark. And the moment I handed it to the Committee of Public Record, it stopped being a margin and became the text — subject to their review, their structure, their *contributions of irregular character.*

They would not burn it. Burning is the old regime's method; it wastes good paper and looks bad on tablets. They would absorb it. File it. Verify it into harmlessness. And in thirty years a schoolmaster would teach children that fifty-one died at Tannery Gate — no, forgive me, that forty-three died, verified — and the correction would live nowhere except in a lead-lined box under a burned orchard, if it lived at all.

* * *

Maren was closing up when I got to the bindery. Pip and Sefa had gone out on a paper errand — real paper, innocent paper, though nothing in this city has been innocent for years — and Bram was asleep in the chair by the stove with his practice letters still clutched in one fist, the backward B on top like a small flag of a country that doesn't exist yet.

"Well?" Maren said.

I laid the gilt-bordered invitation on her counter. Then I laid beside it a plain sheet, and wrote out what I intended to send instead, and turned it so she could read.

*To Deputy Oren Vasse, Committee of Public Record — I am honored by the Committee's confidence. Regrettably, my duties as a census taker concluded with the fall of the old government, and I find I have no further counts to make. What records remain in my keeping are incomplete, unverified, and of purely private character. I recommend the Office consult the widows directly. They remember more than any clerk.*

Maren read it twice. Her jaw did the thing it does — a small sideways shift, a door checking its own lock.

"'Purely private character,'" she quoted. "That's a lie."

"It's a distinction. The archive isn't private — it's *unaccountable*. There's a difference. Private means mine. Unaccountable means nobody's — not theirs either. It belongs to the dead, and the dead don't file returns."

"They'll come asking anyway."

"Yes."

"And when they ask harder?"

I looked at Bram, asleep, breathing the slow breaths of a child who has not yet learned that history is written by whoever holds the pen longest, and that the trick of staying human is to keep a second pen hidden somewhere the first one can't find it.

"Then," I said, "we should probably finish moving the plates."

Maren blew out the candle, and in the dark she said, quietly, the way she says the things she means most:

"For what it's worth, census man — I'd have hated you if you'd taken their offer."

"I know," I said. "That's rather why I couldn't."

Outside, somewhere toward the Custom House, a bell rang the hour, and I thought of all the hours the bells had rung for men whose names fit in neither column — the printed one or the penciled one — and how many nights we had left before someone came to count *our* heads, and whether, this time, we'd be home when they knocked.
