## 26. Flight Through the Print District

I have been hit before. I was married to a man who mended books for a living and argued like a falling bookshelf, so I want that understood: I know what it is to be struck by something heavier than you expected.

But I had never been hit by a soldier's gauntlet until the night of the ninth of Frostmonth, and I will tell you now what I told no one then — that it rang my skull like a cracked bell and put a taste in my mouth like a coin held too long between the teeth. And I will also tell you why I never told anyone, which is that what came after it was not self-defense. It was not defense of anything. It was ferocity wearing my body like a borrowed coat, and when it left me standing over a man in the alley behind Quill & Sons Bindery, it did not leave an apology behind.

It left Sefa, though. That much it left.

* * *

The trouble began, as trouble in Vellham always did, with someone else's courage.

We had heard for three days that the Committee of Public Record was drawing lists — not of printers this time but of sympathizers. Bookbinders, paper-sellers, ink-grinders, anyone whose trade touched truth at any point along its manufacture. The rebels had learned quickly what the old regime had known all along: that you cannot rule a city until you control what its people believe happened to them. A committee had been formed. A committee is always formed. There is a particular kind of man who hears the word *history* and reaches for a pen and a list of names, and he does not care whose flag is flying over the customs house when he does it.

Wendell wanted us scattered before the lists were finished. "Binders next," they said, pacing the orchard rows with their hands jammed in their pockets the way they do when they're frightened and pretending to be cold instead. "Printers first, binders second, paper merchants third. You're second, Maren."

"I've been second my whole life," I said. "It's a comfortable rank. Nobody watches the second."

"Maren."

"Wendell." I looked at them until they stopped pacing. "Sefa sleeps above my shop. Pip and Bram are with the Widow Hessel two streets east. If your soldiers come for me, they come through my door, and my door has a bar on it and I have a husband's mallet within reach. We are not scattered. We are distributed."

That is the sentence I keep returning to, because it was true for exactly four more days, and then it wasn't.

* * *

On the ninth of Frostmonth, Sefa was carrying plates.

This was not her idea and it was not mine either, if I am honest, though I approved it, which in the ledger of blame comes to nearly the same sum. The magistrate's name page — the one we had voted on among the trees while a child decided the dead deserved better than our caution — existed in three sets of plates. One set lived under Wendell's font case. One set was already buried in the root cellar with the press. The third set was in transit, because Wendell wanted it out of the shop entirely after the inspectors' second visit, and Sefa had hands like a surgeon and feet like a cat and fourteen years of practice looking unremarkable in a city full of people trying very hard to look unremarkable.

She carried them in a bread basket. Two flat loaves on top, plates wrapped in oilcloth beneath, and the whole arrangement balanced on one hip the way girls carry baskets in every market in every city since cities began, which is precisely why it worked eleven times and failed on the twelfth.

The twelfth time, there was a checkpoint where there had not been a checkpoint at noon.

* * *

I was in the bindery when Pip came running.

Pip does not run like other children run. Other children run toward things or away from things; Pip runs *through* things, elbows first, as if the world were a crowd at a hanging and he'd found out the hanging had been cancelled. He came through my door without opening it properly, took the frame with his shoulder, and said, breathless, "Soldiers at Cooper's Lane. They've got a girl stopped. They've got her basket."

I did not remember picking up the mallet. I want to be clear about that, because later — in the years after, when certain nights visited me — I tried to reconstruct those seconds and could only find gaps. I remember the door. I remember the wet cold of the street. I remember thinking, with a clarity I have rarely achieved about anything else in my life: *they have her basket, and the loaves are on top.*

Cooper's Lane was forty paces from my door. In those forty paces I aged, I think, several years, and made several decisions I have never once regretted, and prepared myself for a scene I had rehearsed in nightmares: my girl — not by blood, by everything else — surrounded by men with rifles, and the oilcloth, and the plates, and the name of a magistrate pressed into lead like a wound into flesh.

What I saw when I rounded the corner: two soldiers, one corporal, and Sefa, standing very straight with her chin up, doing the thing she does best, which is being nobody. She was saying something about bread for her aunt. Her voice carried. Steady. Proud of her even then, God help me, proud of her while terrified of for her.

And the corporal had lifted the top loaf off the basket with two fingers, the way you lift a stone to see what's underneath, and the oilcloth corner was showing white against the dark rye, and he had noticed, because noticing was his job, and his hand was going down into the basket.

Everything after that happened quickly, and I will set it down plainly because setting it down plainly is the only penance I believe in.

I crossed the lane. The nearest soldier turned — I was a woman of fifty-odd years in an apron, and whatever he saw, it did not read as danger — and I brought the mallet, my husband's mallet, the one that had closed ten thousand spines, down on the side of his head below the rim of his helmet. He went down the way sacks go down. The sound was worse than I had imagined such sounds would be, and I had imagined it, I admit that freely; I had imagined it many nights since the inspectors came to Wendell's shop.

The corporal straightened up. He was reaching for his whistle or his rifle — I genuinely do not know which, and I have refused, all these years, to decide — and I hit him too.

Not the way I hit the first man. The first blow was craft; I knew where, I knew how hard. The second was not craft. The second was every night since Bram stood under the apple trees and said *names are all they have left*, and every widow Silas had written down in his forbidden book, and every apprentice of mine who had ever flinched at a knock on the door, all of it arriving in my arms at once and leaving through them.

When it was done, the corporal was on the cobbles and not moving, and the first soldier was groaning, and Sefa was staring at me with the basket clutched to her chest and her face gone the color of the flour she'd been smuggling lead beneath.

"Aunt Maren," she said. Which is what she calls me. Which is what all three of them call me, though I never asked any of them to and would deny, under oath, that it undoes me every single time.

"Run," I said. My voice came out level. I have no explanation for that. "Don't run like you're guilty. Run like you're late."

* * *

We ran like we were late.

Sefa went east toward the Widow Hessel's with the basket, taking the route we'd drilled — through the tannery yard, over the low wall, past the shrine where the old woman who feeds cats keeps a eye out for anything with a uniform. I went the other way, back toward the bindery, because a woman fleeing the scene leaves a trail and a woman walking home from market complaining about the price of tallow leaves nothing at all. I dropped the mallet in the canal. I have regretted dropping the mallet. Not for what it had done — I regret nothing about protecting that child — but because it had been Aldous's, and there are few enough pieces of him left in the world, and I fed one to the water like it was guilty instead of me.

By the time I reached the bindery my hands were shaking so badly I could not get the key in the lock. That is the honest order of events: ferocity first, shaking after. The shaking is the bill. It always arrives after, and it always arrives.

Inside, I lit no lamp. I sat on the floor behind the sewing frames in the dark with my back against the press — my own press, a small one, harmless, a binder's press — and I listened to the city, and somewhere in the middle distance a whistle blew twice, and then a great deal more than twice.

* * *

They came for the print district at dusk.

Not just for me, I understood quickly — for everyone. Doors on Tallow Row, doors on the Lane, doors at the paper merchant's and the ink grinder's and the little shop where the blind man sold pamphlets by touch. The Committee had its list, and the list had my name on it, and the knock, when it came to my own door at half past eight, was polite in the way that only terrible things are polite.

"Madam Quill? Provost's office. A routine inquiry regarding disturbances this afternoon."

Routine. I have been alive long enough to know that when an armed man says *routine*, he means *we do not yet know what we are allowed to do to you*, and the politeness is the sound of that uncertainty.

I opened the door with an apron on and flour on my hands — real flour; I had thought ahead, in whatever part of me had kept working while the rest of me sat shaking behind the sewing frames — and I gave them a performance I am still, privately, rather proud of. A widow, annoyed, tired, frightened in the ordinary way widows are frightened, which is to say of poverty and illness and never of anything interesting. Yes, I had heard shouting on Cooper's Lane. No, I had not been out; bad hip, wet evening, ask my neighbor. Yes, the girl Sefa lodges above the shop; no, not this evening, she's at her cousin's — the Hessel house, ask anyone, the girl is forever running errands for that saintly old woman. No, officer, I don't hold with violence. I mend books. Books don't fight.

One of them, a young one with chapped hands, looked at my shelves a long moment. Every spine in my front room was legitimate — hymnals, ledgers, a book of sermons I keep specifically for inspections, bound so beautifully it has probably saved my life twice. He touched the sermons. He said, almost shyly, "Fine work," and I said, "My husband's tools, my hands," and something in his face suggested he had a mother, and then they went away.

I closed the door and stood with my forehead against it and discovered that I had been holding a paring knife behind my back the entire conversation, and that at some point during it I had decided, quite calmly, exactly what I would do with it.

I did not sleep. But I did not run either, and there is a difference, and the difference is the children. You cannot scatter a family by deciding to. It scatters itself, like mercury, and your job — the whole job, the entire miserable sacred job — is to be the surface it rolls back together onto.

* * *

They came to me one at a time that night, the way cats return after a thunderstorm: Pip first, through the coal chute, filthy and whispering that the Widow Hessel had sent him ahead because soldiers were asking at her door too; then Wendell near midnight, gray-faced, having watched from a doorway as the Committee's men nailed a seizure notice to Winterbourne & Son and walked inside with crowbars; then, near dawn, Sefa herself, soaked to the knees, the basket empty, the plates delivered — delivered! — to the root cellar before the checkpoint had even finished counting its own fallen men.

She put the empty basket on my table and looked at me across it, and I braced myself, because I knew what question was coming. Children always know more than we think. They see the shape of a thing even when we hide the details.

"The corporal," she said. "Is he—"

"Alive," I said. Because I believed it. Because the alternative was a weight I could not carry and a confession I could not afford, and because I had hit him hard enough to stop him and — I chose to believe — not harder. "He'll wake with a headache and a story he can't tell anyone, because telling it means admitting a binder's widow took him off his feet with a bookbinding mallet. Men like that would rather swallow the shame than report it. Pride will protect us better than silence would."

Which may even have been true. I have decided it was true. There are decisions like that, and you make them once, and then you carry them, and the carrying gets lighter only in the sense that your shoulders get used to it.

Sefa nodded slowly. Then she came around the table and put her arms around me, flour and canal water and all, and said into my shoulder, very quietly, "You came. Forty paces away, and you came."

"Of course I came," I said. "You had the plates."

She laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh — and that was the right thing to have said, and I knew it while I said it, and I also knew it was a lie, and I let the lie stand, because some truths are not for fourteen-year-olds. Some truths are barely for fifty-year-olds. Some truths sit in the dark behind the sewing frames with a paring knife and wait for a quieter war.

* * *

By morning the print district wore its new government like a bruise. Seizure notices everywhere, brown paper and official ink. Wendell's shop sealed. Three arrests on Tallow Row that we knew of, and names whispered of others. The Committee of Public Record, which had promised the people their history, had begun by confiscating everyone capable of making one, and the city — exhausted, hungry, half in love with its own revolution — mostly applauded.

We gathered in the smoke of the burned-out chandler's on the Lane, the five of us plus the Widow Hessel's information network, which consists of the Widow Hessel, and took stock the way you take stock after a flood: what's lost, what's saved, who's breathing.

"They have the shop," Wendell said. Their voice was flat in a way I had never heard from them, and I understood that behind the flatness was twenty years of their father's type being catalogued by strangers. "They have the presses. Everything in the open, they have."

"And everything buried?" I asked.

Wendell looked at me, and then at Sefa, and the ghost of something that was not quite a smile moved across their face. "Everything buried is ours."

"Then we go to the orchard," I said. It was not a suggestion. I had spent one night learning exactly what I was willing to do to keep my family whole, and I was not going to stand in a burned chandler's shop waiting for the Committee's next list to reach the next name. "All of us. The cellar holds the press and the plates, the trees hold the rest of us, and Silas — wherever Silas is hiding his ledger — needs to hear it from us before he hears it from a notice board. We regroup where the roots are."

Bram, who had slept through a revolution on the Widow Hessel's kitchen floor and woken furious about it, tugged my sleeve and asked if the apples would still come in spring, even after everything.

"Trees are stubborn," I told him. "Stubbornness runs in orchards the same way it runs in families."

And then I shouldered the empty bread basket, because a woman walking with a basket draws no eyes, and led my scattered, distributed, entirely unbroken family west along the wall, toward the trees, while behind us the new masters of Vellham nailed up their version of the world one brown-paper notice at a time.
