## 27. The Orchard Holds

The orchard smelled of smoke before I ever reached it.

Not our smoke — the whole city was smoking by then, the way a sick man sweats. Vellham had taken to burning things the way other cities take to gossip: indiscriminately, enthusiastically, and with no regard for what might be useful later. But underneath the general stink of burning barricades and burning ledgers there was a thread of woodsmoke that I knew personally, intimately, the way you know your own name — apple wood, damp bark, the particular green rot of the old trees behind the foundry wall.

I came in through the gap in the wall where the stones had fallen twenty years ago and nobody had ever bothered to raise them, because only smugglers, lovers, and children find such gaps useful, and none of those three are consulted on matters of masonry. My ribs ached where a Committee man's boot had found me two nights past, and my hands were shaking, and I had run most of the way from the print district with my collar up like some cheap villain in a penny dreadful.

I am fifty-one years old. I have set type through two changes of government, one fire, one census, one war, and the entire slow catastrophe of being myself in the wrong city at the wrong time. And still, when I dropped over that wall into the orchard, my heart did what it always does.

It settled.

* * *

They were all there. That was the first miracle, and I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere — that on the fourth day after the raid on sympathizers, every last one of us came back to the trees.

Silas sat against the oldest tree with his wrists still ringed red from the irons they'd put him in and taken off, because Maren had bartered him out of custody with a ruthlessness I will describe elsewhere and never fully understand. He looked thinner. Prison agrees with very few people and Silas Ash is not among them; he wears authority like a coat that fits badly, and in a cell they'd taken even the coat.

Maren stood over a cookfire built in the shell of the old root cellar stairwell, feeding it small sticks so the smoke would stay thin. Her knuckles were split on both hands. She saw me look at them and said, before I could ask, "Don't."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were composing the question. I could see the type being set." She jabbed a stick into the fire. "Some questions don't get printed."

Sefa sat apart from the others, wrapped in a blanket, staring at nothing with an expression I recognized from my own mirror. She'd been carrying plates when the raiders came — our plates, the magistrate's page, the faces of dead men cast in lead — and she'd gotten away with them because Maren had done whatever Maren had done. A fourteen-year-old girl should not know how to sit like that. The century has taught her. I hate it for her.

Pip was on lookout in the crook of the second-oldest tree, which is Pip's post and has been since he was small enough to fit in it without complaint. He whistled the all-clear down at me — three notes, low, our signal since the spring — and then scrambled down and hugged me around the middle hard enough to aggravate the bruised ribs, and I let him, and paid for it gladly.

Little Bram was asleep. Curled in the lee of the root cellar door under two coats, his thumb near his mouth in a way he would deny to his dying day, his face slack and round and utterly unguarded. Somebody's feet stuck out of the coats next to him — Sefa's, I realized; she hadn't let go of him even awake. The boy who said *print it, names are all they have left* was asleep in the dirt of the only place in Vellham that had not yet betrayed us.

I stood there a moment, counting them, the way Silas counts households.

Five. All present. Battered, hungry, hunted, and whole.

"Well," I said, when I could trust my voice, "lovely turnout. Shall we call the meeting to order? I'll need a gavel and someone to take minutes, and I warn you both candidates are currently unconscious or up a tree."

Maren threw a stick at me. It was the correct response.

* * *

We ate first. There is no point planning anything on an empty stomach; hunger makes cowards of the brave and martyrs of the foolish, and we could not afford either. The meal was barley gone grey with boiling, a heel of bread Sefa had carried flat under her shirt for two days, and apples — late, windfall, bruised, sweet as sin. The orchard pays its rent in fruit even now, even burned at one corner, even with half its rows trampled by men hunting paper.

Silas ate slowly, favoring his wrists. When he finished, he turned his tin cup in his hands the way he does when a sentence is being composed behind his eyes, and I braced myself, because Silas composes sentences the way I compose pages — carefully, and with consequences.

"The Committee of Public Record published its first bulletin today," he said.

"Did they." I kept my voice level. "Do I feature?"

"Not yet. They've begun with the war dead. A roll of honor." His mouth did something that was not a smile. "Four hundred names. I knew eleven of them personally. Three of those eleven died before the Rising — in the provost's cellars, in the winter rounds, vanished. One of them is my brother."

Nobody moved. Bram slept on. Somewhere beyond the wall, the city shelled itself politely, a distant coughing, the new government and the old fighting over the corpse between them.

"They've folded the murdered into the honored," Silas went on. "One list. No distinction. No dates. No causes. Just names, arranged alphabetically, so that a man dragged out of his bed by the provost's men and a boy shot on a barricade appear to have met the same gentle end." He looked up. "It's elegant. I'll grant them that. Erasure doesn't have to be loud. It can be a footnote. It can be a comma."

"And ours?" Maren asked quietly. "Our four hundred pages?"

"Are evidence now. To whichever side wins." He set the cup down. "If the Committee holds the city, the archive is sedition — testimony against their new order's tidy history. If the holdouts retake the district, it's treason — testimony against the old order, and worse, proof that printers and census men conspired." He spread his hands, red-ringed wrists and all. "There is no side coming that will thank us. I've spent three days in a cell learning that the revolution and the regime use the same ink."

That was the sentence the whole chapter of our lives turned on, though none of us knew it yet. *The same ink.* I have thought about it every day since. A compositor learns early that the type doesn't care whose words it holds; lead is loyal to no one. Apparently neither is victory.

* * *

"So we stop," Sefa said.

Her voice came out cracked from disuse. We all turned. She hadn't spoken since the raid — Maren had told me that much on the walk in, in the wordless shorthand of women who have survived things together.

"We finish the pages we have," Sefa said, "and we stop. We bury it, we disappear, we live. Pip is twelve. Bram is nine. You keep saying the archive matters more than us, and I'm telling you it doesn't. Dead people don't read archives."

The fire popped. Nobody rushed to answer her, because she deserved better than a rush.

"She's right," I said.

Every head swung to me, including, briefly, Pip's from the lower branches. I am, by general consensus, the stubborn one on this particular question. I cut the type. The archive lives in my hands the way a child lives in a mother's arms, and I have never once pretended otherwise.

"Sefa's right," I repeated, and my ribs hurt and my hands were steady, which is how I know I meant it. "Not about stopping — about the order of things. Listen to me. I have spent my whole life learning that you cannot print truth on an empty press. The press needs a body. Hands. Someone alive to stand at the stone and pull the lever. If we die getting these pages made, the pages die with us, and the Committee wins anyway, and it won't even have to burn anything. We'll have burned ourselves for them, gratis. Very economical of us."

"So what do we do?" Pip called down. "Just quit?"

"No." I took a breath. "We change what the archive *is*. We stop thinking of it as something to publish and start thinking of it as something to survive."

Silas was watching me with that expression he gets — the census-taker's expression, the one that takes inventory of a person and finds them more than the form allows for. "Go on."

"It's already half-built," I said, and nodded toward the root cellar. "You buried a press under this orchard in October. Whole press, plates, spare type, in a vault lined with lead off the old font cases. We built it for exactly this day — the day printing openly means death. Well. Here's the day. So we finish the work — all of it, every testimony still owed to us, every name — we print one master copy, and then we seal it underground with the press, and we tell no one, and we wait."

"Wait for what?" Maren asked. Not hostile. Genuinely asking.

"For kinder hands." I heard the words come out of me and realized I believed them. "Governments are weather, Maren. Regimes come and regimes go and each one rewrites the last, and every single one of them believes it will last forever. But lead lasts longer than any of them. Paper in wax, in lead, in dry earth under apple roots — that outlasts committees. Outlasts empires. Some day — maybe not our day, maybe not these children's day — somebody digs in that ground for a cellar or a pipe or a grave, and finds four hundred pages that say *these people existed, here is how they died, here is who did it,* and no committee alive gets to strike a single line."

The wind moved through the rows above us. The trees are old enough to creak. It sounded, as it always does, less like wood than like voices deciding whether to agree.

"And until then?" Sefa said. Her voice had lost its crack. "We just — hide it? All that work, and nobody reads it?"

"Somebody reads it," Silas said softly. "In fifty years. In a hundred. Sefa — I spent six years as a census taker learning how a state counts people. Do you know what I learned? That counting is power. Whoever keeps the record owns the past, and whoever owns the past tells the living who they're allowed to be. The Committee understands this. That's why their first act was a roll of honor — they're not honoring anyone, they're *filing* them. Filing my brother under H for hero when he died under P for prisoner." His jaw worked. "So we become the other ledger. The one they can't audit because they don't know it exists. We don't need to be read today. We need to *exist* today. Existence is the argument."

Bram stirred in his nest of coats. For one horrible moment I thought he'd heard everything, but he only rolled over and muttered something about apples and slept on. Sefa pulled the coat back over his shoulder. Her hands were shaking slightly. She did the work of steadying them herself, visibly, the way you straighten a crooked line of type — finger and thumb, firm pressure, patience.

"All right," she said finally. "Finish it. Hide it. But I want it said plainly, once, among the trees where the trees can hear it: if it comes down to the archive or one of us — the little ones, I mean — the archive loses. Agreed? Say it out loud."

Silas looked at her a long moment. Then he said, "Agreed. The archive serves the living or it serves nothing."

"Agreed," Maren said.

"Agreed," I said, and discovered it cost me something real to say, and was glad of the cost, because anything free isn't worth the lead it's set in.

Pip slid down from the tree and landed beside us, twelve years old and grave as a magistrate. "Agreed," he said, because he was not going to be the only one left out of a vow, and none of us laughed, because it wasn't funny. It was a covenant, and he had earned his signature.

* * *

We worked out the terms by firelight, the way conspirators have worked out everything since fire was invented, and I took minutes in my head, setting them in imaginary type, because that is how I pray.

First: the remaining testimonies. Silas had a list — seven witnesses still outstanding, including, he said, in a voice he kept very carefully flat, the mother of Tomas Wren. I didn't ask. I saw what it cost him to say the name and I set the line and moved on; that is the courtesy of the trade.

Second: the final press run. One master copy of everything — the testimonies, the magistrate's page, Silas's own account of the rolls struck and rewritten. Four hundred pages, sewn, waxed, wrapped in oilcloth. I would set it all. My hands, my candlelight, my risk. I said this and Maren started to object and I said, "Maren. Your hands are needed for binding, and they're currently in no condition to argue," which ended the discussion, because her hands flexed involuntarily at the mention, and winced, and proved my case.

Third: the hiding. Deeper than the press vault — a second chamber beneath it, dug into the clay below the frost line, lined with the rest of the lead. The press stays accessible, in case the world improves faster than expected. The archive goes below the press, below the reach of any spade aimed casually. Only apple roots above it. Only time.

Fourth — and this was mine, and I said it last, and I say it now so it stands in the record — the names. Ours. Not on the archive itself, not where a finder could be endangered by association. But somewhere in that vault, in our own hands, a line: *set by, witnessed by, bound by, carried by.* Not for pride. For accountability. If the archive is ever found and doubted, let it come with names attached, the way good type comes with a founder's mark on the shank. Let whoever digs it up know that real people bled real hours into it and signed their work. Craftsmanship should be accountable. Even — especially — the dangerous kind.

Maren argued for anonymity until she was blue. Silas argued for it too, gently. I held. I don't entirely know why, except that I have lived my whole life as an unsigned impression, and I am tired of it, and if I am to be erased by one committee or another regardless, then let me erase myself *into* the record instead of out of it. There is a difference. The difference is everything.

They gave in. Maren gave in the way she gives in — by declaring that if her name goes in, her spelling goes in as she spells it, Quill with two Ls, guild style, and no compositor's corrections. I agreed to the two Ls. Love makes concessions.

* * *

Near dawn the fire burned down to a red eye and the others slept in shifts, Sefa and Pip and Bram piled together like pups, Maren sitting watch with a billhook across her knees, doing with a tool what she'd already done with worse. I walked the rows.

Smoke damage at the east corner — three trees scorched, one dead past saving, its bark blistered black. I stood with my hand on it a while. Twenty years ago this orchard was derelict, a dumping ground behind a dying foundry, and I chose it precisely because no one wanted it, and it became the only place in Vellham where I was ever wholly true. Now the city had tried to burn it along with everything else, and here's the thing I stood there learning:

It held.

Trees are not clever. They don't strategize or take sides. They just put roots down and stay, season after season, through every regime of weather, and they keep making fruit for whoever is humble enough to pick it up off the ground. The city above the wall could rewrite its rolls, shell its own streets, file its dead under H — and this handful of acres would go on quietly disagreeing, root by root, year by year.

We could do worse than to be like the orchard. In fact, I thought, watching the sky go grey over the foundry roofline, we had already decided to. Put down roots. Hold. Wait for kinder hands.

Behind me, at the edge of the rows, Silas appeared with his satchel — a new satchel, the old one lost to the clerk who'd hunted him, and this one nearly empty — and he stood beside me in the cold without speaking for a long moment. Two men looking at burned trees.

"Seven witnesses left," he said at last. "And one of them sent word tonight. Through the bindery. She wants to come *here.* To the orchard. Tomorrow night, after dark."

"To us," I said. "To the trees."

"To the only place in Vellham where her son's name is still true," Silas said. His breath hung white in the cold air. "Wendell — she's bringing something. She wouldn't say what, over the message. She said only that it proves a name, and that after tomorrow she can't keep it safe anymore, and neither, she thinks, can we."

I looked down the dark rows, at the sleeping children, at the blackened tree under my hand, at the ground where the vault waited with its lead lining and its terrible patience.

"Then we'd best dig," I said.
