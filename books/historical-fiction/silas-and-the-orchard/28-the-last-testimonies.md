## 28. The Last Testimonies

The witnesses come at night now. That is not a rule anyone wrote down, but it might as well be carved above the orchard gate in lead: *after dark, knock twice, bring your grief and your memory, leave your hope at the wall where it can't be pickpocketed.*

I keep the ledger of hours. It is a small conceit of mine — I who count everything for the state, counting the hours until the state stops mattering. The bread queues lengthen by forty minutes each week. The patrols double after the second bell. The shells we hear to the east have moved closer by perhaps three miles since the equinox, judging by how long it takes the sound to arrive after the flash. These are the arithmetic of a city dying on its feet, and I take them down in my neat census hand because it is the only handwriting I trust myself with anymore.

Tonight there are two waiting when I arrive.

* * *

The first is an old ferryman named Colm Brase, who worked the north crossing for thirty years before the regime decided his route was a security concern and his boat was confiscated along with, eventually, himself. His wife has come in his place. She stands in the lamplight of the root cellar with her hands folded like a woman at a graveside that has no stone, which is precisely what she is.

"My husband was taken on the fourth of Thawmonth," she says. "Four years ago."

"Fourth of Thawmonth," I repeat, writing it. "What hour, if you know?"

"Dusk. He'd just moored. The patrol came aboard without asking leave, as though the river were theirs." Her mouth does something that is not quite a smile. "I suppose by then it was."

I have learned not to rush these testimonies. Wendell taught me that without meaning to — watching them set type, letter by letter, no letter hurried, no letter skipped. A name set in haste comes out crooked. A testimony taken in haste comes out false. So I let Mistress Brase wander where her memory wanders: the smell of tar on her husband's coat, the way he counted oars under his breath like a rosary, the debt he still owed the chandler, which she has been paying quietly ever since because *a man's debts outlive him and so should his honor.*

It is the debts that break me, more often than the deaths. Anyone can report that a man vanished. Only a wife remembers what he owed.

When she finishes, I read it back to her, every word. She listens with her eyes shut. At the end she says, "Yes. That's him. That's us." And then, because they almost always say this: "Will it survive?"

"It will," I say. "It's going into lead."

She looks at me as though I've promised her heaven, which is a weight I don't deserve but will carry anyway, because the alternative is telling her the truth — that lead survives only if men and women are brave enough to bury it deep enough — and she has carried enough truth for one lifetime.

Wendell sets her husband's name that same night. I watch from the compositor's bench while their hands move through the case, sure as water finding a channel, pulling out each character and setting it upside down and backward, because that is how type lives, mirror-wise, waiting for paper to make it right again. There is a sermon in that somewhere. I have never had the heart to preach it.

COLM BRASE, FERRYMAN OF THE NORTH CROSSING. TAKEN AT DUSK, THE FOURTH OF THAWMONTH. HE COUNTED HIS OARS LIKE PRAYERS.

Wendell adds nothing of their own invention. Never has. But sometimes, after a name, they set one line more than the testimony strictly requires — a detail the witness gave, lifted out and given its own line, its own white space around it. Tonight it is the oars. When the press comes down and the page comes up, wet and gray and true, Wendell holds it to the candle and says nothing, and I say nothing, and the silence between us has long ago stopped needing translation.

* * *

The second visitor comes alone, near midnight, when even Maren's orphans — our couriers, our lookouts, our ragged nervous system — have been sent home to the bindery over Maren's own objections, because Bram has a cough and Sefa fell asleep standing up against a pear tree like a small drunken soldier.

The knock is soft. Twice. Then a pause too long, and I am already moving toward the tool rack when the voice comes through the wood:

"I was told to ask for the man who keeps the count."

No one is told to ask for that. We don't exist. We are a rumor with an orchard. My hand closes on the cold iron of a setting knife, and I open the door the width of a hand.

An old woman stands in the dark, hooded against the drizzle, small enough that the night seems to lean over her. She pulls back the hood, and the lamp catches her face, and four decades fall away from me all at once.

I know her. I knew her before I knew anything about anything. This is Widow Hessel. This is Marta Hessel, who lived two doors from my mother, whose kitchen smelled of caraway bread, who once fished me out of the millrace canal when I was seven and screaming and certain I would die, and hauled me out by the collar and said, "Well. The river didn't want you either," and gave me bread.

She looks at me now with eyes gone milky at the edges but still sharp underneath, and I watch her do the same arithmetic — the boy in the canal, grown, older, thinner, holding a door.

"Silas Ash," she says. Not a question.

"Marta Hessel." Also not a question. "Come in out of the rain, or I'll have fished you out of something worse."

That gets the ghost of a smile. She steps inside, and Wendell, who has appeared silently at my shoulder the way they do, takes her cloak and settles her by the stove with the particular wordless competence that still catches me off guard, after everything, in the chest.

"You were told to come here," I say. "By whom?"

"A girl. Small, fierce, red hair. She said if I meant it, I should knock twice and bring my memory." Marta folds her hands. Pip. Of course Pip; the child runs half the printers' quarter's whispers now without ever seeming to raise her voice. "And I mean it. I've meant it for twenty-two years."

Twenty-two years. Before the Rising, before the Provost's lists, before any of it. I sit down across from her slowly, and I hear myself ask the question I already dread.

"Whose testimony do you carry?"

She looks at me for a long moment. The stove ticks. Somewhere overhead an owl — or the wind wearing an owl's coat — moves along the roof beams.

"You'll want to write this carefully," she says. "So I'll tell it straight."

And then she says a name, and the floor tilts.

"Tomas Vey."

* * *

Every family has a ghost that isn't theirs. Ours was Tomas Vey.

He was my brother Alden's friend — not merely a friend; the friend, the one my mother set an extra plate for, the one who taught Alden to swim in the same canal that nearly drowned me, the one whose laugh could be identified from three streets off, which was, in the end, I think, part of what killed him. Loud boys don't vanish quietly. They're made to vanish loudly, as examples.

Tomas was taken the winter Alden was seventeen. Not for anything he did — for a pamphlet found in his coat, which he hadn't printed, hadn't written, may never even have read. He was nineteen. They held him eleven days and returned nothing, not a body, not a fine, not a sentence, only a silence shaped exactly like him. Alden went quiet after that, the specific quiet of a boy learning what questions cost, and stayed quiet for years, and I used to believe that quiet was grief. I understand now, sitting in an orchard cellar listening to an old woman talk about a boy dead longer than some nations have existed, that it was also guilt. Alden had lent Tomas his coat that morning. It was cold. That was all. Cold, and kindness, and a coat.

Alden spent the rest of his life trying to pay that debt. You know how the story goes from here, if you've been reading along: the protest, the square, the registry, the strike of my brother's name from the rolls as though ink could unmake blood. The reason I became a census taker fits in a single sentence and weighs as much as a font case.

But here is what I never knew. Here is what my brother took to whatever grave the new government hasn't gotten around to digging yet.

"Tomas wasn't taken because of the pamphlet," Marta says. "The pamphlet was the excuse. He was taken because he saw something. Because he *wouldn't stop seeing it.*"

My pen has stopped moving. Wendell has stopped breathing beside me, or close enough.

"He worked deliveries for the magistrate's office — you knew that much, everyone knew that much, good steady boy, always carrying things. What nobody knew is what he carried. Papers. Lists. Names collected before the disappearances started being called disappearances. There was a clerk in that office — a frightened little man with ink-stained cuffs — who couldn't live with what he was filing, so he passed copies out through the delivery boys. Tomas kept his. Kept them hidden. Kept asking questions with that laugh of his, loud, in the wrong rooms." She shakes her head. "They didn't take him for sedition. They took him because he was holding evidence, and because he was the kind of boy who wouldn't understand that holding it was already dying."

"The papers," I manage. "What happened to them?"

"Gone. Searched out of the house within a day. But not everything." Her hands, folded in her lap, tighten on each other. "Tomas came to me the week before. Came to my kitchen, sat where you're sitting, ate my bread, and told me if anything happened to him I should remember three names. Three names he'd seen on those lists, marked for taking, before it happened. He said, 'If they come for me, Aunt Marta, it proves the lists are real. So remember the names. Say them to whoever will write them down.' " She unfolds her hands. "I was fourteen years finding someone who would. Now I'm told there's a book."

"There's a book," I say. My voice sounds like someone else's, someone dredged up from a cold canal. "Tell me the names."

She tells me. Three names, three streets, three dates, three trades. Ordinary people. A laundress. A clockmaker. A boy of sixteen who delivered fish. All taken within the month after Tomas. All absent from every record this city keeps. All present, as of tonight, in mine.

I write until my hand cramps. I read it back. She corrects me twice — a date, a spelling — because even ghosts deserve accurate spelling, and I make the corrections, and when I finish she nods once, the nod of a woman closing a shop at the end of a very long day.

Then she reaches into her coat and produces something wrapped in oilcloth, and sets it on the table between us, and unwraps it, and I forget how to speak entirely.

A scrap of paper. Old, foxed at the edges, folded and refolded until the creases have gone soft as cloth. On it, in a boyish hand I last saw on a birthday note tucked into a shoe twenty-two years ago — *for Alden, so he owes ME for once* — are columns of names. Dozens. Dates beside them. Marks in the margin.

"Tomas hid it in my flour jar the night before they took him," Marta says. "He said, 'Keep it with the bread. Nobody searches where the bread is.'" A pause. "He was wrong about that, in the end. But he was right about the flour jar for twenty-two years."

"You kept it," I say stupidly. "Through two searches. Through—"

"Through everything. I'm an old woman, Silas Ash. Old women are furniture. Nobody interrogates a cupboard." She pushes the paper toward me. "It belongs with the rest. With *him.*" And here her voice, steady through all of it, finally cracks like ice on the canal in early spring. "You were his friend's little brother. You were the boy I pulled out of the water. Tell me the truth, since you deal in it. Will this end up in some committee's drawer, stamped and shelved and forgotten?"

I look at the paper. At the names. At the margin marks that are, I realize with a lurch, tally marks — Tomas counting the disappeared the way he counted everything, in a loud clear voice no one could pretend not to hear.

"No," I say. "It goes into lead. Every name. And the original goes into the ground with them, so that whoever digs it up someday holds the paper his hand touched."

Wendell makes a sound — small, struck-through, a sort of typographic gasp — and turns away to the type case, and begins setting, because that is how they pray.

* * *

We work until the third bell. Marta sleeps by the stove, an old woman finally allowed to put her burden down, snoring gently with the unembarrassable peace of the righteous exhausted. Wendell composes. I copy. The press speaks its one-word sermon into the dark: *truth, truth, truth,* at a pace no revolution can match and none has ever survived without.

TOMAS VEY, DELIVERY BOY, NINETEEN YEARS OLD. HE KEPT LISTS NO ONE WANTED KEPT AND ASKED QUESTIONS IN LOUD VOICES. HIS FRIEND'S BROTHER SETS THESE PAGES. HIS FRIEND'S BROTHER IS STILL HERE.

I asked Wendell, once, why they add those extra lines. They said: "Because a record tells you what happened. An epitaph tells you who. The committees will manage the first. Someone ought to attempt the second."

Somewhere past the second bell, my hand stops, and I look at the page, and I understand what has happened tonight, and I start laughing — quietly, horribly, helplessly — until Wendell looks over and Marta stirs.

"What's funny?" Wendell asks, wry, tired, kind.

"Nothing. Everything." I wipe my eyes with an inky knuckle. "I spent my whole life thinking I chose this work. The counting. I thought it was duty, or guilt, or spite at the registry. But it wasn't. It was a circle, and I've been walking it since I was seven years old. Marta pulled me out of the canal. Twenty years later I pull her out of silence. Tomas hid the proof in a flour jar, and it comes to rest in a hole in the ground under an apple tree, and someday strangers will dig it up and read a dead boy's handwriting and know he was real. That's not a career, Wendell. That's a debt coming home."

Wendell considers this the way they consider everything — as though typesetting it first.

"Then we'd best finish paying it," they say. "There's room in the case for maybe thirty more names before we seal the vault."

Thirty more names. Thirty more knockings at midnight, more widows and ferrymen and frightened clerks with inkstained cuffs, more bread and memory and grief, all of it funneled down into lead and paper and earth. Enough, I find myself hoping, praying, calculating in my cursed census-keeper's way — enough.

But the city has other ledgers. Because when I walk Marta Hessel home through the drizzle before dawn, taking the long way past the shuttered print shops, I see it pasted fresh on the provost's notice board, still wet, the paste glistening like something newly wounded:

*A reward for information concerning unauthorized printing and the unlawful collection of records. Report to the Committee of Public Record.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, the words that turn my blood to canal water:

*All informants' identities protected. All harbored materials seized. The orchard district will be canvassed beginning tomorrow.*

Tomorrow.

I stand in the rain reading it twice, memorizing it the way I memorize everything, while behind me Marta waits patiently with her empty flour-jar hands and ahead of me the whole city sharpens its knives, and I think: thirty names, one vault, one night, maybe less.

I think: we are going to need to dig faster.
