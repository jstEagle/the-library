## 29. Printing in Wartime

There is a particular silence a press makes just before you pull the bar. Not the silence of nothing happening — the machine is never silent, not truly; the iron breathes, the tympan settles, the ink sings in its own frequency too low for ears and too high for nerves. It is the silence of held intention. Forty years I have stood at that threshold, and forty years the press has waited for me to decide what truth we were about to make.

On this night — the third of Frostmonth, with the sky over Vellham doing its best impression of a drum — the press and I were both holding our breath over page three hundred ninety-seven.

Four hundred pages. I had said it aloud weeks ago as though it were a modest thing, the way a man says he'll fix the roof. Four hundred pages set in lead, printed on paper Maren had coaxed out of hoarders, smugglers, and one despicable but effective arrangement involving the widow of a tallow merchant and her late husband's grudges. Four hundred pages of names. Of testimonies. Of the cooper's wife who watched soldiers take her husband for asking after his brother's grain allotment. Of the student struck from the registry as if the university's ledgers could unmake a heartbeat. Of every soul Silas Ash had spent years collecting from the margins of a city that preferred them erased.

And somewhere past midnight, with the shells falling far enough away to be merely weather — a distant, arrogant thunder rolling off the river hills — I pulled the bar on the final sheet.

* * *

I should explain how a man prints four hundred pages while a city tears itself apart outside his walls, because I suspect the answer will be useful to someone someday, and I am told archives exist precisely for people who come later with questions.

The answer is: badly, at first, and then with a kind of grace I did not know was available to us.

We worked in the root cellar beneath the orchard, which is no longer accurate to call an orchard, since Fire in the Rows took the trees and left us twenty years of blackened skeletons standing like witnesses who refused to leave. The cellar survived. The old foundry masons had built it to last through anything short of divine displeasure, and apparently even the war had decided not to test them. Down there, by lamplight — real lamplight, oil we traded half our winter stores for — the world above ceased entirely to matter, except when it didn't.

Maren Quill ran the bindery end. She had converted the corner nearest the stairs into something between a workshop and a field hospital for paper: damp sheets flattened under boards, signatures folded with a bone folder she'd carried since her apprenticeship, thread waxed against her palm until the whole cellar smelled faintly of beeswax and iron gall. She did not speak unless speech was required. Maren's silences have always been load-bearing.

Pip and Sefa ran the delivery of finished quires up into the false floor of the old well-house, where they'd be packed inside hollowed hymnals and seed sacks and whatever else Bram's errands required. Bram himself sat near the lamp with the master copy open on his knees, checking each signature against Silas's original manuscript with the ferocious concentration of a boy who had learned, far too young, that a misplaced name is a small death.

Silas read proofs. That was all. He had offered to do more and been refused by unanimous vote — Maren's word, my second, and even Pip's, delivered with the gravity of a twelve-year-old quoting scripture. "You collected them," Sefa had said. "You don't get to be the one who ruins your eyes checking commas." So Silas sat with the proofs and read each name aloud once, softly, before it went into the form, and I confess I came to depend on that liturgy more than I ever admitted. A name spoken is a different thing than a name set. The type gives it weight; the voice gives it breath. Between the two of us, we made each dead citizen briefly, impossibly, alive again.

And I set type. God forgive me, I set type like a man possessed, because I was, because I have been since the day my father put a composing stick in my hands and told me the family trade was making things true.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about craft under mortal pressure: it clarifies.

In ordinary times, my hands wander. I think about dinner. I think about the lie I've lived, about whether Master Wendell Winterbourne's collar sits right, about whether the census taker across the table noticed the fineness of my wrists or only pretended not to. In ordinary times, setting a line of type is an act performed alongside a hundred small anxieties, each one elbowing for room.

But on the nights when the shelling crept closer and the lamp flame trembled in drafts from the stairwell, and Maren's bone folder clicked against the stone like a metronome counting down something — on those nights, there was room for exactly one thought, and it was this: *justify the line.*

Justify the line. Make the spaces even. Let the words sit in their measure the way a body sits in a grave — properly, with dignity, with nothing left hanging out. When a shell fell close enough to sift dust from the ceiling beams onto the forme, I brushed it away and justified the line. When Pip coughed through the tail of a fever that had nearly taken him in Winter Rations and Maren had paid for the medicine with silver that had been her husband's, I heard that cough and I justified the line. There is a transcendence in it, and I do not use that word lightly, because I use almost no words lightly; a compositor learns that every word costs exactly the space it occupies.

The craft does not care that you are afraid. That is its mercy. The stick weighs what it weighs. The em quad takes the space it takes. You cannot lie to a line of type, and so for eight hours a night, I got to stop lying to everything else.

* * *

We lost time to interruptions, of course. War is a jealous collaborator.

On the fifth night, the shelling shifted south and the concussion cracked the cellar door on its hinges, and we spent two hours bracing it with the spare press bed while Sefa held the lamp and made jokes about how at least now the door matched her mood. On the seventh, a deserter — a boy younger than Pip, uniform hanging off him like laundry on a line — stumbled down the orchard path and we fed him and sent him toward the river with a hymnal full of bread instead of pages. He wept into it. I have wondered since whether he ever opened it and found our names inside, and I hope he did, and I hope it kept.

On the ninth night, the ink ran low enough that Maren proposed — deadpan, in the way that meant absolutely serious — that we finish the run in her blood if necessary. We laughed. Then we stretched the remaining ink with linseed oil and a technique my father would have disowned me for, and the last hundred pages came out greyish, and I have never loved greyish pages more in my life.

Because here is the thing I understood somewhere around page three hundred, standing at the bar with my palms blistered and my shoulders ruined and the distant artillery keeping a rhythm like a heart that wouldn't quit: these pages were not going to be beautiful. They were going to survive. And survival, in the economy of that winter, was worth more than beauty — though I will say, looking back, that the greyish ink and the slightly cocked impressions and the signatures sewn with thread Maren had to wax twice because the first batch kept snapping — those flaws are the truest typography I ever produced. A perfect book lies about the conditions of its birth. Ours told the truth twice over: once in content, once in manufacture.

* * *

The folding happened at the long table, all five of us, because folding is work that forgives conversation, and conversation had become a ration we allowed ourselves like sugar.

It was during the folding that Sefa asked the question. She was fourteen that winter and had the habit of aiming questions the way other children throw stones — suddenly, accurately, and then pretending innocence.

"Wendell. When they find this someday — the vault, the pages, all of it. What do you want them to say about us?"

I considered giving her the dignified answer. The archival answer. Something about historical witness and the obligations of memory. Instead I said, "That our commas were correct."

Pip snorted. Bram looked up from the master copy with genuine alarm, unsure whether I was joking, which is the correct state for a child to be in around me.

"He means it," Maren said, not looking up from her fold. "He's spent forty years being precise about things that don't matter so he'd be ready to be precise about things that do."

"That is," I said, "the single most insulting accurate thing anyone has said about me."

But she was right, and she knew she was right, and I knew she knew, and the folds kept coming — quarto, octavo, the bone folder singing down each crease — and I thought: yes. This is what I was rehearsing for. All those years of cutting fine type with hands too fine for the lie, all those years of being Master Wendell in the shop and Wendell nowhere at all — it was rehearsal. For this table. For this lamplight. For four hundred pages that would say *these people existed, they were taken, they were loved, and somebody refused.*

* * *

The sewing was Maren's alone. None of us had the hands for it, and she permitted no apprenticeship in the matter, which was unlike her and therefore deeply telling. Each signature she sewed as though stitching a wound — tight, clean, unshowy. I watched her work through the last dozen books and saw something in her face I had seen perhaps twice before: not grief exactly, but grief's opposite number. The face of a woman putting the world back together one thread at a time, knowing full well the world would tear again, choosing to stitch anyway.

"Say something," she murmured at one point, without lifting her eyes. "You've been staring at me for an hour like a portraitist."

"I was thinking that in fifty years, some archivist will hold one of these and wonder whose hands bound it."

"And?"

"And they'll be wrong about everything except the skill."

That earned me half a smile, which from Maren Quill is a decoration usually reserved for coronations.

* * *

Near dawn on the eleventh night — the last night — the shelling stopped.

Not ended. Stopped. The way a storm stops to reposition itself. And in that unnatural quiet, we finished. Bram checked the final signature against the master copy and nodded, once, and that nod went through the cellar like a bell through water. Maren sewed the last gathering. Sefa stacked the completed copies — forty of them, each one a complete archive of the vanished — into the false-bottomed crates. And I stood at the press with ink to my elbows and four centuries of my father's trade in my fingers, and I pulled the bar one final time, and the sheet came up perfect, greyish, true.

Then Silas did the thing I will remember when I have forgotten everything else, including, eventually, my own name. He took the very last printed copy — the one we'd agreed should go into the vault beside the press — and he read the colophon aloud. Our colophon. The one we'd argued about for two nights, the one Maren finally settled by declaring that modesty was a luxury of peacetime.

*"Set by W. Winterbourne. Witnessed by S. Ash. Bound by M. Quill."*

Our names. Out loud. In a city where names were currently the most dangerous currency in circulation. Pip and Sefa went still. Bram's hand found Maren's sleeve. And I felt something crack open in my chest that I had been soldering shut since my father died — not pain, or not only pain. Recognition. Whoever found these pages would know we had existed. Would know we had chosen this. Would know that in the winter of the Rising, five people in a burned orchard had committed the radical act of writing things down correctly.

"It's done," Silas said quietly, closing the cover.

It was done. And that was when Sefa, watching from the stairwell with her hand on the door, said the sentence that undid the peace of the room:

"There's someone at the top of the orchard path. Three someones. And they're not wearing Committee colors."

Maren was moving before the lamp stopped swaying.
