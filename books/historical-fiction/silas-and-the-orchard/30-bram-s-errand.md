## 30. Bram's Errand

The hymnal weighed more than it should have. That was the first thing I noticed, standing in Maren's kitchen with my coat buttoned to my chin and my heart going like a drum at a hanging. A hymnal ought to weigh about as much as a hymn, which is to say not much, which is to say nothing at all. But this one sat in my hands like a brick dressed up for church.

"That's because it isn't only a hymnal anymore," Sefa said, watching me heft it for the third time.

I knew that. I wasn't stupid, whatever Pip liked to say. I was nine years old and I had lived through two sieges of flour prices and one whole winter where we ate turnips so often I dreamed in white. I knew what went into books that weren't books. I'd helped sew signatures by lamplight until my fingers smelled of glue for a week. But knowing a thing and holding a thing are different animals, and the thing in my hands had four hundred names inside it — names of people who were dead because somebody decided they should be — and it was heavy the way lead is heavy, except you can't see the lead, and that makes it heavier somehow.

"Say it back to me," Maren said.

"Church first," I recited. "Then the market. Then home before the lamps are lit. I'm going to hear Reverend Hollis preach on the Prodigal Son because Mam said I needed it."

"And if anyone asks what's under your arm?"

"A hymnal. Because I'm going to church." I shrugged. "It's not even a lie. Look at it. It has *Hymns* written on the spine in gold letters and everything."

Sefa laughed, quick and quiet, the way she did when she thought laughing might get someone in trouble. Maren didn't laugh. Maren took my chin in her hand — her hand was rough from the bindery, and warm, always warm, even in the coldest month of a cold year — and she looked at me the way she looked at a book she was deciding whether to mend or scrap.

"Bram," she said. "You know what happens if they find it."

"They'd ask Wendell some questions he can't answer," I said, because that was the phrase everyone used, over and over, like a prayer nobody believed in. "And then worse things."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"You asked Pip first," I said. "Pip's got the cough. And Sefa's too old. They stop girls Sefa's age. Nobody stops boys my age. Boys my age aren't interesting yet."

Something went across Maren's face then, something that looked like pain wearing the mask of pride, and she pulled me into her middle and squeezed until my ribs complained. She smelled of paste and leather and woodsmoke, the way she always did, the way I hoped she always would, though I didn't have the words then for why a boy would hope such a thing.

"Back before the lamps," she said into my hair. "Or I come looking. And Bram — I look *loud*."

* * *

The checkpoint on Tanner's Bridge was new since Sunday.

That was the trouble with the city that winter: it rearranged itself faster than you could learn it. Barricades went up on Tuesday and came down on Thursday and went back up somewhere else by Friday, and every time the soldiers changed, the rules changed with them. The men on Tanner's Bridge last week had been rebels, mostly boys with red armbands who waved people through and argued philosophy at each other. The men there today wore grey coats with no armbands at all, and they had a table set up across the footway, and behind the table stood an officer with a face like a closed ledger.

I joined the line and became boring.

This was a skill, being boring. I had practiced it my whole life, being the smallest and therefore the least noticed of Maren's three apprentices, though Pip would tell you he was least noticed because he was quietest, which was nonsense; Pip was noticed constantly, mostly by his own cough. But I knew how to make my eyes go dull and my shoulders round and my feet shuffle, how to be exactly the sort of boy a soldier looks at once and never thinks of again. I had my hands jammed in my pockets. I had the hymnal under my arm where a hymnal belongs. I hummed a little, tuneless, the way bored boys do.

The line moved. The man ahead of me had a sack of onions and got them all dumped out onto the table, every onion rolled and sniffed, and the officer wrote something down about him in a book, and the man went away looking smaller than he'd arrived. That was the trick of the new government, or the old government, or whichever government was winning this particular morning: they couldn't catch everybody doing everything, so instead they made everybody feel caught all the time. Silas had explained it to me once, sitting on a crate in the orchard with his census-book face on, the flat careful face he used for facts. *Fear is cheaper than prisons,* he'd said. *They only need enough prisons to make the fear honest.*

Next was me.

"Purpose?" said the soldier at the table. He was young, younger than Sefa almost, with chapped lips and a rifle held the way you hold something you've been told is important but haven't been told why.

"Going to church," I said. "Reverend Hollis. Saint Aldric's."

"Hymnal?"

That threw me for half a second, because it wasn't a question I'd rehearsed. Was he asking if I had one? Was he asking to see it? My stomach did a slow roll, and I heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised me, the way your own voice always surprises you when it decides to be brave without asking permission.

"Yes, sir." I lifted it so he could see the spine. Gold letters. *HYMNS.* Everything.

He looked at it. He looked at it for a long time — three seconds, five, seven — and inside those seven seconds I learned something no one had told me: that lying doesn't feel like anything at the moment you do it. It feels afterward. At the moment itself you're just a boy showing a book to a tired soldier, and the book weighs what it weighs, and the sun comes down on the bridge the same as ever, and somewhere below us the river goes on minding its own business.

"Open it," he said.

* * *

Here is what I knew, in the part of my head that stayed cold while the rest of me caught fire: the archive was sewn into the binding. Maren had done it herself, at night, with her good thread, splitting the hollow of the spine and laying the folded pages in like a second heart inside the first. From the outside it looked like any hymnal swollen with use. If you opened it to the middle you'd find hymns, real ones, printed proper, because Wendell had insisted — *if it must pass inspection, let it inspect well* — and between certain hymns, pages had been pasted together, sealed shut along their edges, and it was those sealed pages that held the dead.

So when the soldier said *open it*, I opened it to the front, where the title page was, because a boy opening a book opens the front. Anybody knows that.

"Name?" he said, running his thumb along the top edge.

"Bram Quill."

"Quill." His eyes came up. "Any relation to Maren Quill, the binder?"

My heart stopped, considered its options, and restarted. This was the danger nobody had warned me about — not checkpoints, not rifles, not the officer with the ledger face, but a soldier who knew names. The binders' guild was known in this quarter. Maren was known. Known meant remembered, remembered meant followed home, and followed home meant the orchard, and the orchard meant everyone, Pip coughing in his cot, Sefa with her knives, Wendell with his ink-stained hands, Silas with his terrible patient eyes, all of it balanced on whether a nine-year-old could answer one question right.

"My aunt," I said.

It was the first lie. The hymnal lie hadn't counted — the hymnal was true, it was a hymnal, it merely had a secret job. But *my aunt* was a coin minted fresh out of nothing, and I felt it leave me like blood leaves you, warm and irreplaceable.

"Nice woman," the soldier said. "Bound my mother's prayer book after the flood year. Didn't charge her."

"She does that," I said, and this part was true, and saying something true felt like putting my foot back on solid ground after stepping off a roof. "For widows. For people the flood took things from."

He nodded. Something moved behind his face — not suspicion; I'd seen suspicion on the bridge all morning and I knew its shape now, the narrowed eyes, the little forward tilt like a dog scenting. This was something else. This was a young man thinking about his mother.

"Go on, then," he said. "Don't be late for the sermon. Reverend Hollis gets sour."

"He does," I said solemnly, and walked off Tanner's Bridge into the free half of Vellham with a dead man's name sewn against my ribs and a living woman's kindness in my mouth, and I did not look back, because looking back is the one thing everyone knows not to do, even boys who have never lied before today.

* * *

Saint Aldric's smelled of candle smoke and wet stone, and Reverend Hollis was indeed preaching on the Prodigal Son, which was lucky, because I'd have hated to waste the lie on a different sermon. I sat in the back among the old women and I opened the hymnal on my knees and I sang, or mouthed, and between verses I read the sealed pages with my fingertips, feeling the edges where Maren's paste held four hundred lives shut against the world.

There was a name in there I knew. Not personally — he died the year I came to Maren — but Sefa had told me about him, a cooper named Aldous Vey, taken from his shop in broad daylight, and his wife had stood in our bindery and told her story in a voice like a flat iron, pressing everything smooth, and Wendell had set the type himself and then gone out to the orchard and sat alone among the trees until dark. Names Without Graves, we called the pages, privately, though we didn't call them much of anything out loud. You didn't say the words in the city. The city listened.

I thought about that cooper while the choir wheezed through the third hymn. He had hands once. He had made barrels that held things — beer, nails, flour, the ordinary cargo of a life. And now all that was left of him was lead letters in a hidden seam, carried through a hostile city by a boy who'd eaten too many turnips, and if I dropped the hymnal in the river, or if a soldier with chapped lips got curious tomorrow, that would be it. No barrel. No name. Nothing.

Nobody had told me the errand would feel like this. They'd told me the route, the passwords, the times. Nobody had told me that carrying the dead feels like carrying water — that you can do it, but only carefully, and only for so far, and your arms will shake.

I stayed through the whole service. It seemed disrespectful otherwise, and besides, the longer I sat in a pew with an open hymnal, the truer my story became. When Reverend Hollis gave the blessing I sang the last amen louder than anyone, because I have a good voice and it seemed a shame to waste it, and an old woman next to me pinched my cheek and told me I was a credit to my family.

If she only knew, I thought, walking out into the grey afternoon. If she only knew what family, and what credit.

* * *

The trouble started on the way home.

I was three streets from the bindery, the light going amber at the edges, when I saw the wagon. It stood outside the chandler's shop with its horse still harnessed, and the men around it wore grey coats, and they were loading things — paper, mostly, reams and reams of it, and a press bed strapped upright like a coffin standing on end. A seizure. Somebody's press, somebody's stock, gone before suppertime. A knot of neighbors watched from doorways with the particular silence of people memorizing faces for later.

And beside the wagon, checking names off a list, stood the officer from Tanner's Bridge. Ledger-face himself.

I did the arithmetic fast, the way Sefa taught me — not numbers, situations. He'd seen me. Not remember-me, probably; boys were rain that morning, dozens of us crossing the bridge. But he'd seen me here, in this quarter, three streets from a seized press, carrying nothing now — I'd stowed the hymnal inside my coat, against my chest, where it beat double-time against my heart — and boys carrying nothing who linger near seizures get asked questions, and questions near seizures get answered with arrests, and arrests get followed home.

So I didn't linger. I also didn't hurry. Hurrying is a flag. I walked, at the pace of a boy late for supper but not late enough to be whipped for it, and I passed the wagon at a distance of ten feet, and I kept my eyes on the middle distance like a good boring child, and the officer looked up from his list, and I felt his gaze land on me the way a hand lands on your shoulder —

— and slide off.

Boring. Rain. Nine years old, nothing in his arms, nothing in his pockets, nothing in his head but supper. He looked away. I turned the corner at the end of the street, walked another fifty paces with my lungs full of knives, and only then did I lean against a wall and breathe.

Inside my coat, the hymnal pressed warm against me. Four hundred names, riding home on the heartbeat of a boy who'd lied twice in one day and found, to his horror, that he was good at it.

That was the part I didn't tell Maren. Not that night, not ever, maybe. Everyone talks about the first lie like it's a door you fall through, all darkness and falling. Nobody tells you the floor on the other side is level. Nobody tells you that you walk on it fine.

* * *

Maren was in the doorway before I reached the step, which meant she'd been in the doorway for an hour.

"Well?" she said.

"Church," I said. "Sermon was the Prodigal Son. Reverend Hollis gets sour if you're late. I wasn't late." I pulled the hymnal out of my coat and held it up, and her hands closed over it like a bird coming back to the glove.

"Any trouble?"

"No trouble," I said. And then, because she was Maren, and because her eyes went through you like an awl through board, and because I was so tired of carrying things: "One soldier asked if I was kin to you. I said aunt."

The silence lasted three seconds. In it, I heard Pip cough upstairs, and the kettle ticking on the stove, and my own heart finishing its long slow stumble back to normal.

"An aunt," Maren repeated.

"It worked better than 'strange lady who makes me sew books.'"

Her mouth twitched — fought it — lost. She laughed, short and startled, and then she pulled me inside and shut the door and put both hands on either side of my face, and the laugh drained out of her and left something else behind, something older.

"You lied well," she said quietly. "I'm sorry for it. I'm proud of it. Both at once, and don't ask me to sort them out, because I can't."

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked — Sefa at the window, keeping watch, always keeping watch — and then creaked again, faster, and her voice came down the stair in a hard whisper:

"Maren. Riders at the end of the lane. Grey coats. Six of them, maybe seven."

Maren's hands tightened on my face, just once, and then she was moving — snuffing the lamp, sliding the bolt, her voice low and even as she called the others. Outside, hooves on cobblestones, unhurried, the sound of men who expected to be obeyed wherever they stopped.

I stood in the dark kitchen with the taste of lies still in my mouth and understood, with terrible clarity, that the hymnal had been the easy part.
