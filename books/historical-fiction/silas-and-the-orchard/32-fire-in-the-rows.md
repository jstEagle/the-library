## 32. Fire in the Rows

I have set ten thousand pages in my life, and I can tell you this: no composition is ever finished. There is always one more line to correct, one more em-dash misplaced, one more widow at the bottom of a column glaring up at you like an unpaid debt. I used to think that was the cruelty of the trade. I know better now. The cruelty is that everything you make can be unmade — by a careless hand, a damp cellar, or, as I learned on the worst night of my life, by a man with a lantern and a grudge and a bottle of lamp oil.

The shot still hung in the air when the shouting started.

Maren had fired it. I will not pretend otherwise, though she never spoke of it again, not once, not in forty years, and neither did we ask. The man she shot had come over the wall with a pistol in one hand and a list in the other — a list with names on it, our names, spelled correctly, which frightened me more than the pistol. Old-regime holdouts, the last loyal dogs of a magistrate whose name we had printed in lead and buried in earth, come to dig up what we'd buried.

The shot dropped him. The others scattered into the rows of trees. And for perhaps ninety seconds there was silence — that thick, ringing silence after gunfire, where you can hear your own heart arguing with your own lungs — and then someone among those trees struck a light.

"Oil," Bram said. He was fifteen now, tall as his own fear, standing at the root cellar hatch with a pry bar he'd barely learned to use. "They've got oil. I can smell it."

He was right. The smell came through the orchard on the river wind, sweetish and wrong, like a kitchen fire in a house you love. And then the first tree went up.

It was one of the old Russets, planted before any of us were born, back when the foundry's workers kept the ground behind the wall as their own small commonwealth. Its crown caught all at once, the way dry autumn trees do — a soft whump, almost gentle, and then a column of orange that lit every face in the orchard and turned us all into strangers.

"The press," Maren said. Not shouted. Said, in the flat voice she used for orders that mattered. "Press and plates first. Everything else is wood."

*Everything else.* Twenty years of trees. The rows Pip had learned to walk in. The grafts Sefa had coaxed along for three seasons with string and patience and whispered nonsense. The oldest tree at the center, under which we would one day bury everything we were — though I did not know that yet; on that night I only knew it was burning too, its bark peeling black, its hundred years of rings offering themselves to the fire like a confession.

But she was right, and being right is a cold comfort that keeps your hands moving.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next hour, in the order my mind has stubbornly preserved it, which is not the order it happened:

I remember the hatch. It was built to look like nothing — a slab of fieldstone over a root cellar that had been empty since before the Rising, since before the war, since before any of us. We'd spent two days in the spring widening it, Maren and I, swearing at each other over the dimensions of a hand-press in pieces. A press comes apart into seven pieces if it is a sensible press. Ours was secondhand and therefore sentimental about its construction, and the frame alone took both of us and a lever to shift.

I remember the weight of it coming up out of the dark, piece by piece, while the orchard burned around us and the heat pressed down like a hand between the shoulder blades. Iron does not burn. That is the whole theology of my trade: iron and lead endure, and everything human is only ink on top of them, waiting to be smudged.

I remember Sefa, sixteen and furious, carrying plates two at a time against her chest like a mother hauling children from a flood. She tripped once on a root and went down hard, and I heard the crack of her knee against stone and saw her get up anyway, and I have never in my life been so proud of anyone or so helpless to say so.

I remember Pip dragging the type cases — the archive sorts, the ones I'd hidden inside a font case of broken letters during the loyalty inventory, back when the danger had seemed so large and so abstract. They weren't abstract now. Nothing was abstract. Abstraction was burning twenty feet above my head.

And I remember Bram, who should have been running with the rest of them, standing instead at the edge of the burning rows with the pry bar still in his hands, watching the trees go.

"Bram," I said.

"They're just trees," he said, in the voice people use when they mean the opposite. "Master Winterbourne, they're *just*—"

"I know," I said.

Because they weren't. They were the place where we had voted. Where Silas had laid out his archive like a man laying out cards and asked me, in effect, to help him cheat death. Where Maren had caught me hauling paper and demanded entry as if the orchard were a guild and she its rightful master. Where three orphans had become couriers, and a census taker had become a witness, and I had become — well. Whatever it is I became, out there among the apple trees, with my father's trade in my hands and my father's name doing work my father never intended.

Twenty years of trees. The fire took them in forty minutes.

* * *

We got everything out. Press, plates, type, paper — the paper last and worst, because paper burns the way memory burns, all at once and then in patches, and we came back for the third load to find the drying shed already gone. Sefa's knee swelled to the size of a grapefruit and she let Maren bind it without complaint, which from Sefa was the loudest scream in Vellham.

The raiders themselves? Gone. That is the thing I cannot make tidy, however many times I set the scene in my head. They fired the orchard and walked away. No siege, no pursuit, no final confrontation under the burning boughs where virtue triumphs or pays for itself in blood. One of theirs lay dead by the wall, and Maren covered him with sacking because she is who she is, and the rest simply left — back toward the river district, back toward whatever ruined cause still fed them, having accomplished in one hour what the old regime had failed to do in five years.

They hadn't even come for the archive specifically. I understood that later, talking it through with Maren while the coals ticked and settled. They'd come to burn the *possibility*. To send word through what remained of their faction: *this is what happens to people who keep records.* The list of names was bait and threat at once. They wanted us to run, to scatter, to carry our little truth into the open where it could be taken whole.

Instead we stood in our own ashes and counted our inventory like shopkeepers.

"One press," Maren said. "Complete. Seven crates of plates. Four fonts, plus the archive case." She looked up. Smoke had put a grey stripe across her forehead like a widow's band. "And four hundred sheets short."

"We can buy paper," I said.

"With what?"

"With whatever I haven't already sold to buy medicine and bribe clerks." I sat down on the hatch-stone, which was warm through my coat. "With the silver, if any of it survived. With favors. You know how it's done; you wrote the book."

She didn't laugh. None of us laughed. Somewhere behind us the oldest tree groaned and dropped a limb the size of a coffin, and sparks went up into a sky that had no business being that beautiful — clear and cold, stars sharp as punch-marks, the river glinting far off like a line of unset type.

Bram sat down beside me. He didn't say anything for a long time. When he finally did, his voice had gone strange and careful, the voice of a boy assembling himself out of parts.

"You told me once," he said, "that a compositor's job isn't making words. It's keeping them safe until someone's ready to read them."

"I don't remember saying that."

"You said it. Last winter. I was minding the quoin key and you were drunk on two glasses of wine, which is all it takes." He turned to look at me, and the firelight was in his eyes, and he was suddenly much older than fifteen. "So the trees are gone. But we're not done keeping anything safe. Are we."

It was not a question. Children learn interrogation from the adults around them, God help us all.

"No," I said. "We are not done."

* * *

Silas came at dawn.

Of course he did — he had been across the city when the raiders moved, working the last witnesses, and he arrived to find the orchard a black geometry of stumps and smoke and four exhausted people guarding a hole in the ground full of iron. I watched him come through the broken wall, taking it in piece by piece the way he took in everything, that terrible census-taker's eye missing nothing: the dead man's sacking shroud, Sefa's leg, the scorch line where the drying shed had been, the ash on all our faces.

He stopped in the middle of what had been the third row. He put his hand flat against a stump, the way you'd check a fever.

"Wendell," he said. My name, just my name, and nothing else, and I understood it was the most he could manage.

"It's all out," I told him. "Press, plates, type. Everything that matters."

He nodded slowly. Then he looked up at the ruin of it — the bare rows, the fallen limbs, the oldest tree standing charred but alive at the center, half its crown burned away and half somehow, absurdly, untouched — and something crossed his face that I had seen only once before, the night he first told me about his brother.

"They'll say it was the war," he said quietly. "When they write this down, whoever writes it — rebels, royalists, committee men, it makes no difference — they'll say the orchard was lost in the fighting. An accident of artillery. Nobody will record that eight men came over a wall on a clear night to burn a garden." His jaw tightened. "That's how erasure works, Wendell. It doesn't always wear a uniform. Sometimes it just declines to mention."

"Then we mention," I said. "That's rather the entire point of us, isn't it? It's the only point we've ever had."

He looked at me then, really looked, and some of the grief in his face made room for something else. Gratitude, maybe. Or the particular relief of a man who has carried a doctrine alone and finds, arriving late to the ruins, that everyone else kept the faith without him.

"The Committee knows about this place now," Maren said from the hatch. She'd been listening, of course; Maren always listens, it's half her craft. "Dead men talk when their friends drink. By tomorrow the new government's men will be walking these rows same as the old ones did. Different oath, same boots."

"Then we move it tonight," Silas said.

"*Move* it?" Bram was on his feet. "We just — we dug it in, we lined it, it took—"

"And it stays buried exactly long enough for them to search here and find ash," Silas said. "Then it moves. All of it. Somewhere they've already looked once and won't look twice." He glanced at me, and I saw it land in him at the same moment it landed in me — the shape of the plan, and its cost, and who would pay.

"Someone has to draw them," I said. "Someone they're already watching. Someone whose arrest buys the rest of us a quiet night."

The orchard was very quiet. Ash drifted down like the world's saddest snowfall, settling on the type cases, on our shoulders, on the stump of the third-row tree. Sefa had stopped moving. Even Maren had gone still, and Maren goes still only when she is calculating at speed.

"Don't," Silas said, before anyone could speak. He smiled — that thin, bureaucratic smile he wore into interrogations, the one that had gotten him out of the provost's office alive. "Don't all start arguing at once. I'm the census taker. I know precisely how I count, to whom, and in what ledgers. I am, as it happens, the single most expendable document in this orchard." He picked up his satchel, the battered official one, heavy with forged returns. "Besides. If they take me, they'll want me to recant. And I have a great deal I intend to read aloud first."

He was already walking toward the wall. He did not wait for permission, because he knew — the way I knew, the way Maren knew, the way we had all learned in twenty years of trees — that some offers are made in the form of departures.

"Silas." I said it louder than I meant to. The ash stirred. He stopped, halfway to the broken wall, silhouetted against a sky going grey at the edges, and waited.

"There's a page in the archive," I said. "Set in the fourth case, wrapped in oilcloth. Your brother's name is on it. First line. I set it myself, the night you first told me about him, and I never told you, because some things you print and some things you keep where the fire can't reach them." My voice did something I did not authorize it to do. "If you're going to be dragged somewhere and made to talk — talk about *that*. Talk about him. Make them listen to a name they already erased. It's the one weapon they have no answer for."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he touched two fingers to his hat brim, the way he did when closing an interview, and stepped through the gap in the wall, and was gone into the waking city.

Maren put her hand on my shoulder. Bram picked up the pry bar. Behind us, in the black rows, the oldest tree stood smoking and half-alive, holding its ground the way we intended to hold ours.

"Right," Maren said. "Everyone up. We've got until dark, and a press to move twice."

We began to dig.
