## 33. Silas Taken

There is a particular art to surrendering oneself, and I regret to report that no one teaches it. The census bureau taught me how to count a household, how to weigh a man's answers against the set of his shoulders, how to fill a ledger so completely that no inspector could find room for suspicion in it. It never once taught me how to walk toward the people hunting me with my hands visible and my face arranged into something resembling civic calm.

I had three days' warning, which was two more than I expected.

Maren brought the word home — *home* being, by then, whatever corner of the orchard's ruined edge we were pretending was livable — with ash still in her hair from the fire and a look on her face that I have since learned to read the way sailors read weather.

"They took Ollin," she said. "The bindery boy who carried for us in the east rows. And he knew your route."

Ollin was sixteen. He had a gap in his teeth and a habit of whistling when he thought no one was listening, and he knew my route because I had walked it aloud to him twice, teaching him, because teaching felt like the one generous thing left to do in a city that had begun eating its children.

"He won't talk," Maren said, and then, because she was Maren and not a liar: "He might talk. He's sixteen."

* * *

We met under what remained of the oldest tree. The fire had taken the orchard's crown but not its roots; the great tree stood blackened and stubborn, like an argument nobody had won. Wendell sat on the cellar hatch with their hands folded, wearing that expression they wore when they had already done the arithmetic and hated the answer.

"Say it plainly," Wendell said. "You've been composing something in your head for an hour and I'd rather hear it than watch you rehearse it."

"I'm flattered you think I rehearse."

"You rehearse everything. You rehearsed your first lie to me on a doorstep six years ago, and it was terrible, and you've been improving ever since." They patted the hatch beside them. "Sit. Say it."

So I said it. The Committee's men had Ollin and a description of me — not a name, mercifully, the Committee dealt in descriptions the way the old regime had, names being too useful for their own lists — and by morning they would have the route, and the route passed within four streets of the vault. The archive was packed. It had been packed since the fire, waiting for a night safe enough to move it, and there would be no such night now, because every safe night in Vellham had been requisitioned by whichever side was currently winning, and both sides were currently winning.

"They want a census taker," I said. "They've wanted one since the interrogation. A man who kept irregular returns is useful to them twice over — once as an example, once as a tool. If I give myself up tomorrow at the river gate, loudly, in front of witnesses, they will spend three days processing me. Intake, questioning, the hearing, the paperwork. Their paperwork is excellent. It's the only thing about them that is. Three days of me is three nights of open road for the cart."

Nobody spoke. Bram was there, sitting cross-legged against the burnt trunk with Sefa's arm around him, and I wished with a violence that surprised me that he had been anywhere else.

"No," Wendell said.

"Wendell—"

"I said no. That isn't discussion. That's a finding." But their voice cracked on the last word, which meant it was exactly as much discussion as either of us could bear. "Silas, they don't process men like you. Men who resist get processed. Men who cooperate get processed. Men who are *useful* get kept, and kept means you're inside the Committee's building when the next purge comes, and there is always a next purge. You told me that yourself. Chapter and verse. Your own brother's file proved it."

"My brother's file proves they keep records of everyone they destroy," I said. "It does not prove they'll destroy me. There's a difference, and the difference is that I am very good at paperwork."

"You're good at surviving paperwork," Maren said quietly. "That's not the same animal. I've skinned both."

I looked at her. She looked back with the flat patience of a woman who has already lost a husband to a regime and refuses to lose anyone else to a committee merely because the committee has better typography.

"Pip is sick again," she went on. "Bram hasn't slept properly since the fire. Sefa flinches at boots. And you want to hand yourself to them so the rest of us can run faster." She shook her head slowly. "I've watched men do that arithmetic before. My husband did it. He decided his life weighed less than ours and spent it like a coin, and do you know what it bought us? Twenty years of wondering if we should have stopped him."

"That's not fair," I said.

"No," she agreed. "Nothing about this is fair. Fairness left this city when the bells started. But unfair doesn't make it wrong." She turned away, which from Maren was the loudest agreement she owned.

Wendell said nothing at all. That frightened me more than the shouting would have. Wendell Winterbourne, who negotiated with inspectors through a font case and lied to provosts in perfect iambic pentameter, sat on the cellar hatch and looked at their own hands, and I understood suddenly that they were not going to argue, because arguing would require admitting out loud what the silence was saying: that we had built something in that orchard worth more than any one of its builders, and that the mathematics of love are brutal and do not care whose name is on the ledger.

"Three days," Wendell said finally. "If you're not out in three days, Maren goes after you, and I cut type for your memorial page while she does it. Those are my terms. Take them or leave them, census taker."

I took them. We shook hands among the apple trees for the second time in our lives, and this time neither of us pretended it was a covenant about print.

* * *

I dressed carefully the next morning, which sounds like vanity and was actually strategy. A man surrendering himself wants to look like what they expect: a bureaucrat, tired, slightly stooped, dangerous only in the way ledgers are dangerous. I wore the grey coat. I left the satchel — empty, deliberately empty — at Maren's, because a man carrying nothing looks like a man who has already hidden everything, and I wanted them thinking along those lines from the first step.

At the river gate the queue stretched half a street. The Rising had made checkpoints fashionable; the old regime had needed walls, but the new government needed *sorting*, and sorting requires queues. Two Committee men worked the line with the bored efficiency of men who had discovered that power is mostly stamping things. One of them had a list. Of course he had a list. I have never met a revolution yet that didn't arrive with a list.

I stepped out of the queue, which drew the eye better than any shout, and said, in the clear bureaucratic baritone I had spent twenty years perfecting for doorsteps:

"Gentlemen. I believe you're looking for me. Census taker, eastern districts, irregular returns. My name is Ash."

The younger one reached for his baton. The older one reached for his list, found my description on it — I confess I was absurdly pleased to see my own words quoted back at me, *grey coat, precise speech, carries ink stains on the right hand* — and looked up with the expression of a fisherman who has felt a tug without casting.

"Well," he said. "You've saved us a walk."

"It seemed polite."

He smiled at that, the first and last joke either of us would share, and put his hand on my shoulder with the gentle authority of a man steering furniture.

* * *

The Committee's building had been the Customs House before the Rising, and before that a merchant's palace, and the marble in the entrance hall had seen three governments come and go without changing its opinion of any of them. They marched me through it past clerks who did not look up, which told me everything: a man dragged in shouting gets glances. A man marched in quietly is expected. Somewhere upstairs a form had already been opened in my name, and the machinery was simply receiving its part.

Intake took four hours. I know because I counted, counting being my sacrament. A clerk with beautiful handwriting asked my name, my trade, my district, my household, and whether I had dependents of irregular standing, and I answered each question with the exact truth minus everything that mattered, which is the census taker's whole art distilled to a single interview.

"Ink stains," he observed, nodding at my right hand. "You print?"

"I count."

"Mm." He wrote something. In the new government, everyone wrote something; it was how they touched themselves reassuringly.

Then the questioning began, and here I must be honest, because if this record survives to be read — and I have staked everything on the possibility that it will — then whoever reads it deserves to know that the darkest hour is not dramatic. It is tedious. It is a cold room and a lamp and a man named Verrek asking the same eleven questions in rotating order for nine hours, while your back learns the chair and your mouth learns the shape of your own lies well enough to say them asleep.

Verrek was good. Not brilliant — brilliance would have been a mercy — but thorough, patient, possessed of the terrible gift of seeming friendly. He brought tea at the second hour. He apologized for the wait at the fourth. At the sixth he leaned back and said, conversationally:

"You understand our position, Master Ash. The old regime erased people. We are correcting that. Every name restored, every record made honest. That's the entire mission. So when a census taker keeps *private* books — books outside the correction — you see how that looks. Like hoarding. Like a man who finds bread in a famine and doesn't share."

"The bread metaphor is interesting," I said, "given the bread riots your committee dispersed with cavalry in the autumn."

Something flickered behind his eyes — irritation, or perhaps respect — and he made a small note. I watched him write it and thought, with a clarity that frightens me even now: *he writes everything down. Everything they do, everything I say, it all goes into a book. And books can be stolen, copied, buried, outlived.* The whole machine ran on paper. The whole machine was paper. I had spent my life among paper. Paper and I had an understanding.

"Where are your private records, Master Ash?"

"Burned. When the Rising came, I burned them. I assumed the new history office would render them redundant."

He smiled at that, genuinely. "You think we're redundant?"

"I think every history office in history has been redundant," I said. "The truth has a way of filing itself elsewhere."

He wrote that down too. Good. Let it go into the machine. Let the machine carry it.

* * *

They gave me a cell with a window, which I chose to read as either oversight or arrogance, and through it I could see the river and, far off, the black stub of the orchard wall. On the second night — the middle of my three — I lay on the plank bed doing the only work available to a prisoner: arithmetic.

Had they moved the vault? The plan called for two nights, using the funeral cart of Maren's guild contact, plates wrapped in mourning cloth, Bram riding on top as grief-stricken nephew because no soldier alive searches a child crying at a funeral. Had the cart come? Was Wendell at this moment walking beside it with their collar up, lying beautifully with their whole body the way they did? Or was the cart impounded somewhere, and Maren in a cell, and Bram—

No. Stop. This is the trap of the darkest hour: it convinces you that everything depends on you, and the truth is worse and better — everything depends on *everyone*, on the covenant, on the handshake among the trees, on a bindery woman's ferocity and a compositor's steady hands and a boy who once said *names are all they have left* in a voice that settled the matter forever. I could not carry them from inside a cell. I could only be what I had volunteered to be: weight on the scale. Three days of me, purchased at full price.

On the second evening, Verrek returned, and this time he brought papers.

"Your ledgers," he said, laying them out. Forged ones — Wendell's forged ones, planted where they'd be found, clean and boring and perfect. "Eastern district returns, six years. Immaculate work." He tapped the columns. "Almost too immaculate. Real census takers make mistakes, Master Ash. Rounding errors. Smudges. Grief in the margins when a household loses someone. These have no grief anywhere."

"Perhaps my districts didn't lose anyone."

"In the eastern districts. During the purges." He let that sit. Then, softly, almost kindly: "Your brother was registered in the eastern districts, wasn't he? Before his name came off the rolls."

And there it was. They had pulled my file — of course they had, they pull everyone's file — and found the wound, because every interrogator since the invention of the profession has known that you don't break a man with questions. You break him by showing him he's already broken.

I looked at my brother's district on the forged ledger, at Wendell's flawless invented figures, and I felt the grief rise in me like floodwater, and I did the thing I had decided I would do if this moment came, the thing I had rehearsed on the doorstep of my own conscience:

I let him see it. All of it.

"Yes," I said, and my voice cracked, and I did not repair it. "My brother was registered there. His name was struck from the rolls by men with handwriting worse than mine, and I became a census taker because I thought — I *thought*, God help me — that if someone counted honestly enough, counted *everyone*, the erasing would become impossible. And then I kept private books, yes. Books of the vanished. Names no court would hear. And when your revolution came I burned them, because I watched your committee seize the printers' quarter and draft loyalty oaths, and I realized the bread had simply changed bakeries."

The cell was very quiet. Verrek studied me for a long moment, pen motionless.

"Do you know," he said at last, "most men deny all of it. Even the true parts."

"Most men haven't spent twenty years listening to widows," I said. "Denial is a luxury of the unrecorded."

He closed the ledgers. "The hearing is tomorrow. You may yet be useful to the correction, Master Ash. Men who understand why records matter — we can always use them." He paused at the door. "Even the ones who hate us. *Especially* those. Hatred keeps a man honest."

After he left I lay in the dark and laughed until it turned into something else, quietly, into the blanket, because tomorrow was day three, and day three was the hearing, and the hearing was the stage I had chosen. Whatever happened in that room, every minute I stood in front of them was a minute the cart rolled west with the archive under mourning cloth. My trial was a decoy's whole purpose: to be looked at.

Let them look. I had names to spend, and I intended, if it came to it, to spend every single one aloud.

Somewhere across the city, under mourning cloth, four hundred pages were moving toward the ground. I held onto that the way a drowning man holds a spar, and slept — actually slept — until the boots came.
