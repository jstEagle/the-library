## 34. The Trial That Isn't One

They gave me three days in a cell to prepare my defense, which was generous of them, since there was no defense to prepare and no trial to have. The Committee of Public Record had learned from the old regime the way a younger brother learns from an elder: all of the cruelty and none of the patience for it. Where the Provost's men would at least have staged a tribunal with witnesses and a gavel — theater matters, theater costs nothing — the new government preferred what they called a *review of irregularities*. Two hours in a requisitioned schoolroom. A table. Three men and one woman with minutes to take. No audience, no appeal, no record that anyone outside the room would ever be permitted to read.

The irony was not lost on me. I have made a career — such as it was, such as it is — of records. And here I sat, the keeper of four hundred pages of other people's names, about to be erased myself by men who would write down only what flattered them.

The cell was in the old wool customs house, which the rebels had repurposed within a week of taking the district, because every revolution inherits its prisons before it inherits anything else. Bread once a day, water twice, and a view of a wall that someone had whitewashed so recently the lime still smelled sharp. On the second day I scratched nothing into the plaster. I want it noted that I thought about it, and refrained. Even my defiance has standards.

I slept, more or less. I rehearsed, less or more. And I did what I always do when the future looks narrow: I made lists.

*What they will ask:* Have you kept unauthorized records? Have you consorted with printers of seditious material? Do you recant?

*What I will answer:* Yes. Yes. No.

*What will happen then:* Something regrettable.

It is a strange comfort, having done the arithmetic in advance. Wendell would understand this. Wendell cuts type the same way — measuring every consequence before the metal ever meets the paper, so that when the moment comes there is nothing left to decide, only to endure. I learned that from watching them work by candlelight: courage is mostly bookkeeping done early.

On the third morning they came for me with a clerk carrying my own official census books, stacked and tied with government string, like evidence, which is precisely what they were — just not the evidence they thought. The true books, the ones that mattered, were under forty feet of orchard earth by then, wrapped in oilcloth beside a press that weighed more than any of us. Let them hold their string-bound copies. Copies are what you keep when you're afraid of losing something. Originals are what you bury when you're afraid of *them* losing something.

* * *

The schoolroom smelled of chalk dust and cold stove iron. They had set the table where the headmaster's desk used to be, I suppose, though no one had taught children here since the shelling started. Rain worked at the high windows. Four faces looked up at me, arranged with careful asymmetry so it wouldn't look like a panel, because panels imply process, and process implies judgment, and judgment implies that someone might someday be judged back.

The woman in the middle I recognized: Deputy Corvane of the Committee of Public Record, whom I had last seen on a barricade platform explaining that the new history of Vellham would be "a history of which every citizen could be proud." She had a way of speaking that made pride sound like a tax. To her left sat a thin man whose name I never learned and whose function, I came to understand, was to look disappointed; to her right, two clerks with good pens and bad posture.

"Silas Ash," Corvane said. "Census taker, second grade. Former."

"Former," I agreed. "Though I find the work difficult to put down. It gets into the blood. Counting things."

"This is not a trial."

"I know. I read the summons. It says *review*. Very soothing word, review. Like being dusted."

The thin man looked disappointed. He was going to be very good at his job.

Corvane opened my census books. "Your returns for the autumn quarter show discrepancies. Households recorded with persons absent from the master registry. Names appended in margins. Annotations of a — " she consulted a slip of paper — "*commemorative* nature."

"That's accurate."

"You understand that the registry is maintained by lawful authority. Appending names to it that the authority has not verified is falsification of public record."

"Deputy Corvane," I said, "may I ask you a question? You may decline. It's not a trial."

A flicker at the corner of her mouth. Permission, grudging.

"The people in those margins. The cooper Marta Ellery's husband, taken from his shop in the night of the twelfth of March, two years ago. The student Ferren Odu, struck from the university rolls eleven days after he was seen being put into a cart. The widow Ansel's sons. The ferryman. The chandler's daughter." I said the names slowly, the way you set type — one at a time, each one seated properly before the next begins. "Does your committee count them?"

"They are not germane to this review."

"They are not *germane*." I let the word sit between us on the table like a dead bird. "Four hundred pages of testimony say otherwise. But you haven't read those, have you. You've read summaries of summaries. Somewhere above you, someone decided what this hearing would find before anyone asked me a question. That's how it works. That's how it always works, under whichever flag happens to be flying. I counted households under the old flag, madam. I know exactly how a government counts."

The clerks' pens had stopped. One of them glanced at Corvane, who gave no signal, which meant continue, which meant she wanted the transcript to exist.

"Let us proceed to the substance," she said. "Did you compile, alone or with accomplices, an unauthorized archive of persons deceased, disappeared, or otherwise removed from civil record?"

"Yes."

"Did you commission its printing?"

"Yes."

"Do you know the identity of the printer?"

Here it was. The hinge of the whole morning, and everyone in the room knew it. If I named Wendell, the review became a trial after all — a real one, with a real prisoner, and my cooperation noted favorably somewhere in a file that might someday buy me lighter treatment. If I didn't, I became a man with nothing left to trade.

I thought about Wendell's hands. Too fine for the lie, I'd thought the first time I saw them cutting type, before I understood that the hands weren't lying at all. The hands were the truest thing in the shop.

"No," I said.

"You don't know."

"I don't know."

The thin man leaned forward. "Your census books contain marginal annotations in at least two additional hands. Correspondence recovered from a bindery on Cooper's Lane suggests a network of — "

"Recovered," I said. "From Maren Quill's bindery, which your men emptied on Tuesday while her apprentices watched from a cellar. Is that in the summary of summaries too? That you arrested a woman's livelihood and left three orphans without their supper?" I hadn't meant to raise my voice. I raised it anyway. There is a time for the measured tone and there is a time for the truth spoken loudly enough to echo off schoolroom windows. "You want the printer's name. You want the binder's name. You want the names of three children who carried paper through checkpoint lines because adults told them the truth mattered more than their safety — and we did tell them that, and I will answer for it, but not one syllable of it leaves my mouth today except in one form."

Corvane folded her hands. "And what form is that?"

"The form they deserve." I stood up. The thin man made a small motion, and a guard by the door shifted his weight, and nobody stopped me, because part of them wanted to hear it — that is the secret shame of every committee, they still want the thing they're forbidding. "You have minutes. You have a record. Fine. Then let the record have something in it worth keeping."

And I began to read the names.

* * *

I did not have the archive in front of me. I didn't need it. I had spent two years setting these names, copying these names, carrying these names through streets where saying them aloud could cost you your teeth. They lived in me now the way multiplication tables live in a schoolchild, the way a hymn lives in someone who no longer believes but still sings.

"Marta Ellery's husband, Josiah, cooper, of Tanner's Row. Taken the twelfth of March. His wife waited nine months before she believed it, and another year before she could say it aloud, and when she finally did she said it to me in a kitchen that smelled of glue and vinegar, and she asked me to make sure that somewhere, someone wrote it down."

"Ferren Odu, student, of the university, entered in the rolls on the fourth of September and struck from them on the fifteenth, though he was neither graduated nor dead by any lawful finding. His mother keeps his coat. She showed it to me. It still fits her across the shoulders. She is a small woman. Her son was smaller."

"Ansel's sons, Tomas and Bram — no relation to our Bram — ferry workers both, taken together, which I suppose saved the soldiers a second trip."

I went on. The rain got louder against the glass, or perhaps I did. Corvane let me run through the first dozen with the weary patience of a woman waiting out weather. At twenty, the thin man objected — *the witness is grandstanding* — and Corvane said, in a voice I will credit her with forever, "Let the witness grandstand. It goes in the minutes." Whether she meant it as indulgence or trap, I couldn't say, and I've turned it over enough since. Perhaps even she understood that a record is a record, and hers would carry what I put in it, and some future clerk with better intentions might open those minutes and find forty names where the Committee wanted none.

So I gave the record everything I had.

The chandler's daughter, Liss. The night watchman who signed a loyalty oath and was taken anyway, because oaths are receipts, not protections — I'd learned that one myself, recently, expensively. The widow from the printers' quarter who had been my first interview, back when this whole mad enterprise was one man's guilt with a notebook attached. Her husband's name went into the air of that schoolroom along with the date of his vanishing and the color of his coat, because she had told me the color of his coat, gray with green buttons, and had wept when I wrote it down, because no one had written it down, because writing it down was the crime.

Somewhere past thirty names the clerks stopped pretending to take minutes and simply listened, pens idle, the way people listen at funerals. The thin man looked less disappointed and more stricken, which I count among the small victories of my life. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, a tram bell rang, absurd and ordinary, the city going on with its business of not knowing.

At forty-one names, Corvane stood.

"That will be sufficient."

"It isn't," I said, and kept going. Forty-two. Forty-three. My voice was going rough. I hadn't planned the number; I'd planned to stop when they stopped me, and I recommend this method to anyone facing a review of irregularities. Never decide your own ending. Let them do the arithmetic.

Forty-four. Forty-five. The guard by the door took a step. Forty-six —

They took my arms at forty-six. Not violently; they were almost gentle about it, which was somehow worse, the bureaucratic tenderness of men doing a job they'd been assured was necessary. I kept reading. My feet left the floor's assistance, boots dragging, and I aimed the names at the ceiling, at the rain, at the whitewashed cell waiting for me, at whatever future opened those minutes.

"— Ansel's sons, Tomas and Bram, ferry workers, taken the —"

The door closed on the rest. I heard it through wood and iron: my own voice, muffled, still counting. Then boots on stairs, and rain, and silence, and a cell again, though a different one, deeper in the building, colder.

They had not made me recant. They had not made me deny a single name. Whatever else this morning had been — and it had been a farce, a purge wearing procedural clothes — the record now held forty-six names that the Committee of Public Record had officially, in its own minutes, permitted to be spoken. Let them redact that. Redaction leaves marks. Marks can be read.

* * *

That night, alone in the dark, I did what I always do. I made lists.

*What they will do next:* Decide I am either harmless or useful. Harmless men go to labor camps. Useful men go to offices and die slower.

*What I am:* Neither, if I can help it.

*Who knows where I am:* No one who can act quickly. Maren will try. Maren always tries, and her trying frightens me more than the Committee does, because Maren's favors come due with interest, and she has already paid more than any of us should have asked of her.

*What remains undone:* Everything that matters. The archive is buried but unproven. The war is unfinished. And somewhere out there, Wendell is free, and does not know where I am, and will blame themselves, because Wendell blames themselves for weather.

I lay on the plank shelf they called a bed and I said the remaining names to myself, silently, in order, the way you check a forme of type before locking the chase. All present. All accounted for. Held fast.

They think they took a census taker. Let them. In the morning they'll discover what the census taker took with him: every name they tried to erase, now entered permanently into the one ledger they cannot burn — the minutes of their own committee, witnessed by their own clerks, filed in their own archives.

I have hidden records before. I am getting rather good at hiding them where no one thinks to look.

Footsteps in the corridor, past midnight. Keys. More than one pair of boots stopping at my door.

Either they had decided I was useful after all —

— or Maren had found me first.
