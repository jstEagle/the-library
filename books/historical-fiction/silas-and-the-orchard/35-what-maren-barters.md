## 35. What Maren Barters

There is a thing no one tells you about grief, and it is this: after enough of it, you stop crying and start counting.

I counted on the walk from the trial hall to the binders' guildhall — four hundred paces along the river road, past the fish stalls that hadn't opened since the bells, past the Committee's new placards still smelling of fresh ink, which is a smell I used to love and now cannot abide without wanting to put my fist through something. I counted because if I didn't count I would think about Silas Ash being marched out of that room with his wrists bound and his chin up like a schoolmaster late for lessons, and thinking about it would stop my feet, and my feet could not stop. They had work to do.

They had taken him at the orchard road three days ago. Two men in the Committee's gray, polite as tax collectors, apologetic even, as though they were doing him a courtesy. *The Committee of Public Record requires your consultation regarding irregularities in the historical returns.* Consultation. I have been a bookbinder for thirty years and I know a ledger being balanced when I see one, and what they were balancing was Silas's mouth against their clean new history of Vellham. A man who reads vanished names aloud into an official record until they drag him out by the arms is not a man they can release back into a city full of presses. He was a splinter under the skin of their victory, and they meant to leave him somewhere he'd never be found.

So I went to see Odile Brannoch.

You should understand something about guild favor before I go further: it is not friendship. Friendship is what you have with someone when you'd help them move a piano. Guild favor is what you have when you've helped them move eleven pianos over twenty years and kept your mouth shut about the twelfth, the one they dropped down a stairwell and blamed on a dead apprentice. It accrues. It compounds. And on the morning after they took Silas Ash, I called in every farthing of it I had spent thirty years saving.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of hide glue and cold coffee, the way it always has, the way I imagine it will after we're all dead and buried, because glue outlasts everything. Odile Brannoch held the mastership now, silver-haired and square as a sewing frame, and she received me in her office above the workshop with the wariness of a woman who has known me since we were both apprentices and knows exactly why I've come.

"Maren." She didn't offer tea. Tea would have been a lie between us. "I heard about the census man."

"Everyone heard about the census man. The Committee made sure of it. Irregularities in his returns." I sat without being asked, which she permitted, because asking would have cost her something too. "Odile, I need him out."

"You need him out," she repeated, flat. "And how exactly does a widow from the bindery propose to extract a prisoner from the Committee of Public Record? We're binders, love. We mend books. We don't bend governments."

"No," I said. "We do something better. We know everyone who mends the government's books."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she laughed, one short bark, the laugh of a woman who has just realized she's been outmaneuvered by someone she taught to sew endbands. "Sit there. Don't touch anything." And she went down into the workshop and came back ten minutes later with a slate and a piece of chalk, and we began to count.

That's what the next two days were. Counting.

Hollis Fenwick, who binds for three of the Committee's councilors and has owed me since I covered for his drinking during the Aldermoor commission run. Tam Wicker, whose sister vanished in the second year of the old regime and whose name — I happened to know, because Wendell sets type and Wendell forgets nothing — appears in our archive, witnessed and dated. Old Mother Cass, who trains half the clerks in the Record Office and has opinions about the Committee's paper requisitions that could curdle milk at forty paces. One by one, I walked the print district and the riverside and the guild quarter, and I asked, and people gave — some gladly, some with their eyes down, all of them knowing what it meant and what it would cost them later, because nothing in this city is free, least of all mercy.

But favors open doors. They don't change minds. For that, I needed the other thing. The guilty secret.

* * *

Magistrate Corven Aldous sat on the tribunal that condemned Silas. I want you to hold that name in your mind the way I held it: Corven Aldous. Under the old regime, before the Rising, he had been a minor functionary in the Provost's office — the man who stamped the transit orders, the papers that moved prisoners from the registry jails to the garrison at Marsh Point, from which no one returned. When the rebels took the city, Aldous did what clever men do: he produced letters he'd secretly written criticizing the old government, swore loyalty to the Revolution with tears in his eyes, and was rewarded with a magistrate's chair. He now signs death warrants with a red ribbon in his buttonhole and calls it justice.

He is also, according to a witness whose testimony sits sewn into a page in a certain archive beneath a burned orchard, the man whose signature sent her husband to Marsh Point in the winter of the third year. She remembered his handwriting. She described the flourish on the *C* the way you describe a face.

Wendell set that testimony himself. Chapter and page. I'd watched him do it, hands steady, jaw tight, and afterward he'd said only, "If it ever needs to be used, it will be used carefully." Wendell is careful the way deep water is careful.

It needed using now.

* * *

Aldous kept offices in the old customs house, and outside those offices I waited two hours in the rain with a parcel under my arm like any tradeswoman come about a binding job, until he emerged at midday with two aides and an umbrella he didn't share with anyone. I stepped into his path.

"Magistrate Aldous. Maren Quill, of the Binders' Guild. I've brought you a book."

His aides moved; he waved them off, the way powerful men do when they think they're being flattered. "The Committee's printing contracts are handled through—"

"It's not a contract. It's a private commission." I held out the parcel. He took it — vanity, always vanity; a magistrate cannot resist a gift — and unwrapped it right there in the rain, and inside was a beautifully bound volume, marbled boards, gilt tooling, the works. My finest recent craftsmanship, and I say that with a craftsman's honesty. Blank pages. Every one of them blank.

"A notebook?" he said.

"An account book," I said. "For entries yet to be made. Though I should tell you, Magistrate — the last man who kept accounts against the names of the disappeared is having rather a bad week of it. You may have read about his arrest. *Irregularities,* they're calling it."

Something shifted behind his eyes. Rain ran off the brim of his hat between us. "I don't take lectures from tradeswomen."

"No," I agreed. "You take them from history. Which is what I've actually brought you." And I told him.

Not everything. I am not a fool, and neither was Wendell, and the archive's location died with the orchard's burning as far as the world knew — or so we let the world believe. But I told him this: that somewhere in Vellham existed a written record, witnessed and dated, of every transit order stamped in the Provost's office in the last years of the old regime, including a great many bearing a signature with a very particular flourish on the *C*. That the record predated the Rising and therefore could not be dismissed as rebel propaganda. That copies existed in more than one place, held by more than one pair of hands, and that those hands had so far declined to deliver the record to the Committee of Public Record out of a scrupulous desire not to embarrass the new government with the sins of its converts.

"And what," Aldous said quietly, "do these hands want?"

"One census taker. Silas Ash. Released. Alive. Unharmed. Walked out the front gate, not carried out the back."

"And if I refuse?"

I looked at him the way I look at a book that's been badly repaired by someone else's hands — with pity, mostly, and a professional assessment of how much force it would take to undo the damage. "Then the record goes to whoever asks next. And there are a great many people asking, Magistrate. The Committee would love it. Your enemies on the council would love it more. And the widows—" I smiled at him, and I confess I enjoyed it, "the widows already know it exists. Word travels in the guilds faster than it travels in your offices. You'd be surprised what binders hear, holding other people's secrets in our laps all day."

He stood very still in the rain. I watched him do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic every guilty man does, the weighing of one life against his own neck — and I watched the arithmetic come out the way I'd built it to come out, because I'd spent thirty years learning how men balance their ledgers and this one had been cooking his books since before the Rising.

"The tribunal reconvenes tomorrow," he said at last. "Sentences are... subject to review. There are procedural mechanisms."

"There are," I said. "Use one."

I took back the blank book — symbolic, I'll admit, but I'm a binder, symbolism is my trade — and I left him standing in the wet with his aides pretending they hadn't heard, and I walked away without once looking back, because looking back is how you show a man he's got hooks in you, and I had spent every hook I owned and couldn't afford to seem catchable.

* * *

That night I went to the orchard.

What's left of it. The rows are black sticks now, the ground ash and cinder, and the root cellar door hidden under a fallen pear tree that we dragged there ourselves for cover. The smoke smell hasn't washed out of anything — not the clothes, not the hair, not Bram's stuffed hare, which he refuses to let me boil despite my assurances that boiling fixes everything, it's practically my motto. We sleep in the cellar among the press parts and the plates, packed together like spoons in a drawer, and if you'd told me two years ago that I'd find comfort sleeping in a hole in the ground with a printer, a census taker's empty bedroll, and three orphans, I'd have sold you a very fine lie-bound quarto and shown you the door.

Wendell was setting type by lamplight when I climbed down — of course they were, it's what Wendell does with fear, turns it into little metal letters, alphabetizes the apocalypse. Pip was asleep against Sefa's shoulder. Bram was supposed to be asleep and was instead watching Wendell's hands with the total devotion of a boy who has decided what he wants to be when he grows up, God help us all.

"Well?" Wendell said. Not *did it work*, not *is he alive* — just *well*, in that clipped way, because Wendell Winterbourne has spent a lifetime practicing not hoping out loud.

"I saw the tribunal list posted at noon. Sentence review scheduled for tomorrow, tenth bell. Aldous's own hand on the amendment notice."

Bram made a sound like a kettle starting to sing. Sefa woke up, took one look at my face, and started quietly crying, which she'd deny to her grave. Even Wendell — Wendell closed their eyes for a moment and let out a breath that seemed to have been held for three days.

"Don't celebrate yet," I said. "Aldous is buying his own neck, not his freedom. The moment Silas walks out that gate, we are a loose thread on a magistrate's coat, and magistrates pull threads. We finish the archive, we seal it, and we get clear of this city before the ink on his pardon dries. All of us."

"Where would we even go?" Sefa asked, wiping her face with the heel of her hand.

"Anywhere that isn't here," said Pip, waking up with the impeccable timing of the young. "Can I be anywhere that isn't here? I hate it here. The trees are dead."

"They're not dead," Wendell said mildly, turning back to the type case. "Apple trees burn to the root and come back. Ask any orchardist. It's practically their defining virtue."

I looked at them then — all of them, in the lamplight, ash-smeared and hungry and alive — and I thought about what I'd spent. Thirty years of guild favor, gone in two days. Every favor I'll ever need again in this city, spent on a man most of the guild thinks is a stranger to me. And I found, examining the account, that I regretted none of it, which frightened me rather more than Aldous did.

Because here is the other thing no one tells you about grief, the thing underneath the counting: somewhere along the way, the dead stop being the ones you mourn and start being the reason you keep the living alive. My husband is eight years gone. My sons — well. That's a different chapter and I won't write it here. I bartered for Silas Ash with everything I had because I have already buried everyone I was given, and these ridiculous people — this careful printer, this stubborn census man, these thieving, loyal children — were not given. They were chosen. And I do not surrender what I choose.

* * *

Tenth bell. The tribunal yard.

They brought him out at five past, blinking in the light like a man emerging from a cellar — which, professionally speaking, I recognized. Gray-faced, thinner, wrists raw where the bindings had been, walking between two guards with that infuriating upright posture, as though he were inspecting their escort formation and finding it adequate. The amendment notice was nailed to the yard board: *sentence commuted pending administrative review, remanded to civic custody.* Civic custody meaning nobody, meaning free, meaning Aldous wanted no chain of paperwork connecting himself to whatever happened to Silas Ash next. Clever man. Wrong profession. He should have been a binder.

Silas saw us at the gate — Wendell, the children, me — and I will grant the man this: he did not run. He walked the last fifty yards at a scholar's pace, and only when Bram crashed into him hard enough to stagger them both did the composure crack, and he held onto that boy with both hands and put his face down into Bram's hair and stayed there long enough that the guards stopped watching.

"You're supposed to be dead," Pip informed him. "Maren fixed it."

"That sounds about right," Silas said hoarsely, looking at me over the boy's head. "Maren Quill. What did it cost?"

"Everything," I said. "And I'll send you the bill."

He laughed — a cracked, rusty sound, like a hinge in a house nobody's lived in for years — and then his eyes went past us, toward the burned rows behind the wall, toward the cellar door hidden under the pear tree, and the laughter went out of him and something harder came in.

"We bury it tomorrow," he said quietly. "All of it. Before they find another reason to come."

"We bury it tomorrow," Wendell agreed.

And high above the tribunal yard, on the customs house balcony, small as a stamp on a flyleaf, stood Magistrate Corven Aldous with a red ribbon in his buttonhole, watching us go — and I tipped my hat to him, because a bargain is a bargain, but there's no law says the buyer can't know the seller remembers.
