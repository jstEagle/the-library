## 36. The Burying

The ash was still warm when we started to dig.

I don't mean that as metaphor, though I've earned the right to a few metaphors in my time. I mean it literally: we knelt in the black ruin of the orchard three days after the fire, and where our hands pressed into the soil, it gave up its heat like a dying animal giving up its breath. Maren worked with her sleeves rolled and her face streaked so thoroughly with soot that only the set of her jaw told you it was Maren. Pip dug with a broken spade and refused all offers of better tools, on the grounds that he had claimed this one and intended to see its service through. Sefa hauled buckets of ash out of the pit without a word, which from Sefa was a sermon. And little Bram sat at the rim with his legs dangling into the hole we were making, asking questions.

"Will it rot?"

"No," I said. "Lead doesn't rot."

"Will they find it?"

"Not if we do this right."

"But *will* they? Someday."

I stopped shoveling. It's a strange thing, being asked a question by a nine-year-old that you've spent two weeks refusing to ask yourself. I looked across the pit at Silas, who was measuring the depth against a length of knotted cord with the same fussy precision he brought to census forms, and he looked back at me, and neither of us answered. We had gotten good, in the orchard years, at answering each other without words — but there are questions words can't carry even in silence, and that was one of them.

"Yes," Bram said, before either of us could. "Someday they will. That's the whole point, isn't it? Otherwise it's just burying rubbish."

Out of the mouths of children. The regime used to say that too, though for different reasons.

* * *

I should say something about what we buried, because it deserves an accounting. Four hundred pages, sewn and folded, every name we could gather in six years of listening: the cooper's wife who came to us shaking; the student struck from the registry; the dockworkers; the printers; the ones whose families didn't know until we told them their own griefs. Set in my type. Witnessed by Silas's hand. Bound by Maren's. Carried through checkpoint lines inside a hymnal by a boy who should never have had to learn to lie, and who learned it anyway because someone had to, and because we asked him to, which is a debt I will spend the rest of my life trying to repay and will die owing.

And the press itself. Our second press — the old Kellner, bought secondhand in the days when buying secondhand was still merely thrifty rather than treasonous. We had dismantled it in the autumn and oiled every screw and wrapped every piece in tallow-cloth, and now it lay in pieces at the pit's edge like the skeleton of some great patient animal waiting to be put back together by whoever found it. A press is a simple machine, in the end. Letters, ink, pressure, paper. Everything else — the fear, the hope, the lying, the dying — everything else is supplied by people.

We lowered the lead box first. Maren's cousin in the tinsmiths' guild had cast it for us — a long coffin-shaped thing, seams soldered double, and when we tested it with water it held like a promise. Inside it went the archive, wrapped in oilcloth, along with a fresh font of type I'd cut over four sleepless nights: a full alphabet in pica, plus sorts enough to reset anything we'd missed. If they were going to find it someday, they were going to find it *usable*. That's the compositor's creed. You don't just leave words behind. You leave the means of making more.

The box went down on a bed of gravel Maren insisted on, for drainage, because "there's no point preserving the truth if it drowns in its own cellar." Then the press, piece by piece, each part laid in the order of its reassembly, with a diagram I drew in my own blood-and-ink mixture — no, actually, ordinary iron-gall ink, but I confess the romantic in me considered it.

* * *

It was Silas who proposed the objects.

We were standing around the half-filled pit that last evening — the light going amber and long through the burned trees, which even dead made a kind of architecture against the sky, black fingers holding up nothing — and Silas said, quietly, in his census-taker's voice, the voice that made officials believe forms were holy documents:

"We should each put something in. Something of our own. Not for the archive. For whoever opens it. So they'll know we weren't just names on a page ourselves."

Maren snorted. "They'll know that from the colophon."

"They'll know our *names* from the colophon," Silas said. "That's different."

And that was Silas all over. Six years of counting the erased had taught him the difference between a name and a person, and he would not let us blur it, not even now, not even for convenience.

Pip went first, naturally. He'd been waiting, I think, since the digging began. He took off his boots — his good boots, the ones Maren had bought him after the winter rations chapter of our collective history, the ones he guarded like crown jewels — and he put one of them in the pit. Just one.

"What in God's name," Maren began.

"So they know I was small," Pip said, with terrible simplicity. "When I did it."

Nobody laughed. Nobody trusted themselves to.

Sefa placed a wooden comb, carved herself, the only object she'd ever made that she hadn't sold or given away. She set it down with both hands, the way you handle something alive. Bram thought for a long time, and then produced from his shirt a smooth grey stone he'd carried, I later learned, since the night of the hymnal errand — a touchstone, he called it, because you touched it when you were frightened and it reminded you that stone doesn't burn. He placed it beside Sefa's comb with the solemnity of a bishop consecrating an altar.

Maren went last of the children's elders, and she was brisk about it, because Maren met grief the way she met everything, head-on and slightly ahead of schedule. She put in her late husband's guild pin. The silver she'd already traded for medicine; the pin she'd kept back, she said, because "some things you trade and some things you *spend*, and this one's for spending on something worth it." She dropped it in without ceremony, but I saw her hand close briefly over her heart afterward, an old habit, a wife's habit, and I looked away to give her the privacy of it.

Silas placed his brother's census card. The original. The one with the line struck through it in bureaucratic violet ink — *removed from rolls, irregular standing* — the document that had started all of this, that had turned a mild clerk into an archivist of the vanished. He held it a moment over the pit and said, "You're counted now, Tomas. However long it takes." Then he let it fall.

Which left me.

* * *

I stood at the edge with my hands empty and realized I had nothing.

Not literally — I owned a print shop and several disguises and one extremely convincing false ledger. But nothing that was *mine*, in the way Silas meant. My life for twenty years had been a costume worn so long I'd forgotten whether there was anyone underneath it. What do you put in the ground for strangers to find, when your whole self has been a forgery executed with loving care?

Then I understood, and almost laughed.

I reached into my coat and took out the ring of keys to the Winterbourne foundry — my father's keys, brass worn bright by forty years of his hand and ten of mine, keys to a building confiscated twice and liberated once and currently flying somebody else's flag. Keys that opened nothing anymore. Keys that had been, all these years, my proof of inheritance and my chain.

"They opened a door once," I said, not really to anyone. "Maybe they'll open another."

And I threw them in, farther than I needed to, and heard them land among the type with a sound like a small bell.

Silas's hand found mine at the edge of the pit. His fingers were cold and dirty and certain. Neither of us said anything. There was a covenant we'd signed years ago under these very trees with a handshake, terms of silence, and it had never required speeches. But his thumb moved once across my knuckles, and I understood it to mean: *we were real, whatever they write.*

Then we filled it in.

* * *

We worked until dark, all six of us, passing the shovels hand to hand, and it occurred to me somewhere in the third hour that this was the best day of my life, which is an absurd thing to think while burying the most dangerous object in Vellham in the corpse of an orchard. But there it is. Every spadeful was a sentence. Every stone we packed down was a period. By full dark the ground was level and Maren had scattered ash and debris over it until it looked like exactly what it was — the sad corner of a burned orchard that no committee, old or new, would ever trouble to examine.

Bram planted the last thing. Not in the vault — above it. An apple seed, from the oldest tree, the mother tree, the one that had survived the fire better than any of us deserved, scorched on one side but alive, budding stubbornly where the bark wasn't charred through. He pressed the seed into the ash-rich soil directly over the vault and tamped it down with his palm.

"For when they dig," he explained. "So they have to go *through* the tree to get to it. So the tree gets a vote."

The tree gets a vote. I have been called many things in my life — master, madam, sir, traitor, counterfeiter of men — but I have never been out-argued by an apple seed before, and I did not intend to start.

We walked home through the ruined streets in the dark, six figures, no lantern, and behind us the orchard kept its new secret under its new tree, and somewhere across the river the Committee of Public Record was drafting its clean, official, empty history, and I found I was smiling.

Let them have their version. We had ours, in lead, underground, patient as metal.

It was Silas who broke the quiet at our gate, in the tone of a man reading the last line of a form:

"You know they'll come asking eventually. About the fire. About us."

"I know," I said.

"And?"

I looked back toward the dark bulk of the orchard hill, where a seed slept over a coffin of truth, and I found my answer had been written years ago, in a handshake among apple trees.

"And," I said, "we tell them exactly the truth they're prepared to hear — and not one word more. Starting tomorrow. Because there's a man at the end of our street who's been counting houses, and he isn't one of ours."

Down the lane, past the gutted lamppost, a figure stood beneath the broken clock, taking notes by moonlight.
