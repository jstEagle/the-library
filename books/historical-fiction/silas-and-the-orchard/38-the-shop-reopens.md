## 38. The Shop Reopens

The sign went up on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays are for beginnings that don't want to admit they're nervous.

I had painted it myself — WINTERBOURNE & QUILL, TYPEFOUNDERS AND PRINTERS — in letters I cut from stencils and filled by hand, standing on a ladder I no longer entirely trusted, while Pip held the bottom rung and offered commentary the whole time like a magpie with opinions about kerning.

"Left side's heavier," he said.

"It is not."

"It's a hair heavier. You've got an extra stroke on the R."

I climbed down and looked. He was right. He was nineteen years old and he was right, and I hated him for it in the way one hates only the people one loves most completely.

"There," I said, after correcting it. "Now it's perfect."

"Now it's *ours*," he said, which was better.

The shop on Chandler's Row had been closed eleven years. Eleven years of dust sheets and locked shutters and me telling anyone who asked — the tax man, the guild assessor, old Mrs. Fenwick across the lane who asked about everything — that the Winterbourne foundry was between proprietors, that Master Wendell was retired to the country, that no, there was nothing worth buying, thank you kindly. Eleven years of living half a life in a house above a dark shop while the city outside learned new names for its streets and pretended the old ones had never been carved anywhere.

And then, last spring, the Committee of Public Record finally, quietly, without ceremony or apology, collapsed under the weight of its own paper. The new Assembly published an accounting — thin, grudging, but real — of the disappearances. Not all of them. Not even most of them. But some, with dates, and the word *killed* where the old regime had preferred *relocated*. Silas read every line of it twice and then set the official gazette down on my kitchen table with the expression of a man who has been handed a loaf made of sawdust and told to be grateful.

"It's something," Maren said.

"It's less than nothing," Silas said. "It's nothing wearing our dead as a coat."

But it was enough. It was enough that when I walked past my own shuttered windows in April and saw a boy chalking a hopscotch grid on the cobbles outside, I did not flinch at being seen. It was enough that I could stand in the doorway of the shop with the dust sheets coming off the presses and think: *yes. Now. While it lasts.*

One learns not to say *forever* in Vellham. One says *now*, and one means it devoutly.

* * *

The reopening itself was almost indecently small. That was deliberate. We had discussed — at length, over Maren's terrible stew, which she insists is excellent and which we eat because we love her — whether to make a day of it. Ribbons. A broadsheet announcement. Pip lobbied hard for fireworks until Sefa pointed out that fireworks are what you set off when you want the provost to know exactly where you are, and that some habits ought to outlive their necessity if only because they're correct.

So instead: on a Tuesday morning in September, I unlocked the door of the Winterbourne shop for the first time in eleven years, and the bell above it rang the same cracked little note it had rung when Father was alive, and Bram — who is not so little anymore, though he will be Bram-the-youngest to me until one of us is in the ground — stood in the street and laughed at the sound of it.

"That's the worst bell in Vellham," he said.

"That bell has outlasted two governments," I said. "Show some respect."

The first customer came within the hour, which surprised me. A widow from the tannery district wanting funeral cards printed for her husband — proper ones, with his name spelled correctly, which apparently the government office had gotten wrong on the death certificate and refused to amend without a fee she couldn't pay. She stood at the counter wringing her hat in her hands and apologizing for the smallness of the order, and I looked at her, and I thought of a cooper's wife twenty years ago telling her story to Silas among the apple trees while I wrote it down with shaking hands.

"Madam," I said, "we will print forty cards, and we will spell him right, and you will pay what the cards cost in lead and ink and not a copper more. The spelling correction is free. It always will be."

She cried a little. I pretended to be very busy with a ledger. Some kindnesses are best received sideways, like light through a curtain.

By evening we'd taken orders for three hundred hymnal pages, a run of school primers, and — this one made Silas laugh until he had to sit down — an official commission from the Assembly itself: five hundred copies of a municipal address, to be printed *by the Winterbourne shop*, the name of which appeared nowhere in any file the Committee had ever kept. The revolutionaries had burned the census archives during the Rising, or lost them, or eaten them; no one knew. As far as the new government was concerned, Wendell Winterbourne was a printer of modest history and impeccable obscurity.

"They want US," Pip said, turning the commission letter over as if it might bite. "The government wants us."

"The government wants type set neatly and delivered on time," I said. "That we can do. That, we have always done."

Sefa, who was seventeen now and had Maren's way of cutting straight to the bone of a thing, said: "It means they trust us."

"It means," said Silas, from his corner, where he was cataloguing our stock of paper with the tenderness other men reserve for love letters, "that obscurity was the best armor we ever bought, and it turns out we get to keep wearing it."

* * *

I should say something about the teaching, because the teaching is the part that undoes me.

Pip had learned printing already — had been carrying type and wiping presses since he was eight years old, back when those were crimes rather than trades. But he'd learned it the way soldiers learn: fast, rough, in the dark, for survival. What I gave him that autumn was the slow version. The version where you learn why the lead wants what it wants. Where you learn that a well-cut sort sings against the composing stick, and a badly cut one mutters, and you can hear the difference before you ever see it. Where you learn that setting a page is not arranging letters but *listening* to them — that white space speaks, that a margin breathes, that a line too tight is a sentence clenching its jaw.

He fought me on it for a month. Of course he did. Speed had kept him alive once.

"Pip," I said to him one night, over the candle — we still worked by candle sometimes, out of habit, and neither of us suggested changing it — "when you set a page in a hurry, what are you running from?"

He looked at me with those quick clever eyes and didn't answer, because he knew I knew. Running from the checkpoints. From the knock at the door. From the memory of Sefa nearly caught with plates sewn into her coat lining, and the courier we lost whose name we do not say in the shop, and all the rest of it.

"And what happens," I said, "to a page set in fear?"

He looked down at the forme in front of him. He pulled it apart. He started again, slower, and by the third line his shoulders had come down from around his ears, and the type began to sing.

"There," I said. "That's the trade. Everything else is just moving metal."

Sefa took to the binding side, under Maren, and it turned out she had a gift for it — a patience none of us knew she carried, hands that could fold four hundred signatures without creasing a single one wrong. She and Maren would work the long table by the window in the afternoons, sewing and pressing, and the talk between them was easy and constant and mostly about nothing, which is how you know two people are truly at ease. Maren had raised her from twelve years old, and watching them together was like watching a river that has worn a comfortable channel through stone.

Bram stayed on at the orchard, actually. The trees had come back — slower than we'd hoped, faster than we'd feared, the way living things do when nobody is shooting at them anymore — and someone needed to keep the place, mend the wall, mind the bees we'd gotten from a man in the river districts who traded hives for printed almanacs. Bram had declared at fifteen that the orchard was his, in the tone of voice that ended discussions in our family, and it was his. He sent baskets of apples down to the shop every week, and every week Maren sent up bread and complaints about how thin he'd let himself get, and every week he ignored the complaints and ate the bread.

Silas — well. Silas never officially worked at the shop at all. Officially he was a retired clerk of no fixed occupation who lodged with the Winterbourne household and was given to long walks. Unofficially he sat in the corner every day doing what he had always done: keeping accounts, reading everything, writing things down. He had begun, quietly, without announcing it, a second archive. Not of the dead this time. Of the *found* — the returned, the pardoned, the ones the official record got wrong and the unofficial record could correct. He said it was for himself. Maren said he was a liar. Both statements were true.

And me? I lived openly. Let me write that plainly, because there was a time I could not have written it at all: Wendell Winterbourne, master of the house, mistress of nothing, simply *myself*, in daylight, on my own doorstep, answering to whatever pronoun the moment produced without checking first whether the street was safe. The city had bigger questions now. Or perhaps I had simply stopped caring whether it did. Both, probably. Freedom arrives partly as a gift and partly as a decision, and I decided.

Mrs. Fenwick across the lane, who had spent eleven years believing Master Wendell was in the country, took the news of my return with characteristic grace.

"Back from the country, are you?" she called over the wall. "How was it?"

"Damp," I said.

"Hmph. Country always is." And that was the whole of it. Twenty years of terror, resolved by an old woman's indifference. There is a lesson in that, and I decline to learn it.

* * *

There was one more thing, though. There is always one more thing, in our family.

It happened on a Thursday in late October, near closing. A young woman came into the shop — early twenties, ink-stained fingers, the particular hungry look of someone who has read too much and been trusted with too little. She wanted an apprenticeship. She had references from a bindery in Marrowfield that had folded two years ago. Her name was Odile Ferrant.

Maren interviewed her at the long table while I pretended to take inventory and listened to everything. The girl answered well. Too well — rehearsed answers, polished, the kind you prepare when you've been turned away before. When Maren asked why she'd left Marrowfield, there was a pause of exactly the wrong length.

"My mother's family is from Vellham," the girl said finally. "I wanted to come home."

"Your mother's name?" Maren asked, mild as milk.

"Ferrant. Like mine." Another pause. Then, quieter, watching Maren's face with those hungry eyes: "My grandmother was Annoushka Ferrant. She was a compositor. Before."

The shop went very still. I heard the bell above the door tick in a draft, that cracked little note.

Annoushka Ferrant. Page forty-one of the archive. Vanished in the winter purges, along with her press. Her testimony was one of the first Silas ever collected — I remember setting her name in lead, remembering the weight of it, remembering thinking *someone should know this woman could set type like breathing.*

"She was good," I said, before I had decided to speak. "From what we've heard, she was very good."

The girl stared at me. "You've — heard?"

"We keep records," Silas said mildly, from his corner. "Of people who mattered. Someone has to."

Odile Ferrant looked around our shop — at the presses, at the font cases, at Maren with her arms crossed, at Pip pretending not to hang on every word, at Silas in his corner with his papers — and I watched something move behind her eyes, some recognition arriving ahead of understanding. She had grown up on her grandmother's stories, I'd wager. Stories of a city where printers vanished. Perhaps stories of an orchard, if Annoushka's circle had overlapped with ours, which in those days every thread in Vellham seemed to do eventually.

"When can I start?" she asked.

Maren looked at me. I looked at Maren. Twenty years ago we negotiated terms of silence among apple trees; now we negotiated with glances, which is faster.

"Monday," Maren said. "Bring your own apron. We're not a charity."

She left lighter than she'd come. After the bell rang her out, nobody spoke for a moment.

"You realize," Silas said at last, folding his hands, "that they'll keep finding us. The grandchildren. They'll come looking for the shops and the orchards and the people from the stories, and they won't all be as easy to spot as a girl asking for work."

"Good," said Pip.

"Is it?" Silas asked, and there was something in his voice — not fear exactly. Something older. Something that remembered satchels abandoned in alleys and friendly witnesses who weren't.

"Good," I said, "because it means the stories survived. Whatever else they do with us, the stories survived." But I confess I did not entirely believe my own bravado, and I suspect Silas saw that.

That night I climbed to the shop's little attic, where we stored the things that were not stock — Maren's husband's silver, replaced years since by a grateful guild and kept anyway because Maren is Maren; the cracked bell's twin, saved from the old foundry; and, in a font case of broken sorts, hidden in plain sight the way it had been hidden through the searchings of '49, a single galley proof. The dangerous page. The magistrate's name, set by my own terrified, meticulous hands, printed once and never distributed. Our rehearsal for damnation, kept as a relic.

I took it out and read it by lamplight. Then I wrapped it carefully and put it back, and I sat for a while in the cold attic listening to the house settle, thinking about Annoushka Ferrant's granddaughter setting type on Monday, thinking about the orchard regrowing on the hill, thinking about how everything we buried was going to want digging up someday, and sooner than any of us would choose.

Downstairs, I heard Silas moving — he keeps late hours now, older men do — and the scratch of his pen. Writing things down. Always writing things down.

In the morning, a letter came from Bram at the orchard. Just a note, in his careful boyish hand, tucked inside a basket of late apples:

*The oldest tree's come round full again. Fifty years next spring, by Pip's counting. You should all come up. We should decide what to do about it.*

I read it twice. Then I put it in my pocket, next to my heart, where we keep such things, and I went downstairs to open the shop.
