## 40. On the Record

The tram line was supposed to run straight through the old orchard district, and the fact that it didn't — that it now bends, absurdly, around a single tree — is because of me. I want that on the record too. If we are keeping records.

My name is Elsie Vann, and in the spring of 1912 I was twenty-three years old and the most junior archivist in the Office of Civic Records, which meant I was the one they sent to stand in a muddy trench while men with shovels complained about me. The city wanted to lay track from the river district out past the old tanneries, and by law any excavation deeper than a man's knee required an archivist present, on the off chance Vellham's soil coughed up something older than gravel. In eleven years of service, the Office had recovered: three coins, half a boot, and a great deal of gravel.

I was not expecting an orchard.

Well — the stump of one. The trench cut through ground that was still, even then, even after fifty years, different from the ground around it. Darker. Softer. The foreman, a barrel of a man named Hobb who addressed all clerks as "miss" regardless of evidence, struck wood at nine in the morning and swore at it until ten, when he finally let me look.

"It's roots," he said. "Big ones. Whole row of 'em under here."

Not roots, as it turned out. Roots and boards. Roots grown *through* boards, deliberately, the way ivy takes a trellis — as if someone had invited them to.

I should say now what everyone said later, which is that nobody working for the city in 1912 remembered the Printer's Rising as anything but a paragraph. We got it in school between two worse wars: there had been a rising, there had been a Committee, there had been a fire in the orchard district, order was restored, the end. My grandmother used to say her mother wouldn't buy apples. When I asked why, she said, "Because she was strange about apples," and that was the whole inheritance I had of those years before the trench.

So when Hobb's shovel came up against something that rang — metal, flat, deliberate — I climbed down into the mud myself, which ruined a skirt my sister had lent me, and knelt over a lid of grey lead stamped with nothing at all except, scratched small near one corner, what looked like a bird. An ox, maybe. Something patient, anyway.

"Miss," said Hobb, "if it's treasure, it's the city's treasure."

"If it's treasure," I said, "it's somebody's grave, and you'll want me to be cross with you later."

It was neither, or it was both, or — having spent my career since learning the difference barely exists — it was a third thing entirely.

* * *

The vault came up in four pieces because it was built to be opened by people who knew where the seams were, and we did not know. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were books. Not ledgers, not parish rolls — I knew those shapes the way you know your own hands — but sewn signatures bound in plain board, dozens of them, packed tight as bricks, and beneath them a press. A hand press, iron and walnut, disassembled with care and greased against rust, every screw bagged and labeled in a copperplate hand so fine and sure it made my own penmanship feel like a child's scrawl.

The labels said things like *bed piece — mind the register*. And one bag, holding a dozen letters of type, said only: *W.'s sorts. Broken but true.*

Hobb wanted the press for the Works museum, the mayor wanted whatever the mayor always wants, and I did the thing I have never regretted and have been reprimanded for twice: I sat down in the mud and opened the first book, and I read until the light went.

Here is what I read.

*Names,* page after page of names, each with a date, a street, a trade, a person who remembered them. A cooper's wife testifying that her husband was taken from his shop on a Tuesday and that the Tuesday mattered because he'd promised to mend a neighbor's churn and the churn stood unmended, and she wanted the unmendedness recorded, because grief needs somewhere to put its hands. A student vanished from the university registry, described by his landlady down to the whistle he whistled. Pages and pages of testimony no court ever heard, printed in type so clean it might have been set yesterday.

And at the bottom of the final page of the first volume, a colophon. I have it memorized now; I could recite it drowning. It reads:

*Set by W. Winterbourne. Witnessed by S. Ash. Bound by M. Quill. Carried by B. — counted at last.*

Counted at last. As if whoever set that line knew — must have known — that someone would count these pages someday, and wanted us to understand that the counting was the point. Not the paper. Not the press. The counting.

I am a junior archivist. Counting is my entire religion.

* * *

They gave me the collection, eventually, after the mayor's office decided old pamphlets weren't politically useful to anyone still living — a judgment that tells you everything about how much Vellham had learned in fifty years. The Office assigned me a corner desk and a budget of almost nothing, which suited me, because the work didn't need money. It needed time, and it needed the other thing the archive itself taught me to look for: witnesses.

Because here is what the books don't contain, and what took me four more years to gather: the people. The colophon gave me initials, and initials are doors if you knock hard enough.

M. Quill was the easiest. The Binders' Guild keeps its registers like scripture, and Maren Quill appears in them across forty years — admitted 18—, raised to journeyman, master binder, and then, in her last decade, listed as instructing apprentices whose surnames do not match hers: a Pip Winterbourne-Quill, a Sefa Ash, a Bram — well. Bram's entry made me sit back from the ledger and breathe, because the surname column simply says *carried*, in someone's defiant hand, and the guild apparently let it stand.

Sefa herself was still alive when I began, ninety-one years old and sharp as a freshly cut quire. She received me in a room full of grandchildren and made me tea I didn't want and interrogated me for an hour before she'd speak of it.

"You're the fourth official to come asking," she said. "The others wanted to know if the papers were genuine."

"Are they?"

"They're better than genuine." She smiled, and I saw the girl who had once run plates through checkpoint lines inside her coat. "They're accurate. There's a difference. Genuine means it wasn't forged. Accurate means it was true. The men who ran this city for fifty years understood genuineness perfectly and accuracy not at all."

She told me about the orchard — the trees her brother Pip had grafted in the burned rows, the root cellar, the winter they ate bread and onions and called it a feast. She told me about Wendell Winterbourne, who lived half a life as a man named Master Wendell to keep a foundry out of confiscating hands, and who set type with such tenderness that Sefa, apprenticed at eight, believed for years that all adults touched their work that way.

"She used to say," Sefa told me, "that lead remembers pressure. That every name pressed into it stays pressed. That's why she set the dead by hand, slow, letter by letter, instead of using the casting machine. She said the machine was honest but the hand was faithful."

I wrote that down and have never improved on it.

Silas Ash she spoke of differently, quieter. He had died decades before, in exile, teaching census methods to archivists in a country that would take him; among his effects, returned to Sefa after his death, was a notebook with one line circled: *A name unrecorded is a door bricked shut. My brother is behind it. I will not stop knocking.* Sefa let me copy that line into my notes and watched me do it, and when I finished she said, "Now you've witnessed it too. That's how it works. That's how it always worked. One witness handing the page to another."

Bram — B., carried by B. — I found last, and he was the one who undid me. An old man running a small bindery of his own by then, three apprentices of his own, none of them his blood, all of them his. When I showed him the colophon, the actual leaf, he held it in both hands the way you hold something that might startle.

"I was seven," he said. "Pip was twelve and Sefa was ten, and they voted on whether to print the magistrate's name, and they let me vote too, which was either madness or the point. I said print it. Names are all they have left." He looked up. "I've been carrying something ever since. Fifty years. You get to put it down now. Thank you."

He asked me one question, and I have built my career on it since: *"Who counts them next?"*

* * *

The Office of Civic Records did not want the Winterbourne–Ash–Quill Archive. Let me be precise, because precision is the whole moral of this story: the Office did not refuse it. Refusal leaves a record. The Office simply declined, over six years, to find shelf space, funding, a catalogue number — the bureaucratic equivalent of a smile that never reaches the eyes. The same smile, I learned, that the Committee of Public Record wore in the 1860s when Silas Ash declined its invitation, and the same smile the old regime wore when it inventoried printers' loyalty, and the same smile every power wears when it is deciding which dead people it can afford.

So I resigned. It took me a year to dare it and one afternoon to do. Hobb — retired by then, still calling every clerk "miss" — helped me move the crates to a rented room above a stationer's shop in the print district, three streets from where the Winterbourne foundry once stood. We call it the Orchard Room, though there isn't a tree within sight, unless you count the engraving Sefa sent us of the regrown orchard as it looked on the fiftieth anniversary, when the survivors walked the rows together one last time and agreed, according to Pip's diary, that the truth should wait for kinder hands.

We are trying to be the kinder hands. We catalogued everything. We verified every testimony against guild rolls, parish books, shipping manifests, anything the regimes themselves kept without realizing they were corroborating us — because tyrants are meticulous, and their meticulousness convicts them. We printed a finding aid on a small press Bram donated, set by a young compositor named Odile who apprenticed with us and has hands, Sefa said when she visited, exactly like W. Winterbourne's descriptions suggest: too fine for any lie.

And on the first day of the new year, we did the thing the whole archive had been waiting fifty years and one careless tram line to have done.

We counted them. All of them. Out loud.

Four hundred and eleven pages of testimony. Two hundred and sixty-eight named dead, restored to the record with their trades, their streets, their unmended churns and whistled tunes. We read from morning until dark, taking turns, and neighbors came in off the street because the windows were open, because January in Vellham is cold but some things are colder closed, and by evening there were more listeners than readers and the stationer brought down chairs.

When we reached the final page, Odile set it fresh — she insisted, and I'm glad; the original leaf is too fragile now to handle — and we read the colophon together:

*Set by W. Winterbourne. Witnessed by S. Ash. Bound by M. Quill. Carried by B. — counted at last.*

An old woman near the window — I never learned her name — said, quietly, "Counted at last," and then, "and not last, either," which is the truest sentence anyone said all day.

* * *

The tram still bends around the tree. The city fought it, then priced it, then gave up, and now the 9 Line makes a small courteous arc through what used to be the orchard, around a tree that grew from a graft Pip Winterbourne-Quill planted in ash the spring after the fire. In autumn it drops apples onto the tracks, and the transit authority has stopped complaining, because the drivers like the smell.

I walk out there sometimes with the catalogue under my arm, though I don't need it anymore. I know where everything is. That is the whole of my profession, learned from people who buried a press under apple roots and trusted the dirt to keep faith: you find out where everything is, and you tell someone.

There is room in the Orchard Room. There is always room; that was Maren Quill's rule and it's ours. Last month a woman came in with her grandfather's letters and a story the official histories have no paragraph for, and Odile pulled a chair, and I reached for a pen.

Because a name unrecorded is a door bricked shut.

And we are still knocking.
