## 1. The Clock That Kept Wrong Time

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which Tamsin Oyelaran considered a bad omen on principle. Nothing good had ever come to her on a Tuesday. Tuesdays were for teeth being pulled and rents being raised and letters from officials beginning with the word *Regarding*. The summons itself was printed on paper stiff enough to shave with, and it said, in the officious hand of the town clerk:

*Mlle. Oyelaran is requested at the Observatory, nine o'clock sharp, regarding the master clock, which has ceased to keep time. Bring tools.*

"Regarding," she read aloud, in the toll house kitchen, to nobody in particular except Marta, who was mending nets by the stove, and Kolya, who was pretending not to listen over his bowl of coffee, which he drank as though it had personally wronged him.

"The master clock," Marta repeated. She said it the way other people said *the plague* or *the tax collector*. "That thing hasn't kept honest time since before you were born."

"That's why they're asking for me." Tamsin folded the letter into quarters, then eighths, then a shape that could only be described as hostile origami. "It's stopped entirely. Father Aubry says the whole hill's been deaf without it. Says the nuns have been ringing the chapel bell by guessing."

"Guessing," said Kolya darkly, in his accented French. "In my experience, everything important in this country runs by guessing. Trains. Governments. Weather."

"Well. Then perhaps I'll fix one thing that doesn't." She stood, brushed crumbs of yesterday off her apron, and went to fetch her tool roll from under the floorboards — pausing, as she always did, with her palm flat against the boards for a moment longer than strictly necessary, because under them lived everything she owned worth owning, and a person learns to touch her treasures the way she'd touch a sleeping child.

Marta watched her go with an expression Tamsin chose not to interpret.

* * *

The observatory sat on its hill like a bald old man who had once been handsome and knew it. Four storeys of pale stone, a dome gone green at the seams, and rising through its heart — visible from every street in Verreuil, from the bridge district, from the water meadows, from the middle of the river itself if you were fool enough to be out there in a boat — the great clock tower, with its four dials and its bell that had once told the entire town when to rise, when to eat, when to sleep, and during the occupation years, when to hide.

Tamsin climbed the hill in the grey morning light, her tool roll across her back like a soldier's pack, and let herself feel, briefly and privately, excited. This was the trouble with clocks: she could never stay angry at an assignment involving one. A clock that had failed was a clock that wanted telling things, the way a sick animal wanted telling things, if you were quiet enough and patient enough and didn't flinch at what it showed you.

The door at the base of the tower was unlocked. Inside, the stairwell smelled of dust and pigeon and old machine oil, and Father Aubry met her on the second landing, breathless, round, apologetic, holding a lantern though the windows gave plenty of light.

"Mlle. Oyelaran. Thank heaven. Nine o'clock sharp, you see, you keep better time than the clock does—"

"I'm an apprentice, Father, I can't afford not to." She was twenty-three and had been an apprentice since she was eleven and expected to be one until she died; this did not gall her as much as it might have, because apprenticeship meant usefulness, and usefulness meant invisibility, and invisibility had kept her alive through five years of occupation while louder people were taken away. "When did it stop?"

"Three days ago. Or — we think three days ago. That's rather the problem, isn't it?" He laughed, a small nervous laugh, and led her up. "The army took most of the mechanism's documentation when they garrisoned here, you know. And the last keeper died in the influenza. It's been nobody's clock for years."

*Everybody's clock,* Tamsin thought, but did not say. *Nobody's clock.* That was how the town talked now, in careful negatives. Nobody's brother. Nobody's shop. Nobody buried behind the customs house whose grave nobody tended because tending it was asking questions.

The clock room occupied the top of the tower, beneath the bell chamber, and it was magnificent in the way of neglected cathedrals. The great movement filled the wall — wheels the size of cartwheels, a pendulum eleven feet long hanging dead still, weights down at the bottom of their shafts like men who had given up. Grey light came through the louvres. Pigeons muttered somewhere above.

She set down her tools and simply looked at it for a long moment. Father Aubry, wisely, held his tongue.

"Escapement's seized," she said finally. "And someone's let the oil go to varnish. And — hm." She climbed the ladder bolted beside the frame and put her ear against the going train, then her fingers. "Someone's been in here. Recently. These plates have been disturbed — see, the screw heads are bright where a screwdriver slipped. Not old scratches. Days old, maybe less."

Father Aubry went slightly greyer. "Soldiers?"

"Soldiers don't slip with a screwdriver. Soldiers take screws out with bayonets and lose them." She frowned. "Who else has keys to this room?"

"The clerk. The garrison commandant, formerly. The keeper, God rest him. And—" he hesitated, "there was a man came about the archive. Before the armistice talks started. An archivist, he said. Very polite. He catalogued the papers downstairs for two weeks."

"And then?"

"Went away. Left no card." Father Aubry spread his hands. "People do that now."

They did. Tamsin had done it herself more than once.

The failure itself was almost disappointingly ordinary: a pivot worn through its bushing, grit in the escapement teeth, and one weight cable frayed nearly through, which explained the stoppage but not the disturbance. She worked for two hours in the cold, sleeves pinned back, tongue between her teeth, cleaning and re-oiling and filing a new bushing from a scrap of brass she'd brought up the hill on the chance — because a clockmaker who climbs hills without spare brass deserves whatever disappointment finds her, as her master used to say, and her master had been right about nearly everything except when to leave.

By half past eleven the pendulum swung again — a slow, enormous heartbeat, tick, and pause, and tock — and the going train turned overhead with a sound like a ship changing its mind. Tamsin sat back on her heels and permitted herself the small vanity of satisfaction.

Then the bell chamber above her shuddered, the great hammer lifted, and the clock struck noon for the first time in three days, and the sound rolled down through the stone and out over the town, and she heard — faintly, impossibly far below — dogs barking and someone cheering and a church bell answering late and out of tune, and she grinned up into the dusty dark like a fool.

Which is why she noticed the draft.

A cold thread of air was moving past her ankle where no air should move. She knelt. Behind the great wheel of the striking train, low near the floor where the case panel met the stone, a section of panelling sat a finger's width proud of its frame. New screws. Bright slots. Screws put back in a hurry by someone who cared about hiding a thing, not about doing it beautifully.

Tamsin looked at the draft. The draft looked at her.

She got out her smallest screwdriver.

* * *

The compartment was shallow — a hand's depth, cut into the space between the case and the old stone, lined with oilcloth against damp. Inside lay a bundle wrapped in sacking, and inside the sacking, a book.

Not a book. A ledger. Foolscap, board covers swollen with damp, a spine repaired once with string. On the first page, in a small precise hand, was written:

*Register of persons conscripted, deported, detained, or removed by order of the occupying authority, Verreuil district, 1914–1918. Kept contrary to orders. May God forgive the omission of any, and forgive us who kept it.*

Below that, columns. Dates. Villages. And names — hundreds of names, thousands, each with a disposition entered after it in abbreviations she learned to read within a page: *conscr.*, *deported*, *det., customs hse*, and the coldest one, appearing again and again as the war years darkened down the pages: *erased* — with nothing after it. No date, no place, no grave. Just the word, and the blank.

She understood, sitting back on her heels with the ledger open on her knees in the cold tower room, that some clerk or priest or keeper had copied these entries in secret, night by night, from the garrison's own records, and hidden them here in the one place in Verreuil that no soldier would ever think to search, because to soldiers a clock is furniture, and furniture does not remember.

She also understood that she should close it, wrap it, put it back, screw the panel home, and climb down the hill and tell absolutely no one.

She turned the pages instead. Wary curiosity, she told herself. Only that.

The entries marched backward through the years as she turned forward through the pages. Names she knew — the Vasseur boys from the tannery, conscripted, 1915. Old Sable the ferryman's son-in-law, detained. Whole families from the marsh villages, deported, their dispositions trailing off into *erased, erased, erased* until the column looked like a row of pulled teeth.

And then, near the middle of the book, under the autumn dates of 1916, in the same small hand, slightly pressed harder into the paper as though the writer's own fingers had resisted:

*Oyelaran, Emmanuel R. Age 19. Labour battalion, deported east, 14 October. Disposition: erased.*

The tower was very quiet. The pendulum swung. Tick, and pause, and tock. Somewhere below, the town went about its noon, buying bread, arguing, laughing at something, alive.

Manu. Nineteen years old. He'd gone out for coal and come back without it and never spoke of where he'd been, and six weeks later the lorries came, and he'd kissed the top of her head — she'd been fourteen and furious at being treated like a child — and said, *Keep the shop running, Tammy, clocks can't wind themselves,* and that was all. No letter ever came. The family that took her in afterward never asked twice about the brother, because asking twice was dangerous, and everyone in Verreuil had learned to leave blanks where people used to be. *Erased.* All those years she had carried the word *missing* around like a stone in her shoe, and the stone had always had a name written on the bottom of it, and the name was *erased*, and it had been written down here the whole time, twelve hundred steps from her bed, ticking over her head in the dark.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them the way you notice weather — remotely, as a fact belonging to somebody else.

Down the page, the entries continued. More names. More blanks. And at the foot of the October page, a final line in different ink, added later, cramped into the margin:

*If found: these are not all. The garrison burned its own copies in March '18. This is the only record left in the district. Whoever you are — it fell to you. I am sorry.*

Tamsin sat in the dust at the foot of the great dead-then-living clock, in the smell of oil and pigeons, and heard the pendulum count out the seconds of her old life, one by one, until they ran out.

Then she closed the ledger, wrapped it in the sacking, and did not put it back in the compartment. She put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where it lay warm and heavy as a sleeping animal, and she screwed the panel home so neatly that even she would never find it again, and she climbed down the tower stairs past Father Aubry, who called up something cheerful about payment, and she answered, "Send it to the toll house," in a voice that sounded, remarkably, like her own.

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold. The hill path dropped steeply toward the river, toward the bridge, toward home. Tamsin stood a moment at the top of it, feeling the ledger's edge press her ribs with every breath, and thought: *the army will come back for records. Everyone knows it. The delegation's coming, the garrison's packing crates already — and somewhere in a warehouse sits a list of what they mean to seize, and archives are always first, because archives are confession.*

She started down the hill, faster than was sensible on that path, one hand pressed flat against her coat.

She had to get it under the floorboards before anyone knew it existed.
