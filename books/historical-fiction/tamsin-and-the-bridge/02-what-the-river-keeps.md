## 2. What the River Keeps

The road down from the observatory was a mile of switchbacks, and Tamsin took it at a pace that would have shamed a funeral. She had the ledger inside her coat, pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and every step she took she could feel it — not heavy, exactly, for it weighed no more than a hymnal, but *present*, the way a splinter is present, the way a debt is present.

The river came into view at the third bend, grey-green and unhurried, wearing its morning mist like a borrowed coat. Below it lay the bridge district: two rows of leaning houses, the toll house with its crooked slate roof, the bridge itself — old stone, nine arches, patched in four places with whatever stone the masons could find after the army took the good blocks for their fortifications. The bridge had stood for three hundred years and looked like it intended to stand for three hundred more out of pure stubbornness.

Tamsin stopped where the road met the water and let herself breathe.

*You are useful and invisible,* she told herself, as she had told herself every day since she was twelve years old. It had been a survival strategy once. Somewhere in the last four years it had become something closer to a creed. But walking down that hill with a stolen ledger full of dead men's names against her heart, she felt neither useful nor invisible. She felt like a lit lamp in an unshuttered window.

She made herself walk normally the rest of the way.

* * *

The toll house smelled of woodsmoke and onions, which meant Marta was home, and of candle wax, which meant Brother Anselm had been by, and of gun oil, which meant Kolya was pretending to clean a rifle he had sworn up and down he'd thrown in the river years ago.

"Back already?" Marta called from the kitchen without turning around. Old Marta kept the bridge the way other women kept chickens or grudges — daily, fiercely, and with a running commentary. She was seventy-one and claimed to have been keeping the toll gate since before the war, though everyone knew she'd inherited the post from her late husband and simply refused to give it back to the town council on principle. "The master clock of a whole observatory, and they send you up in the morning and you're down by noon? Either you fixed it fast or you broke it worse."

"Neither," Tamsin said.

Marta turned then, wiping her hands on her apron, and her eyes — quick black eyes set in a face like a walnut shell — went straight to the shape under Tamsin's coat the way a ferret goes to a rabbit hole. Marta missed nothing. It was one of her most exhausting qualities.

"You've brought something home," Marta said.

"I've brought something home."

"Is it going to eat us?"

"It might," Tamsin said honestly.

Marta considered this. Then she jerked her chin toward the stairs. "Kolya's in his room playing soldier with that rifle he definitely doesn't have. Anselm's due back from the convent by vespers. I suggest we decide what we're doing before we're outnumbered by people who'll ask questions."

"You're not going to ask questions?"

"I'm saving them up," Marta said. "I find questions are like firewood. Better to stack them properly first."

* * *

They told Kolya in his room, which was less a room than a large closet he'd annexed through sheer persistence, and which contained a cot, a chair, forty-seven books (Tamsin had counted), and the rifle, which sat disassembled on a cloth like a patient on an operating table.

Kolya listened all the way through without interrupting, which was unlike him. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, twenty-six years old, with a deserter's permanent watchfulness behind eyes that wanted very much to be kind. When Tamsin laid the ledger open on his cot and turned to the page with her brother's name on it, he leaned over it for a long time.

"Emmanuel Oyelaran," he read aloud, quietly. "Conscripted. Deported." He looked up. "That's your brother."

"That's my brother."

"They wrote him down," Kolya said. "All this time I thought—" He stopped. He did that; he had learned in the army that sentences could get you killed, and some habits stayed. "All this time nobody wrote anything down. The whole war, nothing was written down. And here it is. Every name. They kept books, Tamsin. They were *proud* of their bookkeeping."

"Someone hid it in the clock," Tamsin said. "Someone who thought someday somebody would come looking."

"And instead they sent you," Marta said from the doorway, "because you're small and honest-looking. God has a sense of humor. It isn't a kind one."

"What do we do?" Kolya asked.

It was the question Tamsin had been turning over for a mile of switchbacks, and she still didn't have an answer. All she had was the ledger and the fear, and the fear had teeth.

"For now," she said, "we keep it."

* * *

The floorboards of the toll house had been lifted twice in living memory: once when Marta's husband had hidden a smuggler's bale of tobacco during the last bad year of the old century, and once when Kolya had hidden himself during the retreat, three days while soldiers searched house to house and Marta fed him through a trapdoor and lied to their faces with the serenity of a bishop. The boards near the hearth came up easier than anywhere else in the district. They knew the trick of it by now. That was the thing about hiding places, Tamsin thought, kneeling in the warm ash-smelling dark with the ledger in her hands — a hiding place gets better with use, the way a path gets worn into a meadow. The house itself learned how to keep secrets.

She wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, set it in the cavity between the joists, and fitted the boards back down. From outside, nothing. Just floor. Just a poor toll house with a crooked roof and an old woman's kettle on the stove.

"There," Marta said. "Now it's done and we're all accessories. Tea?"

"Marta—"

"Tea first. Guilt after. That's the order of operations in this house and it has served us well."

* * *

Brother Anselm arrived at vespers as predicted, a thin, stooped man of sixty with ink permanently staining the edges of his right hand and the gentle, abstracted air of someone whose body attended conversations while his mind was off cataloguing something. He had been a monk at the riverside priory until the army burned the priory's records and scattered the brothers; now he said mass in the chapel on the far bank and copied out damaged documents for anyone who asked, because, he said, a thing written down twice is harder to kill than a thing written once.

He listened to Tamsin's account with his hands folded, and when she finished he was quiet for so long that Kolya started to fidget.

"A ledger of names," Anselm said at last. "In the clock case."

"The clock keeps wrong time," Tamsin said. "It always has. I used to think it was badly built. Now I think—" She stopped, surprised by her own thought. "Now I think maybe it was built to hold something. Maybe the wrongness was the point. A clock that runs slow makes people climb up and look inside."

Anselm smiled faintly. "A door disguised as a machine. Yes. There's a long tradition of that sort of thing." His smile faded. "And what will you do with it, child?"

"Nothing. For now. Keep it safe."

"And if the keeping becomes dangerous?"

"Marta says guilt comes after tea," Tamsin said. "I'm following the house rule."

But that night she couldn't sleep. She lay in her narrow room above the workshop — her own room, with her own window over the water, the first room that had ever been hers alone — and listened to the sounds of the house settling, and the river going about its business beneath everything like a slow pulse.

This was the thing she couldn't say out loud, not to Marta with her stacked questions, not to Kolya with his careful silences, not to Anselm with his folded hands. The ledger was her brother. It was also, she understood with growing coldness, a threat to everything else she had. Because names in a ledger were evidence, and evidence was dangerous, and dangerous things attracted soldiers, and soldiers had already taken one family from her. If they came again — and armies, she had learned, come again the way weather comes again — they would come to the toll house, and they would find Marta, and Kolya, and Anselm, and the crooked roof and the kettle and the forty-seven books and the worn floorboards, and they would take it apart looking for what she had hidden inside it.

*I should put it back,* she thought. *Climb the hill tomorrow, put it in the clock, let the clock keep its secret.*

But even as she thought it she knew she wouldn't. Because the clock belonged to the army's requisition office now, or soon would; because the observatory archive was already being talked about in whispers as a place that might need *sealing*; because somewhere in the occupying command there were clerks with tidy minds who believed that a thing not written down had never happened, and who were quite capable of making sure no copy survived.

The ledger was the only book left in which Emmanuel Oyelaran existed.

She got up and knelt on the floorboards over the hiding place, alone in the dark, and pressed her palm flat against the boards the way you'd press a hand to a fevered forehead.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. Not to the ledger. To the house. To the people sleeping in it. "I'm sorry, and I'm going to keep you safe too. Both. Somehow."

The boards said nothing. Boards are good at that.

* * *

Two mornings later, Kolya came in from the bridge with his collar up and his face carefully arranged into an expression Tamsin had learned to read as *bad news, delivered calmly*.

"Soldiers on the north road," he said. "Full column. Wagons, requisition papers, the lot. Sergeant at the front with a list as long as my arm." He paused. "Tamsin. They're asking for grain, livestock, billets — and every official record in the town. Town hall, parish registers, the observatory archive. Everything with writing in it. They say it's for the armistice commission."

Marta set down her ladle very slowly. "Every record."

"Everything," Kolya said. "Except," and here he looked at Tamsin, and his voice gentled, "except the one book they don't know exists. Hidden under the floor of the one house they're about to start searching first."

Outside, far off, the sound of boots on the north road, steady as a clock ticking.

Tamsin put down her tea untasted.
