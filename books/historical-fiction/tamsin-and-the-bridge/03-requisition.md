## 3. Requisition

Kolya Vetrov had learned to measure the quality of a peace by the sounds it made.

A bad peace — like the one that had followed the occupying army out of the river town two winters ago — sounded like nothing at all. Like held breath. Like a town listening to its own heartbeat and not quite believing the war was over. But a good peace, a real one, made noise. It made the clatter of Marta's toll wheel turning for no reason except that she liked the sound of it. It made Brother Anselm arguing with a fishmonger about the theological implications of eels. It made Tamsin humming in the toll house kitchen, badly, while she soldered something that clicked when it ought to have chimed.

That morning, the peace was making all its usual noises, and Kolya was carrying water up from the river because it was his turn and he'd lost the argument about whose turn it actually was.

"Three buckets," Marta had said, holding up three fingers as though he might have forgotten how numbers worked since yesterday. "And don't slosh this time. The step's been damp for a week and Anselm says it's growing a conscience."

"It's moss," Kolya had said.

"In this house even the moss is judged."

So he was on the second bucket, halfway up the bank path with the morning sun warming the back of his neck, when he heard the drums.

Not festival drums. Not the church kind. Marching drums, coming down the far road from the ridge — the road that led away from the town, the road the army had taken when they left. Kolya set the bucket down very carefully, the way he'd learned to do in another life, another uniform, and looked east.

They came around the bend in a grey line: infantry, a supply cart, an officer on a horse too fine for the mud. A requisition column. He knew the shape of it the way you know the shape of a scar.

He stood there long enough that the first bucket went cold in him, then he turned and ran for the toll house.

* * *

By the time the column reached the bridge district, the news had run ahead of it the way bad news does, faster than any drummer, slipping through shutters and under doors. The market stalls were half-abandoned, their owners standing in doorways with the particular stillness of people who have done this arithmetic before: what can be carried, what can be hidden, what will simply be taken.

Kolya found them all in the toll house kitchen — Marta at the window with her arms folded like a fortress gate, Brother Anselm wringing his hands over a cooling loaf as though bread could be reasoned with, and Tamsin sitting very straight on the bench, her hands flat on the table, her eyes on the middle distance where numbers were being counted.

"They'll want the granaries first," Kolya said. "Then the mills. Then—" He stopped.

"Records," Tamsin said quietly.

"Records," Kolya agreed. "Every official archive in the town. They did it in Vellmark, in my— before." He didn't finish that sentence either. Nobody asked him to. In this kitchen, sentences were allowed to trail off into the places nobody wanted to follow.

"Why records?" Marta demanded. "Grain feeds soldiers. What do papers feed?"

"Papers feed the story," Anselm said sadly. "Who was conscripted. Who was deported. Who paid levies, who resisted, who was registered, who was—" He caught himself looking at Tamsin and stopped, but the word *erased* sat there anyway, unspoken and enormous, like furniture covered in a sheet.

Tamsin hadn't moved. She was still sitting with her hands flat on the table, and Kolya, watching her, saw the exact moment the fear left her face and something else arrived to take its seat. He'd seen that look once before — on a man in his old company who had decided, very calmly, over the course of a single afternoon, to stop being a soldier. It was the look of someone closing one door and opening another, and knowing both would be heavy.

"The observatory," she said. "The archive there. If they seal it—"

"It's already being sealed," Kolya said. "You can hear the carts."

From the window they could see the column drawn up in the market square, small figures moving with the brisk efficiency of men doing a job they'd been trained for and didn't hate enough to refuse. An officer was posting a notice on the customs house door. Even at this distance, Kolya knew what it would say, because such notices are written in only one grammar in all the world: *By order. All records. All grain. Compliance.*

"They're calling it administration," Anselm murmured. "It always has such tidy names. Requisition. Registration. Administration. As if the devil kept ledgers."

"He does," said Marta. "I've seen his handwriting. It looks exactly like an officer's."

The humor landed softly, the way their humor always did — a small lamp lit in a dark room, not to drive out the dark, only to prove the room was still theirs. But Tamsin didn't smile. She was looking at the floorboards. At a specific floorboard, near the cold hearth, the third one from the wall, which she had pried up eleven days ago and replaced so carefully that even Marta, who noticed everything, had needed three days to notice.

Under that board lay a ledger. Kolya had seen it once, by candlelight, when Tamsin had shown them what she'd found inside the master clock's case — every name the occupation had swallowed, entered in a clerk's neat hand: conscripted, deported, erased. Including a name that made Tamsin's voice go strange when she read it aloud, her brother's name, spoken like a prayer she'd only just remembered the words to.

"Now it's the only copy," Tamsin said. Her voice was level. That frightened Kolya more than shouting would have. "If they seal the observatory and carry off the archives, everything in that ledger exists nowhere else. Not on paper. Not anywhere." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terribly awake. "They're not taking our grain, Kolya. Well — they are. But that's not what they've come for. They've come to make sure that when this armistice gets signed, it gets signed over a clean page."

Marta turned from the window. "So we give them a dirty one."

"Marta," Anselm said reproachfully.

"What? I'm agreeing with the girl. It's a novelty. Let me enjoy it."

But Kolya wasn't laughing either, because he had understood something else, something the others were arriving at more slowly: that Tamsin had already gone past the decision and out the other side. She was now merely waiting for the rest of them to catch up. He knew the feeling intimately. It was the feeling of having been a coward for four years and finding, one ordinary morning, that the cowardice had worn through like an old boot sole, and underneath it there was — astonishingly — a foot. A person who intended to walk somewhere.

He thought about the ledger beneath the floor. He thought about the fact that he, Kolya Vetrov, deserter, had spent two years learning to be invisible in this town, useful in the way of a man who knows how to fix wheels and stay quiet, and he thought: *they will ask me to stop being invisible soon, and I will say yes, and I am terrified, and I will say yes.*

"You're going to do something," he said to Tamsin. Not a question.

"I'm going to make tea first," Tamsin said. "Whatever happens next, I refuse to face it uncaffeinated. Anselm, is that loaf edible or are we still negotiating with it?"

"It's risen against me," Anselm admitted.

"Then we eat the evidence and no one can prove anything."

And that — absurdly, warmly, in the teeth of the drums outside — was the moment Kolya felt the fragile peace shatter and felt, underneath the breaking of it, something sturdier being laid down, plank by plank, the way Marta repaired her bridge: not pretending the flood wouldn't come again, but building for it.

* * *

The requisition took three days.

On the first day they took the grain. Kolya watched from the toll house roof, ostensibly mending shingles and actually keeping count, as sacks went onto the supply cart in a slow grey stream. Old Ferenc the miller argued until a sergeant grew tired of arguing, and then Ferenc sat down in the road, and the soldiers stepped around him the way water steps around a stone, and took the mill keys instead.

On the second day they took the records. Carts came down from the observatory hill loaded with document boxes, and a clerk with ink-stained cuffs supervised the loading with the tenderness other men reserved for children. The customs house, the town hall, the parish registry — sealed, inventoried, carried off. Anselm went to plead for the baptismal rolls, on the grounds that souls belonged to God, and came back shaken and empty-handed, reporting that a lieutenant had told him, kindly, that God's paperwork would be returned after the delegation departed. "He smiled when he said it," Anselm reported. "That's the part I can't forgive. He smiled."

"And the observatory itself?" Marta asked.

"Sealed. Chained at the doors. There's a guard on the tower path day and night now." Anselm shook his head. "All that machinery, all those beautiful instruments, and they chain the *doors*. As if truth were a thing that used entrances."

On the third day they posted new notices, and the notices named a date — nine days hence — when the armistice delegation would arrive to sign, and a perimeter, and rules about who might approach it, and penalties for those who approached otherwise. Penalties rendered, as always, in the language of furniture: *removed*, *detained*, *processed*.

That evening the four of them sat in the kitchen with the shuttered lamp between them, and nobody spoke for a long time, which among these particular people was itself a kind of conversation.

Finally Marta said, "Nine days."

"Eight," said Tamsin, "and a night."

"You've been counting."

"I'm a clockmaker's apprentice," Tamsin said. "Counting is the whole profession." She reached down, and Kolya's breath caught — though he knew, of course, that the shutters were closed, that the street was empty, that no one could see — and lifted the third floorboard from the wall.

She brought the ledger up into the lamplight and laid it on the table, and the four of them looked at it: a plain thing, oilcloth-bound, corners soft with handling, containing more of the town than the whole convoy of carts had carried away.

"They think they've got everything," Tamsin said. She ran her thumb along the ledger's edge, gently, the way she handled watch movements. "Let them seal every door in this town. I know one entrance they haven't chained." Her eyes went, briefly, upward — toward the hill, toward the tower, toward the great master clock whose heart she alone knew how to open.

Kolya felt the fear settle into him, familiar as an old coat — and felt, beneath it, the other thing rising to meet it, quiet and certain as dawn.

"Tamsin," he said slowly, "what exactly are you planning?"

She looked up, and outside, far off on the ridge road, the drums began again — and she smiled.

"First," she said, "we need to learn the checkpoints."
