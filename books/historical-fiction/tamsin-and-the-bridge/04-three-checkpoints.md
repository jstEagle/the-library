## 4. Three Checkpoints

The trouble with secrets, Marta liked to say, is that they weigh nothing until you have to carry them somewhere.

Tamsin felt the truth of this every morning now, waking in the toll house with the ledger beneath her floorboard and the knowledge of it pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Three days since Requisition Day. Three days of soldiers at the observatory gate, of clerks hauling archive boxes into wagons, of the town learning to walk with its head down.

On the third evening, Marta drew the shutters, banked the stove low, and set a candle stub in the middle of the toll table — the same table where forty years of crossings had been logged in her spidery hand. Then she looked at Kolya, and Kolya looked at Tamsin, and nobody said *well* or *now* or *it's time*, because they were all three of them people who understood that some conversations begin best in silence.

"Right," said Brother Anselm, who was not technically part of the household but had developed the habit of appearing whenever there was danger or supper, and tonight there was both. He unrolled a sheet of paper he'd taken from the sacristy — an old processional route map, the kind used for Corpus Christi, with the stations marked in faded gold. "I've been thinking."

"That's frightening," said Kolya.

"I'm a monk. Thinking is most of the job." Anselm smoothed the map flat. "Now. If one hypothetically needed to move a hypothetical item from here—" he tapped the bridge district "—to here—" he tapped the delegation's compound on the far side of the river, where the armistice delegation had settled into requisitioned villas like cats who believed themselves temporary guests, "—one would face three obstacles."

"Checkpoints," Tamsin said.

"Three checkpoints," Anselm agreed. "The bridge. The customs house. And the perimeter around the delegation itself."

Kolya leaned over the map, his shadow huge across the little golden churches and processional stops. He had a way of going still when he studied things that reminded Tamsin uncomfortably of a heron deciding whether the water held anything worth striking. "I know two of them already," he said. "I walked past all three today. Casually. As a man does."

"You limped past them," Marta said. "Casually limping is still limping."

"My leg has opinions about cold weather. It is not a crime."

"It will be if a sergeant decides it is."

They bent over the map together, and Tamsin watched their heads — the grey crown of Marta's, the dark tonsured circle of Anselm's, Kolya's untidy brown thatch — and felt the strange doubled ache that had lived in her chest since the night under the floorboards: grief for her brother, yes, still, always, but underneath it something warmer and more terrible, which was how much she loved these people and how much she could lose.

*Useful and invisible,* she reminded herself. That had kept her alive through four years of occupation. But invisible men don't carry ledgers across rivers.

* * *

"The bridge first," Marta said, and told it plainly, as she told everything: "There are six soldiers posted at the near end, two at the far end, and one who walks it. He walks the span every twenty minutes, day and night, and he looks at faces. Not papers — faces. He's got a memory for them, that one. I've seen him stop a woman three days running because he thought she'd changed her shawl between visits."

"Which soldier?" Tamsin asked, though her stomach had already begun to sink, because she could guess.

Marta gave her a long look over the candle flame. "You know which soldier."

"Sergeant Brandt."

"Sergeant Brandt," Marta confirmed. "Who came to your master's shop twice before the war ended, once with a broken pocket watch and once with no excuse at all, just to stand in the warm and talk about gear trains. Who paid you for work you hadn't done because, as I recall, you fixed his watch for free and he couldn't abide owing a debt."

"He said a craftsman should be paid," Tamsin murmured.

"And then he stood in the doorway looking like a man trying to remember how doors worked." Kolya snorted. "So. The one checkpoint guarded by a man who likes you."

"That's not a guard, that's a complication."

"A complication is what I call it when my leg aches," Kolya said. "What you call it is a door."

She didn't answer. She turned the watch over in her mind instead — Brandt's dead son's watch, though she hadn't known whose it was then, only that the case was worn soft at the edges from handling and that the sergeant had held it like something rescued from water. She remembered winding it, listening to it, telling him the mainspring was tired but true, that it wanted cleaning more than mending, that some watches only need someone to pay attention to them. He had nodded as though she'd said something about himself.

That was the trouble with Brandt. Everything she said about clocks, he heard as confession.

"We don't use him yet," Marta decided. "We use him last, if we must, and we spend him carefully. A favor from a grieving man is not an infinite purse." She pulled the candle closer. "The crossing itself. The toll house is watched — they count us now, did you notice? Two old women cross for market, one young clockmaker crosses for the observatory, the monk crosses for his parish. We're catalogued. So nobody crosses alone carrying anything worth taking."

"So the ledger doesn't cross the bridge at all," Tamsin said slowly. "Or it crosses inside something they've already decided not to look inside."

"Inside what?" Kolya asked. "Soldiers look inside everything. It's half the job. The other half is standing."

"Not everything." Tamsin found herself smiling, and the smile surprised her. "The funeral wagon. Old Ferenc's — they buried his wife Sunday, God rest her, and the wagon goes back and forth to the cemetery across the river for the family plot. Nobody searches a coffin. Not even these ones; I watched a corporal wave it through Tuesday and make the sign of the cross while he did it, and he didn't believe a word of the prayers, I could tell by his hands."

Anselm made a small disapproving noise. "Using a coffin."

"Using an *empty* coffin," Tamsin said. "Ferenc owes Marta eleven years of back tolls and would owe her his soul besides. The coffin rides empty going over, weighted with stones so it behaves properly, and comes back—"

"—carrying its passenger," Kolya finished. His heron-stillness broke into a grin. "That's wicked. I approve completely."

"It's also the first checkpoint solved and we haven't left the table," Marta said, but her eyes had gone bright, and Tamsin knew that brightness. It was the look Marta got when the ice went rotten in March and she spent a week inventing ways to keep the ferry running anyway. Planning, for Marta, had always been a form of hope with its sleeves rolled up.

* * *

The customs house was harder.

They talked it through until the candle burned down twice and Marta fed it a third, and by the end the map was crowded with pencil marks in three different hands: Kolya's arrows, Anselm's careful crosses, Tamsin's own tight little notes about sightlines and shift changes, because she had spent three afternoons loitering near the customs yard with a notebook, pretending to sketch the building's cornice, and a clockmaker notices intervals the way other people notice weather.

Two sentries at the door, rotating hourly. A sergeant's desk in the front room, and behind it the search tables, and beyond those the yard with the wagons. The wall on the river side was stone and shoulder-high, and the lane behind it ran along the water — the low-water lane, Kolya called it, where in autumn the river dropped enough to walk the mud shelf under the customs dock.

"In November?" Anselm said skeptically. "The mud would take your boots and possibly your legs."

"Not in November," Kolya said. "In the dark, with a rope off the dock pilings, and me going first because my leg complains less going downhill than uphill, which is the only kindness it has ever shown me." He tapped the map. "And Tamsin goes through the building itself, not around it. Because here's the thing none of them have thought of — everyone sneaks *past* the customs house. Nobody sneaks *through*. They expect rats to go around. So we give them a rat who walks in the front like she owns the place."

"She can't," Marta said flatly. "Her face is catalogued too. I told you — they count us."

"They count the bridge district," Tamsin said. And then, quietly, because saying it aloud made it real: "But I'm expected at the customs house. Sergeant Voss sent for my master twice last month to regulate the office clock. My master's hands shake too badly now to climb a ladder, and Voss knows it, and Voss hates waiting for anything, including justice. If I come to service the clock — with my tools, with my apprentice's apron, with a work order signed by my master — I walk through the front door, up the ladder, into the clock room. Which sits directly above the search room, and has a floor hatch, because the weights drop down through it."

Silence around the table.

"You'd be sealed in a clock room above twenty soldiers," Anselm said, "with a ledger hidden in your toolbox, waiting for the moment between shifts."

"Yes."

"And if they search the toolbox?"

"Clockmakers' toolboxes are sacred ground," Kolya said. "Even soldiers know you don't put your hand in where the knives live." But he wasn't smiling when he said it, and neither was anyone else.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when they came to the third checkpoint, and the candle had burned to a thumb's width, and Marta finally pushed back from the table and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands.

"The delegation's perimeter," she said. "And here, children, is where I tell you the truth, which is that I don't know it. Soldiers guarding delegates aren't soldiers anymore, exactly. They're something else. Nervous. Important. The kind of nervous that shoots first and files the report later."

"There'll be a list," Anselm said. "Names on a list, and only names on the list pass. Petitioners, maybe. Clerks."

"Petitioners," Kolya repeated, tasting the word. "A town full of petitioners, queuing at the wire, and every one of them with a story about grain taken or sons taken or houses burned. You know what happens to a queue like that?"

"Nothing good," Marta said.

"It becomes a crowd. And a crowd at a wire becomes an incident. And an incident—" he looked at Tamsin "—is cover. Noise. A hundred faces all shouting at once, and among them one girl who isn't shouting, just walking, quiet as a key turning."

"No," Marta said.

"Marta—"

"*No.*" It came out flat and final, the voice she used on the ice when the ferry cable sang wrong. "We are not planning a riot. We are not spending this town's fear like coin. Find another way, Kolya, or I'll carry the book myself and you can all explain to me afterward why that was stupid."

For a moment nobody spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere out on the black water, the bridge sentry's boots rang on the planks — twenty minutes, on the hour, faithful as anything Tamsin had ever serviced.

Then Tamsin said, "There might be another way. Through the tower."

They all looked at her.

"The observatory clock tower. It stands inside the delegation's compound now — they requisitioned the whole hill. But the tower has the old maintenance stair, the one the builders used, the outside one, behind the buttress. It's not on any army map. It's barely on ours; my master showed it to me once and swore me to secrecy because he said if the town council learned the tower could be entered without passing the observatory door, they'd find a way to tax it." She heard her own voice steadying as she laid it out, the way it always did when she talked mechanisms. "I know every step of that stair. I know which treads are loose. I know the hatch at the top and the weight channels and the places a body can stand while the great wheels turn. Nobody alive knows that tower better than I do. Not even the army that's sitting inside it."

Marta studied her for a long time across the guttering candle. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft in the way that was worse than shouting. "You're talking about walking into the middle of the signing, child. Into the room where the whole world's watching. There's no coming back from invisible after that. Whatever happens in that room — you'll be seen. Forever."

"I know."

"And you'd do it anyway."

Tamsin looked down at her own hands, ink-stained, oil-burned, steady. She thought of Emil's name in the ledger, written in a clerk's indifferent hand, *deported, 1916, no further record.* No further record. Four words for a whole human boy who used to cheat at cards and whistle flat.

"Somebody has to be seen," she said. "Once. For all of them."

Marta closed her eyes. Kolya reached over and squeezed Tamsin's shoulder once, hard, and let go. Anselm began, very quietly, to gather up the map.

It was Marta who spoke last, and it was not agreement, exactly. It was something older than agreement.

"Then we do it properly," she said. "No heroics, no improvising, no clever ideas on the day. Every step planned. Every step rehearsed. And Tamsin—" she pointed one blunt finger across the table "—the first thing tomorrow, you find out exactly when the delegation signs. Exact day, exact hour. Until we know that, all we have is a plan for a door that might already be locked."

Tamsin nodded. And in the back of her mind, calm as a ticking escapement, sat the obvious answer, the one she'd been circling for three days: there was exactly one man in this town who knew the armistice timetable, and exactly one broken watch in this town he cared about more than his orders.

She just had to decide whether she was brave enough — or cruel enough — to knock on his door and ask.
