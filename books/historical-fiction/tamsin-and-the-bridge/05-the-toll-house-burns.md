## 5. The Toll House Burns

Marta Galloway had kept the bridge for forty-one years, and in that time she had developed firm opinions about fire.

Fire on the river was a thing of two kinds: the honest kind — a lamp dropped, a barge stove left unattended, the occasional fool who thought he could smoke while mending tarred rope — and the dishonest kind, which arrived with boots and orders and an officer's polite regret. She had seen the second kind during the occupation years. She knew its smell before she ever saw it: paraffin poured where it had no business being poured.

So when Kolya came pounding up the toll house steps at half past eleven at night, shouting her name in that accent he'd never quite managed to file down, she was already pulling on her boots.

"Smoke," he gasped. "From the north end. Under the arches."

"Not from the toll house?"

"Not yet."

*Not yet.* Marta took the lamp down off its hook and did not light it. A lit lamp made you a target; a dark woman moving fast through her own bridge was simply weather.

They went out onto the planking together. The river lay black and slow beneath them, carrying the town's few remaining lights like spilled coins. And there, at the far end, under the customs arch where the soldiers had set up their requisition post three weeks ago — a glow. Orange, low, patient. The glow of something that had been *set*, not something that had happened.

"They're burning the barges," Kolya said. "The grain barges they couldn't unload."

"Why burn what you can't steal?"

"To teach us the arithmetic." He said it flatly, but his hands were shaking. Marta had noticed this about Kolya in the four years since he'd deserted into her care: the boy's body remembered things his mouth refused to discuss. "A ton of grain lost for every hour we grumble. That kind of arithmetic."

"Well," said Marta, "I've never been good with figures. It's why I keep the tolls in coins you can count on your fingers."

She said it to make him laugh, and it worked, mostly. But underneath the joke her mind had gone somewhere colder and much more specific: the floorboards of the toll house kitchen. Third board from the stove, the one with the knot shaped like a fish. Under it, wrapped in oilcloth, the ledger.

Tamsin had hidden it there eleven days ago, kneeling in the dark with her sleeves rolled up, her small clever hands doing exactly what they were made for — fitting a thing precisely into a space no one would think to look. *No one searches a toll house,* Tamsin had reasoned. *Everyone knows Marta counts every coin twice; there's nothing under her floor but dust and dead mice.*

It had seemed like sound logic at the time. Marta now reflected, walking as quickly as dignity allowed toward a growing orange stain in the night, that sound logic and safe logic were frequently different animals.

* * *

The fire started honest-looking, which was how she knew it wasn't.

It began in the boat shed at the north end — the shed where old Pieter kept his punt and his nets and his opinions about everyone — and it spread along the pilings with the cheerful efficiency of paraffin meeting dry timber. By the time Marta reached the shed the heat was already pushing at her face like a large rude dog.

Pieter stood in his nightshirt watching it, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man being personally insulted by physics.

"The shed," he said. "Forty years I've kept that shed."

"You'll keep another," Marta said. "Where are the soldiers?"

"Gone." He jerked his chin toward the customs house. "Marched off ten minutes ago, all proper, like churchgoers. Left this behind to do their talking."

Kolya had already grabbed a bucket. Others were coming now — Brother Anselm in his cassock with his sleeves tied back, the widow Hensel, the ferryman's boys, a dozen shadowy figures forming a bucket line from the river stairs. The bridge district had survived occupation, requisition, and two floods; it did not intend to be beaten by a bonfire with delusions of grandeur.

And it might have stayed a bonfire. That was the terrible arithmetic of it later, the sum Marta would turn over and over in her hands like a bad coin: if the fire had stayed where it was set, the ledger would have slept on under its floorboard, and Marta would have slept on above it, and Sergeant Whoever-He-Was would have gotten exactly what he paid for — a lesson, some ashes, nothing else.

But the wind came off the water at midnight, as it always did, and it turned the fire toward the bridge itself. Toward the toll house.

"Line!" Marta roared, and discovered she still had a voice that could stop a barge. "Wet sacking on the planks! Kolya — the roof, get on the roof!"

She should have gone to the roof with him. Or to the bucket line. Or anywhere, frankly, other than inside her own kitchen, which by then was filling with smoke and orange light and the particular groaning sound timber makes when it decides, reluctantly, to become charcoal.

But the third board from the stove. The knot shaped like a fish.

She got down on her knees in the smoke, coughing, eyes streaming, and pried up the board with her fingernails like a girl half her age, because apparently that is what forty-one years of bridge-keeping trains into a body — not sense, but stubbornness. The oilcloth package came up warm. Warm, not hot. There was still time. There is always a moment, Marta believed, when God offers you the exit, and the trick of a long life is noticing it while it's open.

The window was closed. The door was on fire.

Which left the floor hatch — the old coal chute down to the river stair, which she hadn't opened since the flood of '13 and which opened now with a shriek that could have curdled milk. Marta tucked the ledger inside her coat against her chest, the way she'd once carried kittens out of the same flood, and lowered herself into the dark, trusting her knees, her memory, and a staircase she could have climbed blindfolded because she had, several times, after good evenings at the Anchor.

Behind her, the toll house kitchen gave up its argument with the fire and lost.

* * *

She came out at the river stair into cold air and steam, and sat down hard on the lowest step with the ledger hugged to her ribs, laughing — actually laughing, the wild kind that comes out of people at fires and funerals — until Brother Anselm crouched beside her and put a steadying hand on her shoulder.

"Marta. Are you hurt?"

"My kitchen's hurt," she managed. "I'm merely furious."

Above them the bucket line was winning, barely. The toll house would stand, or most of it; the roof was gone and the kitchen was a black cave, but the walls held, and the bridge held, and by two in the morning the fire had been argued down to smoking embers and the district had assembled the only way districts like theirs assembled — with tea. Someone produced a samovar from nowhere, as someone always does. Widow Hensel began cataloguing damages aloud in the tone other women used for gossip. Pieter sat wrapped in two blankets mourning his nets with genuine liturgical gravity.

Tamsin found her there, on the step, still holding the bundle. The girl's face went white when she understood what it was.

"It's wet," Marta said, handing it over. "A bit smoked. You'll want to check every page, and don't scold me about the oilcloth, I know, linen would have breathed better, but the linen was holding onions."

Tamsin didn't scold. She unwrapped the ledger right there on the step, page by page, by lamplight, her fingers trembling so badly Anselm steadied the book for her. The names held. Ink doesn't burn easy under oilcloth, thank God for small stubbornnesses. When she reached the middle of the book she stopped, and pressed her lips together, and Marta looked away to give her privacy with her brother.

"He'll stay readable," Marta said quietly. "That's the whole job now. Keep them readable."

"Marta—" Tamsin's voice cracked clean through. "You went *inside*. For a book. You could have—"

"For a book," Marta agreed. "Now hush and drink your tea. Sentiment keeps."

* * *

The soldiers came back at dawn.

Not the arsonists — those had marched off with theatrical innocence hours before — but a different patrol, crisp and apologetic, led by a lieutenant with a clipboard and a face like a man reading someone else's shopping list. They picked through the ashes of the boat shed. They interviewed Pieter, who gave them nothing but a forty-minute history of his nets. They interviewed the ferryman's boys, who knew nothing, credibly, being asleep, being young, being stupid in the useful way.

Then the lieutenant climbed the toll house steps and looked at the scorched kitchen, and then at Marta, who was standing in her doorway with her arms crossed and soot to her elbows.

"Madam. You were seen leaving the building by the river stair during the fire. Carrying something."

"A cat," said Marta.

He looked at her.

"One of the Hensel cats," she elaborated. "Orange. Stupid as bread. It hides under my floorboards when there's thunder. Or fire. Animals have no nerve at all."

"The witnesses describe a rectangular object, madam. Wrapped."

"Cats come in rectangles if you carry them properly."

The lieutenant wrote something down. Marta watched him write it and felt, with the clarity that comes at the end of a long bad night, the ground shifting under her feet in the old familiar way. She had spent the occupation years being useful and unremarkable, a woman whose comings and goings were as interesting as tide tables. But usefulness attracts attention eventually, like any light. Someone had talked — maybe the boy who'd seen her at the stair, maybe worse, maybe someone who'd seen Tamsin hiding the package days ago and had only now done his arithmetic. It didn't matter which. What mattered was the clipboard.

"I'm obliged to ask you to accompany us, madam. For questioning regarding the fire."

"And if I decline?"

"Then I'm obliged to insist less politely."

Behind her, in the dim interior, she heard Tamsin's breath catch. Kolya had gone very still by the woodpile in the way he went still — soldier-still — and she caught his eye just long enough to pass one message down four years of trust: *stay where you are.*

"Well," Marta said briskly, reaching for her shawl, "someone has to teach you people how tea is properly made. Your lieutenant's lot boiled it last week like they were scalding hogs."

Anselm stepped forward. "I'll come with her."

"You'll stay," Marta said, "and count the spoons. If they've taken my good spoons I want it on record." Then, lower, only for him, gripping his wrist once, hard: "Watch the girl. Watch the floorboards. Whatever happens to me, the names go across those checkpoints — do you understand me? That's the installment I'm paying. See it's not wasted."

The lieutenant cleared his throat. The patrol waited. The river ran on underneath everything, gray and indifferent in the early light, keeping what it always kept.

Marta settled her shawl, lifted her chin, and walked down her own steps between soldiers, past her own bridge, already composing in her head the speech she intended to deliver about tea — and about how a woman who could carry a burning night's grief in one hand and a cat-shaped lie in the other was very far from finished.

Behind her, faint but unmistakable, she heard Tamsin begin to argue with Anselm in a low fierce voice — and Marta smiled to herself, because she knew that tone.

The girl had a plan. God help the checkpoints.
