## 6. A Watch for Sergeant Brandt

The watch arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in a handkerchief that smelled of pipe smoke and gun oil, and Tamsin knew before she unwrapped it whose it was.

Sergeant Brandt had come to the toll house twice during the occupation — once with a jammed pocket watch and once, absurdly, with a music box that played a lullaby a half-tone flat. Both times he'd sat at Marta's table with his cap in his lap like a schoolboy, both times he'd paid too much, and both times he'd said, *you're the one who fixed the observatory clock, aren't you,* as if that were a question about the weather rather than an accusation. He was not a cruel man. That was somehow worse. Cruel men she understood; they were weather, they were floodwater, you built against them. It was the decent ones who frightened her, because decency could be negotiated with, and negotiation meant conversation, and conversation meant being seen.

She unwrapped the handkerchief now, alone in the back room of Brother Anselm's infirmary where she'd taken to working since the requisition, and found a silver-cased watch gone brown with age, its crystal cracked like river ice in March.

On the inside of the case cover, engraved: *For Emil. May your hours be long.*

Emil Brandt. Born 1901. She remembered because Kolya had told her, in his flat way, over the floorboards where the ledger slept: *the sergeant's boy went east with the third transport and never came back.* Third transport. She had read the third transport's names eleven times. Emil Brandt was there, line forty-two, between a cooper's son and a boy from the tannery, and no one had told the sergeant, because no one tells sergeants anything except what to shoot.

Tamsin held the watch up to the window light and felt something turn over in her chest that she did not have a name for.

"He sent it to you?" Anselm asked from the doorway, sleeves rolled, smelling of carbolic.

"He sent it to the toll house." Which had burned. "Marta's arrest made the rounds of the garrison faster than the fire did." She turned the crown gently. The movement inside gave a dry, arthritic click. "He wants it repaired before —" She stopped.

Before the armistice. Before the delegation signed or walked away. Before whatever happened next happened to all of them.

"Before," Anselm agreed, and did not press, which was one of the thousand small mercies she kept count of without meaning to.

* * *

The problem was not fixing the watch. The problem was that fixing it would take an hour, and she needed two.

She worked through Monday night by lamplight, and the watch talked to her the way watches do when you listen properly. The mainspring was sound but tired; someone had wound it hard in grief, probably, cranking at it the way you'd shake a person who wouldn't wake. The balance staff was bent. The escapement was clogged with river sand — he'd carried it in a coat pocket through three years of mud, then. And beneath the dial, when she lifted it with the proper tool, a curl of paper: a lock of fine dark hair, tied with thread gone grey.

She sat very still for a long moment, holding another woman's grief in her tweezers.

"You're going to trade on this," she said aloud to herself, disgusted. "You're going to sit across from him and use his dead son as a door key."

But Marta was in a cell under the customs house, seventy-one years old, charged with *harboring contraband documents*, and the only person who might soften Brandt before the tribunal — if there even was a tribunal, if there even was a soft place left in the man — was the invisible apprentice clockmaker whom sergeants underestimated on principle.

Usefulness as armor. She'd worn it since she was twelve years old, since the first soldiers came and her father put a file in her hand and said, *be useful and be nothing else.* It had kept her alive. It had also, she realized now, holding the watch that ticked wrong, kept her very small.

Well. Small things fit through cracks.

* * *

Brandt came to the infirmary himself, which she'd expected, and came alone, which she hadn't. He stood in the doorway with his cap again in his hands, looking at the rows of cots, at Anselm's patients, at everything except her, and said, "Is it — can it be done?"

"It can always be done," Tamsin said. "It's a question of time."

"How much time?"

"That depends," she said, "on how much time you have."

Something flickered across his face — the recognition of a trap, perhaps, or just the weariness of a man too tired to check them anymore. He pulled a stool to her workbench and sat, heavily, the way old men sit and young soldiers pretend they don't.

"They're giving me until Friday," he said. "Then we march north. Delegation signs Thursday, if they sign at all." He looked at the watch in her hands. "I wanted it keeping time before I go. I wanted —" His jaw worked. "His mother keeps asking what time it was. When the transport left. Nobody will tell us anything, Fraulein Oyelaran. We were the ones doing the telling, and nobody would ever tell us anything."

*Tamsin.* He'd used her name. Not *girl*, not *clockmaker*. Her brother's surname, attached to hers, spoken by an occupying sergeant as if it were simply true.

"The mainspring's tired," she heard herself say. "Someone wound it too hard."

"I did," Brandt said. "Every night. For a year. I couldn't sleep, so I wound it." He almost laughed. "Wound a dead boy's watch every night like saying grace. Then it stopped, and I thought — well. There's God's opinion of that."

Tamsin set down her loupe. Here was the crack in the man, wide enough to walk through, and she hated herself for measuring it even as she measured it.

"Sergeant. Your son was on the third transport out of Saint-Aubin station. Do you know where it went?"

"I know what they told us. Labor service. East."

"I know what the ledger says."

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water. Somewhere in the infirmary a patient coughed. Brandt did not move, but his face did — the slow rearrangement of a man understanding exactly what she was, what she held, and why she was telling him.

"There's a record," he said quietly. "A real one."

"There's a real one. With names. Dates. Destinations." She let that sit. "It exists because someone in your army's own administration believed somebody should write the truth down, even if only to hide it in a clock."

"And you want something."

"Marta Geller is in your cell under the customs house. She is seventy-one. She has kept that bridge since before you were born, and she is guilty of nothing except being old in a town full of soldiers who need someone to blame." Tamsin met his eyes. "Release her. Quietly. A clerical error. Paperwork gets lost in a requisition — you've seen it happen."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the watch keeps time and the ledger stays hidden until after your army leaves, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what was in it." She paused. "Or you'll spend the rest of your life knowing, because I'll send you a copy either way. But Marta walks free on Thursday morning, before the signing, or you learn nothing from me ever again."

It was the boldest thing she had ever said to a uniformed man, and her hands were shaking so badly she had to hide them under the workbench, among the gears, where shaking things belonged.

Brandt looked at the watch for a long time. At the cracked crystal. At the lock of hair she had re-tucked beneath the dial, careful as a nurse.

"Thursday," he said finally. "Morning. If she's released, it wasn't me, and if anyone asks, you blackmailed a sergeant of the occupation army and I'll deny it to my grave." He stood, put his cap on, and paused at the door. "Fraulein. The third transport. Was there —"

"Line forty-two," Tamsin said softly. "Emil Brandt. Between a cooper's son and a boy from the tannery. They were registered together. Whoever wrote the ledger took the trouble to keep the boys together, at least on paper."

He nodded once, twice, like a clockwork figure running down, and left without another word.

* * *

That night she finished the watch. New balance staff, cleaned escapement, mainspring eased and oiled. She set it ticking on the bench and listened to it run — steady, honest, keeping time the way her brother's name deserved to be kept.

Under the floorboards, the ledger waited. In the customs house cellar, Marta waited. On Friday, an army would march north, and somewhere between here and the delegation's perimeter stood two more checkpoints that no amount of decent men's grief would open.

Kolya appeared in the doorway with a lantern and his crooked smile.

"Well? Do we have our sergeant?"

"We have our sergeant," Tamsin said, closing the watch case over Emil's hair. "What we don't have is a funeral."

"A funeral?"

"For the bridge checkpoint. You said the ferryman owed you a favor. I'm thinking it's time somebody died." She looked up, and found she was smiling, and was surprised at how little it cost her. "Somebody official. Somebody with paperwork."

Kolya's grin widened slowly, terribly, in the lamplight.

"Tamsin," he said, "remind me never to make you angry."
