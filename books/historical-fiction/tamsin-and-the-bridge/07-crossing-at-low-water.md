## 7. Crossing at Low Water

Kolya had learned three things in four years of war and one year of peace, and he recited them now like a catechism as he crouched behind the coal sacks at the river's edge. First: no plan survives contact with a soldier. Second: a frightened man runs; a brave man runs slower but in the right direction. Third — and this was the one Marta had taught him, thumping his chest with her knuckles like she was testing a melon for ripeness — *you do not die for paper, you live so the paper means something.*

He intended to honor all three. He intended, very specifically, to survive this night.

The river was low. It always ran low before the autumn rains, baring its ribs of stone and mud along the northern bank, and tonight it ran low on purpose, if a river could be said to have purposes, which Kolya had come to believe it did. The ferryman Vasyl had been paid — not in money, because none of them had money worth the name, but in the thing Kolya had traded away with the most regret: his grandmother's samovar, silver-plated, dented on one side from when he'd dropped it fleeing through a kitchen in Galicia. Vasyl had wept over it. Then he'd agreed.

"A funeral," Tamsin had said that afternoon, laying out the plan on Marta's table with the same careful fingers she used on clockwork, each piece set down exactly where it would be needed. "Nobody stops a funeral. Not even soldiers. Even the ones who made the funerals."

She had not looked at anyone when she said it. Brother Anselm, standing by the stove with his great gentle hands folded, had closed his eyes briefly, the way he did during the worst parts of Mass.

So there was a funeral. A real one, as it happened — old Pietr Vassilyevich, who had died on Tuesday of his heart finally giving out after eighty-one years of stubbornness — and the town had agreed, without being asked twice, to make it a large one. Half the bridge district turned out in their black shawls and good coats, carrying candles guttering in jars, singing the low droning hymn that seemed to come up out of the ground itself. And woven among them, unnoticed as a thread of the same color, went Tamsin, hooded, the ledger flat against her belly under her coat where a woman might have been pregnant in another life, in another country, under any sky but this one.

Kolya's job was the rear. He walked at the back of the procession with a candle of his own and an expression of profound grief, which required no acting whatsoever, since grief was the one language he remained fluent in.

* * *

The first checkpoint was the foot of the bridge itself. Two soldiers, a lantern, a sawhorse painted with white stripes that someone had done drunk or in a hurry, because the lines wobbled like a river seen from a great height. A sergeant with a clipboard — they all had clipboards now, Kolya thought; the whole world had been reduced to lists — held up his hand.

"Procession," said Anselm, who had appointed himself frontman on the grounds that no soldier on earth wanted to argue with a monk holding a cross. "Old Pietr Vassilyevich. You knew him perhaps? He sold you fish, I think, last spring."

The sergeant looked at the candles, at the shawls, at the faces above them, which were arranged in expressions ranging from genuine mourning to the particular blankness of people who have decided to see nothing. He stepped aside. The white-striped sawhorse might as well have been a garden fence.

Kolya passed it with his candle burning steadily and his heart hammering somewhere up around his tonsils. Behind him, at the tail of the procession, he heard one young soldier say to another, quietly, almost embarrassed: "Let them bury him. Whoever he was."

*Whoever he was.* Kolya nearly laughed, and swallowed it down, because laughing at a funeral was the sort of thing that got a man remembered, and being remembered was the entire opposite of what they needed tonight. The whole art of it — Tamsin's art, really, though she'd never call it art — was to be unmemorable. Useful and invisible. She'd survived five years that way. Kolya had survived by deserting, which was a different technique entirely and involved considerably more running.

They crossed the bridge in the funeral's slow tide. At the far end the mourners peeled away toward the churchyard, and Tamsin peeled away too, into the lane behind the tannery, and Kolya lost sight of her dark coat between two buildings and felt the peculiar lurch he always felt when she disappeared — half relief, half terror, like letting go of a rope over deep water.

His own job began now. The customs house.

* * *

The customs house sat at the north landing like a fat grey cat that had decided the river belonged to it. It was the second checkpoint, and the harder one: the road from the bridge district to the delegation's compound ran directly past its doors, and the army had ringed it with wire and posted six men instead of two. Nobody was getting past the front. That had been settled in planning, with Marta rapping the table and saying, "Front is for honest fools and dead ones."

Which left the back, and the back had a flaw in it, because the customs house backed onto the low-water bank, and the low-water bank had a drainage grate, and the drainage grate had rusted loose sometime in August when Kolya himself had gone through it to retrieve a crate of tobacco a smuggler friend had stashed there. He had meant to mention the loose grate to somebody official. He had then, characteristically, forgotten.

Tamsin would go through the grate, along the drain channel, and come up inside the customs yard beyond the wire — because the delegation's compound could only be approached by the north road, and the north road passed through the customs yard, and the yard gate was guarded from outside but not, the gods willing and the corporal asleep, from within.

Kolya's part was to be memorable. Deliberately, gloriously memorable, for the first and only time in his life.

He waited until the signal — three knocks of a stone against the toll bell, muffled with cloth, which Marta had rigged that morning with the tenderness other women reserved for knitting — and then he stood up out of the coal shadows, staggered dramatically into the lantern light at the corner of the wire, and began to sing.

Not well. That was essential. He sang a drinking song from home, the obscene one about the general's wife and the goat, in Russian loud enough to wake the garrison and slurred enough to suggest he'd been at the bottle since noon. He wove. He tripped over a coil of rope. He fell against the wire and set it jangling like a badly tuned piano.

"Hey!" A lantern swung toward him. "You! Stop there!"

"I stop," Kolya announced with tremendous dignity, "for nobody. I sing for everybody." He sang another verse. The goat acquired additional grievances.

Three soldiers came, which was more than planned and also perfect, because three soldiers escorting one drunk was three soldiers not watching the drain grate. They hauled him up by the arms, and he made himself heavy the way drunks are, boneless and complaining, and one of them recognized him — the deserter who swept the church steps, everyone knew Kolya, Kolya who fixed things and told jokes and had once pulled the commandant's motorcar out of the mud with borrowed horses — and that recognition bought him minutes, because men are gentle with the misfortunes of men they know.

"The drunk monk," the corporal said, disgustedly fond. "Anselm's Russian."

"He is my brother in Christ," Kolya agreed solemnly. "Christ has many brothers. All of them drink."

They were laughing. One of them was laughing. Kolya catalogued the laughter like a jeweler cataloguing stones, and let them march him around to the front door, away from the north wall, away from the grate, his heart doing its work and his mouth doing its work and both of them lying beautifully.

Behind him, in the dark, in the smell of river mud and rust, a small figure slipped through a hole in the world.

* * *

It went wrong at the yard gate, because everything goes wrong, because that is rule number one and Kolya had written it himself.

He heard it first as silence — the wrong kind, the kind with a shape — and then a voice, sharp, not one of his three singers: "Halt! Halt, or I fire!"

And then, God help him, he heard Tamsin run.

She should have frozen. Freezing was the doctrine. But she was Tamsin, and Tamsin's whole life had taught her that stillness gets you found, and she broke for the shadow of the woodpile with the ledger hugged to her and her boots throwing gravel, and the soldier raised his rifle with the unhurried competence of a man who had done this to running figures before, and Kolya did not think.

That was the honest account, and he would give it honestly later, over and over: he did not think. He tore free of his escorts — who were holding him loosely, who were still half-laughing — and threw himself across the yard shouting something wordless and enormous, and the rifle came around to him, and he kept coming anyway, arms wide, a big slow target in the lantern light, because a body moving between a shooter and a runner buys seconds, and seconds were the entire currency of the night.

The shot took him high in the left side, below the collarbone, and it felt less like a bullet than like a door slamming shut inside his chest. He went down into the mud, which received him with the indifference of mud everywhere, and through the roaring in his ears he heard — distinctly, absurdly — the sound of the yard gate's latch lifting, and small quick feet on the north road, and fading away.

Gone. She was gone. She was through.

"Idiot," said the corporal above him, kneeling, pressing hard on the wound with hands that shook only slightly. "You drunken idiot. What did you think you were—"

"I thought," Kolya said, and had to stop, and taste blood and mud and the iron tang of his own ridiculous courage, "I thought I heard the goat."

Somebody else laughed, farther off, the young one. Then the corporal shouted for the medic, and the lanterns swung and multiplied, and Kolya lay in the customs yard looking up at a sky scoured clean of stars by cloud, and thought about a samovar with a dent in its side, and about Marta's knuckles thumping his chest, *you live so the paper means something,* and about whether living counted if you did it lying in the dirt while a girl smaller than your rifle carried everything that mattered up a dark road alone.

He decided it counted. He decided it had better count, because he was fairly sure he wasn't going to be doing much else for a while.

* * *

They dumped him — there was no gentler word — in the guardroom of the customs house to wait for the medic, who was attending a sergeant with dysentery, priorities being what they were. The corporal gave him water. The young one, the laugher, sat nearby and pretended to fill out forms and after ten minutes said, without looking up:

"The girl. In the yard."

Kolya said nothing, which was its own answer, and the young soldier nodded slowly, as if a sum had come out even.

"They say the delegation leaves tomorrow at noon," he said, very quietly, to the form. "If they don't sign. My brother's name isn't on anything. Nobody wrote it anywhere. He just — stopped existing." He filled in a blank. "I didn't see anybody in the yard. That's what the form will say."

Kolya lay with his hand pressed to his own leaking chest and understood, dimly, through the pain and the cold, that the family had grown again without anyone voting on it.

Outside, far up the north road, a clock tower stood empty against the clouds, and somewhere between here and there a girl was walking through the dark with every erased name in the world pressed flat against her heart — and only one night left to make them mean something.
