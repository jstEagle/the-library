## 8. The Armistice That Isn't

The news arrived the way bad news always does in a river town: by boat, breathless, and slightly ahead of itself.

A boy came poling a flat-bottomed skiff up the millrace channel just past dawn, shouting before his boat had even touched the bank. Tamsin heard him from the reed bed where she and Kolya had spent a miserable, shivering night, and for one absurd moment she thought he was crying *fish, fresh fish*, which was how half the town's catastrophes were first announced.

Then she caught the words. *"They're leaving! The delegation — packing up — no signature — they say there's nothing to sign for!"*

Kolya, propped against a willow with his bandaged shoulder grey as old ash, began to laugh. It was not a good laugh. It was the laugh of a man whose body had finally decided fear was more efficient than humor.

"Well," he said. "That's that, then. We could have slept in a bed."

Tamsin did not answer. She was watching the hill across the water, where the observatory's dome caught the early light like the eye of something waking. All night she had lain in the cold mud with the ledger strapped against her ribs under her coat, feeling its corners press into her with every heartbeat, and had told herself: *tomorrow*. Tomorrow she would find a way through the perimeter. Tomorrow the delegates would sit at their long table with their pens uncapped, and a girl nobody had ever bothered to notice would walk in and make them listen.

Tomorrow, it turned out, had other plans.

* * *

By mid-morning they had limped as far as the tannery lane, to the house of Widow Pesch, who took in washing, deserter soldiers, and — since yesterday — one wounded Russian and one clockmaker's apprentice with a book of dead names hidden about her person. The widow asked no questions. She had lived through occupation, requisition, and two floods; questions were a luxury she'd traded away years ago along with her good china.

She fed them barley soup and stood over Kolya with her hands on her hips while he tried to insist he was fine.

"You are the color of my kitchen floor," she informed him, "and my kitchen floor has been the color of death since 1911. Drink."

Tamsin sat by the window with the ledger open on her knees — she could not stop touching it, checking it, as if the names might evaporate — and listened to the town talk itself into surrender.

It came in through the window with the smells of soap and river: the delegation had received new orders from its capital. The terms were unacceptable. The army across the river was refusing every clause. Why sign a paper with people who tore up papers? Better to withdraw, let the winter do the negotiating, come back in spring when everyone was hungrier. The delegates' carriages were being brought around to the front of the Grand Hotel at noon tomorrow. Noon. One day.

"Void," said Kolya quietly, when the widow went out to the pump. "If they go without signing, everything reverts. Every order stands. The archive stays sealed. And your book—" he nodded at the ledger "—becomes a story. A rumor. *Some girl says there was a list.* Nobody hangs a general over a rumor."

"They read names at funerals," Tamsin said. "Nobody hangs anyone over those either. But we still read them."

"That is because at funerals nobody is trying to leave before you finish."

She almost smiled at that. Almost. Then she looked down at the page she'd been avoiding all morning — the O's, the middle stretch of the alphabet where the clerk's hand grew cramped and hurried, as if even his pen had wanted to be done with it — and found *Oyelaran, Tomas, age 17, deported east, no return recorded.*

No return recorded. As if her brother were a library book.

"We have until noon tomorrow," she said.

"And a checkpoint between us and them that turned away three priests this morning." Kolya shifted, winced, kept talking — he always kept talking, as though silence were a wound he refused to dress. "Tamsin. Listen to me as a soldier, which I was briefly and badly. You cannot get through. The perimeter is doubled. They are expecting trouble, which means they are shooting at shadows, which means—"

"Which means shadows are exactly what I am."

"Tamsin—"

"I repaired Sergeant Brandt's watch," she said. It came out flatter than she intended, so she said it again with the shape of a plan behind it. "I repaired his watch. His dead son's watch. He talked to me for an hour because I was useful and invisible, and he told me things he shouldn't have — about the timetable, about which officers signed what. He let me walk out past his own sentries because I was carrying his watch and his grief in the same pocket." She closed the ledger. "Usefulness got me through the war. It can get me through one more gate."

Kolya looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled past loaded with furniture — someone fleeing, or someone looting, and in this town the difference had grown politely vague.

"You're going to do it whether I help you or not," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Then God help me, I'm coming too. I can barely stand, but I can lean against things convincingly. It's the one military skill they never trained out of me."

* * *

They spent the afternoon doing what Tamsin did best: taking apart a mechanism to see why it failed.

The perimeter was a mechanism. That was the thought that steadied her, sitting cross-legged on the widow's floor with salt and dried beans arranged on the boards as sentry posts, the soup pot as the Grand Hotel. A perimeter had gates and rotations and habits, and habits were just springs wearing grooves into themselves. Every clock she'd ever fixed had seemed impossible until she found the groove.

The bridge checkpoint was hopeless — Marta's arrest had seen to that; they were searching everyone, turning out bags, making women unpin their hair. The customs house had burned its easy route with Kolya's blood two nights ago. But the delegation's perimeter itself — that was staffed not by the occupying regiment but by the delegation's own honor guard. Different army. Different habits. And honor guards, Kolya explained with the weary authority of a man who had once been one, cared about exactly one thing above all others.

"Not being embarrassed," Tamsin guessed.

"Being *seen* to be correct. Which is better for us. An occupying soldier shoots first and files afterward. An honor guard hesitates, because hesitation looks like procedure." He moved a bean. "There's a delivery gate on the north side. Kitchen supplies. Carts go in twice daily — morning and evening meal. The guard checks cargo, not faces, because cargo is what embarrasses delegations. Poisoned soup makes newspapers. Extra laundresses do not."

"So I go in as cargo."

"You go in as something better. You go in as someone's errand." He fished in his coat — the widow had washed it, poor woman, and it had nearly died of the experience — and produced a crumpled paper: an invoice, genuine, for wine delivered to the delegation's mess, which Kolya had lifted off the ferryman who'd been paid to lose it. "The evening delivery is at six. The cart comes from Vasseur's yard. I know the driver — Ferenc — a coward and a thief, which are the two most reliable virtues a man can have. He'll take coin to hide you under the sacking, and he'll take more coin to forget he did."

"And if they search anyway?"

"Then you are a laundress delivering mended tablecloths, and the invoice is your cousin's, and you are too stupid to be interesting. Can you look stupid?"

"I once convinced an entire garrison I couldn't tell time."

"There's hope for you yet."

But then he stopped moving the beans, and the humor drained out of him the way color drains from a face, and Tamsin understood that they had arrived at the part of the plan neither of them wanted to say aloud.

"If it goes wrong," Kolya said, "if they take you — that book cannot be taken with you. You understand? It doesn't matter what happens to you. It matters what happens to the names. You burn it first. Promise me. Ashes can't testify, but they can't be confiscated either."

Tamsin looked at the ledger. Two nights ago it had been a weight. Last night, in the reeds, it had been a heartbeat. Now it felt like what it had been all along: a hand, reaching out of a locked drawer, asking to be held the last few miles.

"No," she said.

Kolya's jaw tightened. "Tamsin—"

"They didn't burn," she said. "Every name in here — they didn't burn, they didn't drown, they didn't get the mercy of ash. They were *filed*. Neatly. In someone's best handwriting. And then the drawer was locked and everyone agreed the room was empty." She pressed her palm flat against the cover. "If it comes to it, I will tear out pages and eat them before I burn them, because burning is what they do, and I am done doing what they do. That is the whole point of tomorrow."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the street, the boy with the skiff was still shouting the news to anyone with a door, his voice cracking with the effort, and the town absorbed it the way the river absorbs rain — without changing course.

Finally Kolya laughed again, and this time it was a real laugh, small and cracked and warm.

"You know what Marta would say?"

"She'd say I'm too stubborn to drown."

"She'd say worse, but yes." He leaned his head back against the wall, exhausted, and his eyes were bright. "All right. Six o'clock. Ferenc's cart. We put you in the wine."

* * *

Night came down on the town like a lid. From the widow's window Tamsin watched the observatory hill, where lights moved in the tower — soldiers, probably, cataloguing what they meant to seize, measuring the master clock for its crate as if it were furniture and not the only thing in this town that had ever told the truth on schedule.

She cleaned and oiled her tools, though there was nothing left to repair, because her hands needed work the way her heart needed the ticking. Then she took out the ledger and read it, properly, beginning to end, for the first time. Not hunting for Tomas. Not scanning for familiar syllables. Reading. Four hundred and eleven names. She made herself see each line — the ages, the dates, the little bureaucratic annotations like *labor detail* and *transferred* and *no further record* — and she let them be heavy, all of them, not just hers.

Near midnight, Kolya woke feverish and called for his mother in a language the widow wouldn't have understood, and Tamsin held his good hand and gave him water and lied to him gently in two languages until he slept.

And then, alone in the dark with the ledger on her knees, Tamsin Oyelaran stopped being afraid.

Not bravely — she noted that honestly, because she was a clockmaker and precision mattered. It wasn't courage that replaced the fear. It was something colder and cleaner, the way a mechanism goes quiet just before it strikes. Tomorrow at noon the carriages would come, and the pens would be capped, and the world would file another drawer and lock it. Unless a girl walked in through the one door nobody guarded because nobody believed a girl would dare.

Let them believe it one more day.

At six o'clock, she would be under the wine.
