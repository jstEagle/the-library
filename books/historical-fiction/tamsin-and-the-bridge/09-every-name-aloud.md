## 9. Every Name Aloud

The clock tower had always been honest with her.

That was the thought Tamsin clung to as she stood in the shadow of the observatory's outer wall at half past four in the morning, the ledger bound against her ribs with Kolya's scarf and her own fear. The tower did not lie. It creaked when the wind came from the west, it groaned on cold nights when the metal contracted, and its great pendulum — or rather, the silence where a pendulum ought to be — told her everything she needed to know about what waited inside.

They had taken the works out. She could hear it in the way the building breathed. A clock with its heart removed made a different sound than a stopped one; a stopped clock still held its breath, waiting to be wound. This was emptier than that. The army had gutted her master clock for parts, or for spite, or because an order had come down that all instruments of official time were to be seized along with all records of official truth — and somewhere in some clerk's mind, the two had been filed together.

Good, she thought, and then hated herself for thinking it, and then thought it again anyway. Let them take the clock. She had not come back for the clock.

"You're sure about the window?" Kolya whispered beside her. He should not have been there. He was supposed to be lying flat in Marta's borrowed cart under a funeral blanket, being driven very slowly toward safety by a nun who had threatened to pray for him if he argued. But Kolya had climbed out of the cart two streets back, on the grounds — he said — that a man who could not run could still stand in a doorway looking harmless, and harmless men were useful.

"I'm sure," she whispered back. "Third-floor east face. The maintenance hatch. I used to go through it every month for six years."

"Through a window."

"Through a *hatch*. There's a difference. A window you fall from. A hatch you climb through, like a person with dignity."

Kolya laughed, quietly, the way he'd learned to laugh during the occupation — with his whole chest but none of the sound. "Go, then. And Tamsin." His hand found her shoulder in the dark. "When they read your name in the record tomorrow, make them say it right. Oyelaran. All the syllables. Make them practice."

She squeezed his hand once and went up the wall the way she had gone up a hundred times before the war, when the observatory had been hers in the only way a poor apprentice can own anything: by knowing it better than anyone else alive. There were iron rungs behind the drainpipe that no one else had ever noticed, set there by a mason sixty years dead for a scaffolding long since removed. Her fingers remembered each one. Her boots remembered the ledge. The hatch — swollen now, rusted at the hinge — gave way after three hard pulls with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then she was inside, in the smell of dust and old oil and brass polish, home.

* * *

The tower stairs were pitch dark, but darkness was her native language. She counted steps without needing light: forty-one to the movement room, sixty-three to the dial chamber. She kept the ledger pressed tight against her and moved like water finding its old channel.

At the thirty-eighth step she heard voices below and froze.

Two soldiers, in the entrance hall, complaining about coffee. One of them said something about the delegation arriving at nine, and the other said something about hoping they signed quickly because his feet hurt, and Tamsin stood on the stair with her heart going like a flywheel and understood that the entire fate of every name in the ledger currently rested on two men's sore feet and bad coffee.

Then a third voice joined them — low, tired, familiar — and told the first two to get out of the hall and post themselves at the perimeter like they'd been ordered, and Tamsin's heart did something more complicated than racing.

Sergeant Brandt.

He had let her into the garrison office twice. He had traded her his son's broken watch for small true things about the town, and she had traded him information about the armistice timetable disguised as watchmaker's talk — mainsprings and escapements, deadlines and tolerances — until she knew exactly how many hours remained before the delegation signed or walked away. And on the last night, when she had asked him, carefully, whether a man could be in two places at once if one of the places was his conscience — he had looked at her for a long moment and said nothing at all, which from Brandt was practically a signature.

She waited until his footsteps crossed the hall and climbed past her, up toward the signing chamber on the second floor, where the delegates were already gathering hours early because delegations, like clocks, ran ahead of their appointments when everyone was afraid of missing them.

Tamsin followed him up.

* * *

The signing chamber had been the observatory's map room once, back when the town's astronomers had spread their star charts across these tables. Now the charts were gone and in their place lay documents, seals, inkwells, and seven men — five delegates, two secretaries — arranged around the table with the careful formality of people performing peace rather than feeling it.

Brandt stood by the door. He saw her the instant she stepped out of the stairwell, and his hand did not move toward his rifle, and that was the whole conversation they needed to have.

One of the secretaries noticed her next. "You can't be in here—"

"My apologies," Tamsin said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "But I've brought a document that belongs on that table."

Every head turned. She was aware of herself suddenly, horribly — the grease-stained apron, the hair escaping its pins, the ledger wrapped in a dead deserter's scarf, a girl of nineteen standing in a room full of important men. For six years she had survived by being invisible, by being useful, by being furniture that happened to fix watches. Every instinct she owned screamed at her to apologize, to shrink, to become small.

She unwrapped the ledger instead.

"This is a conscription record," she said. "Kept by the occupying army's own clerks, in their own hand, in their own ink. Every name they took. Every man conscripted, every woman deported for labor, every person struck from the town rolls so that officially they never existed at all." She laid it on the table between the inkwell and the seals, and her hands were shaking now, but she put them flat on the cover and let them shake where everyone could see. "There are four hundred and eleven names in this book. My brother is one of them. Tomas Oyelaran. Taken in March of 1916, age seventeen. Officially, according to the records you are about to sign, he was never here."

A delegate — a heavy man with a diplomat's face, smooth and sorrowful and ready for anything except this — began, "Young woman, the annexation schedule does not include—"

"The annexation schedule," Tamsin said, "does not include my brother either. That is rather my point."

Someone at the far end almost smiled. She didn't see who. She opened the ledger to the first page and began to read.

* * *

She read the way she wound a clock: steadily, without haste, because haste was for machines that didn't trust themselves.

"Adeyemi, Solomon. Labor battalion, 1915. Aldana, Róska. Deported, 1917. Amrhein, Josef. Conscripted, died at Verdun, 1916. Anselm—" and here her voice caught, because Brother Anselm had been standing in her doorway three nights ago telling her that names read aloud were prayers with their shoes on, "—Anselm, Piet. Struck from rolls, 1918. Presumed deported. Actually hiding in a convent, but the book doesn't know that, does it? The book doesn't know anyone came home. The book only knows who was taken."

"Miss—" one of the secretaries tried.

"Oyelaran," she said. "Tamsin Oyelaran. Apprentice clockmaker. You may as well learn it; I intend to keep saying mine until you do."

The heavy delegate sighed the sigh of a man whose morning has developed complications. "If we admit this record into the annexes, the other party will demand reciprocal disclosures. The schedule will slip. The signing may not occur today at all."

"Then it won't occur," Tamsin said, "and you'll walk away, and the war will end on paper everywhere except here, where it will go on living in a book in a drawer, and in four hundred and eleven families, and in me. Or you can put the names in the record, and it will cost you an hour and some embarrassment, and those people will have existed. Which seems like a small price for peace. I repair clocks, gentlemen. I know what things cost to fix and what things cost to leave broken. Leaving this broken costs more."

Silence. The kind of silence a room falls into when someone has said the true thing and everyone is checking whether the true thing is allowed.

It was the youngest delegate — a thin woman with ink on her fingers whom Tamsin had noticed earlier only as a blur of gray — who broke it. "May I see the book?"

Tamsin turned it toward her. The woman read the first page, then the last, then the middle, with the speed of someone who had spent years learning exactly which lies lived in which part of a document. Then she looked up at the heavy man and said, "It goes in the annexes. Under protest if necessary. Mine, specifically, and loudly."

The heavy man closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when he is adding up a bill. "Very well. Read them into the record. All of them. Quickly."

"No," Tamsin said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Not quickly. Slowly. Each name gets its whole self — rank, date, fate, everything the clerks wrote down, because the clerks wrote it all down, they were thorough, thoroughness was the one thing they believed in. Four hundred and eleven names will take"—she calculated, the way she calculated everything, automatically, helplessly—"approximately ninety minutes at reading pace. You scheduled the signing for nine o'clock. It is now ten minutes past. You have time."

"She's right," the thin delegate said, already pulling a fresh pen across the table. "We have time. Begin."

And Tamsin Oyelaran — invisible girl, useful girl, furniture that fixed watches — stood at the head of the table of the armistice delegation of 1919 and began, aloud, at the top of page one:

"Abara, Daniel. Labor battalion, 1915. Returned, wounded. Currently residing, bridge district, toll house, burned. Employed, presently, at nothing much."

A secretary wrote it down. The pen scratched. Somewhere below, the gutted clock tower held its empty breath, and Tamsin read on.

* * *

They came for the master clock at noon.

By then the names were in the record — all of them, entered in two hands, the thin delegate insisting on countersigning each page — and by then the soldiers had been instructed, in the passive and deniable grammar armies use, that certain instruments of the former administration were to be seized for archival processing. Four of them carried the movement cases out of the tower while Tamsin watched from the observatory steps, arms crossed, saying nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the book hadn't said better.

Sergeant Brandt came out last. He paused beside her, and for a moment neither spoke.

"They'll melt it down eventually," he said. "Or warehouse it. Things like that happen to clocks."

"I know."

"It was a fine clock."

"It was," Tamsin agreed. "It kept wrong time at the end, though. Badly wrong. Somebody ought to build one that keeps honest time instead."

Brandt looked at her then, really looked, the way he had looked at her over his son's watch — measuring, deciding — and whatever he decided, he put it away somewhere deep and said only: "Your brother's name. They pronounced it correctly. I checked."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Don't thank me. I requisitioned a town once." He settled his cap and walked down the hill after his soldiers and their stolen clockwork, and did not look back, which was its own kind of gift.

Tamsin stayed on the steps a long while. Below her the river ran brown and busy, carrying spring melt and old secrets down toward the bridge, where a burned toll house stood black against the water and a nun was presumably still threatening Kolya with prayer. The town lay open under the pale afternoon — requisitioned, recorded, half-starved, entirely alive.

Four hundred and eleven names existed now. In ink. In triplicate.

She flexed her fingers, stiff from ninety minutes of holding a book steady, and found that what they wanted, more than anything, was work. There was an empty tower behind her and a town full of people who would need to know, once a year at least, exactly what time it had been.

Tamsin went inside to measure the case for a new clock.
