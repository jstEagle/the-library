## 10. Tamsin and the Bridge

Marta had kept the bridge for forty-one years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about waiting: that it went faster with tea, slower without gossip, and not at all if you counted the minutes. So when they released her from the military detention house on a grey Tuesday morning — no apology, just a sergeant's grunt and her own coat thrown over the counter like so much confiscated laundry — she did not count anything. She walked down to the river, put her hand on the toll gate as if greeting an old horse, and said, "Right. What did you all get up to while I was gone?"

The bridge said nothing, being a bridge. But the town answered soon enough.

She heard it first from the fishwives, who had a talent for compressing tragedy and comedy into the same breath. The delegation had signed. The names had been read into the record — all of them, every one, read aloud by a girl with ink-stained fingers who had stood in front of generals and ministers and had not stopped until the last name was spoken, even when her voice cracked on it, even when someone told her she could stop now, *thank you, mademoiselle, that will be quite sufficient.* She had not stopped.

And the army, denied its records, had done the next pettiest thing available: it had seized the master clock. Carted the great case down from the observatory hill on a gun carriage, wheels groaning, while half the town stood along the road and watched in silence that was somehow louder than any protest. Marta heard how Tamsin had run alongside the carriage for the length of the bridge, one hand trailing on the rail, until Kolya — arm still in its sling — had come out of the toll house and simply stood beside her, and between the two of them they made a kind of gate the soldiers decided not to argue with.

"Let them have it," Tamsin had reportedly said, watching the clock go. "It never kept good time anyway."

Marta laughed until she cried when she heard that, which surprised her, because she had thought she'd cried herself dry weeks ago in a cell with a straw mattress and a window too high to see the river.

* * *

Kolya was mending. That was the official word, delivered by Brother Anselm with the serene authority of a man who considered mending his personal department. In practice this meant Kolya sat in the sun outside the toll house with his shoulder bandaged, complaining at a volume and variety that confirmed he would live.

"The soup," he announced on Marta's first evening home, "is a war crime."

"It's barley," said Anselm mildly, from behind his mending basket.

"It is *punishment* in liquid form. I took a bullet for this town. This is how the town repays me."

"You took a bullet because you stepped in front of a girl running through a doorway you could easily have entered yourself," Marta said, hanging up her coat.

"I stepped in front of her *tactically*."

"Tactically." She kissed the top of his head the way she'd once kissed her sons, back when the world had more of them in it. "You're thinner. Eat your punishment."

He ate it. He always ate it. Underneath the complaining he was watching her with those careful deserter's eyes, checking her the way she was checking him — counting ribs, counting shadows under eyes, counting the small tremor in his left hand that hadn't been there before the customs house. They were all doing arithmetic these days. Sums of what remained.

It was Anselm who finally said what none of them would say directly. He set down a sock he was darning and folded his hands.

"She doesn't sleep," he said. "Our Tamsin. She's up at the observatory before dawn and down after dark. There are lights up there all night."

"She's building something," Kolya said.

"She's *avoiding* something," said Marta. "There's a difference. Though with that girl the two are usually the same animal wearing different coats."

* * *

Marta climbed the hill herself on Thursday, ostensibly to bring bread, actually because forty-one years of keeping a bridge taught you that things left unwatched go wrong in ways nobody reports until the collapse.

The observatory looked hollowed. Where the master clock had stood there was now an empty alcove in the tower wall, a pale rectangle of unfaded plaster shaped exactly like everything they'd taken — the case, the works, the weights, the long pendulum that had governed every bell and factory whistle and train departure in the valley for a hundred years. The army had even taken the dust rags, apparently out of spite.

In the middle of the empty room, on a workbench built of two trestles and a door, Tamsin was surrounded by chaos of a very particular sort: brass rods, coils of wire, a wooden pattern half-carved, sheets of paper dense with calculations, and a kettle. There was always a kettle. Marta suspected the girl could conduct a funeral around a kettle.

"Bread," Marta said, setting it down among the drawings.

Tamsin didn't look up. Her pencil moved. "Thank you. If I stop drawing this gear train I'll lose the ratio again and then I'll have to start hating myself all over by morning."

"What is it?"

Now she looked up, and Marta saw the sleeplessness plain on her face, but also something underneath it that hadn't been there since before the requisition — a kind of lit stubbornness, banked coals rather than fever.

"A clock," Tamsin said. Then, hearing herself, she laughed shortly. "Obviously. A *clock*, Marta, as though the world needs another one."

"The world needs yours, maybe. What does it do?"

Tamsin set down the pencil. She picked up a sheet covered in columns of figures and turned it toward the light, and Marta saw that the topmost column wasn't numbers at all. It was names. Dozens of them, in Tamsin's small precise hand, each followed by a figure — a count of seconds.

"Three hundred and eleven names in the ledger," Tamsin said quietly. "Read aloud, they took four hours. The delegates were patient, God bless their tired souls, but four hours is not something a town can carry every day. So." She tapped the paper. "One strike per name. One chime. Once a year, on the anniversary of the reading. Three hundred and eleven strokes. It takes fifty-two minutes. People can stand in the square and listen, or not listen, or curse the noise and shut their windows. But it happens. The names happen. Every year, whether anyone signs anything or not."

Marta looked at the drawings — the escapement designed to fire a hammer sequence rather than ring hours, the great drum that would have to hold three hundred and eleven pins set at exact intervals, the weight system, the governor. It was madness. It was months of work. It was the most sensible thing she had ever seen.

"They'll want to know why the new clock rings wrong," Marta said.

"It won't ring wrong. It'll ring *true*. There's a difference." Tamsin's chin came up, and for a moment Marta saw the girl who had faced down a signing table full of men with the power to erase her twice over. "A clock that only counts hours forgot my brother. This one won't."

Marta sat down on an upturned crate, because her knees had opinions about standing. "Your brother," she said carefully. "Emile."

Something crossed Tamsin's face — grief, yes, but also a strange settling, like a debt acknowledged after years of pretending it was paid.

"When I was little," Tamsin said, "he used to hold my hand on the bridge and tell me the river keeps everything. Everything that falls in, everything that's thrown in, everything people say on it at night. He said the water remembers so we don't have to." She smiled, and it wobbled. "He was wrong, of course. Water forgets beautifully. It's the whole trick of water. We're the ones who have to remember. That's the job nobody wants and somebody has to do."

"And you've decided it's you."

"Somebody has to keep the bridge," Tamsin said, and looked straight at her, and Marta understood she was not talking only about clocks.

They sat together a while, the old woman and the young one, sharing bread and the sound of pencils and the wind off the hill. Before Marta left, Tamsin caught her sleeve.

"There's room in the workshop," she said. "For hands. Mine are steady enough for fine work, mostly, but the frame alone weighs — well. And Kolya's shoulder will heal better if he has something to complain about besides soup. And Brother Anselm claims he can carve wood, which I intend to test cruelly." She hesitated, and the hesitation cost her; Marta watched it cost her. "If you want. Nobody has to. I know the toll house is yours, and the bridge, and—"

"Tamsin." Marta put her hand flat on the drawings, over the names. "Forty-one years I've kept a bridge. You know what I've learned? A bridge isn't the stone part. Any fool can build the stone part. The bridge is whoever walks across it going toward each other." She stood, joints crackling in chorus. "I'll be here at dawn. Don't make the tea weak."

* * *

Winter came early that year, as winters do when a town is rebuilding and cannot spare the time to notice the seasons arriving properly.

But the observatory stayed warm. Word traveled, the way word does, and word said that the girl who read the names was building something up the hill, and people came — at first to look, then to carry, then to stay. The cooper sent oak for the case. Widow Ferencz, whose husband's name was pin number forty-one, arrived one morning with her late husband's tools and asked in a voice like cracking ice where she should start, and started before anyone answered. Two of the nuns from the convent came up twice a week to sand wood and sing hymns slightly off-key, which Kolya declared worse than the shelling, and which everyone else found he hummed along to when he thought no one was listening.

Even Sergeant Brandt came once — alone, out of uniform, hat in his enormous hands. He stood at the door a long moment, looking at the empty alcove where the old clock had hung, and then at Tamsin.

"I brought something," he said. His voice sounded rusted. From inside his coat he drew a watch — the dead boy's watch, the one she had repaired in another life, in another country of the world. "It runs fast. Thirty seconds a week. I can't seem to fix it. Perhaps—" He couldn't finish. Perhaps he meant perhaps you could fix it, or perhaps he meant perhaps you could bear the thing existing somewhere other than his pocket. Perhaps both.

Tamsin took it gently. "Bring it back in spring," she said. "We'll regulate it together. It's a two-person job, regulating. One watches, one listens." She met his eyes. "You watch, Sergeant. I'll listen."

He nodded like a man accepting a sentence, and it was a kindness, and everyone in the workshop pretended not to see him wipe his face on the way out.

* * *

On the last night of November, Marta locked the toll house, banked the stove, and climbed the hill with a lantern, because it was Thursday, and Thursdays were hers.

The workshop smelled of shavings and solder and strong tea. Through the open door she could see them: Anselm at the carving bench, tongue between his teeth, shaping pin number two hundred and six — Sister Odile's brother, though nobody but the sisters knew it yet; Kolya one-armed and magnificent, polishing the great drum and narrating his heroism at the customs house to Widow Ferencz, who was letting him have it entirely; and Tamsin at the center, sleeves rolled, hair escaping its braid, fitting the first of three hundred and eleven pins into the drum with fingers that did not shake at all.

On the wall above her hung the empty picture frame they'd salvaged from the requisitioned archive office, and inside it, instead of a painting, the first page of the ledger — copied out fair, the original itself resting in the national record now, safe in marble and law — so that the first thing anyone saw, coming up the hill, was the names.

Below the window, the town glowed. Below that, invisible in the dark, the river ran on, forgetting everything beautifully.

Marta stood in the doorway with her lantern and let the scene burn itself into whatever place she kept such things. Family, she thought. She'd had one once, made the usual way, and lost it the usual terrible way, and had assumed the account closed. But accounts, she was learning, reopened. You kept the bridge, and the bridge kept bringing people across, going toward each other, and some of them stayed.

Tamsin looked up and saw her, and held up the pin between finger and thumb — tiny, brass, numbered — like a candle flame.

"Number twelve," she said. "Pierre Aubry. He drove a milk cart. His sister still lives by the mill."

"Set it well," Marta said.

"I will. I'm setting them all well." Tamsin fitted it home with a small decisive click, and smiled — tired, lit, certain. "Every single one. And then, Marta—" her eyes went to the empty alcove, to the space waiting for the case taking shape in oak and brass, to the year turning toward them with its anniversary and its fifty-two minutes of bells — "then we teach this town to hear its own heart again."

Outside, far down the valley, a church bell rang the hour — eight o'clock, ordinary, human, wrong in all the ways clocks are wrong and right in all the ways that matter. And up the hill, in a room full of chosen family and unfinished work, the new clock waited in pieces, patient as a promise, for the day it would speak three hundred and eleven names into the cold bright air, and be answered every year, forever, by the sound of a town listening.
