## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

There was an art to being nobody, and Tamsin Sebastian had perfected it over eleven months and four hundred miles.

The trick, she had learned, was not to hide but to be useful in a dull way. Nobody looked twice at the courier who mended his own boots badly, who complained about the weather with the enthusiasm of a man who had nothing else to say, who ate whatever was put in front of him without remarking that it was terrible or wonderful. Sebastian Winterbourne was forgettable the way furniture was forgettable — present, serviceable, beneath notice.

So she sat now on a hard bench aboard the *Persephone*, a diplomatic packet riding low in the water as it beat its way south toward Veymar, and she darned a sock.

It was not even her sock. It belonged to Lieutenant Hargreaves, who had torn it climbing the rigging in a manner he clearly considered dashing and everyone else considered alarming, and who had pressed a coin into her hand with the gravity of a man commissioning a portrait. She took his coin because taking coins was what couriers did, and she darned his sock because a courier with busy hands asked no questions and invited none.

"You're good at that," said Mr. Quill, the under-secretary, settling beside her with a sigh that suggested the sea itself was a personal affront. He was a soft, damp-looking man whose collar never quite fit, whose voice was pitched permanently at apology. "My sister sews like that. Well. Worse. Much worse, actually. She once hemmed a tablecloth to my trousers."

"Sounds like a talented household," Tamsin said, in Sebastian's flat baritone.

"Oh, devastatingly so." He laughed at his own joke, a small hiccuping sound, then glanced toward the cabin where the dispatch case lived under triple lock and the permanent supervision of Colonel Ashe, who watched everything the way a cat watches a mouse hole — patiently, and with the certainty that something would eventually come out of it.

Tamsin kept her eyes on the sock.

She had carried dispatches through three provinces and two changes of government, and she knew the taxonomy of sealed things by heart. There were letters that were heavy with nothing — ceremony dressed in wax. There were letters that rattled faintly when shaken (she did not shake them; she simply noted that they might). And there were letters whose weight had nothing to do with paper, which arrived already humming with consequence, which made the men who entrusted them stand slightly too straight.

The letter given to her at the last port had been the third kind.

Colonel Ashe himself had handed it over, which was unusual — normally it was a secretary's task, done quickly, eyes elsewhere. But Ashe had held out the packet with both hands, formally, the way one hands over a sword or a corpse.

"Winterbourne. You'll carry this personally. Not in the case. On you."

"Yes, sir."

"It goes to Commissioner Lavelle at Veymar. His own hand only. If you cannot reach Lavelle, it burns. Do you understand? It does not fall into any other pocket in that city, however important the pocket believes itself to be."

"Understood, sir."

And Ashe had looked at her a moment too long — long enough that she'd felt the familiar cold prickle at the back of her neck, the fear that eleven months of careful dullness had somehow failed — before nodding once and turning away.

Now the letter rode against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into the lining of her coat where the left pocket ought to have been. She was aware of it the way one is aware of a bruise. Constantly. Especially when forgetting it.

* * *

The trouble with being invisible, Tamsin reflected that evening over the ship's supper — salt beef, biscuit, and a cheese that tasted of the hold — was that invisibility was a service other people used. They handed you their secrets precisely because you were nobody. You became a walking vault for matters you were forbidden to understand, and the better you were at your work, the more of other people's lives passed through your hands without your leave.

"Three days to Veymar," Hargreaves announced, tearing bread with more optimism than the bread deserved. "I hear the harbor's still open."

"Barely," said Quill. "Half the fleet's gone east. The other half is arguing about whether to go east."

"And the city?"

"Holding." Quill said this the way one says *the soup is technically edible*. "Holding is the word we've all agreed upon. Nobody has yet defined what it means."

Tamsin chewed and listened. This was another discipline: eat slowly, look bored, collect everything. In eleven months she had assembled a picture of the republic's collapse from dinner-table fragments alone, the way a beggar assembles a coat from cast-off rags. The armies were closer than the dispatches admitted. The ministers lied in ascending order of rank. The evacuation lists — ah, the evacuation lists — those were the true currency now, traded in whispers, worth more than gold, and every official with a cousin had suddenly discovered a great deal of cousinly feeling.

"The Commissioner will want your letter the moment you land," Quill remarked, apropos of nothing, watching her sidelong.

"He can have it the moment I land," Tamsin said. "I deliver. I don't read. That's the arrangement."

"Very professional." Quill smiled his damp smile. "You know, most couriers say that. Very few mean it. I once knew a fellow who steamed open a letter with a kettle and found instructions about grain shipments. Nearly died of boredom."

"And did you hang him?"

"We reassigned him to grain shipments." Quill chuckled, then stopped chuckling rather abruptly, as though remembering something. "This one isn't about grain."

He said it lightly, and rose lightly, and went off to be seasick in private, and Tamsin sat very still with her supper cooling in front of her while the *Persephone* groaned and rolled and the lamp swung its little shadow across the bulkhead, back and forth, back and forth, like a metronome counting down to something.

* * *

She did not intend to open it. Let that be recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the universe kept of such intentions: she did not intend to open it.

But on the second night, unable to sleep, she took the letter from its hiding place merely to check that the wax seal was whole — a courier's habit, defensible, routine — and held it near the lamp, and turned it over.

And there, in the corner of the outer wrapping, small and neat, was a mark she recognized.

A compass rose, drawn in a single unbroken line, its north point finished with a tiny crossbar. Her grandmother had drawn it on every letter she ever sent, on flour sacks and fence posts and the inside covers of books, a family flourish going back generations. A thing so ordinary within the family that Tamsin hadn't thought of it in years. A thing no stranger could possibly know.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake.

She turned the letter again. The address was correct: Commissioner Lavelle, by the hand of the bearer. The seal was intact. The mark was in the corner like a watermark, like a signature, like a brand.

Somebody in the ministry knew. Somebody had taken a letter bound for Veymar and marked it — or addressed it deliberately — with the sign of the Sebastians. Which meant either that the letter concerned her family, or that it came *from* someone in her family, or that someone wished it to appear so. Three possibilities, each worse than the last, arranged neatly like steps descending into a cellar.

Tamsin set the letter down on her bunk and looked at it for a long time.

Duty said: deliver it unread. Duty was simple, and simple things were the luxury of people whose names weren't written in other people's letters. Eleven months ago she had buried her old name under a new one precisely so that duty could remain simple — so that she could be Sebastian Winterbourne, dull as furniture, safe as furniture.

The lamp swung. The shadow crossed the letter and crossed it again.

In the morning they would make landfall at Veymar, and the harbor guns would count her in, and Commissioner Lavelle would put out his hand for the sealed thing she carried. Whatever it named — whoever it condemned — it would pass out of her keeping and become real, and irreversible, and hers forever anyway, because she had carried it.

Tamsin picked up her needle. She finished Hargreaves' sock. Her stitches, she noticed, had gone perfectly, terribly even.

Then she took up her knife, and slit the wax.
