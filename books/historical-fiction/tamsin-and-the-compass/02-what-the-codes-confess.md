## 2. What the Codes Confess

Tamsin did not sleep that night. She had learned long ago that sleep was a currency she could not afford to spend carelessly, and tonight the exchange rate was ruinous.

The packet lay on the narrow table of her lodging above the chandler's shop, lit by one candle she had trimmed to a miserly flame. Around her, the house settled into its noises: the chandler below snoring like a man apologizing for it, a cat arguing with something in the walls, the distant groan of ships at their moorings in the besieged harbor. The city breathed around her, and under its breath it whispered *evacuation* the way a family whispers *funeral* — quietly, and only when necessary.

She turned the letter over again. It was the fourth dispatch in her satchel, the one addressed to Commissioner Vasseur at the naval office, sealed in plain grey wax without ornament or crest. Unremarkable. She had carried forty letters like it and never once broken one open, because breaking one open was the first step on a road that ended at a wall and a firing squad, and Tamsin Sebastian — or Winterbourne, as the world knew her — had every intention of dying old, toothless, and thoroughly unremarkable.

But the letter bore her family's name inside it. She had seen it through the paper.

Not *seen*, exactly. Held to the candle, the grey wax thinned to translucence, and there in the folded page beneath, in the commissioner's own cramped hand, a phrase had surfaced like a body rising in a harbor: *the Sebastian correspondence*. Her name. Her father's name, dead these six years and still dangerous. The true name she had buried along with him when she cut her hair, bound her chest, and became the invisible young man every official handed secrets to without a second glance.

Invisibility had felt, until this evening, like her own clever invention.

It was not. Someone had designed it. And designs have purposes.

* * *

The cipher was an old one. That was the first insult.

Tamsin had learned it at her father's knee, back when his knee was attached to a man who traded in shipping insurance by day and in other people's secrets by night, and who thought teaching his daughters ciphers was a form of affection. *A lock is only honest if you know how it opens*, he used to say, tapping her nose with the pencil. *Everything else is just decoration.*

The letter's inner text was written in a numeric substitution he had called "the grocer's trick" because it looked like nothing so much as an inventory list. Forty-one barrels. Sixteen crates of tallow. She worked through it with the pencil stub and a scrap of waste paper she would burn before dawn, and the translation came slowly, then all at once, the way ice goes out of a river.

*The Sebastian correspondence confirms alignment. The elder brother has been cooperative since March. Berth allocations may proceed as arranged — three berths held against the final sailing, names to follow under separate seal. Burn upon reading.*

She read it four times.

Her brother. Aldous. Who had written to her exactly once since she vanished into her new life, a letter so bland it might have been a recipe, and which she now understood had been a signal — *I am well, I am watched, say nothing.* Cooperative since March. It was nearly November. Eight months of cooperation, which was a word men like Commissioner Vasseur used the way surgeons used the word *procedure*: technically accurate, morally hollow.

And berths. Three berths held against the final sailing. There were perhaps four hundred souls left in this quarter of the city who needed passage, and room for sixty, and here was a letter explaining, in the language of groceries, that the room had been sold. Traded among officials and their favorites while the harbor filled with people telling each other the ships would come, the ships always came.

Every choice costs something. Her father had said that too. He'd been talking about buying a horse at the time, but he had been right about larger things more often than anyone liked.

* * *

Her sister found her at dawn.

This required no explanation; Céleste had a genius for appearing precisely when Tamsin least wanted witnesses, a talent she'd possessed since childhood, when she'd once materialized behind the garden wall at the exact moment Tamsin was burying a dead sparrow with full military honors and a eulogy.

"You look terrible," Céleste announced, slipping through the door in a wash of cold air and cheap violet scent. "Like a man who hasn't slept. Which is good. Very convincing."

"I haven't slept."

"Even better." She dropped onto the stool across the table, all elbows and sharp dark eyes, twenty-two years old and built entirely out of nerve. Then she saw the papers. The humor went out of her face like a tide going out. "You opened it."

"I opened it."

"Tamsin." Just the name, flat and horrified. Céleste was the only person alive who used it.

"Aldous is compromised. Has been since spring. And the berths—" Tamsin pushed the translation toward her. "Three berths, held against the final sailing. They're being traded, Céleste. Sold, probably. While the dock crowds stand out there telling each other lies."

Céleste read. Tamsin watched her sister's face do what their father's face had done: go very still, very bright, working. When she finished, she set the page down and slid it deliberately back across the table, away from herself, as though it were hot.

"Say nothing," she said.

"They're selling places on the last ship."

"And you're a courier. Couriers carry. They don't open, they don't copy, they don't *judge*." Céleste leaned forward. "Do you know what happens to a courier who reads mail? Not the wall. Worse. The wall is quick. They'll want to know who else you told, and they'll keep asking until you tell them something, anything, and then they'll use it. You'll give them me without meaning to. You'll give them Aldous twice over."

"He's already given."

"He's *cooperative*," Céleste snapped, and then caught herself, and Tamsin saw it land — the word, what it meant, what it cost their brother every day to be it. Her sister pressed her lips together. When she spoke again her voice was lower. "He wrote to me in June. One line. *Keep her small.* That's all. Keep her small. He meant you, Tam. He meant keep you invisible and uninteresting and alive. If you start burning down berth-trading commissioners two weeks before the evacuation, you will be the most interesting woman in this city, and interesting women don't get berths. They get questions."

Tamsin looked at her sister — really looked, at the chapped hands and the mended hem and the ferocity worn like armor over terror — and understood that Céleste was not asking her to be a coward. Céleste was asking her to stay alive. It was a different request wearing the same coat.

"I'm not going to burn anything down," Tamsin said quietly.

"Thank God."

"But I'm not going to pretend I didn't read it either."

"Tamsin—"

She was already moving, feeding the translation scrap to the candle, watching the grocer's arithmetic curl and blacken and become ash. What remained was her memory, and memory could not be seized from a satchel. She copied nothing onto paper — no, that was a lie she told Céleste with her face, even as her hands, later, alone, did otherwise. Because a courier who burned everything kept nothing, and Tamsin Sebastian had spent six years learning that the difference between survival and surrender was often a single copied line, held close, held quiet, held *just in case*.

She copied the codes. In her own private shorthand, disguised as a dressmaker's order book — lengths of ribbon, numbers of buttons, three berths, sixteen crates, forty-one barrels. Insurance, their father would have called it. Treason, Vasseur would call it, if he ever saw it. Love, probably, was the truest name, and love had never once kept anyone out of trouble.

* * *

Two days later, the summons came.

It arrived the way such things did — a boy with a note, a note with a seal, a seal she recognized: her handler, Monsieur Ferrand, requesting that *Winterbourne* attend him at the customs house before the evening tide. Routine. A final delivery assignment, surely. The last dispatches before the last ship.

Routine, except that Ferrand's notes never came by boy. They came by ciphered slip, by dead drop, by channels so careful they were practically invisible.

Visible was a choice. Visible meant someone wanted her seen walking there. Wanted witnesses to where Winterbourne went, and when, and whether he ever came out.

Tamsin stood at the window with the note in her hand and watched the crowd already gathering along the harbor road — hundreds of them, bundles on their backs, eyes fixed on the water, waiting for the ships that three berths at a time were being sold out from under them.

Céleste appeared beside her, following her gaze, and said nothing at all, which from Céleste was the loudest thing she could say.

Tamsin put on her coat, tucked the dressmaker's order book against her ribs, and went to meet the man who had made her invisible — and who, she was beginning to understand, had known exactly what he was hiding her from, and why, since the very first mile.
