## 3. The Last Berth

The harbor at dawn looked like a wound that had learned to dress itself. Tar and smoke and the copper smell of blood from the fish market, all of it wrapped in fog so thick the masts seemed to grow out of nothing. Tamsin stood at the foot of the quay with her dispatch case strapped tight against her ribs, watching the *Persephone* take on her final passengers, and tried to breathe like a man.

It was harder than it used to be. Three weeks of pretending had worn the disguise thin as cheap paper, and this morning — with agents in grey coats drifting along the waterfront like gulls deciding where to land — she felt every seam of it. The binding across her chest. The boots two sizes too large, stuffed with wool. The name *Sebastian Winterbourne*, which she had worn so long it answered when called, like a dog.

One berth left. She had known it since midnight, when the purser's list had passed through her hands along with the mail sacks — couriers saw everything, that was the whole point of them — and she had read it twice to be sure. One berth aboard the *Persephone*. The last diplomatic evacuation out of a city that was already burning at its edges.

And three people who needed it: herself, her brother Luc, and her sister Odile.

Luc was compromised — had been since the autumn, though he didn't know she knew. His name sat in the intercepted correspondence like a fly in amber. If Luc boarded that ship, the ship became a target for arrest the moment it made any port with an extradition habit. Which meant, though it cut like broken glass to think it, the choice was not between three.

The choice was between two.

She heard him before she saw him — she always did, the soft drag of his left heel, the one he'd never have mended because mending implied you planned to keep walking. Mr. Quillon, her handler, emerged from the fog at the harbor stairs with his hat at its usual angle of ironic apology.

"Winterbourne." He said the name the way a man reads a menu he wrote himself. "You look well, considering."

"Considering what?" Tamsin kept her voice low and level, pitched down where Sebastian lived.

"Considering everything." He glanced out at the water, at the ship, at the grey coats on the quay, cataloguing it all without appearing to look at any of it. "You have the letter?"

"I have the letter." Her hand went to the case. "I'm to deliver it to Captain Voss before sailing."

"Mm." Quillon smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "You know, I've been meaning to tell you something for about three hundred miles now. Since the first mile, really. Since the coach out of Valcourt."

Tamsin's heart performed a maneuver no courier should permit it. "Sir?"

"You hold your reins wrong," he said. "A man who learned to ride as a boy holds them like he's angry at the horse. You hold them like you're apologizing to it. Your sister taught you, I'd wager. Older sister. Patient. Terrible rider herself, probably."

The fog seemed to close in, warm suddenly, close as a held breath. "Mr. Quillon—"

"Tamsin," he said gently. "Do stop. You've done it beautifully — better than most men manage their own names — but there are men on this quay in grey coats who were paid to find you specifically, and they found you Tuesday. I've been spending my mornings misdirecting them, which is exhausting work and has ruined my digestion. So let us not spend your last hour pretending."

"Bait," she said. The word came out flat, small, like a coin dropped on stone. "That's what I was. From the first mile. They weren't chasing the letter. They were chasing me, and you let them think they'd lost me so they'd follow me straight to—"

"To whoever cared enough to come for you." Quillon shrugged, unapologetic and faintly apologetic at once, which was a trick only Quillon could do. "Your family is either innocent or it isn't, Winterbourne. The Republic needed to know which. And the only way to make treason move is to threaten something it loves. You were the threat. I'm sorry. I'd offer to buy you dinner by way of apology but I suspect we're both short on evenings."

Somewhere down the quay a bell rang twice — twenty minutes to tide. Tamsin's hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside, everything was shouting; outside, Sebastian's hands stayed quiet, and she was grateful to him, her invented brother, for lending her his calm.

"The letter," she said. "You've read it."

"I've read it."

"It names them. All of them. My mother's name, spelled right, which almost nobody manages. Luc's dealings. Odile's—" She stopped. "If that letter reaches the Commission, they're finished. If it burns, the plot proceeds and other people die. I've turned it over every way there is to turn it and there is no side of it that isn't someone's ruin."

"Yes," said Quillon. "That's generally how letters are."

"And the berth."

"One berth." He didn't soften it. She respected him for that, in the way one respects weather. "Yours, by rights. Courier's privilege. You carry the codes; the codes go where the Republic goes; the Republic is going aboard the *Persephone*."

* * *

Later — years later, when she told this part of the story, and she would tell it many times, usually with wine — she would try to explain the strange clarity of those next minutes. There was no thunderclap. No grand internal struggle scored by violins. There was just the bell, and the fog, and the faces in her mind: Luc, laughing too loudly at things that weren't funny because he was frightened; Odile, patient, terrible-ridered Odile, who had taught her to hold the reins like an apology and who could read a cipher faster than any man in the foreign office and didn't know it.

Odile didn't know it. That was the thing. Odile thought she was the plain one, the spare one, the sister who stayed home and copied documents. But the codes — Tamsin had copied them, yes, as insurance, night after night by candle stub — and Odile was the one who could *use* them. Who had been using them for years, quietly, without credit, the way useful women do everything.

"Then I'll waste no more of your evening," Tamsin heard herself say, "because I intend to commit several crimes in the next ten minutes and I'd like witnesses who owe me favors."

Quillon raised his eyebrows. "Go on."

"The berth goes to Odile Sebastian." She said the true name aloud for the first time in three weeks, and it tasted like cold water. "Not Luc — if he sails, they'll take the ship, you know it as well as I do. Not me. I'm the bait, remember? Bait stays on the hook. But Odie carries the codes, and the codes are worth more than the letter ever was, and if you have one scrap of the sentiment you pretend not to have, you'll see she gets aboard safe and gets paid the courtesy of being underestimated all the way to freedom."

"And the letter?"

Tamsin drew it from the case. Heavy paper, official seal, her family's future folded into eight panels. She had imagined burning it a dozen times and each time her hand had stopped, because duty is a heavy thing and it sits on the wrist.

But the bell rang again, and down the quay one of the grey coats had stopped walking, and was looking at her, and then at Quillon, and then back.

"Dinner first," Tamsin said. "You still owe me that."

She walked to the brazier at the harbor master's door — the one the old men warmed their hands over, the one that smelled of fish and tar and, this morning, of endings. She held the letter over the coals until the seal blistered, then let it go. It caught slowly, the way important things do, reluctant, and she watched her mother's name curl brown and then black and then into nothing at all.

"There," she said softly. "Now it's only ash, and ash doesn't testify."

Quillon stood beside her, hands behind his back, watching the fire with an expression she couldn't read and decided not to try. "They'll still want someone," he said quietly. "The grey coats. A story needs a body in it, and you've just burned theirs."

"I know." Tamsin straightened Sebastian's collar one last time, out of habit, out of gratitude. Down the quay, the *Persephone* was running up her flag, and a girl in a borrowed coat — Odile, hood up, clutching the satchel of codes to her chest like a child — was being handed up over the rail by strangers.

The grey coat was walking toward her now, unhurried, certain.

"Well," Tamsin said, and found — to her genuine astonishment — that she was smiling. "Let's go meet the neighbors."
