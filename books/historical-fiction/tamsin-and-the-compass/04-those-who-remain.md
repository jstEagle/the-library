## 4. Those Who Remain

The *Persephone* weighed anchor at dawn, and Tamsin watched her sister become a smaller and smaller figure at the rail until the sea haze took even that.

She had expected to weep. She had prepared a whole interior speech about it, the way one prepares a defense before a tribunal: *I did not abandon you, I chose you, there is a difference, however thin.* But what she felt instead was something closer to the stillness after a fever breaks — the strange, clean emptiness where dread used to live.

"You're still here, then," said a voice behind her.

Marta, the oyster-wife from the third pier, arms crossed against the morning chill, a basket of smoke-dark shells on her hip. She had watched Tamsin pace the dock for three days and had apparently drawn her own conclusions.

"For now," Tamsin said.

"Mm." Marta set down the basket and studied her with the frank appraisal of a woman who has gutted ten thousand oysters and is not easily shocked by what she finds inside anything. "The soldiers come at eight. Old habit of theirs. You'll want to eat something first. Fainting makes them smug."

Tamsin blinked. "You know why they're coming."

"I know everyone's business on this dock, love. It's my only talent besides the oysters." She pressed a heel of bread into Tamsin's hands. "Eat. And stand up straight when they take you. It annoys them terribly."

* * *

They came at half past eight, which told her everything about the state of the republic's administration — even its arrests had grown lazy. There were two of them, young, one with ink-stained cuffs and the other with the haunted look of a man who had been given quotas instead of convictions. The ink-cuffed one read out her name in full, all of it, the name she had buried under six years of Winterbourne.

"Tamsin Sebastian," he said, and waited for her to flinch.

She didn't. She had spent the whole night not flinching; she was rather good at it now.

"That's me," she said. "Though I've answered to worse."

* * *

The room they put her in smelled of lamp oil and old paper, which she found almost homey after the salt of the harbor. A clerk with spectacles too large for his face sat across from her with the letter — or what remained of it. Her burning had been thorough but not total; a corner survived, enough to show the seal, enough to prove it had been official correspondence destroyed by an unauthorized hand.

"Explain this," the clerk said, without much hope in his voice. He seemed a man who asked questions the way other men sighed.

"A letter came into my keeping. It accused my family of treason. I burned it."

"Why?"

"Because it was lies dressed as truth, and fire doesn't much care about the difference."

The clerk made a note. Then, unexpectedly, he looked up over his spectacles. "The codes your sister carried. Do you know where they went?"

"No," said Tamsin, and for the first time in days the lie came easy, because it was the kind of lie you tell for someone else, and those sit lighter on the tongue.

He wrote again. Tamsin watched him, and understood, with the particular clarity of a woman with nothing left to lose, that this man was not her enemy. He was simply another person left holding a job while the world it belonged to dissolved around him. Half the officials in the city were already gone. The ships kept leaving. Somebody had to stamp the papers of those who stayed.

"Where will they send me?" she asked.

He considered. "There isn't anywhere to send anyone anymore. The prison on Rue Vernet flooded last month. You'll be held locally. Under watch." He hesitated, then added, as if confessing to a small kindness he'd hidden in his pocket: "Under light watch. There aren't enough of us for heavy watch."

* * *

Light watch, it turned out, meant lodging above the harbormaster's office with a door that locked from neither side, guarded chiefly by Sergeant Pell, a round, wheezing man who spent most of each day arguing with Marta about the price of oysters and losing.

"They think I'm dangerous," Tamsin remarked on her second evening, watching Pell lose another round.

"He thinks you're company," Marta corrected. "We all do. It's very inconvenient of you, being interesting."

And that was the strangest part — how quickly the left-behind folded her in. The dock had its own nation, she discovered, its own laws and liturgies. The net-menders who paid their debts in mended nets. The chandler's widow who fed anyone who'd listen to her stories about her late husband, all of them contradictory. Old Ferro, who had been a smuggler before the war made smuggling patriotic, then a patriot after it made patriotism profitable, and was now simply an old man with excellent opinions about rope.

None of them asked what she'd done. They asked what she could do, which turned out to be a great deal: she could cipher, so she untangled the harbormaster's ledgers, which had not balanced since the previous spring. She could write a fair hand, so she drafted letters for sailors' wives to absent husbands. She could keep a secret, so people gave her more of them than she wanted, handed over like small warm birds.

"Your sister," Marta said one night, sharing a bottle that claimed, improbably, to be Bordeaux. "She'll make it?"

"She'll make it." Tamsin turned her glass in the lamplight. "I put everything she needed in the lining of her coat. Even if they search her, they'll find nothing but a frightened girl with good posture."

"Frightened girls with good posture have toppled better governments than this one."

* * *

A month passed. Then two. No orders came for her — the machinery that might have produced them had rusted or fled. The war moved elsewhere, the way weather does. On clear mornings you could see the evacuation fleet's old anchorage, empty now, gulls standing sentry on the mooring buoys as though they'd been promoted.

It was Ferro who finally named what had happened to her, one evening at the chandler's widow's table, over a stew that was mostly turnips and entirely delicious.

"You thought belonging was a thing you escaped to," he said, jabbing his spoon at her like a fencing master making a point. "Over the horizon, on a ship, in some other life. But look around. Belonging's a thing you stay for. Nobody ever belonged anywhere by leaving it."

"I stayed because I surrendered," Tamsin pointed out.

"You surrendered," said Marta comfortably, "because you're honest. It comes to nearly the same thing, and the stew's better here."

The table laughed. Tamsin laughed too, and realized somewhere in the middle of it that her eyes were wet, and that no one minded, and that this — this crooked table, these borrowed candles, this company of people the great world had forgotten — was the thing she had been posing as for six years without once possessing.

Sebastian Winterbourne had been invisible everywhere he went. Tamsin Sebastian, disgraced traitor of the third pier, could not cross the dock without being greeted by name.

* * *

In the spring, a letter arrived.

Pell brought it up himself, breathing hard from the stairs, wearing the expression of a man delivering both mail and theater. "Foreign post," he announced. "Very foreign. Smells of oranges."

The handwriting was her sister's. Tamsin carried it unopened to her window, to the light, and let herself feel every beat of her own heart before breaking the seal.

Inside, one page, and beneath the page — slipped loose into her palm — a single strip of paper bearing a line of cipher. Her own cipher. The insurance copy she had made and mailed ahead, the codes that could save whatever remained of everything.

Her sister's letter was short.

*They listened. It worked. You were right about the good posture. Come find us when they let you — and Tamsin, they will let you, because I have spent the last four months becoming exactly the sort of person who can arrange such things. Until then: the harbor says hello.*

Tamsin read it twice. Then she folded the codes away — some habits are for keeping — and leaned out the window toward the water, where Marta was shouting prices at the morning boats and Ferro was wrong about rope and the whole loud, stubborn, left-behind nation of the dock was going about its day.

"Good news?" called Marta, without looking up.

"The best," Tamsin said, and meant it in both directions at once. "But not yet. Ask me again tomorrow."
