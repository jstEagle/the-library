## 1. The Weight of Small Hands

The bell at Meridian Trading rang at half past six, as it had every working morning since before Tamsin Pemberton could remember, and by a quarter to seven she was already at her stool in the counting house, sleeves rolled to the elbow, mending the morning's torn paper.

Not real nets—those belonged to the fishermen who came up from the wharf at dusk with their hands bleeding and their eyes on nothing. Paper nets. That was what Supervisor Crane called the ledger work when he was feeling generous: *we mend our nets same as anyone, girl, only ours catch money.* Tamsin had learned, at nine years old, to nod at such sayings as though they were wisdom rather than weather. Eleven years of nodding had made her very good at it.

Her station was the third desk along the east wall, wedged between Fenwick the junior clerk—who was twenty and already going soft around the middle from sitting—and a window that looked out over Greywater Harbor. She had chosen that seat herself, or rather she had arranged to be assigned it, by being so reliably punctual and so unremarkably competent for two years running that no one thought twice about where they put her. The window mattered. From it she could see the tide gauge, the fish market awnings going up, and the long gray line of the breakwater where the divers launched their boats before dawn. Her father had gone out past that breakwater every morning of his working life.

She did not look at it often. Looking cost something, and she could not afford to spend anything she didn't have to.

"Morning, Tam." Fenwick dropped into his chair with a groan that was mostly performance. "You beat the bell again. You'll outlive us all."

"That's the plan," she said, and he laughed, because Fenwick laughed at everything, and because there was nothing threatening in a small girl with ink-stained fingers saying something dry about outliving people. Invisibility was not silence. Silence drew the eye after a while, like a hole in a conversation. Invisibility was being *predictable*—dry, useful, slightly amusing, never clever enough to be interesting, never dull enough to be dismissed.

She took up the day's first casualty: an invoice from the chandlery, water-spotted where a crate had sweated through its wrapping, three lines blurred to gray ghosts. The clerk who'd written it had pressed too hard and then not hard enough, all in the space of four words, which told her he'd been cold, then warm, then thinking about his supper. She could read a hand the way Maren Ostrow read clouds off her boarding house porch—not the words only, but the man behind them, his hurry or fear or laziness, all of it baked into the slant of a letter.

That was not a skill anyone at Meridian paid her for. On paper—in the great bound book Crane kept locked in his office—Tamsin Pemberton, indentured, was entered under *domestic and errand labor*. She fetched, carried, swept, ran messages between the counting house and the warehouse, and occasionally, when a clerk was ill or drunk, she was permitted to sit at this desk and *copy*, copying being different from reading, and reading being different from writing, and writing being a thing indentured girls were not taught because teaching made a person valuable and value made them expensive.

What Crane did not know—what no one at Meridian knew—was that Tamsin had been reading since she was seven years old, crouched behind her father's knee while he sounded out the dive contracts he was signing away pieces of himself to, Wendell Pemberton tracing each word with a blunt finger and murmuring it aloud because that was how he held a thing in his mind. *Consideration. Indemnity. Salvage rights accruing to the Company until satisfaction of debt.* She had learned to read the way children learn to swim in Greywater: thrown in early, by necessity, and taught by someone who loved her enough not to pretend the water was safe.

Writing she had taught herself after he drowned, alone, at night, with a stub of pencil and the backs of delivery manifests destined for the stove. Her letters were neat and unremarkable on purpose. A distinctive hand was a signature; a plain one was a mask.

She copied the blurred invoice, matching the dead clerk's slant, filling the ghost-words with their most probable originals. *Twelve coils hemp rope. Four barrels pitch. One —* She paused. The blur sat where a quantity should be. She let her eye travel back through the page, found the chandlery's own tally column, and saw that eleven coils were charged but twelve delivered. Someone had shaved a coil somewhere between the dock and the book, and someone else would pay for the difference, and it would not be either of the someones with offices.

She corrected nothing. Correcting things got you noticed. Instead she copied it faithfully as written—eleven coils—and folded the discrepancy away in the back room of her head, where she kept everything.

* * *

By eight o'clock the counting house filled with its ordinary orchestra: the scratch of nibs, the cough of the stove eating coal, Crane's boots on the floorboards overhead in a rhythm she knew as well as her own pulse. Heavy heel, light toe—he favored his left knee, an old injury he never spoke of. When the rhythm broke, something was wrong. When it stopped altogether above the stairwell, he was coming down, and everyone became busier than they had been a moment before.

"Tamsin." He appeared beside her desk without appearing to walk there, the way big quiet men do. He smelled of pipe smoke and the camphor he rubbed into his knee. "The Harbormaster's office wants last quarter's salvage tallies run down to them before noon. Take the copies. Leave the originals."

"Yes, Mr. Crane."

"And—" He lingered. His eyes went over the desk, the invoices, her hands. Crane's inspections were always gentle, which was what made them inspections. "Fenwick says you've been correcting the chandlery figures."

Tamsin felt the floor tilt a half-degree beneath her stool, and kept her face exactly where it was: pleasant, blank, a little slow. "Correcting, sir? I only copy what's wrote."

"He says you pointed out the rope."

"I asked if it should be eleven or twelve," she said, "on account of not wanting to put down wrong numbers and have you think I can't count." A smile, shy, self-deprecating—the smile of a girl whose highest ambition is to avoid embarrassment. "I can count, Mr. Crane. Reading's harder."

Something moved behind his eyes—satisfaction, maybe, or the small pleasure of a trap confirming itself empty. "Counting's enough for you, then. Keep to the copies. The reading's for clerks."

"Yes, sir."

He moved on. She bent to her work and let her heartbeat come back down to its mooring, one knot at a time. *Reading's harder.* It was the best lie she owned, worn smooth as sea glass from use. Every month she fed Crane a little more evidence of her own limitation, the way a smuggler pays a small bribe to keep the inspectors trusting. Fenwick, though. Fenwick had noticed the rope. She would need to be more careful about what she let Fenwick see her notice.

Underneath the relief, though, in the place where pride lived—which was a small, stubborn, well-hidden place, much like herself—something glowed. She *had* caught the rope. Nobody else in the room had. Eleven coils charged, twelve delivered, and the difference worth perhaps four shillings, which was nothing to Meridian and a week's bread to whoever lost it. The world was full of holes like that, and she could see them, and seeing them was hers, private, untaxable. They could own her labor until she was twenty-one. They could not own what she knew.

* * *

At noon she carried the salvage tallies down to the Harbormaster's office, which meant forty minutes of the harbor itself, and the harbor was the one thing Greywater gave away free.

October had come in hard that year. Gulls wheeled screaming over the fish stalls; men tarred the pilings against winter; the ferry from the pearl grounds wallowed in past the breakwater, riding low, and Tamsin counted the divers on deck without meaning to—a habit, like counting stairs. Nine. There should have been eleven. Two boats' worth of crews stood on the quay pretending not to watch the ferry come in, the way families did, the way she had once done, arranging their faces into patience over the top of dread. When the gangway dropped and both missing men walked down it, whole, sunburned, complaining loudly about the price of rum ashore, the quay exhaled all at once, a sound like wind through rigging.

Tamsin had stood on that quay as a child. She remembered the other sound, the one nobody talked about: the sound when the gangway dropped and somebody didn't walk down it. A silence with edges. Her mother had heard it twice before the fever took her, and Tamsin had heard it once, aged nine, and then been taken straight from the quay to the counting house to sign her father's debts into her own small name, because grief was inconvenient to the schedule and the Company kept its books current.

She delivered the tallies, received a grunt, and stopped on the way back at Ostrow's boarding house, ostensibly because it was on her route and she had a message for Mrs. Ostrow from a warehouse foreman. Really because Maren Ostrow fed people who looked hungry, and she had a gift for looking hungry without ever asking.

Maren was on the porch shelling peas into a bowl that had seen three generations, a broad woman with forearms like hawser rope and a face that gave away nothing except when she wanted it to. Half the harbor's widows ended up at Maren's table eventually, and half the harbor's secrets came with them.

"You've got ink on your chin," Maren said, by way of good morning, and pushed a bowl of stew at her without being asked. Tamsin ate on the second step, where the light was, while gulls argued over the gutting tables across the lane.

"The ferry came in short," Maren said, not looking up from her peas. "Nine men."

"It came in full. Both stragglers were aboard."

"Mm." Shell, thump, shell. "When I was your age we watched for the boats the same way. My Tomas dove eleven seasons." She said it evenly, the way you'd state a depth or a price. "Eleven seasons, and then the twelfth, the Company changed his gear contract and the new suits leaked at the collar seam. Cheap leather. He knew it. Dived anyway, because refusing is debt." Thump. "The collar seam killed him forty feet down, and Meridian docked his final pay for the suit."

Tamsin set down the spoon. This was not a story Maren told strangers. This was a story Maren told *her*, deliberately, the way you pass a lamp down a dark passage to see whether the next person will carry it or drop it.

"My father's suit leaked too," Tamsin said quietly. "The year he died."

"I know whose daughter you are." Maren looked at her then, direct, brown eyes with the harbor in them. "Half this street knows. We don't say, because saying does no good and Crane has ears." She went back to the peas. "But you remember it. That's worth something, a person who remembers."

Tamsin walked back to the counting house with the message delivered and the stew warm in her belly and Maren's words lodged under her ribs like a splinter working toward the surface. *Worth something, a person who remembers.*

* * *

The afternoon passed in copies. She ran manifests to the warehouse, swept the front step, sharpened four pens that weren't hers to sharpen, and at half past three Crane called her up to his office to fetch the post, which was the part of the day she hated most and would have died before admitting it.

The mail tray sat on the corner of his desk, and she was required to stand there, sorting outgoing from incoming, while Crane read his correspondence and occasionally dictated a note for Fenwick. Sorting meant seeing addresses. Seeing addresses was permitted; reading was not. So she read nothing, and memorized everything, the way she had for years: names, return marks, the weight and shape of envelopes, the companies whose seals appeared too often, the sums mentioned aloud in Crane's muttering. It went into the back room of her head with the rope coil and the collar seams and eleven years of small wrongnesses, stacked and ordered, waiting for a purpose she hadn't yet invented.

Today's tray held the usual: bills from the chandlery, a letter with the Voss & Sons seal—that would be for the centenary history the Company was commissioning, everyone spoke of it with the reverence people reserve for monuments—an insurance notice, and one letter, creased soft from a long journey, salt-stained at one corner, addressed in a hand that—

Tamsin's hands did not shake. Eleven years of training. But for one full second the letter in her grip might as well have been a live coal.

She knew that handwriting.

She knew it better than her own, because she had learned her letters tracing it: the strong downstrokes, the *d* with its flat-topped loop, the way the crossbar of the *t* slashed leftward like a man in a hurry who still refused to make an ugly letter. Wendell Pemberton's hand. Her father's hand, which she had last seen on a contract dated the spring before his last dive, and which she had mourned along with the rest of him for eight years, privately, in pencil stubs and manifest margins.

The envelope was addressed to the Company. The return mark, in the same impossible script, named a port town two hundred miles north that she had never heard of.

"Tam." Crane's voice, mild. Too mild. "You're staring at that letter."

"It's damaged, sir." Her voice came out level. Somewhere far away she admired it. "Salt stain. I was checking it ain't opened in transit."

"Give it here."

She gave it here. She watched Crane turn it over, frown at the return mark, weigh it in his palm as though the paper itself might confess. And then he set it in the incoming stack, unopened, second from the top, and said, "That'll be all," and Tamsin curtsied the little curtsy she saved for supervisors and walked out of the office on legs that belonged to someone else.

* * *

She finished the day. That was the discipline: whatever happened, the work got finished, because a clerk who faltered was a clerk who got looked at. She copied, she filed, she swept, she banked the stove, and when the five o'clock bell released the counting house into the evening she walked home through the harbor crowd with her face arranged into its ordinary tiredness while inside her everything screamed.

A dead man's handwriting, alive and traveling, addressing the company that owned her.

In her loft room above the cooperage—two shillings a week, one window, a view of chimney pots and a slice of gray water—she lit her candle, checked the door latch twice, and sat down at her table with the stub of pencil she kept hidden in a cracked teacup. On the back of an old delivery manifest, in her plainest, most forgettable hand, she wrote the port town from the return mark. Then the date stamp from the mail bag. Then, because memory demanded it and grief insisted, she wrote the letters of her father's name, W-E-N-D-E-L-L, and looked at them a long time.

He had been dead for eight years. She had stood on the quay. She had heard the silence with edges.

And yet.

Outside, the harbor settled into its night noises—halyards ticking against masts, the fog bell starting up at the point, someone in the lane below laughing at something that would not matter tomorrow. Tamsin blew out the candle and sat in the dark with her father's handwriting burning behind her eyes, and understood that the careful, invisible, survivable life she had built, letter by letter, was about to be tested by a single piece of paper.

Two days later, she would learn, a stranger would carry that paper through Meridian's front door and ask her father's name out loud.

But tonight she only sat in the dark, holding her breath the way divers hold theirs, waiting for the surface.
