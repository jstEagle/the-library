## 2. Letters for a Drowned Man

The counting house smelled of ink and cold tea, as it always did on the second Thursday of the month, when Supervisor Crane unlocked the correspondence drawer and let the clerks sort the Company's mail. Tamsin Pemberton had been sorting other people's words for nine years. She knew the weight of an envelope by its business: thin and stiff for bills of lading, soft-cornered for private letters, heavy with wax for anything from the Meridian head office in Corvane. She could tell a widow's letter from a creditor's by the way the paper was folded—widows folded small, as if to make their grief easier to carry.

She was seventeen now, and her hands had learned the work so thoroughly that they seemed to do it without her. Her fingers moved down the day's stack, flicking each envelope into its tray, while her eyes—the eyes nobody watched, because watching Tamsin was like watching the tide come in, too slow and too constant to be interesting—her eyes read.

That was the trick of it. Nine years of mending nets made of paper. Every invoice that passed under her hands taught her another column of figures. Every shipping manifest showed her which captains lied about tonnage. She had learned the Company's handwriting the way other girls learned hymns: Crane's tight little fist, all angles; old Hollis in Receiving, whose sevens looked like broken oars; the head office in Corvane, which wrote in violet ink as though purple could make cruelty respectable. She kept it all in her head, because paper could be burned or locked away, but memory was hers alone. Nobody had ever thought to indenture a girl's mind. They'd bought her hands at nine years old and never noticed the rest of her came along.

"Pemberton." Crane's voice from the high desk, not looking up. "The dive accounts go back before noon."

"Yes, sir."

They always went back before noon, because she was fast and because speed was the safest thing she owned. A slow clerk got watched. A fast one got forgotten. She had spent her whole indenture being useful in exactly the measure that kept her invisible—competent enough to keep, unremarkable enough to ignore. It was a narrow channel to steer through, but she had been steering it since she was small enough to sleep in the loft above the drying sheds, listening to the harbor breathe below her.

She carried the dive accounts to his desk. The ledger lay open where he'd left it, and her glance fell across the columns the way rain falls on a roof—touching everything, keeping nothing, or seeming to. *Dive season 1889. Wendell P., salvage credited, debt adjusted.* Her father's name, abbreviated to initials and a letter, the way the Company shrank men down to fit its arithmetic. She had seen that entry a thousand times. She saw it every month of her life, because her indenture was written against his debt, and every pearl he'd ever brought up from the grey water still owed them something.

She set the accounts on his desk and stepped back into her shadow between the filing cabinets, and that was when the bell over the counting house door rang.

* * *

Nobody came to the Meridian Trading Company on a second Thursday. The harbor folk paid their accounts at the pawnshop on Rope Street because the pawnbroker at least pretended not to enjoy taking what you loved. The counting house was where people were summoned, never where they arrived. So when the door opened, everyone looked up—Crane, Hollis's boy, the two junior clerks—and Tamsin looked up last, from habit, because being last to look was part of her camouflage.

The man in the doorway filled it the way weather fills a window. He was big without being fat, broad through the chest and shoulders in the way of men who had spent years hauling lines, with forearms roped and browned and a face that had taken salt wind long enough to wear grooves in it. His coat was oilskin gone soft with age, patched at the elbows with canvas, and his boots left wet prints on the counting house floor—harbor prints, working prints, wrong for this room of ledgers and lamplight. He stood a moment just inside the door, blinking against the lamp-yellow after the white morning outside, and Tamsin watched him take in the room the way she imagined a fish took a hook: slowly, then all at once.

"I'm looking after someone," he said. His voice was low and roughened, like a hull scraping a quay. "Wendell Pemberton. Diver. Would've dived out of Greywater maybe—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, as if the name were something breakable he was carrying. "Nine years back. Maybe ten."

The room did the thing rooms do when a dead man's name walks in wearing boots: nothing, loudly. Hollis's boy kept copying. The junior clerks exchanged the flat glance of men who had heard a hundred names and cared about none of them.

Crane looked up from his desk with the expression he wore for beggars and bill collectors, which was the same expression, which was the same he wore for most of the harbor. "Pemberton," he said, tasting it. "There was a diver of that name. Died. Some years ago. The Company holds no further interest in him." He said it the way he said everything, like a receipt being torn off. "If you're here about his estate, there is none. If you're here about his debts—" a thin smile, "—those died with him. More or less."

"More or less," the stranger repeated. Something moved behind his eyes, quick and dark, gone before it finished arriving. "I'm not here about money."

"Then I fail to see—"

"I was told he had a daughter." The man's gaze swept the room, and Tamsin felt it coming the way you feel a wave gathering under a boat—that lift in the stomach before the impact. "A girl. She'd have been a child when he drowned. I was told the Company took her on."

"She's indentured to us, yes." Crane's tone cooled by degrees, the way water cools with depth. "What business is that of yours?"

"No business." The stranger spread his big hands, palms out, a gesture that wanted very badly to look harmless and didn't quite manage it. "Only a kindness. I've got letters for her. From her father."

Tamsin heard the words arrive, and they didn't make sense, and then they made sense all at once, and the floor of the world tilted the way a deck tilts when the swell comes in sideways.

Letters. From her father.

Her father was eleven years drowned. She had stood on the pier at eight years old—no, nine, the same season they'd bound her to the Company, the year had taken both her father and her name and handed her a ledger number instead—and watched them bring up his gear without him. She remembered the mask, cracked along the temple. She remembered Maren Ostrow putting bread in her hand because she couldn't make her own fingers close around it. She had mourned him the way children mourn, in fragments and tides, and then she had mourned him the way the harbor mourns, which is to say quietly and forever. There were no letters. Dead men don't write.

But the stranger was reaching into his coat, and Crane was saying, "This is highly irregular—" and the man said, "Everything worth doing is," and produced three envelopes, worn soft as cloth at the corners, tied with a length of tarred twine.

He held them out toward Crane, but his eyes were moving around the room, searching. Looking for a girl who would be seventeen now. Looking, Tamsin understood with a strange floating clarity, for her—and not finding her, because he was looking for someone who would step forward, and stepping forward was the one thing Tamsin Pemberton had trained herself out of ever doing.

"These are in his hand," the stranger said. "His writing. Anyone who knew him would know it. He had a hand like—" a rough half-laugh with no humor in it, "—like a spider had drowned in the inkwell. But it was his."

Tamsin's heart was beating somewhere high in her throat. Because she knew that handwriting. Not from letters—she had no letters—but from somewhere else entirely, somewhere she had never told a living soul.

When she was small, before the drowning, her father used to practice his letters at the kitchen table in the evenings, a stub of pencil and whatever scrap of paper the sea gave him. He was learning to write properly so he could read his own dive contracts and catch the Company cheating him—which they cheated everyone, but he wanted to catch them at it, wanted it like some men want God. *Watch this, Tammy,* he'd say, and write her name in great wobbling capitals, and she'd laugh because the P always leaned like a drunk against the next letter. He'd copied his letters from a primer he'd bought secondhand, and his lowercase *d* had a loop that swung too wide, and his crossings-out spiraled, and there was no other hand in the world like it, because there was no other man in the world like him.

The envelopes on the counting house desk, held in a stranger's rope-scarred fingers, were addressed in that hand. She could see the leaning capitals from six feet away. She could see the swinging loop of the *d*.

*For my daughter, when she can read it.*

The floor did not merely tilt. It dropped. She felt it go—a physical falling sensation, her stomach rising, the counting house lamps suddenly far away and small, the whole ordered architecture of her life sliding like scree. For nine years she had built herself into a person who could survive anything because nothing surprised her anymore. She had made a religion of the predictable. And in the space of one minute, on a second Thursday, a dead man's handwriting had walked into the room and put a boot through all of it.

*He's alive.* The thought arrived first, huge and blinding, before any doubt could temper it. *He's alive and he wrote to me and he let me grieve him—*

And then, hard on its heels, colder: *Or somebody wants me to think so. Or somebody is using his hand. Or—*

"—no such correspondence will pass through this office," Crane was saying, in the voice he saved for closing doors. "The girl is a minor under indenture. Any communication concerning her passes through me. Those letters—" he extended one pale hand, "—will be surrendered for review."

The stranger did not move. The envelopes stayed where they were, and something in the quality of his stillness reminded Tamsin of her father's stories about octopi: the moment before the color change, the held breath.

"They're not for the Company," he said. "They're for the girl. I'll deliver them myself or not at all."

"You'll deliver nothing, Mr.—"

"Reeve. Callum Reeve." He said it plainly, offering the name like a man laying a card face-up. Too plainly, Tamsin thought. Men with clean histories don't announce themselves so carefully. "And I'll thank you to tell me where she's kept."

"She is not *kept*, she is employed—" Crane's jaw tightened. He disliked, visibly, being unable to simply crush things today. "If the girl wishes contact with persons claiming acquaintance with her late father, she may petition through proper channels. Proper channels," he repeated, savoring it, and Tamsin knew those channels: they were the ones with tolls, and the tolls were always paid by people like her.

"I see." Reeve gathered the letters back with exaggerated care, retying the twine with thick fingers that nevertheless moved like they'd done finer work once. "Then I'll find her the harbor's way."

"There is no harbor way."

"Aye, there is." For the first time, Callum Reeve smiled, and it transformed his face into something younger and sadder. "There's always a harbor way. Word travels faster than mail in a place like this. By tonight, every dock and dive shack from the breakwater to the tar yards will know Callum Reeve came asking after Wendell Pemberton's girl. And she'll know where to find me." He tipped his head, almost politely, toward Crane. "You can't stop the tide, sir. You just get to decide whether to look like a fool standing against it."

He turned to go, and Tamsin—standing in her shadow between the filing cabinets, invisible, safe, nine years practiced at being nobody—did the hardest thing she had ever done in her life.

She said nothing.

It cost her everything. Every muscle in her body strained toward speech. One word—one syllable, *here*—and the letters would be hers. But Crane was watching. Crane would take the letters for review, and reviews at Meridian ended with things disappearing, and whatever those letters held, whoever had written them, they would vanish into the Company's drawers the way her father's salvage had vanished, the way everything vanished. And if her father was alive—if he had let her believe him dead for eleven years—then there was a reason, and reasons required patience, and patience was the one skill her invisibility had taught her best.

So she stood in the shadow and let him walk past her, close enough that she caught the smell of tar and cold sea on his coat, close enough to see the salt-bleached scar running through his left eyebrow, and she kept her eyes down on the ledger in front of her like a good clerk, like furniture, like nothing at all.

The bell over the door rang. The wet footprints stayed on the floor a few minutes, then faded, as if he'd never been there.

* * *

That night the loft above the drying sheds was cold, and the harbor spoke to itself below in its usual language of hawsers and halyards and water worrying the pilings. Tamsin sat on her cot with a candle stub and a scrap of wrapping paper, and she did not allow herself to think yet. Thinking could wait. First, work.

She closed her eyes and built the counting house in her head, the way she built it every night—not because she needed the practice anymore, but because the ritual steadied her, a clerk's evening prayer. The desks. The trays. Crane at his high desk with the correspondence drawer open. And the stranger standing in the doorway with the light behind him, holding three letters in a dead man's hand.

She opened her eyes and wrote, in careful block letters she'd invented herself precisely because they resembled no one's:

CALLUM REEVE. ASKING FOR WENDELL PEMBERTON. THREE LETTERS. TWINE. SCAR THROUGH LEFT EYEBROW. SAID HE'D BE FINDABLE BY NIGHTFALL.

And beneath it, because her hand demanded it even as her mind resisted, she added:

HARBOUR TAVERN? THE PIER?

No. That was guesswork dressed up as fact, and guesswork got people caught. She struck it through. Then, because the paper was hers and the night was long, she turned it over and wrote the only true thing she knew:

HE IS DEAD OR HE IS NOT. BOTH OF THESE ARE UNBEARABLE.

She looked at that sentence a long time. Candlelight made the letters tremble slightly, or her hand did, it hardly mattered which.

Because here was the thing she had never said aloud, not even to the dark: for eleven years, being an orphan had been the worst thing in the world, and also the only fixed point she had. Everything else moved—Crane's moods, the seasons, the debt that never shrank no matter how the numbers danced—but her father's death was solid ground. She had built her whole careful life on it. And now a stranger with a scarred eyebrow had pulled the ground out and replaced it with three soft-cornered envelopes tied in tarred twine, and beneath her feet there was nothing but the question, vast and black and patient as the harbor itself.

If he lived, why had he let her mourn? Why had he let her be sold into indenture on the strength of his debts—if he wasn't dead, the debts weren't settled, the arithmetic didn't close, unless—

Unless someone had wanted the arithmetic to close. Unless someone had profited from a death that wasn't a death. The thought slid into place with a click she felt in her teeth, cold and precise, and she didn't know yet what it meant, only that it mattered, only that somewhere in the Company's ledgers there was an entry that explained why a living man had needed to be a dead one.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the water below, and made herself a promise in the shape of tomorrow.

In the morning she would finish the dive accounts early. She would be fast and quiet and forgettable, and she would watch Crane the way she'd learned to watch everyone, and when the counting house emptied for the midday tide, she would walk down to the harbor and ask, casually, idly, the way a girl asks about the weather, whether anyone had seen a big man with a scarred eyebrow come off the packet boat.

And if he had letters from her father—if that leaning capital P, that swinging looped *d*, belonged to a living man—then she would finally learn to read the one document that had governed her whole life: the true account of Wendell Pemberton.

Below her, the tide turned. Above the rooftops, far out past the breakwater, a single lamp moved along the water, steady as a heartbeat, going somewhere.

She watched it until it passed from view, and then, in the dark, she began to plan.
