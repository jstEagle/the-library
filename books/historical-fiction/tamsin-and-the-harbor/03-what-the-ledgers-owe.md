## 3. What the Ledgers Owe

The debt ledger lived on the second shelf of the iron cabinet in the counting house's back room, and Tamsin Pemberton had dusted it every Tuesday for eleven years.

She had learned early that the best way to know a thing was to be the one who cleaned it. Nobody watched a girl with a rag. Nobody wondered what a girl with a rag saw. She could have recited the shelf from memory: insurance policies above, correspondence below, and between them the great green-bound volumes of the Company's accounts—cargo manifests, payroll books, and there, third from the left, the ledger of indentures and debts, the book that held her name the way a lock holds a key.

*Pemberton, T. — ward of Meridian Trading Co., bound against account of W. Pemberton, diver. Balance outstanding.*

Her balance had been outstanding since she was nine years old. It would be outstanding until some supervisor decided otherwise, and supervisors at Meridian did not decide things out of kindness.

On this particular Tuesday, the last week of October 1894, she knelt before the open cabinet with her rag in one hand and her heart beating somewhere up near her throat, because three nights ago a stranger on the pier had said her father's name to her like a question, and she had not been able to stop turning it over since.

*Wendell Pemberton.* Callum Reeve had said it the way you'd say the name of a man you'd shared bread with. And he'd carried letters in that handwriting—the strong downstrokes, the little hook on the W like a gull's wing—that Tamsin knew better than any hand alive, because she had learned her letters tracing it, sitting on the floor of their rented room above the chandlery while her father mended his diving suit by lamplight.

A dead man's handwriting. She had copied the return address onto the inside of her cuff by candlelight and worn the cuff for two days without washing it, as if the ink might speak if she kept it close enough.

Now she needed something older than grief. She needed arithmetic.

The rag moved along the shelf. Her other hand, quiet as a crab under a stone, drew the debt ledger an inch from its neighbors. The room behind her was empty—Supervisor Crane was at the harbor office, young Fenwick had gone to the customs house with the manifest copies, and old Mrs. Halloway in the front room was half-deaf and fully absorbed in her newspaper. Still, Tamsin worked the way she did everything: slowly, invisibly, with the patience of someone who had learned that speed got you caught.

She carried the ledger to the worktable near the window, where the light came in gray and generous off the water, and opened it to the P's.

There it was. *W. Pemberton, diver, entered into service 1881. Advance for gear, lodging, family keep: £34, 12s.* Then the long column of years, each dive entered as a credit against the debt, each entry in a clerk's neat copperplate—and then, here and there, corrections. Entries struck through. Figures rewritten. Margins annotated in a heavier, blunter hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next page.

Tamsin knew that hand too. Everyone at Meridian knew it. Supervisor Crane signed his requisitions with that same blunt certainty, the crossbar of his t's driven through like nails.

She turned back to 1889, the year her father died. March. The *Providence Belle* had gone out with six divers to the beds beyond the point; only five had come up. She remembered that day from the outside—from waiting, from Maren Ostrow's arms around her shoulders on the pier, from the sound the widows made, which was worse than screaming because they didn't scream at all. She had been thirteen.

The ledger's March entries ran down the page in tidy rows. Dive credits, salvage weights, small deductions for lamp oil and suit repair. And then, beside the final entry—the one dated two days after the *Belle* came back shorthanded—a correction.

The original figure had been scraped. Not crossed out, which would leave the ghost beneath readable if you held the page to the light, but scraped away with a knife and written over. Tamsin tilted the leaf toward the window. The paper thinned where the blade had worked, and underneath, faint as a watermark, she could make out the shadow of the first number. It had been larger. Considerably larger.

Her father's last dives had earned more than the ledger now claimed.

She set the book down. Her hands were steady—her hands were always steady; that was what being useful got you, hands that didn't shake when shaking was wanted—but something behind her ribs had begun to move, slow and cold, like a current under harbor ice.

*One entry,* she told herself. *Paper rots. Clerks err. One scraped line proves nothing.*

But she was already turning pages.

* * *

The night her father died—or the night the sea took him, which was the phrase the Company used, as though the sea had acted alone, uninvited—had begun like forty other nights.

He had sat on the edge of their bed in his wool undershirt, working grease into the cuffs of his suit, and told her about the bed of shells he'd found past the point, in thirty fathoms where the Company's maps went blank. "Not oysters, Tam. Pearl shell, thick as roof slates." He'd tapped his temple. "I've marked it here. Three more good pulls and we're square with them. Maybe four. Then I walk into Crane's office and put our papers on his desk and we watch his face do something it's never done."

"What's it never done?"

"Lost." He'd grinned, and the lamplight had caught the gray coming into his beard early, the diver's gray, salt and age arriving together. "Then I'm taking you out of that counting house and we're going somewhere the water's warm."

She had been nine. She had believed him the way children believe lighthouses—not because they understand the mechanism, but because the light keeps coming.

The morning of the last dive, fog had sat on the harbor like a lid. She'd walked him to the pier with his lunch wrapped in cloth, and he'd crouched to her level the way no other grown man ever bothered to, and said, "Mind the numbers today, Tam. If anything reads wrong, mind it. Numbers don't lie, but men make them lie, and a number remembers even when a man doesn't."

It had sounded, then, like a father teaching his daughter sums.

The *Providence Belle* had come home at dusk with her deck dark and her crew silent, and Wendell Pemberton's line had come up cut clean, and the Company's report had said *hose failure, regrettable accident, diver presumed lost,* and Supervisor Crane himself had come to the chandlery stairs with his hat in his hands—which everyone later agreed showed real feeling—and had explained, gently, that her father's debts did not drown with him. That the Company, in its mercy, would take her instead. That she should think of it as a kind of inheritance.

For eleven years she had thought exactly that. Gratitude was cheaper than grief, and she had never been able to afford much of either.

Now, standing over a scraped ledger line with October light dying in the window, she understood that her father had gone down owing less than the book said. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the opposite of nothing.

And the number that remembered, when the man couldn't, was in her hands.

* * *

She worked until the light failed, copying figures into the margin of her order pad in shorthand she'd invented herself—little fish-hooked marks that looked like flourishes, like doodles, like nothing. Crane's original entries she marked with a dot. The corrected ones with a slash. By the time Fenwick came back smelling of pipe smoke and customs-house tea, the debt ledger was back on its shelf, squared to the finger-width it always occupied, and Tamsin was hunched over invoices with the expression of a girl thinking of absolutely nothing at all.

"Crane wants the quarterly abstracts by Friday," Fenwick said, hanging his coat. "He's in a mood. Something about the merger paperwork."

"The merger," Tamsin said, mild as milk. "That's still happening, then?"

"Signed by spring, if the lawyers don't strangle each other first." Fenwick dropped into his chair. "Half these old records will go to storage or worse. Crane's been talking about clearing the annex." He said it carelessly, the way people say things that are ordinary to them, and Tamsin felt the words land somewhere deeper than he'd thrown them.

Clearing the annex. The annex held the original dive contracts. The annex held the payroll books, the daily tallies, the divers' own marks—the crosses and initials scratched by men who could write nothing else, receipts for breath and labor, the only signatures most of them would ever leave in the world.

If the annex was cleared, cleared how? Burned, more likely than boxed. Paper was cheap and fire was free, and companies about to merge liked clean histories the way brides liked white.

"I'll have the abstracts ready," she said, and bent again to her invoices, and inside her chest the cold current kept moving.

* * *

That night she climbed to the loft above the counting house where the Company lodged its indentured staff—two rooms, six girls, walls thin as confession. The others were asleep or pretending. Tamsin sat on her cot with a stub of candle shielded by an upturned basin and copied out her fish-hook marks into plain figures, checking herself twice, three times, because a girl who meant to accuse a supervisor of falsifying accounts could not afford a single error of her own.

The pattern assembled itself slowly, the way a harbor assembles itself out of fog.

From 1886 onward—in Crane's own district, on the boats Crane supervised—the same story, told in different hands: a diver's salvage credits rising toward his debt's horizon, and then a correction. A scrape. A rewrite. Always downward. And always, within weeks or months of the correction, either the diver bought his way free and vanished inland, glad to go—or the diver died.

Six deaths in eight years. Six hoses that failed, six lines that parted, six *regrettable accidents* reported to a coroner who was also, she happened to know from filing his correspondence, a shareholder in Meridian Trading.

She wrote the names down. Okafor. Lindqvist. Bray. Two brothers named Serrano. Pemberton.

Her own name looked strange at the bottom of that list, attached to her father's, and she sat for a long time looking at it, the candle guttering, the harbor breathing below the window with its low tide-slap against pilings.

Here was the shape of it, if she dared say it even to herself: a man deep in Company debt was a man who dove whether the weather argued or not. A dead man's debt passed to whoever could be bound for it. And the ledger, scrubbed and rewritten, ensured that the debt never ended—that the salvage a diver bled for flowed into Company coffers while the number owed stayed forever ahead of him, a horizon painted on a wall.

Her father had found a pearl bed thick as roof slates. Three more pulls, maybe four, and we're square. And then his line had come up cut clean.

She had spent eleven years believing the sea had acted alone.

The candle burned down to a blue nub. Tamsin pinched it out and lay in the dark with her eyes open, listening to the other girls breathe, and let the thing inside her finish changing. It had started as grief, then as suspicion; suspicion was a splinter, and splinters could be ignored. But what settled over her now as she listened to the tide was neither of those. It was purpose, hard and smooth as a pearl formed around a wound—built layer by layer in the dark, and worth nothing until it was brought into the light and shown.

They had taken her father's name off his own earnings. They had taken eleven years of her life and called it mercy. And now they were going to burn the paper trail and call it tidiness.

No. She was done being useful and unseen. Unseen was a strategy, not a destiny, and strategies existed to be spent at the right moment.

Tomorrow was Wednesday. She dusted the insurance shelf on Wednesdays.

And tomorrow evening, she thought as sleep finally took her, she would walk past the chandlery and the netmenders' walk and out along the north pier, to the boarding house with the blue door where Maren Ostrow took in widows and sailors and anyone else the harbor had bruised—because if letters had come for a drowned man, Tamsin would wager the price of her own freedom that hers had not been the only doorstep they landed on.

Somewhere out there, other families were mourning men whose lines had come up cut clean.

It was time the harbor compared notes.
