## 4. The Historian's Apprentice

The Meridian archive lived in the east wing of the counting house, behind a door that required two keys and a disposition the Company trusted, and Elias Voss had all three. He was twenty-six years old, youngest ever appointed to the post of company historian, and he had been told so many times in his first month that he no longer heard it as praise—only as a bill that would one day come due.

He arrived each morning at half past seven, before the clerks, before Supervisor Crane's boots could be heard striking the corridor like a metronome with opinions. He lit the lamps himself, one by one down the long aisle of shelving, because he liked the way the light woke the room in stages: first the ledgers nearest the door, their spines cracked and sun-faded to the color of old rope; then the map chests; then, at the far end where the windows gave nothing but gray harbor light and the smell of tar came up through the floorboards, the deep shelves of the founding years—the ones nobody consulted anymore.

His task was simple on its face. The Company would turn one hundred years old in nineteen months, and there was to be a book. A fine book, gilt-stamped, distributed to shareholders and civic dignitaries, telling the story of Meridian Trading from a single salvaged cargo of indigo to the greatest pearl concern on the coast. Elias was to write it. His predecessor, Mr. Aldous Crane—not yet supervisor then, merely ambitious—had assembled forty years' worth of celebratory material before his promotion carried him upstairs. What remained for Elias was the pleasant work of arrangement: triumphs in chronological order, portraits of founders rendered in prose the way they'd been rendered in oils, jaw set, eyes fixed on some horizon of profit.

Elias had wanted this job since he was a boy copying inscriptions off monument stones. History, real history, was not the marble version; it was the sediment, the silt that settled into the cracks of official accounts if you knew how to look. And here he sat, at twenty-six, with keys to a century of it.

He should have been happy. For the first three weeks, he nearly was.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with an arithmetic error too small to matter to anyone but a man who loved numbers the way other men loved music.

He was compiling tonnage figures for the year 1861—a routine chapter, *The Expansion Years*, full of ships and dividends—and cross-checking the annual summary against the quarterly dive reports. The summary said the *Providence* had made eleven salvage runs that season. The quarterlies recorded eleven. But the eleventh quarterly report listed a crew of nine, while the season-opening manifest had listed eleven divers signed to the vessel, and nowhere—not in the winter returns, not in the discharge papers, not in the death registry he kept finding himself consulting though it was technically outside his remit—did those two missing names appear.

Men did not evaporate. Men signed articles, dove, and either surfaced or were entered somewhere: discharged, deceased, deserted. Every ledger in the building agreed on this principle. It was the foundation of double-entry itself—one thing given, another thing owed, the balance always struck.

Two names, unbalanced.

He noted them on a slip of paper—*J. Ferrow? T. Oakes?*—the ink faded past certainty—and told himself he'd resolve it by Friday. It was Tuesday. On Friday he found the 1861 dive register and discovered that pages forty-one through forty-four were gone. Not torn out in haste; razored. The cut was clean, surgical, made by someone who understood that a ragged tear invites questions and a straight edge does not. The stubs of the pages remained in the binding, and someone had gone over the stubs with a blade again later, trimming even those closer, so that the gap looked less like absence than like a book simply made slightly thinner by time.

Except books did not thin themselves evenly. He spent that evening walking the shelf run for the 1860s, checking bindings by thumb, and found four more volumes with the same neat excision—always dive registers, always the same years clustered around 1859 to 1863, always trimmed by the same confident hand.

Elias sat down on the iron stool between the shelves and listened to the building settle around him. Somewhere above, a clerk dropped something metal and swore. The harbor gulls argued outside the high windows. None of it touched him. He was thinking about what a man must believe about himself in order to take a razor to a page and feel that he was performing a service rather than a theft.

He thought: *I could ask Crane.*

And then he thought about asking Crane, and the thought arrived already dressed for its own funeral.

* * *

Mr. Aldous Crane received him in the supervisors' corridor office on Monday, because historians, unlike clerks, were permitted to knock on that particular door without being made to feel they'd tracked mud in. Crane was fifty-odd, silvering at the temples in a way he had clearly decided was an asset, and he greeted Elias with the warmth of a man greeting an investment he had personally selected.

"Voss. How goes the centenary?"

"Well, sir. I'm through the Expansion Years."

"A fine period. Ships, growth, the founders' vision bearing fruit." Crane gestured him into a chair. "You'll want color, of course. Anecdote. Readers forgive dry figures but never a dull paragraph. There are stories about old Merriweather and the first cargo—you'll find them in the commemorative file."

"I've found the file, sir. I had a question about the dive records from the same era. Some volumes appear to have had pages removed. I wondered whether duplicates existed elsewhere—perhaps in the annex—"

Something happened to the room. Nothing visible. Crane's smile remained precisely where it had been, calibrated to the degree. But the temperature of the attention changed, the way water changes around a drop of ink.

"Removed," Crane repeated.

"Some pages. Cleanly cut. I assume there was a reason—damage, perhaps, or redundancy—"

"You assume correctly." Crane folded his hands atop the desk. His cufflinks were small pearls; Elias had never noticed before, and now would never stop noticing them. "Archives are not museums, Voss. They're instruments. A record serves the Company or it does not, and when it ceases to serve, keeping it is sentimentality. Sentiment is expensive. You'll learn that."

"And the removed material, sir? If it bore on my history—"

"It doesn't." Crane said it mildly, the way a man says *the tide comes in*. "Your history is the Company's history. Its triumphs, its growth, its century of benefit to this city—which, might I remind you, exists largely because we exist. Greywater was a fishing village with delusions before Meridian built a harbor worth the name. Write that story. It's a good story. It happens to be true, in every way that matters."

"In every way that matters," Elias repeated, and hated himself for the echo, for the little flattery of repetition that clerks used on supervisors and that he had deployed without deciding to.

Crane stood, signaling audience concluded, and clapped him once on the shoulder. "Curiosity is a fine quality in a novelist, Voss. In a historian in my employ, it is a disease. I've seen it take men your age. It starts with a question about a page and ends with a career in teaching school in some town whose name you'd be ashamed to speak. Guard against it." The smile returned, warm as lamplight. "Now. The anecdote file. Old Merriweather and the storm of '48—it's a marvelous bit. Use it in the second chapter. Readers love a man who risks everything for his ship."

When the door closed behind him, Elias stood a moment in the corridor, listening to his own pulse. *Risks everything for his ship.* He wondered which divers had gone down with Merriweather's cargo, and whether their names had survived any better than the pages about them.

He went back to the archive. He wrote the anecdote into the second chapter. His hand was steady. That steadiness frightened him more than the razor cuts had.

* * *

There was one person in the building who might remember what had been in the removed pages, and she was not a person the Company acknowledged existed except as a pair of hands.

The archivist's assistant worked the loft above the record annex—an indentured girl, Pemberton by the payroll rolls, who fetched boxes and re-inked faded spines and, according to the annex keeper, could find any document in the building faster than the catalog could. She was invisible in the way only the very useful and the very disregarded can be. Elias had passed her a dozen times on the stairs; she had the habit of flattening herself against the wall when supervisors came through, a habit so practiced it looked like posture.

He went up to the loft on Thursday under the honest pretense of verifying the location of the founders' correspondence. She was there among the boxes, sleeves rolled, mending the broken spine of a ledger with paste and linen tape—mending, he noted, not replacing. Her hands moved with a certainty he associated with surgeons and sailors.

"The 1861 dive register," he said. "The original. Do you know its condition?"

She didn't look up. "East aisle, third shelf, grey box. Pages forty-one through forty-four are out."

He stopped. "You know that."

"I shelve it." She smoothed the tape down with her thumbnail. "A body notices gaps, sir. Same as anyone."

"Do you know when they were removed?"

"No, sir." Now she looked up, briefly—brown eyes, level, giving away exactly nothing and somehow communicating anyway that she had answered more questions today than she intended to answer in a week. "Anything else for the centenary, sir?"

There was a whole conversation standing behind her face, sealed shut. He recognized the expression because he wore its twin every time Crane praised his discretion. Two people in one building, practicing invisibility from opposite directions: hers to survive, his to advance.

"That will be all," he said, and despised the sentence as it left him.

At the stairwell he paused. "Pemberton. If you ever notice anything else—a gap, an irregularity—you can bring it to me. Discreetly."

"Yes, sir," she said, in the voice people use for weather.

* * *

That night he took his supper alone at the Anchor & Chain, at the corner table where the gaslight reached but barely, and opened his notebook under the pretext of drafting chapter three.

What he wrote instead:

*1861. Providence. Eleven signed, nine accounted. Two names unbalanced. Registers 1859–63: six volumes cut, same hand. Crane: "instruments, not museums." Question: who trims a page, and what does the trimmer fear more—the record, or the reader?*

Then, beneath it, smaller:

*I said nothing I couldn't unsay. I asked nothing I couldn't withdraw. This is called prudence. Find out tomorrow what it's actually called.*

He understood his position with perfect clarity, which was the cruelest part. He was not brave enough to be a martyr and not dull enough to be a tool, and the space between those failures was exactly wide enough for a career—if he kept his eyes on the gilt-stamped future and his hands off the razored past. Nineteen months. Write the book. Collect the credit. Take a post in the head office, marry sensibly, become a man whose signature appeared on things.

And underneath that tidy architecture, load-bearing and unexamined, the other truth: he had not become a historian to arrange triumphs in order. He had become one because somewhere in him lived the conviction that the dead were owed their entries—that every name signed to an article deserved either its discharge or its grave, and that a ledger which swallowed men whole was not an instrument but an accomplice.

Six volumes. One hand. One orderer, presumably, standing behind the hand.

He closed the notebook. Through the tavern window, across the black water, the Meridian warehouse lamps burned in their orderly rows—every one of them, he realized, paid for by pearls raised from the dark by men whose names he could not currently verify had come back up.

On Friday morning he unlocked the archive at half past seven as always, lit the lamps as always, and stood a moment in the aisle with the grey box from the east shelf in his hands.

Then, instead of carrying it to his desk, he carried it to the far end, where the harbor light came thin and cold, and opened it there, where no one passing the door could see what a company historian chose to look at, and for how long.

Inside, the register lay open to the stubs of page forty-one. And pencilled faintly in the gutter margin—in a hand he did not recognize, small and hurried, the writing of someone who had known exactly how much room a razor leaves behind—were two words that had survived the cutting because they lived in the crease of the binding where no blade could reach:

*W. Pemberton — owed.*
