## 5. Salt and Widow's Bread

The Ostrow boarding house sat at the crooked end of Saltspray Lane, where the cobblestones gave up pretending to be a street and became a mudflat the tide owned half the week. Tamsin had passed it a hundred times on errands for the Company—collecting a diver's outstanding tally, delivering a notice of arrears—and every time she had kept her eyes on her boots and her pace brisk, because the women of Saltspray Lane did not love Meridian clerks, even small ones, even quiet ones.

Today she carried no ledger. No satchel, no ink-stained fingers she hadn't thought to scrub twice, no Company pin on her collar. Just a paper-wrapped loaf bought with three coins she'd saved from mending other clerks' gloves, and a story rehearsed so many times on the walk over that it had worn smooth as sea glass.

*You're collecting nothing,* she told herself, climbing the sagging steps. *You're asking.*

The difference felt enormous. It also felt like stepping off a pier into water of unknown depth.

She knocked. From inside came the smell of bread and brine and something medicinal—camphor, maybe, for joints ruined by cold water—and then the door opened on Maren Ostrow herself.

Tamsin had seen her before, at a distance: at the annual Blessing of the Fleet, standing apart from the other widows with her arms crossed as though the harbor owed her money personally. Up close she was taller than expected, broad through the shoulders like a woman who'd hauled her own nets when there was no man to do it, with grey shot through her dark braid and a face that had stopped bothering with softness somewhere around the year her husband drowned.

Her eyes went to Tamsin's empty hands first, then to her face, and narrowed.

"You're Pemberton's girl," she said.

Not *a Meridian clerk*. Not *the little one from the counting house*. Tamsin's throat tightened around words she hadn't planned for.

"I'm—yes. Ma'am."

"Pemberton the diver." Maren didn't step aside. Behind her, down the hall, someone coughed in the wet ratcheting way of men whose lungs had spent years learning what pressure does to them. "Eleven years dead this past March."

"Ten years and seven months," Tamsin said, before she could stop herself.

Something shifted in the widow's face—not warmth exactly, but a recalibration, the way a compass needle settles. She stepped back and held the door wide.

"Well then. Come in before the lane comes in after you."

* * *

The kitchen was the largest room in the house and clearly did all its work: a scarred table seating eight, a stove that had been fed so long and so faithfully it had achieved a kind of permanence, laundry lines strung near the ceiling dripping salt-stiff shirts. Two boarders occupied the table—a young deckhand asleep face-down in his arms beside an untouched mug, and an older man with a chest like a barrel who was cleaning a pipe with the concentration of a surgeon.

"Out," Maren said, not unkindly.

The old man looked up, assessed Tamsin with one glance—the clerk's posture, the careful hands—and gathered his pipe and his dignity and left without complaint. The deckhand slept on. Maren let him.

"Bread?" Tamsin offered the package. It seemed suddenly absurd, small, the kind of bribe a child brings. But Maren took it, weighed it in one hand, and set it on the counter with a nod that said the gesture had been correctly read: not payment, not flattery. An offering between people who understood what things cost.

"Sit."

Tamsin sat. The chair was hard and the table's edge was worn to a shine by decades of elbows, and both felt more honest than anything in the counting house.

"You'll want tea," Maren said, already making it, "and you'll take it plain because I'm not wasting milk on whatever this is." She set the cup down. Steam rose between them. "Now. You've come here on a Sunday, out of uniform, carrying bread like it's Christmas, and looking at me the way my husband used to look at a gauge he didn't trust. So we'll skip the part where you pretend it's about lodging rates."

Tamsin wrapped both hands around the cup. The heat steadied her. She had planned to circle—to ask about the house, the boarders, whether divers' families still got Company pensions—and now found the circling pointless. Maren Ostrow would see through it the way her father had seen through murky water: by knowing exactly where the bottom was.

"Did you ever get a letter," Tamsin said, "from your husband? After he died?"

The question landed in the room and lay there. Maren didn't move. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; the tide slapped at the pilings below the house, patient as a creditor.

"Say that again," Maren said quietly, "and slower, so I know I heard it true."

"A letter. In his own hand. Dated after—" Tamsin swallowed. "After the date they gave me for his death."

Maren pulled out the opposite chair but didn't sit. She stood over it, hands flat on the backrest, and Tamsin watched ten years rearrange themselves behind the widow's eyes.

"How long have you had yours?"

The floor dropped. Tamsin gripped the cup. *So it's true.* She had come to test a hypothesis and found it confirmed before she'd even stated it fully, and the confirmation was worse and better than any guess: worse because it meant her father might be alive and had chosen silence for a decade; better—God forgive her, better—because she was not mad, not alone, not the only woman in Greywater being haunted by a familiar handwriting.

"Three weeks ago," Tamsin said. "A stranger brought it to the counting house. Asking questions. I don't think he knew I was—" She stopped. *His daughter.* The word wouldn't come out in this room yet. "He didn't know who I was."

"And you said nothing."

"I copied the return address off the envelope and I said nothing."

For the first time, Maren smiled. It was a thin thing, more blade than warmth, but real. "Good girl," she said, and sat down at last. "That's the first rule. You say nothing until you know who's listening."

"There are others." Tamsin leaned forward. "Other letters. Other families."

Maren turned her cup a slow quarter-circle on the table, watching the tea instead of Tamsin. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading terms aloud.

"Four families," she said. "Mine. The Quill family—their boy Tomas dove the deep beds off Cutter Rock, lost in 'eighty-six. The Hallorans—Sela Halloran, her man Rennie. And the Vasseys, down on Anchor Row. Four envelopes, four different years, each one dated months after the drowning it should've been impossible to survive."

Tamsin's heart was beating somewhere high in her throat. "What did they say?"

"Different things. Sela's told her Rennie was alive and sorry and couldn't come home. Mine said—" For just a moment the flatness cracked, and Tamsin saw the young wife underneath, twenty years gone. "Mine said he'd made arrangements for me. That I shouldn't grieve past usefulness. That was all. No name signed that I recognized, but the hand—" She shook her head once, hard, like a swimmer clearing water from her ears. "It was Aldous's hand. Twenty-two years of his tally books, I'd know it blind."

"And the fourth family? The Vasseys?"

Maren's jaw tightened. "The Vasseys' letter was never delivered. Or it was, and it was taken back." She met Tamsin's eyes. "Which brings us to the part of this conversation where I tell you something I've told no living soul, and you decide what kind of person you are, sitting there in a Meridian face even if there's no pin on your collar."

"I'm not—"

"I know what you are. I know whose daughter you are, and I know you mend nets for the Company and keep your head down and your hands quick, and I know that's kept you alive." Maren raised a hand, forestalling protest. "I'm not shaming you. Survival's a trade like any other. But hear the price of what I paid before you go deciding we're allies."

* * *

The tide had turned while they talked. Through the kitchen's low window Tamsin could see the harbor's exposed mudflats gleaming, and beyond them the Meridian counting house rising black-timbered above the quay, its windows catching the afternoon light like a row of closed eyes.

Maren poured herself tea she didn't drink and told it plainly, the way she presumably did everything.

"Eighteen months after Aldous drowned, a man came to this door. Not from the Company—or wearing no Company colors, which amounts to the same thing in Greywater. Well-dressed. Soft-spoken. He had an envelope for me and an offer inside it." She slid her hand into her apron pocket and set something on the table between them: an envelope, yellowed, sealed with plain wax, addressed in a hand Tamsin recognized across the table's width with a jolt that went through her like cold water.

The same hand. The same slanted, patient capitals. The same small hook on the final stroke that Tamsin had memorized from her father's three surviving letters, the ones she kept sewn into her mattress lining and read by moonlight until the paper went soft.

"That's—" Her voice failed. She started again. "That's the same writing."

"It is." Maren's eyes were dry and terrible. "And under the letter was a bank draft for forty pounds. Forty pounds, girl. I hadn't seen forty pounds in my life. The rent on this house was six a quarter and I was taking in washing besides." She pushed the envelope an inch toward Tamsin, as if it burned. "The man said the draft was from 'friends of the deceased.' He said the letter would explain. And he said—if I accepted the money—I'd accept conditions with it."

"No inquiries. No claims against the estate. No talk."

"No talk," Maren agreed. "No petition to the Company, no questions to the port authority, no letters to the newspapers. And if anyone else came asking—like another widow, say, or a curious clerk—no mention that I'd ever received such a thing." She finally drank her tea, grimaced at it as if it were the world's fault. "Forty pounds buys a lot of silence. It bought mine for eleven years."

"What did the letter actually say? Yours?"

Maren hesitated. Then, with the air of a woman opening a wound on purpose to show it had healed crooked, she worked the wax seal—it cracked like old bone—and drew out a single folded page.

Tamsin did not touch it. She bent over it instead, drinking in the handwriting like a woman dying of thirst, and read:

*M — The accounts are settled in a way you will not like but must accept. Do not look for me in any harbor. What was taken from us will be returned by other means. Keep the house. Keep the children fed. Grief is expensive; do not buy more than you need. — A*

"'Grief is expensive,'" Maren repeated softly. "Twenty-two years married and that's his farewell. A lesson in household economy." She refolded the page along its worn creases. "I hated him for a year. Then I thanked him for a year. Then I stopped counting and just lived. That's what forty pounds does to a person, Tamsin Pemberton. It doesn't heal you. It hires you."

Tamsin sat very still. *Do not look for me in any harbor.* Her father's letter—the one Callum Reeve carried—had said almost nothing, only her name and an address, as if the message itself were dangerous. But the shape was the same. Men who had gone into the water and been given back to their families as dead, writing afterward, from elsewhere, in careful code. Paid-for silence. Conditions.

"The others took it too," she said. "Quill. Halloran."

"Sela Halloran burned hers unread, then regretted it for a decade and asked me fifty times what mine said. Old Quill took the money and drank most of it and died mean." Maren shrugged. "People do what they can. But the Vasseys—" She paused, and Tamsin saw her choose her next words with visible care. "The Vasseys refused."

Tamsin looked up. "Refused?"

"Took neither letter nor money. Sent the man away. And within the month, Corinne Vassey lost the lease on her shop—her landlord suddenly needed the premises, imagine that—and her son lost his berth on the coastal packet, and the harbor master found irregularities in her late husband's dive tallies that froze his pension payout indefinitely. By winter they were gone. Left Greywater entirely. Some say up-coast, some say they emigrated." Maren's mouth twisted. "Some say."

"You think the Company drove them out."

"I think the Company has a way of letting the harbor know what happens to families who don't sign." Maren leaned forward, and now her voice dropped to the register Tamsin imagined she'd used to calm frightened boarders through storms. "Here's what keeps me up at night, girl, and why I'm telling you this at all. Four letters. Four men officially drowned. And every one of those drownings happened on a Meridian contract dive, on a bed Meridian surveyed, with a tender Meridian hired and a rope Meridian supplied." She tapped the tabletop once, hard. "Dead divers don't finish paying their debts. Dead divers' salvage reverts to the Company. Do you understand what I'm telling you? A drowned man is worth more to Meridian than a live one."

Tamsin had known the ledgers were wrong—had seen the altered entries, Crane's neat corrections, the arithmetic that never quite forgave a debt. But this was a different order of knowledge. This was the pattern underneath the entries, spoken aloud in a kitchen that smelled of bread, by a woman who had priced her own grief and found the market efficient.

"My father," Tamsin said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Wendell Pemberton. His letter—the one I saw—it only had my name and an address. No explanation. But if he wrote to the others, if he wrote to your Aldous—"

"He may not have written to anyone." Maren held up a finger. "Look at the dates. These letters arrive months, sometimes years after the deaths, always through intermediaries, always with money attached. If these men were alive and free, why not write plain? Why route a widow's goodbye through a stranger with a bank draft?" Her eyes were sharp. "Unless the letters aren't goodbyes at all. Unless they're payments. Insurance. Something arranged in advance, by men who knew how likely it was they'd need it."

*Men who knew how likely it was they'd need it.* The words settled over Tamsin like cold water filling a diving suit. Her father had gone down healthy and come up dead, and the Company's records said his salvage barely covered his debts—but Tamsin had seen Crane's alterations, and alterations cut both ways. What if the debts had been inflated? What if the salvage had been understated? What if Wendell Pemberton had known exactly what the Company intended to make of his death, and had prepared for it the way a careful diver checks his air line twice?

"Who was the man who brought yours?" she asked. "The one with the draft. Would you know him again?"

"I'd know his voice." Maren's answer came instantly. "Soft. Educated. He called me 'Madam Ostrow,' which no one in this harbor has ever done, before or since. And he had a habit—" she frowned, retrieving it, "—he counted. Under his breath, while he waited. Little numbers. Like a man keeping books in his head even when his hands were empty."

A clerk. A Company man with a soft voice and a counting habit, dispatched like a collection agent to purchase grief at forty pounds per unit. Tamsin filed it away next to Crane's altered entries and Callum Reeve's return address, another piece of a picture assembling itself faster than she dared look at directly.

"Maren." She made herself say the name, claim the alliance out loud. "I'm going to find out what happened. To my father, to your husband, to all of them. Whatever the letters are—goodbyes or payments or traps—I'm going to find out. But I can't do it alone, and I can't do it from inside the counting house, because the moment Crane notices I'm asking questions, I lose everything. My position. My—" *freedom*, she almost said, and didn't; her indenture wasn't freedom, only its distant cousin. "Everything."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed once and fell silent, and the tide ticked against the pilings, and upstairs the wet-lunged man coughed himself back to sleep.

"You know what they'll call us," Maren said at last. "If this goes anywhere. Widows and orphans making trouble for the Company that feeds the whole harbor. They'll say we're grieving past reason. They'll say we're greedy." She smiled that blade-thin smile again. "They'll be right about one thing. We are greedy. We want what we're owed."

"Then you're in?"

"I'm in." Maren reached across the table and turned the envelope so it faced Tamsin squarely, the dead man's handwriting staring up at them both. "On one condition. You don't go to the Vasseys' trail alone, and you don't go to whoever wrote these letters alone. Whatever you've found, whatever stranger's been asking after your father—you bring it to this kitchen first. Everything comes to this table. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

Maren nodded once, business concluded, and began stacking cups with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had fed a harbor's worth of hungry men for twenty years. But as Tamsin rose to leave, the widow spoke again, quieter, not quite looking at her.

"Your father dove with my Aldous, you know. Before. On the Halcyon beds, when you were small enough to be left on the dock with a biscuit." A pause. "Aldous said he was the steadiest man on the line. Said he could feel a bad current coming before the water moved." Her hands stilled on the teapot. "Steady men don't drown by accident, Tamsin Pemberton. Remember that, whatever else you learn."

Tamsin walked back along Saltspray Lane with the mudflats gleaming and the counting house waiting, and the words rang in her like a struck bell. Steady men don't drown by accident.

She was halfway across the quay, head down, invisible again, when she saw Supervisor Crane standing at the counting house door—waiting, apparently, for no one in particular, in the manner of a man who is waiting for someone specific—and as she drew near he smiled his mild, bookkeeper's smile and held the door open for her.

"Pemberton," he said pleasantly. "Long errand. We should have a word about where you've been."
