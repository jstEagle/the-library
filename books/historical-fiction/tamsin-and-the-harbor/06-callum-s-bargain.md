## 6. Callum's Bargain

The tide went out at half past ten, and with it went the last of the day-boats, their lanterns shrinking to embers against the black of the channel. By eleven the Meridian pier belonged to the gulls and the night watchman, and by quarter past it belonged to me.

I had told Maren I was walking. She had looked at my face a long moment—the way she did, like she was reading a manifest—and said only, "Take the shawl. Fog comes off that water meaner than it looks." Now the shawl was around my shoulders and the fog was indeed coming in, threading itself through the pilings, softening the lamps along the quay into blurred coins of light. Somewhere behind me the harbor breathed its usual breath: rope creaking, water slapping wood, the far-off clang of the foundry running a night pour.

He was already there. Of course he was. He'd been there since dusk, when I'd passed him the note folded inside a returned invoice—*Pier 4, after the tide turns*—and watched him read it with a face that gave away nothing except, perhaps, the smallest tightening around his eyes. A man who could hide behind weathered skin and a stranger's accent, but not entirely. Not from me. I had spent nine years learning to read faces across counting-house desks, because faces were the only books I was permitted to open freely.

Callum Reeve stood at the pier's end with his hands in his coat pockets, looking out at the water as though it owed him money. The fog beaded on his beard. Up close, in daylight, he had struck me as merely old—fifty, maybe more, the kind of salt-cured age that divers and fishermen wear like a second hull. But standing alone at night with the lamps low, there was something else in him. A stillness. The stillness of a man who has spent years waiting for something and has gotten very good at it.

"You came," he said, without turning.

"You expected otherwise?"

"Expected nothing. Hoped." He turned then, and the lamplight caught his eyes—grey, sea-worn, and steady in a way that made my chest hurt, because I knew those eyes. Not his face. His face meant nothing to me. But the way he held still, the way he let silence sit between words instead of rushing to fill it—that was my father's way. That was how Wendell Pemberton listened, and seeing it worn by a stranger's face was like hearing a dead man's laugh come out of someone else's mouth.

"I've been asking myself," I said, "what a man like you wants at the Meridian counting house. Asking after a drowned diver, eleven years after the fact. There's no pension claim worth filing. No salvage left to divide. So either you're a fool, or you're a liar, or—" My voice did something I hadn't authorized. It cracked. I pressed on before it could do worse. "Or you're exactly what you appear to be, and I don't know what to do with that."

"And what do I appear to be?"

"A man who knew my father."

The fog moved between us. He didn't flinch from the words, didn't perform surprise or grief or any of the theater I might have expected. He simply nodded, once, slow, like a man confirming a bearing.

"Aye," he said. "I knew him."

* * *

We sat on the bollards at the pier's end, where the workers ate their lunches in fair weather, our backs to the town so no one passing on the quay could read our lips even if they cared to. He produced a flask—oilcloth-wrapped, dented, older than I was probably—and offered it. I shook my head. He drank, capped it, set it down between us like a shared thing, which I supposed it now was: two people sitting over the memory of one dead man.

"Tell me your name first," he said. "Your whole name. I want to say it right."

"Tamsin Pemberton." Saying it aloud felt strange. At Meridian I was *girl*, *clerk*, *Pemberton* at best, and mostly not even that. "Tamsin Wendell Pemberton."

"Tamsin." He tried it twice, shaping it. Then something moved through his face—grief, but an old grief, one that had been carried so long it had worn smooth, like sea glass. "He talked about you. Did you know that? Every surface interval, every long pull back to port. 'My Tamsin,' he'd say. 'She'll count circles around any clerk in Greywater by the time she's fifteen.' He wasn't wrong, from what I saw of you today."

"You saw me for four minutes."

"Long enough. You corrected the ledger clerk's arithmetic without moving your lips and he never knew. Your father used to do sums the same way. In his head, quick, while the other men were still wetting their pencils." He looked out at the dark water. "You sit like him, too. Like the chair owes you rent and you're deciding whether to pay."

I laughed—a short, involuntary sound that escaped before I could stop it, and immediately hated myself for it, because I had come here to demand answers, not to be charmed. I made my voice flat again. "The letters. You showed them to Mr. Quill like a man showing a deed. Where did you get them?"

"He wrote them to me."

"When?"

Callum was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice of a man stepping off solid ground. "Over the last three years. Fourteen letters. I have twelve of them still. Two I burned, on his instruction, after reading."

"Three years." The arithmetic landed cold. "He died eleven years ago. The Company paid death benefit to no one because we were indentured—we were the benefit, we were the collateral—but they entered him in the fatality register. Drowned off the Kettle Shoals, October of '83. I was nine years old. I stood in that counting house and watched them write it." I heard my own voice rising and pulled it down. "If he wrote letters three years ago, then he wasn't drowned in '83. Which means the register is false, which means—"

"Which means you're quicker than the clerks, just like he said." Callum turned to face me fully. "Aye, girl. He faked it. Staged the drowning off the Kettle Shoals with my help and one other man's, neither of whom has spoken of it since, and both of whom kept faith until now. He didn't die in '83. He walked out of the water at a place called Marrow Point, up the coast, where a smuggler friend of mine was waiting with a boat, and he rowed south until Greywater couldn't see him anymore."

The pier seemed to tilt. Eleven years of grief rearranged itself in a single sentence—not lifted, not eased, but *tilted*, like cargo shifting in a hold, everything I'd stowed so carefully sliding toward one side. I gripped the cold iron of the bollard.

"Why," I said. It came out small. I made it bigger. "*Why.*"

"Debt." The word dropped from him like a stone into deep water. "You were indentured. Do you know what your father's account actually stood at, the year he died—or didn't die? Not the figure they showed him. The real one."

"They showed him a figure every season. It never went down. It went down and then it went back up—equipment charges, chart fees, the interest compounded quarterly—"

"The interest compounded *daily*, and half the entries weren't interest at all. They were invented. Salvage he brought up got logged short—oysters reported empty that I helped fill the baskets of. Dives logged as failed that weren't. Fines for damaged gear that came back undamaged. Crane's predecessor started it and Crane perfected it, but the system was older than both of them, girl. The Company doesn't lend to divers. It *breeds* debt, the way an oyster breeds pearl—around whatever grain of a man it can get hold of." His jaw worked. "Your father ran the numbers one winter, same as you'd run them, I'd wager, if you ever got the full books in front of you. He figured out that no matter how much he brought up, the account would never close. That you would serve your term and then inherit his, and your children would inherit yours. That Pembertons would belong to Meridian until the sea itself was emptied."

"So he ran."

"He *died*," Callum said. "On paper. Because a dead man's debts pass to the Company's insurance pool and stop growing, and a dead man's daughter becomes a ward-indenture instead of an inherited debtor—cheaper for them to keep you as you were than to pursue the account. It was the only door out of the trap that didn't leave you in it. And God forgive us all, it nearly worked."

"*Nearly.*" I seized on it. "Nearly what? What went wrong?"

And here—here is the thing I will remember about that night, the thing I would turn over for weeks afterward like a coin I couldn't identify: he stopped. Not hesitated—stopped, the way a man stops at the edge of ice he can hear cracking. The lamp-light and the fog and the dark water all seemed to lean in around that silence.

"No," he said quietly.

"No?"

"There are things I'll tell you tonight because you've a right to them, and things I won't, because telling them would put you in a room you're not ready for yet. What went wrong falls in the second kind."

"My father may be alive." The anger came up in me like floodwater through a bilge, sudden and total, and I let it, because I had earned it, eleven years of it. "He may be *alive*, Callum Reeve, and you sit there rationing the truth out to me like hardtack. He wrote fourteen letters and sent you instead of coming himself. Fourteen *years*, I—eleven years I lit a candle for him every Drowning Day. I stood at the seawall where they said he went under. I was a *child*—"

"I know how old you were." For the first time his voice cracked too, just a hairline fracture in all that weathered calm. "I know better than you do. I was in the boat at Marrow Point when he asked me to promise. He made me swear on the anchor ring that if anything happened to him—if word needed carrying—I'd find the girl, and I'd find her when she was grown enough to bear it, and not before. 'Not before,' he said. 'She's got her mother's stubbornness and my luck, and if you tell her too soon she'll go digging, and Meridian eats diggers.' So I stayed away. Eleven years I stayed away, doing work I'm not proud of, keeping his letters dry, watching the shipping lists for anything out of Greywater. And then things went wrong, and I came, and I found you mending their paperwork in their counting house with your head down and your eyes everywhere, and I thought—he was right about her, and God help me for what finding her costs her."

"What went *wrong*?" I demanded again. "Is he dead? Is that it? Did he die after all, somewhere south, alone, and you've been carrying his corpse around in your pocket along with his letters?" My voice broke wide open on the last word and this time I let it. Let the whole harbor hear, if the harbor was listening. "Because if he's dead I deserve to know it. If he's alive I deserve to know *that*. Whatever it is, I am not nine years old anymore, and I have been useful and quiet and unseen for eleven years, and I am *done* being protected by men who think grief is a cargo they can decide when to unload."

He took it. All of it. He sat through the storm of me without moving except to take off his cap and hold it, which was such an unbearably familiar gesture—my father did that when he was receiving hard news; every diver in Greywater did—that it stopped my anger mid-surge and left me trembling instead.

"You're right," he said. "It's yours, not mine to guard. And you'll have all of it, Tamsin Pemberton, every page, I swear on the same anchor ring I swore on for him. But not tonight, and not because I'm cruel. Tonight, because what went wrong isn't a fact—it's a *door*, and on the other side of that door stand the same men who burned ledgers last week and called it housekeeping. You start pulling that thread tonight, tomorrow you're not a clerk anymore. You're a threat. And a threat with no allies, no evidence, and an indenture around her neck." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his grey eyes held mine. "Let me build the wall before I show you the fire behind it. Give me a week. Give me the widows—I know you've been to Maren Ostrow's, don't look surprised, I've asked after you in every boarding house and bait shop on the waterfront since I landed. Give me time to bring the others who received letters out of their silence, and to find out what's become of the third man who was in the boat that night. Then you'll have it all, and I'll stand beside you when you do whatever you mean to do with it. Because you do mean to do something. I can see it. It's the exact look he had, the last month, when he stopped diving scared and started diving angry."

The fog had thickened around us until the town was gone entirely—no lamps, no quay, no Meridian clock tower striking midnight somewhere behind the murk. Just the pier, the bollards, the black breathing water, and this man wearing my father's silences like a borrowed coat. I wanted to hate him. I wanted it the way you want a simple thing when everything else has gone complicated. But underneath the anger, tangling through it like rope through kelp, was the other thing: longing, vast and specific. This man had eaten breakfast with my father. Had heard him talk about me. Had sat in boats with him in weather I would never know about, and carried his handwriting against his own skin for years. Every hour Callum Reeve had spent in Wendell Pemberton's company was an hour stolen back from the sea, and some starving part of me wanted to interrogate him for all of them, wanted to make him tell me the sound of my father's laugh, whether he whistled when he worked, whether he ever mentioned my mother, whether—

Whether he was alive.

"One week," I said at last. My voice was hoarse. "Then everything. Sworn."

"Sworn." He put his cap back on.

I stood, and my legs were steadier than I expected. I turned to go, then stopped, because there was one more question, and it had been sitting in me since the counting house, patient as a barnacle.

"Callum. The return address on those letters. Port Sallow." I watched his face carefully in the murk. "That's a real place?"

Something flickered there—too fast to name. "It's real enough."

"Then whoever wrote those letters was within two hundred miles of Greywater. Within two hundred miles for three years." I pulled the shawl tighter. "While I lit candles at a seawall."

He had no answer for that, and to his credit, he didn't insult me by pretending to look for one.

* * *

I was halfway down the pier before his voice reached me through the fog, low and urgent.

"Tamsin. Wait."

I turned. He had come to his feet, and there was something new in his posture—all the patience gone out of it, replaced by a coiled alertness that raised the hair on my arms. Beyond him, far down the quay, a lantern bobbed. Moving fast. Toward the pier.

"That's a Meridian watch light," he said quietly. "Two hours early for the round, and coming straight on. Somebody told somebody somebody was meeting somebody on Pier 4 tonight." He was already moving, gathering his flask, his coat. "Go home the long way, past the fish market, not the counting-house lane. And girl—" he caught my arm, brief, his grip hard as a cleat, "—whatever Crane offers you this week, however sweet he makes it sound, remember whose arithmetic built this harbor's books. Nothing Meridian gives free is free."

The lantern swung nearer. I slipped off the pier's end into the dark of the fish market alley, heart hammering against my ribs like a fist on a locked door, and behind me I heard the watchman's hail roll out across the empty water—heard Callum answer it, easy and unhurried, a stranger with a legitimate grievance about a lost brother's pension, nothing to see here, officer, nothing at all.

I did not go home the long way.

I went straight to Maren Ostrow's, because the week he'd asked for had just gotten shorter, and there were widows who needed waking.
