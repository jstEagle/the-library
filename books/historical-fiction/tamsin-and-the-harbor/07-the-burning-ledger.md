## 7. The Burning Ledger

The order came down on a Tuesday, in the way that ruinous things often do—wrapped in routine, delivered without ceremony, initialed by a man who would not remember signing it.

Elias Voss was at his desk in the archive annex when the memorandum arrived, carried by a boy so young his collar still chafed. The annex occupied the ground floor of the Meridian Trading Company's older warehouse, a long brick room that smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of paper left too long in salt air. Elias had worked there eleven months. In that time he had catalogued four thousand documents, drafted sixty pages of the official centenary history, and learned to drink his tea cold because the stove was on the far side of the room and the work would not wait.

He broke the seal and read the memorandum twice.

*In anticipation of the forthcoming consolidation with Harrowgate & Sons, all records of a superseded nature are to be culled from the company archive. Materials designated for disposal are to be prepared for incineration no later than the first of March. A schedule follows.*

There was a schedule. Eleven pages of it, itemized by shelf and case and year, in the tidy hand of Supervisor Crane himself—or, more likely, in the hand of some clerk doing what clerks did, which was write down what they were told. Elias ran his finger down the columns. Payroll books, 1861 through 1879. Dive contracts, miscellaneous. Correspondence, harbor operations. Fatality reports—

He stopped.

*Fatality reports, diving operations, 1858–1881. Disposal.*

He read it again, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something less damning. They didn't. Somewhere in the building above him, men were dividing a hundred years of the company's history into two piles: the pile that would survive the merger because it made Meridian look prosperous, and the pile that would burn because it made Meridian look like what it was.

Elias set the memorandum down and looked out the narrow window at the harbor. Greywater's water had a color all its own—a greenish iron-grey, like a blade left out in weather—and this morning it lay flat and dull under a sky the same shade. Gulls wheeled over the fish market. Somewhere a winch groaned. It was an ordinary morning, and he resented it for that.

"Mr. Voss?" The boy was still standing there. "Supervisor Crane says you're to begin with the east wall. He says the historian's to sort, not to question."

"He said that?"

"Those were his words, sir." The boy shifted. "I only repeat them."

"I know," Elias said, gentler than he felt. "Go on, then. Tell him I've begun."

* * *

The east wall held the oldest materials, and Elias had spent the better part of autumn among them, mapping the archive the way a surveyor maps a coastline—case by case, drawer by drawer, noting not just what was present but what was absent. Absence was the historian's second trade. He had found gaps already: whole seasons of correspondence missing from 1863; a run of dive logs that ended mid-summer in 1866 and resumed, thinner, in 1867; a ledger whose spine had been cut open with a razor, its middle pages excised so cleanly that only the shadow of glue remained, like a scar where something had been.

His mentor, old Aldous Wren—who had held the historian's chair before the chair became a desk in a basement—had laughed at him for it. Not unkindly. That was the worst part: the laughter had been fond.

"You keep looking for the body, Voss," Wren had said, pouring tea in his rooms above the chandlery. "Every company has its razored pages. You think the Harrowgate ledgers are clean? You think any fortune on this coast is? Pages get cut. That is not a mystery. That is bookkeeping."

"But which pages," Elias had pressed. "That's what I want to know. Which pages were worth cutting?"

Wren had looked at him then with an expression Elias had come to think of as the historian's malady—the look of a man watching a younger version of himself walk toward a cliff with good posture. "Curiosity is a career-ending disease," the old man said. "It presents early, with symptoms of ambition, and by the time you notice it has taken hold, you've already written a letter to someone you shouldn't have. Take my advice. Write the history they're paying for. The truth keeps. Truth is patient. It will be there when you're old enough that losing your position won't matter."

"And if I'm already old enough?"

"You're twenty-six," Wren said. "You are precisely the age at which losing everything matters most."

Now Elias stood at the east wall with the disposal schedule in his hand and understood that he had perhaps six weeks in which to decide what kind of historian he intended to be.

He began, because there was nothing else to do. That was the trap of obedience—it always looked like work. He pulled the first cases down, sorted according to Crane's categories, marked each document with chalk: *keep* or *burn*. The chalk was white and dry and left a fine powder on his fingers that no amount of washing quite removed. By noon his hands looked like a baker's, and he had condemned three hundred documents to the fire without reading past their headers.

That was the technique, he realized. That was how it was done. You kept a man moving fast enough that he never read anything. Sorting was not the opposite of destroying; sorting was the instrument of it. A document read closely might raise questions. A document chalked and stacked raised none.

Elias slowed down.

He told himself it was thoroughness. He told himself a proper historian verified his classifications. He took down the case marked *Diving Operations — Personnel Files, 1874–1877*, and instead of chalking the lot, he opened the first folder.

Divers' files were simple things. A contract. A physical examination record. A running account of gear advanced against wages, nets repaired, oil purchased, interest compounded quarterly at rates Elias had to calculate twice to believe. And at the end, in most cases, a single line added in a different ink: *Account closed — deceased.* No date of death in several instances. No cause. Just the closing, the way one closed an account for a man who had moved inland.

Except these men had not moved inland. These men were at the bottom of Greywater Harbor, or the strait beyond it, or whatever stretch of cold water their last contract had assigned them to.

Elias worked through the folder file by file, slower now, and the annex grew quiet around him in a way that felt deliberate, as though the building itself were holding its breath. Lamplight pooled on the desk. Dust turned slowly in it. Outside, the harbor went on being the harbor—gulls, winches, the slap of water against pilings—and inside, Elias read name after name after name.

At the bottom of the case, folded into a folder that belonged elsewhere, he found the fatality list.

It was a single foolscap sheet, unsigned, undated except by its own internal evidence—a summary of diving fatalities across nineteen years, compiled in some clerk's cramped hand. Name. Year. Contract number. Salvage credited to account at time of death. Debt outstanding. There was a final column, unlabeled, filled with figures Elias could not immediately parse: small numbers, mostly, sometimes negative, sometimes startlingly large.

He began doing arithmetic in the margins of his mind, the way he did, the way he could not help doing, and halfway down the page the pattern surfaced like a body rising in water.

The unlabeled column was the difference between salvage credited and debt owed. When a diver died owing the company money, the figure was positive—the company absorbed a loss, wrote it off, closed the account. But when a diver died with his salvage exceeding his debt—when the man had finally, after years of drowning work, climbed within reach of paying himself free—the column went sharply negative.

Negative meant profit.

Elias sat very still. The lamp hissed. His tea had gone cold on the corner of the desk, as it always did, and he stared at it without seeing it.

A diver who neared freedom was a diver whose accumulated salvage—pearls brought up from the dark at the cost of his lungs, his joints, his eyesight—became the company's outright property upon his death. Every man who died rich in pearls died enriching the very concern that had kept him poor enough to dive. And the deaths clustered, he saw now, scanning the years, clustering around the accounts closest to clearance. Men died in their last season. Men died just before the final payment. The harbor would call it the sea's hunger. The list called it something else, in its silent clerkly way. The list called it a business model.

His eye went back up the page, hunting names, half-hoping and half-dreading, and stopped twice on the same line.

*Pemberton, W.*

Twice. Once in 1874—an entry, a correction, the name struck through with a single line, the annotation *recovered, returned to rolls*. And once further down, in the last section, dated 1878. *Account closed — deceased.* Salvage credited: a sum that made Elias blink. Debt outstanding: nearly nothing.

The difference was enormous. The difference was a girl's freedom bought and paid for and never delivered.

He did not know why he was certain of that last part. He had never heard of any Pemberton. But certainty settled over him anyway, cold and complete, the way fog comes off the harbor—not arriving so much as simply being there, all at once, having crept in while he wasn't watching.

* * *

He should have put the page back.

This was the thought he carried with him for the rest of the day, through the sorting and the chalking and the carrying of stacks to the staging room where the burnable material was crated. He should have put it back, because taking it was theft—company property, and he a salaried officer of the concern, and the memorandum explicitly instructing disposal, and what right had he to substitute his judgment for Crane's? Wren's voice murmured in his ear about careers and diseases and letters written to people one shouldn't write to. The stove in his rented room. The centenary history with his name on its spine. Twenty-six years old, precisely the age at which losing everything matters most.

And yet.

He kept seeing the struck-through name. *Recovered, returned to rolls.* Which meant Pemberton had died once already and come back. Had gone down into the green-black cold and been given up for dead, and had come up anyway, and been entered back onto the rolls like a piece of equipment returned from repair—and then, four years later, had died properly, at last, with his account almost clear, and the difference between his salvage and his debt had vanished into a column with no heading.

Elias found himself wondering who had collected it. Who signed for a dead man's freedom and pocketed it. Whether somewhere in Greywater there was a family—a widow, children—still paying off a debt that had been discharged twenty times over in pearls.

The annex clock struck five. Then six. The staging crates filled. His chalk wore down to a stub.

It was nearly seven when he came to the fatality list again, because he had set it atop the *burn* stack hours ago and it had sat there all afternoon, face-down, like a thing pretending to sleep. The staging room was dim. The crate gaped. All he had to do was drop it in with the rest—all he had to do was obey, cleanly, completely, and tomorrow he could write his chapter on the founding partners' shipping ventures with a clear conscience and a warm stove, and in forty years old Aldous Wren could pour him tea and tell some new young man that curiosity was a disease.

Elias picked up the page.

He stood there long enough for the lamp to gutter once. Then, in a motion so quick it seemed to belong to someone else—some other, braver clerk in some other life—he folded the foolscap sheet into quarters and slid it inside his waistcoat, against his chest, where he could feel it with every breath like a second heartbeat.

The crate received the empty folder. He chalked the case *disposed* and wrote his initials beside it, and his hand, he noted distantly, was perfectly steady. It was the rest of him that shook.

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the harbor road, because the long way passed the fish market and the fish market at night was empty and loud with the sound of water, and he wanted noise that had nothing to do with him. Fog was coming in off the strait. Lamps appeared along the quay one by one, doubled in the wet cobbles, and the masts of the moored fleet rose into the murk like the ribs of drowned ships.

Against his chest, the folded page pressed its corners into him with each step. Four folds. Nineteen years of the dead, folded to the size of a letter.

What frightened him was not that he had stolen it. What frightened him was how easily the decision had arrived, how it had waited all day beneath the surface of his careful obedience like a diver beneath a hull, and risen at the end without splash or struggle. As though some part of him had known since the memorandum came—that part of him that had counted the razored pages, that had asked Wren *which pages were worth cutting* and never accepted the answer *none*.

Complicity, he understood now, was not a thing you decided. It was a thing you drifted into, one chalked document at a time, until the drift itself became your character. And conscience, apparently, worked the same way in reverse. He had not chosen courage this evening. He had simply failed, at the crucial moment, to choose fear quickly enough.

At the mouth of Tanner's Row he stopped. Behind him, the Meridian warehouse loomed, its windows dark except one—the counting house, where a light burned late, as it had been burning late all week. Ahead, the fog took the street in soft gray bites.

He thought of the struck-through name. *Recovered, returned to rolls.* Someone had survived the company's arithmetic once. Perhaps someone connected to that survival was still alive too—some daughter, some clerk, some person who had grown up in the shadow of a debt that had already been paid in pearls and lung-rot and cold water.

Tomorrow he would verify the payroll cross-references. Tomorrow he would find out who Wendell Pemberton had been, and who he'd left behind, and what the company owed them.

Tonight he climbed the stairs to his rented room, lit the stove, unfolded the page on the table, and weighted its corners with his teacup, his inkwell, his father's brass compass, and the cold remains of his supper. Nineteen years of the dead lay flat in the lamplight, and Elias Voss bent over them like a man bent over a chart of shoals, reading the water for the first time honestly.

In the margin, beside the second Pemberton entry, in pencil so faint he had missed it all day, someone—years ago, some clerk with a conscience of his own—had written four words:

*Ask the girl. She counts.*

Elias sat back. Outside, far off across the water, a bell buoy rang once in the fog.

Somewhere in Greywater was a girl who counted.
