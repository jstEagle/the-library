## 8. A Name in the Margin

The loft above the counting house smelled of lamp oil, dust, and the slow rot of paper that no one had wanted for twenty years. Tamsin climbed to it the way she had learned to climb everything—quietly, at hours when no one counted her movements, with a candle cupped in one hand as though she were carrying something ashamed of being seen.

She had told herself she was only fetching a box of spare invoice forms. That was the story she'd rehearsed on the stairs, in case Supervisor Crane's door stood open, in case Fenwick glanced up from his columns and asked where she was going. *Spare invoice forms, sir. Third shelf of the loft.* It was even plausible. That was the trick of survival in this building: never lie when the truth could be bent to serve.

But once the loft door closed behind her and the harbor noise dropped to a murmur through the floorboards, she let the pretense fall away like an apron untied.

She knelt before the back shelves, where the payroll books went to die—the ones too old to matter and too numerous to burn, because burning cost money and Meridian Trading Company wasted nothing, not rope, not ink, not people. She pulled down the volumes by feel: *Divers' Payroll, 1879–1884. Divers' Payroll, 1884–1888.* Her father's years. The years that ended in Greywater Sound with a rope going slack.

She set them beside the page Elias Voss had let her see—or thought he had let her see; she still wasn't certain which of them had been played, or by whom—that gray afternoon in the archive corridor. One sheet, salvaged from a fatality register, names struck through in a clerk's indifferent hand. She had copied it onto scrap paper in her own private shorthand, the one she'd invented at nine years old so that no one could ever take her reading from her again. Now she smoothed it flat on the floorboards and weighted its corners with four brass ledger weights stolen honestly from her own station.

*W. Pemberton.* Struck through.

She made herself begin there, because beginning anywhere else would be cowardice.

Her father's name looked different struck through than it did written whole. Whole, it was a name she knew from a hundred documents—his mark converted to letters by some payroll clerk, *Pemberton, Wendell*, entered beside dive tallies and salvage weights. Struck through, it was a sentence. *This man is finished. Strike him from the account.*

Tamsin opened the 1886 payroll book and found him.

March 1886. *Pemberton, W. — dives: 41 this quarter — salvage brought up: 214 pounds shell, est. pearl yield high — debt standing: £38, 12s.*

She read the line twice. Then a third time, her lips moving though no sound came, the way she'd taught herself to read as a child, alone, forbidden, furious at every word that wouldn't come.

Then she turned back to the fatality list and checked the date against the strike-through.

*W. Pemberton — struck March 1886.*

He hadn't died in March. He had died in October. She remembered—God help her, she *remembered*—the cold coming early that year, Maren Ostrow's husband hauling her away from the pier by the collar of her coat while the boats came back light. October. Seven months after someone at Meridian had drawn a line through her father's name and moved on to the next column.

Unless the strike-through meant nothing. Unless clerks struck through names for all sorts of administrative reasons. Unless—

Unless she stopped making excuses for men who kept books the way butchers kept hooks.

Tamsin sat back on her heels and breathed, slow and deliberate, the way her father had taught her to breathe before a dive—not hers, not yet, she'd been too young—but the breathing of people who worked under weight. In through the nose. Hold. Out. *Panic kills faster than water,* he used to say. She hadn't understood then that he was teaching her how to survive him.

She turned to the next struck-through name. *Aldous Grebe. Struck January 1885.*

Payroll book, January 1885. *Grebe, A. — salvage: 190 pounds shell, pearl yield exceptional — debt standing: £22, 4s.*

And beneath it, in the same quarter, an entry in a different ink: *Debt adjustment per supervisor review: £22, 4s — carried forward at revised interest.*

Revised interest. She'd seen those words a thousand times without ever seeing them. Every diver's ledger had them, sooner or later. *Adjustment per supervisor review.* She had assumed—it was so easy to assume—that it meant leniency. A kindly company easing a widow's burden.

She pulled three more payroll volumes off the shelf and built herself a little city of open books on the loft floor, kneeling among them in her plain gray dress, her hair escaping its braid, looking for all the world like what she was: a clerk doing her work. Except that the work was hers now, not theirs, and that difference felt like a lit fuse running through her chest.

One by one, she took the struck-through names and hunted them through the payroll books. Nine divers. Nine dead men whose deaths had been recorded, somewhere, in advance.

No—not recorded in advance. *Predicted.* No—she pressed her palms flat against the floorboards until the wood bit into her skin. Not predicted. *Arranged.* She couldn't prove arranged, not yet, not from these numbers alone. But she could prove the pattern, and the pattern screamed loud enough to wake the drowned.

Here was what the numbers said:

Every diver who died—every single one of the nine—died within two quarters of the moment his salvage value exceeded his outstanding debt. Aldous Grebe: crossed that line in November, dead in January. Tomas Reeve—no relation to Callum, or perhaps some relation, the harbor was woven tight as netting—crossed the line in August, dead in September. And Wendell Pemberton, whose ledger showed a debt of thirty-eight pounds twelve shillings against salvage that the company's own assessor had valued, in the spring of 1886, at *one hundred and forty pounds*.

One hundred and forty pounds. Her father had spent nine years diving to clear thirty-eight. He had been worth more than three times his debt, alive, in a single season—and the company had valued his corpse's paperwork more highly than his living labor.

Because here was the second thing the numbers said, the thing that turned her hands cold and steady all at once: when a diver died, his salvage didn't vanish. It was already in the company's possession—he brought it up; they weighed it; they credited a fraction against his debt and pocketed the difference as "handling." But his *debt*, his debt transferred. To the family. To the widow. To the indentured child. At full value, plus interest, plus "adjustment per supervisor review."

Dead divers paid better than live ones. A living diver had to be fed, roped, insured against nothing, paid a wage of pennies and air. A dead diver left behind everything he'd salvaged and everything he owed, and the Company collected both ends of that equation while the harbor buried him and called it the sea's doing.

Tamsin sat among the open books and felt something in her rearrange itself, quietly and permanently, the way a room rearranges after an earthquake leaves the walls standing.

She had spent eleven years believing her father's death was misfortune. Then, for a few weeks—since Callum Reeve walked into the counting house with those letters—she had believed it might be escape, a staged drowning, a father who chose the horizon over her. She had been angry about that. She had been *heartbroken* about that. She had lain awake composing accusations and forgivenesses for a living man.

Now the numbers told her a third story, and it was worse than either grief.

*Salvage exceeded debt.* She wrote the phrase in her shorthand on the scrap paper, over and over, until it filled the margin. The Company didn't kill divers out of cruelty. Cruelty was wasteful. They killed them—or let the sea do it, or rigged the ropes, or shortened the air lines, or simply sent exhausted men down one more time past the season's end—the Company killed them the way a farmer culls: at maximum weight. When a diver was worth most dead.

Her father had been worth most dead in March of 1886. And someone had struck his name through seven months before the sea obliged.

Seven months. Either Meridian's clerks could see the future, or they could make appointments with it.

Tamsin closed the payroll books, one by one, gently, as if tucking in children. Then she copied everything—names, dates, figures, the strike-throughs, the adjustments—into her shorthand, compressing nine ruined lives into two pages of small hooked characters no one in this building could read. When she finished, she held the pages over the candle and watched her own handwriting curl and blacken, and fed the ash to the draft from the window, and felt nothing but relief.

What remained was in her head. They had taught her to memorize what she wasn't supposed to read; they had made her useful and unseen; and now their own training was the knife turning in their hand. Eleven years of invisibility, and it had never once occurred to any of them that invisibility was also access.

Below her, through the floorboards, she heard the counting house settling into its evening rhythms: Fenwick locking the strongroom, Crane's boots crossing toward the stairs, the last clerk wishing the last clerk good night. Ordinary sounds. Sounds made by men who believed the numbers were safe.

Let them believe it. Numbers were only safe until someone learned to read them.

Tamsin pinched out her candle, gathered the payroll books back onto their dusty shelf, and knelt a moment longer in the dark with her father's name in her mouth. *Wendell.* Not the struck-through version. The whole one.

Whatever the letters said, whatever Callum Reeve knew, whatever her father had intended when he walked into the water in October—the Company had marked him for death in the spring. That much was arithmetic now, and arithmetic could not be grieved away, only answered.

She rose. Her knees ached; she ignored them. There were eight other names on that fatality list, and eight families in this harbor who had been told the sea took their men, and tomorrow—no, tonight, first thing tomorrow—she would start knocking on doors. Maren Ostrow first. Maren, who had hauled her away from the pier by the coat collar, who had fed her ever since out of some stubborn widow's economy, who might already know more than she'd ever dared say aloud.

Tamsin paused at the loft window, looking down at Greywater Harbor as the lamps came on along the quay—one, two, a dozen, the whole dark waterfront stitching itself with light. Somewhere out there, beneath that black water, lay the men the ledgers had spent so carefully. The harbor had always known how to keep accounts of its own. Tally marks on forearms. Songs with dive counts braided into the verses. Widows who could tell you to the shilling what the Company claimed your husband owed.

Tomorrow the harbor's memory and the Company's arithmetic would meet. She would see to it.

She slipped down the stairs past Crane's empty office, past the smell of his pipe still hanging in the air, and let herself out into the evening with the two pages of shorthand pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Behind her, in the darkened loft, the payroll books sat in their dust, keeping their terrible sums, waiting—like everything else in Greywater—for someone brave enough to read them aloud.
