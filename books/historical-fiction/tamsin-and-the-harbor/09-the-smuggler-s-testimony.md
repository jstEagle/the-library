## 9. The Smuggler's Testimony

The tide was going out when Callum led me past the last of the fish stalls, down the shingle spit where the harbor gave up pretending to be respectable. Gulls screamed over the mudflats, picking at the leavings of the morning catch, and the smell was thick enough to chew—brine, tar, rotting kelp, the sweet-sour breath of a city that lived off the water and never once thanked it.

"Where are we going?" I asked, for the third time.

Callum didn't break stride. He'd taken to wearing a fisherman's oilskin since the night on the pier, his fine stranger's coat rolled up in a bundle under one arm, and with the hood up he could have been any of a hundred men who worked these flats. "You wanted testimony," he said. "I'm taking you to the man who saw more than anyone."

"And he'll talk?"

"He'll listen." A pause, boots crunching on shell and stone. "Whether he talks is between him and whatever's left of his conscience."

We came around the shoulder of the seawall to a huddle of beached hulls, old boats dragged up above the tideline and left to die. Some had been converted to sheds, their ribs roofed with canvas and scrap tin; others were just skeletons, black timbers bleaching like bones. At the far end of the row, half-hidden behind a mound of crab pots, sat a boat that had refused to die—a stubby gray-hulled lugger with a stovepipe jutting crooked from its cabin roof, smoke curling from it thin as a fishing line.

Painted on the stern, flaking but legible: *MAGPIE*.

"He was a smuggler," I said flatly.

"He *is* a smuggler. Retired the way a knife retires." Callum rapped twice on the hull, paused, then rapped three times more—a pattern, I realized, not an invitation. "Brannick. It's Reeve."

For a long moment nothing happened except the wind worrying the canvas. Then a hatch scraped open and an eye appeared in the gap, pale and quick as a fish turning in dark water. It looked at Callum. Then it looked at me, and stayed looking.

"Who's she?"

"Tamsin Pemberton," I said, before Callum could answer for me. "Daughter of Wendell Pemberton, pearl diver for the Meridian Trading Company, entered into their books in the year I turned nine. Drowned—or so they told me—off Cutter's Shoal, fourteen years ago this autumn."

The eye didn't blink. Somewhere inside the boat, a kettle began its low complaint toward boiling.

"Wendell Pemberton's girl," the voice said at last. It was a dry voice, like rope dragged over rock. "He said there'd be a girl. Said it like a prayer, some nights."

Then the hatch swung wide, and Old Brannick waved us into the dark.

* * *

Inside, the lugger was warm and close and smelled of pipe smoke, lamp oil, and decades of tea. A pot-bellied stove squatted amidships, and by its light I got my first proper look at the man Maren Ostrow had called, without further explanation, *the only honest thief on the waterfront*.

He was smaller than his legend. Harbor stories made Brannick eight feet tall and invisible besides; the reality was a wiry old man in a knitted jersey gone shapeless with age, white stubble on a browned skull of a face, hands like knotted driftwood. But his eyes were the eyes from the hatch—pale, unblinking, missing nothing—and they weighed me while he poured three cups of tea without asking whether we wanted any.

"You'll sit," he said. Not a question either.

We sat. He lowered himself onto a sea chest across from us with the care of a man whose joints kept their own ledger of debts, took a long pull of tea, and said, "Now then. Tell me what you think you know, girl, and I'll tell you where you're wrong."

So I told him.

I told him about the debt ledger, and the entries altered in Supervisor Crane's careful hand. About Maren Ostrow and the other widows, and the letters that had come back from men the Company had buried on paper. About the fatality list Elias Voss carried folded in his pocket, and the pattern I'd found scrawled across my loft floor in charcoal: *the divers who died were the divers whose salvage outran their debts*. I watched his face while I talked, and I'll grant the old smuggler this—he didn't perform surprise. He listened the way a bank listens, cataloguing every coin.

When I finished, he set down his cup.

"Wrong in one particular," he said. "You say the Company profited off dead divers like it was luck they couldn't help. It wasn't luck. It was design." He leaned forward, firelight catching the pale eyes. "Thirty years I ran cargo past Meridian's inspectors. Pearls out, mostly, before the Company's weighmen could tally them proper. Rum in, later, when the pearls thinned. And do you know what I learned, running that gauntlet all those years? Where the Company's boats went, and when, and who was aboard, and what came back."

"The dive sites," Callum said quietly.

"The dive sites." Brannick nodded slowly. "Meridian held the chart rights to every productive bed in Greywater Sound. Officially, those beds were worked fair—one diver, one tender, company weights, company tallies, salvage credited against the diver's debt same day. That's what the ledgers say." His mouth twisted. "But I ran contraband along the edges of those beds for thirty years, and I'm here to tell you what the ledgers don't. The company boats didn't always come straight home. There were nights—whole seasons of them—when a second boat would slip out to meet them on the water. Unmarked. No lights. And the day's take would be divided before either hull touched a quay."

"Salvage theft," Callum breathed. "Straight off the beds."

"Off the beds, off the divers' baskets, off the scales themselves—their own scales, weighted wrong both ways, heavier when they weighed your father's debt against his take, lighter when they weighed the Company's share for the crown taxes." Brannick spat into the stove, precisely, like punctuation. "A diver could work himself hollow for a season and find, come settling day, that he owed more than when he started. And the ones who fought it—" He stopped. Looked into the fire. "The ones who fought it tended to have accidents."

My hands had gone cold around the hot cup. "Cutter's Shoal," I said. "Was my father's death an accident?"

Brannick looked at me for a long time. Outside, a gull landed on the hull with a thump and began complaining.

"I never saw your father die," he said finally. "And I won't swear to what I didn't see—that's the one rule I've kept clean, girl, and it's why I'm still breathing. But I'll tell you what I did see. I saw the *Kestrel*, Meridian's own tender, anchored off Cutter's Shoal that morning with her dive lines fouled and no diver down, though the weather was flat calm and the water clear as gin. I saw the second boat come out to her that evening, after the search was called off. And I saw what she carried back to the Company quay at midnight, riding low in the water." His jaw tightened. "A dead man doesn't need a full hold."

The stove ticked. My tea had gone cold in my hands and I hadn't noticed drinking any of it.

"You could testify to this," Callum said. "All of it. The transfer boats, the weighted scales, the Kestrel—"

"I could." Brannick rose, stiffly, and stood looking down at us both, and suddenly I understood why the harbor still spoke of him in whispers. There was iron in the small frame after all. "And the moment I swore to it in front of witnesses, everything I am and everyone who ever drank tea on this boat becomes a target. You understand that? I've spent forty years making myself useless to powerful men. The day I stand up in a hearing and put my name to what I know, I stop being useless. I become *necessary*. And necessary men get removed, girl. Quietly. Like ledger entries."

"Then don't use your name," I said. The words came out of me faster than I'd planned them. "Testify however you want—anonymously, behind a screen, in writing, I don't care. But testify. Because right now everything we have is paper, and paper burns—we've both seen what the Company does to paper. What we don't have is a living witness who saw the theft happening with his own eyes, year after year. Paper can be called forged. You can't."

He studied me. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about being nine years old and watching them write my father into a column marked *loss*, and nobody saying a word." My voice didn't shake, which surprised me. "I've had fourteen years to think about silence. I'm done with it."

For a moment the old man said nothing at all. Then he laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a winch engaging after a long winter.

"Wendell's daughter," he said, to the fire more than to us. "He used to say his girl would out-stubborn the sea itself. I told him no child could be that stubborn." He shook his head. "Well. Here's my terms, and they're not negotiable."

* * *

He laid them out while the kettle went around again, and each term was a wall, and each wall was reasonable, and together they made a fortress I couldn't argue with.

First: protection, real protection, arranged before he ever opened his mouth in public—not promises afterward. He named names: the harbormaster's clerk who owed him, a justice of the peace on the civic board with old grudges against Meridian's founders, even, reluctantly, the newspaperman at the *Greywater Courier* who'd been sniffing at the Company's tax filings for a year. "If I testify, I testify under civic protection, entered into the municipal record where the Company can't reach it. Not the company archive. Not the merger lawyers' rooms. *Civic.* Stone, not paper."

Second: the widows first. "You bring me Maren Ostrow's testimony and the others', sworn and filed, before I say one word. I'm not going to be the brave one standing alone on the ice listening for it to crack. If they go first, I follow. That's the order of battle or there's no battle."

Third—and here his voice dropped, and the pale eyes fixed on me with something that wasn't quite threat and wasn't quite plea—"you tell no one I've agreed. Not the historian boy, not Fenwick if you've got one, not your own reflection. Every soul who knows is a door the Company can kick in. You come to me when it's time, and until then I'm a dead man who sells crabs. Are we understood?"

"We're understood," I said.

"Good." He settled back onto the chest, and some of the iron went out of him, replaced by something wearier. "One more thing. Free of charge, since you've the look of a girl who pays attention." He tapped ash from his pipe into the stove. "Your Crane—the supervisor altering the books. He didn't invent that system. He inherited it. The alteration hand changes every fifteen, twenty years—old men retire or get retired, new hands learn the trade. Which means somewhere in those archives, if any of them still exist, there's a chain of hands going back to the founders themselves. Whoever built the trap built it to outlive them. Find the earliest altered entry and you find the beginning of the lie." He smiled without humor. "And beginnings, girl, are what histories are made of."

We climbed out through the hatch into the late afternoon, the tide fully out now, the flats gleaming bronze and endless under a low sun. Behind us the *Magpie*'s stovepipe sent up its thin thread of smoke, and I thought: *there goes a man who has been holding his breath for forty years, waiting for someone to ask him to exhale.*

"He'll do it," Callum said as we picked our way back across the shingle. "You heard him."

"I heard a man set conditions." I hugged my arms against the wind coming off the water. "Conditions mean he's already decided. They just let him feel like he's still choosing."

Callum glanced sideways at me, and there was something in his face I couldn't read—pride, maybe, or grief wearing pride's clothes. "Your father used to say the harbor keeps its own accounts. That no ledger on land was safe from it." He nodded back toward the beached fleet, the dying boats, the stubborn one with the chimney smoking among them. "Today I believe him."

So did I. But walking back along the seawall, the counting house rising ahead of us gray and patient against the darkening sky, I counted what we had and found it thinner than hope: one smuggler's conditional promise, a pocketful of stolen pages, a room full of grieving women, and a historian whose conscience was younger than his fear.

It would have to be enough. We had weeks at most before the merger signed and the archives closed forever.

And when we reached the counting house steps, I saw why our weeks might be days instead.

Supervisor Crane stood in the doorway, lantern in hand, watching the harbor road with the stillness of a man who has been standing there for some time—and as we came up the steps, his eyes moved from Callum's face to mine, and he smiled.

"There you are, Pemberton," he said pleasantly. "We should talk about your evenings."
