## 10. Supervisor Crane Takes Notice

The trouble with being invisible, Tamsin thought on Tuesday morning as she climbed the stairs to the counting room, was that you could only be invisible for so long before someone decided to look.

She had felt the change for three days now—a prickle between her shoulder blades like weather coming off the water. Small things. The ledger boy, Fenwick, glancing up when she passed with her armload of payroll folios. A clerk at the shipping desk murmuring something that stopped mid-word. And twice—twice—she had looked up from her copying to find Supervisor Crane's office door standing open a hand's width, his pale eye fixed on her through the gap like a fish watching through ice.

She had been careful. She had been *so* careful. Her absences were logged in her own hand: *out for ink, out for twine, sent to the customs shed.* Every errand had a paper trail because Tamsin Pemberton understood better than anyone alive how paper made a lie respectable. But Maren Ostrow's boarding house did not sell ink or twine, and Old Brannick's smokehouse by the breakwater sold nothing to clerks of Meridian Trading at all, and somewhere in the past fortnight she had spent one hour too many walking where her feet had no business going.

Now there was a note on her desk, folded once and weighted with a glass inkwell she did not own.

*Miss Pemberton — my office, half past four. C.C.*

Her name in full. Not *girl*, not *Pemberton*. Miss. That courtesy alone was more frightening than any summons.

* * *

She worked through the intervening hours the way she had learned to work through everything since her father went down and did not come up: precisely, quietly, as though the world were a net that would hold if she only kept her knots tight. She copied figures from the salvage intake book until the numbers swam. She sharpened three pens to needles. She drank her noon tea without tasting it while Fenwick chattered beside her about the merger—the great Meridian-Harrowgate consolidation, the talk of every counting house on the waterfront—and about how half the old records were being boxed for destruction, and wasn't it a shame, and didn't she think so, Tamsin?

"Records are records," she said, which was the sort of thing an invisible girl would say, and Fenwick nodded and went back to his sums, satisfied.

At half past four she smoothed her skirt, tucked her hair behind her ears where the Company liked it, and knocked on Supervisor Crane's door.

"Enter."

Crane's office smelled of bay rum and cold tea. It was a tidy room—tidier than any office in the building deserved—with its maps of the harbor flats hung square, its brass lamp polished, its single window looking down on the coal yard. Crane himself sat behind a desk bare except for a ledger, a pen, and two glasses and a bottle of amber liquid that had never, in Tamsin's nine years in this building, been opened for anyone.

He was not a large man. He was a man who took up space anyway, the way a mooring post takes up a whole berth. His hair was iron-grey, combed flat as a chart, and he had a way of folding his hands that suggested hands that had done their folding around other men's throats in younger years. Everyone at Meridian knew the stories about Crane and the strike of '81. No one told them above a whisper.

"Pemberton." He gestured at the chair across from him. "Sit."

She sat. She kept her eyes on the middle distance just past his shoulder, the way servants do, the way invisible people do, and she arranged her face into the mild, attentive blankness that had kept her fed and unremarked since she was nine years old.

"You've been with us eleven years," Crane said. He did not open the ledger. He did not look at it either, which was somehow worse; it sat there like a third participant in the conversation. "Entered as a net-mender's child under the orphan indenture clause, transferred to clerical work at twelve. No incidents. No requests. No complaints filed against you and none filed by you." He unfolded his hands and refolded them. "Eleven years, and I could not tell you one memorable thing about you."

"I try to do my work, sir."

"Yes," Crane said. "That is exactly what you do." He let the silence stretch until it creaked. "And yet."

Tamsin's heart was beating against her ribs like a fish in a bucket. *And yet.* She thought of the payroll books hidden in her loft, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard. She thought of the fatality list Elias Voss had carried out of the archive in his coat, and whether he'd spoken of her, and how much. She thought of Callum's rough voice on the night pier: *Your father trusted me, girl, and look what trusting got him.* She thought of all of it, and she put all of it away behind her face the way she'd once put away her letters and her sums, deep enough that no supervisor's pale eye could find them.

"And yet?" she said carefully.

"Three Thursdays running, your errands have run long. Ink that takes ninety minutes to fetch. Twine that requires a visit to the far end of Ostrow Street—which, I'll note, sells no twine." He said it without heat, almost gently, and that gentleness was the most terrifying instrument in the room. "I am not a suspicious man, Pemberton. I am a thorough one. There's a difference, and the difference is that I check."

"Sometimes the fog delays the trams," she said. "Sometimes I walk. Walking's free."

"Walking is free," Crane agreed. "So is curiosity, and both of them cost you eventually." He reached at last for the bottle, poured two fingers into each glass, and pushed one across the desk toward her. She did not touch it. He smiled faintly, as though she'd passed some test, or failed a different one. "Do you know what happens to the old accounts next month?"

"The merger, sir. Harrowgate Consolidated."

"The merger," Crane repeated, tasting the word. "Meridian Trading Company, founded by four men and a stolen brig, becomes a subsidiary line in somebody else's portfolio. All these ledgers"—he laid one broad palm flat on the desk, and Tamsin understood he meant not the ledger but everything, the whole building, the whole harbor's memory—"become someone else's problem, and then no one's. Records get consolidated. Redundant materials get destroyed. It's all very proper and aboveboard and it happens to every company sooner or later." He picked up his glass but didn't drink. "The past is heavy cargo, Pemberton. Smart companies jettison it."

"That seems a waste of good paper, sir."

Something flickered behind his eyes—amusement, or its cousin. "Does it. You'd be surprised what else gets jettisoned. Old debts, for instance." He turned the glass slowly on its base. "Old indentures."

The word landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

"There are provisions in the consolidation charter," Crane went on, conversational now, a man discussing freight rates. "Standard clauses. Staff retained, staff released with settlement, redundant positions pensioned off. And certain discretionary instruments available to supervisors of long service. Discharge papers. Full and final release of indenture obligation. Signed, stamped, entered in the record—" he paused delicately, "—such of the record as survives. For employees whose discretion makes them valuable. Whose silence, in the matter of old accounts, makes them trustworthy."

The room was very quiet. Down in the counting room she could hear the faint chorus of pens, the tick of the harbor clock, the ordinary sounds of a world that did not know what was being offered in this tidy office above the coal yard.

Freedom. Eleven years of mended nets and memorized columns, eleven years of belonging to no one because she belonged to the Company, and here it was set on the desk between them like a pearl on black velvet: her name, signed off, free.

All it cost was everything.

"What old accounts, sir?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, distantly, the way you're proud of a stranger's bravery.

Crane studied her for a long moment. Whatever he was looking for, she couldn't tell if he found it. He leaned back in his chair, and the leather creaked, and when he spoke again it was with the weariness of a man explaining tides to a child.

"There was diving done out of this harbor twenty years ago that paid for the buildings we're sitting in. Some of the accounting around that diving was—" he chose the word with care, "—expedient. Men who keep expedient accounts don't enjoy having them read. When the archive closes, the reading stops. Until then, there are people—widows, old smugglers, sentimental fools—who like to dig. And occasionally, it appears, they send a clerk to dig for them." His pale eyes rested on her, weightless and total. "I would take it as a personal kindness, Pemberton, if any such digging that crossed your path simply... stopped crossing it. In return, when the consolidation papers are drawn, your name goes on a particular list. The short one."

He slid a card across the desk. On it, in his flat iron hand, was written a single line:

*Discretion is remembered.*

"Think about it," Crane said. "You have until the signing. Nineteen days. Come to me before then and say yes, and you'll walk out of this building a free woman with a reference that opens every counting house on the coast. Say no—" he shrugged, a small, economical movement, "—and you'll be exactly what you are now. An indentured clerk with irregular errands, noted in my book. And my book, Pemberton, survives consolidations. My book always survives."

* * *

She walked home along the seawall because she couldn't bear the tram, because the walls of the tram car pressed too close, because she needed the wind and the gray slap of the water to scour the taste of that office out of her mouth.

*Nineteen days.*

The harbor was doing its evening business around her—gulls screaming over the gutting tables, the ferry coughing smoke, boys running sprat along the pilings—but she moved through it like a ghost through a feast. Crane had known. Not guessed—*known*. Knew about Maren's. Knew about Brannick. Maybe knew about the strongbox in the flooded cellar, maybe knew about Elias, maybe knew about Callum Reeve sleeping in the sail loft with a knife under his coat. Or maybe he knew only the shape of it, the outline of a conspiracy the way you can see a wreck through green water, and was fishing with a freedom hook to see what rose.

Either way, the trap had closed gently, the way a hand closes around a bird. No violence. No accusation. Just an offer and a deadline and a card with six words on it, and the understanding—unspoken, complete—that the bird was already caught.

What frightened her most was how much of her wanted to say yes.

She hated herself for it even as she felt it, that hot shameful pull. Freedom papers. To sign her own name and have it mean she belonged to herself. To walk out of Meridian's doors and have no one able to call her back. She was twenty years old and had been owned since she was nine, and Crane had held that fact up to the light like a jeweler assessing a stone, and he had found it flawless, and he was right, and that was the unforgivable part. He hadn't offered her a bribe. He'd offered her the exact shape of her own longing, and dared her to be too principled to want it.

By the time she reached the foot of Ostrow Street the lamps were lit and Maren Ostrow's boarding house stood against the dark with its windows gold, smelling of fish stew and woodsmoke. Through the parlor window she could see them: Maren with her grey braid coiled like rope, the Widow Quill with her knitted shawl, old Sera Dane who had buried two husbands and a son in the harbor, heads bent together over teacups, talking the slow careful talk of women comparing wounds.

They were the ones Crane meant by *digging*. They were the ones whose husbands' names were struck through on the list Elias had pocketed. If she said yes—if she took Crane's papers—they would never know, and she would carry that silence the rest of her life like a swallowed stone. If she said no, she stayed a slave with a target painted on her, nineteen days from whatever Crane did to clerks who declined.

Unless the answer was neither. Unless the answer was to make Crane's offer worthless—to make sure that when the archive closed, the truth was already somewhere he couldn't reach. Entered elsewhere. Entered publicly. Entered in ink that didn't burn.

Maren looked up through the window and saw her standing in the street like a fool, and lifted her chin in invitation, and pulled the door wide before Tamsin could decide whether to knock.

"You look like a woman who's been swimming in deep water," Maren said, "with her boots on. Get inside. Stew's on, and there's a matter we need your head for."

Tamsin stepped over the threshold into the warmth and the noise and the smell of other people's lives, and behind her, out on the dark water, the harbor bell rang the change of tide.

Nineteen days. She had come to tell them they were running out of time.

She only hoped they had a plan for what came after.
