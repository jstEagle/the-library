## 11. Maren's Room Above the Water

Maren Ostrow had a rule about the room above the water: no one cried alone in it.

She had made the rule the winter her husband Halvard's body came back to her in pieces, when she'd caught herself weeping behind the flour barrels like a woman ashamed of her own grief, and decided, with the same iron practicality that kept her boarding house standing through two floods and one fire, that shame was a luxury the harbor could no longer afford. So on Thursday evenings, after the lodgers were fed and the lamps were trimmed low, she unlocked the back parlor—the one with the window that overlooked the black water—and let whoever needed it come.

Tonight there were five chairs drawn around the stove. Six women. And Tamsin Pemberton, sitting on the settle by the door with her hands folded in her lap like a schoolgirl called before the magistrates, not yet sure whether she was guest or intruder.

The wind off the harbor found every gap in the clapboard and worried at them. Below the window, the tide slapped the pilings in its slow, patient way, as though counting something.

* * *

Petra Vance spoke first, because Petra always spoke first. She was the youngest widow at the table—thirty-one, maybe thirty-two, with forearms still corded from years of mending dive lines for other people's husbands—and she held her cup of chicory coffee in both fists as if it might try to escape her.

"Joss's last dive was the Corvella wreck," she said. "You all know that. But what nobody knows—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "What I never told anybody is that he knew. He knew before he went down that morning. He kissed me at the door and he said, 'Pet, if the tally comes back wrong again, don't fight them this time. Sign whatever they put in front of you.' Wrong again. He said it like we'd been over it. Like there'd been a before."

"There was a before," Maren said quietly.

"Nobody fights the tally," said old Sela from the corner chair. Sela Ketteridge had been a widow so long that the harbor had half forgotten she'd ever been a wife; her hair was white as gull-feather and her voice carried the flat authority of someone who had buried a husband, a brother, and a son in Greywater water. "That's the first thing they teach you, isn't it? Not how to dive. How to lose."

Petra's knuckles whitened around the cup. "He came up that day. You understand that? The Corvella didn't kill him. He came up with his bag full—full, Maren, I saw the pearls myself, spread them on our own table—and three days later Meridian's man comes to my door and tells me Joss died of the cramp, and the salvage barely covered his debt, and here's ten dollars for my trouble, sign here."

She set the cup down. Her hands were shaking now, but her voice had gone very level, the way voices do when they pass through fury into something colder underneath.

"I signed. God help me, I signed."

* * *

Tamsin listened the way she had learned to listen in the counting house: completely, silently, cataloguing.

But this was different from listening to Crane dictate letters, different from memorizing ledger entries she wasn't supposed to understand. This was listening to her own future being described by women who had already lived it. Every story at this table ended the same way—a door, a paper, a signature, a sum too small to matter to anyone but the widow signing for it—and Tamsin felt the shape of her own life pressing against that pattern like a hand against glass.

Wendell Pemberton hadn't left a widow. He'd left worse: a daughter, nine years old, valuable precisely because she could be indentured. The Company hadn't even bothered with the ten dollars. They'd given her a cot in the clerks' annex and taught her to mend their paperwork instead of their nets.

"Maren," she said, before she could stop herself. "When Halvard dove—did you ever see the weight ledgers?"

The room went still. It was the kind of stillness Tamsin recognized from the counting house, the stillness of people deciding how much truth a moment can hold.

Maren Ostrow looked at her across the lamplight for a long time. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a wharf bollard, with a face that seemed carved for weather rather than expression. When she finally answered, she answered slowly, choosing each word like a woman selecting stones for a seawall.

"I saw enough," she said. "Halvard dove eleven years. Eleven years, girl. In all that time his debt never went down more than a few dollars a season, no matter what he brought up. I used to work it out on the back of my laundry lists. A good pearl, a real one, pays twenty dollars to the diver's account. Halvard brought up good pearls. Some seasons, four and five of them." She turned her cup on the table, a slow circle. "Eleven years of four and five twenties against a debt that never moved. You tell me what that adds to."

"It doesn't," Tamsin said. "It can't. Unless the pearls are being entered at a fraction of their worth—or not entered at all."

"And who would know?" said Sela, not unkindly. "Us? We can't read their books. Half of us can't read anything. That's not an accident, either. My Tomas could cipher like a church deacon, and do you know what happened? Meridian offered him a clerkship. Inside work, dry socks, double pay. All he had to do was stop diving and start writing, and the first thing they'd have him write was the falsifying of his own mates' accounts. He laughed in the supervisor's face. Six months later he was dead of the lung, and his account—which should have shown near forty dollars of credit—showed nothing. Nothing at all. As if eleven years of him had been wiped off their paper."

Sela's voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about it. It stayed as flat as the harbor in a dead calm, and somehow that was worse than weeping.

"They don't just take the pearls," she said. "They take the arithmetic. They make it so a man's whole life works out to zero. And then they hand the widow a paper and call it charity."

* * *

The fifth woman at the table had not yet spoken. Her name was Ilse Brandt, and she was the newest widow in the room—newer than Petra, newer than anyone—her husband Rudi lost only fourteen months past. She sat with her shawl pulled tight and her eyes on the floorboards, and everyone let her sit, because everyone understood that grief has its own tide table and you don't rush it.

Now, in the quiet after Sela's words, she lifted her head.

"Rudi's last dive," she said, "I wasn't even told about until after."

The others turned. Ilse's voice was small, but the room had gone so quiet that it filled every corner of it anyway.

"He came home Tuesday night and said he'd been put on the deep stations. Off the shoal, where the current runs. I begged him—Rudi, tell them no, you're not rated for the shoal, your ears haven't been right since the winter. And he said—" Her breath hitched. "He said the company man told him it was voluntary. Voluntary. But Rudi owed ninety-four dollars, and the same man reminded him that divers who refused station assignments had their rations docked, and their families' rations too. So it was voluntary the way drowning is voluntary. You can always choose not to breathe."

Maren reached across and covered Ilse's hand with her own broad one. "The shoal took three men that season," she said. "Three men in six weeks, all of them deep-stationed, all of them behind on their accounts."

"All of them close to paying out," Tamsin said.

The words came out of her before she had fully thought them, and once they were out she couldn't take them back, and discovered she didn't want to. Four faces turned toward her.

"What did you say?" asked Petra.

"I've been going through the debt ledgers. Quietly. At night." There was no point pretending otherwise now; the room above the water had a way of making pretense feel obscene. "Entries altered. Supervisor Crane's hand over original figures. And lately I found a fatality list—divers lost between '81 and '89—and I've been setting it against the payroll books. The men who died on the deep stations, the ones whose deaths were called accidents of greed or recklessness—" She stopped, steadied herself. "Every one of them was within reach of clearing his debt. Within a season. Sometimes within weeks. The Company doesn't lose money when a diver drowns. They save it. His salvage comes in free, his debt is struck as unrecoverable against the estate, and the indenture passes to whoever's left alive to inherit it."

Silence. Outside, a foghorn moaned somewhere out past the breakwater, long and low, like something grieving at sea.

"I always knew," Sela said at last, "the way you know weather's coming. In your bones, in your teeth. But knowing in your bones and having it said out loud, plain, with figures behind it—" She shook her head slowly. "Girl, where did you get figures?"

"That's mine to keep safe for now. But I'll tell you everything when I can. I swear it."

Maren studied her across the table, and Tamsin had the sudden, unnerving sense of being weighed—not as a clerk is weighed, against output and obedience, but the way the harbor weighs a hull: for soundness, for whether she'd hold against open water.

"You sound like your father," Maren said.

Tamsin's heart stopped and restarted. "You knew him."

"Everybody knew Wendell Pemberton." Maren's voice gentled by a degree, which for Maren was a great deal. "Big laugh, careful hands. He used to bring me oysters off the north flats when the season was closed and pretend he hadn't. He sat in that chair you're near, more than once, after his wife passed. Asked me would I look after his girl if anything ever—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Well. Something did."

"He wrote letters," Tamsin said. Her voice came out strange, thin. "Before the end. Did he—"

"Another night," Maren said, firm but not unkind. "Tonight is for them." She nodded around the table, at Petra and Sela and Ilse. "For the ones who went down."

* * *

Later, when the coffee was gone and the stove had burned down to a red heart, the talk turned—as it always turned, Maren had learned—to the details. The small things. Because the Company took the big things, the pearls and the lives and the arithmetic, but the small things it couldn't reach, and so the widows hoarded them like coins sewn into a hem.

Petra remembered that Joss hummed when he dressed for a dive, always the same three bars of some sailor song, off-key. Sela remembered Tomas's lucky stone, a gray pebble he'd kiss before every descent, which she still kept in the sugar tin, and would keep until she died, and possibly after. Ilse remembered that Rudi had eaten two helpings of her fish stew the night before the shoal took him, and had said, laughing, that a man should die with a full stomach if he had to die at all, and how she had thrown a dishrag at him for saying it.

And Maren remembered Halvard coming up from his final dive with his mask pushed back and the strangest look on his face—not fear, not pain, but puzzlement, as though the bottom had shown him something he hadn't expected. She'd asked him what. He'd said: numbers, Pet, it's all numbers down there. Then he'd laughed, and coughed blood into his rag where he thought she couldn't see, and gone to sign the morning's tally without reading it, because reading it wouldn't have changed what was written.

"We should have fought," Petra said, staring into the dead stove. "All these years. We should have stood on the Custom House steps and shouted it."

"With what?" Sela asked. "Paper burns. Word against the Company's word is worth nothing. A widow's memory against a supervisor's ledger—" She made a small, dismissive motion. "They built it so we couldn't. That was the whole design. Not just the diving. The silence after."

"Then we build differently," Tamsin said.

They looked at her. She realized she had stood up without noticing, that her hands had curled into fists at her sides, that the settle-warmth of the room had gone out of her voice and something harder had come in.

"Not one testimony. Dozens. Not one widow's word against a ledger, but every widow, every diver still living, every smuggler and rigger and dockhand who ever watched a tally come in short—not memories, but records. Pay stubs, ration chits, marks tattooed, songs counted out. Things they can't burn because they aren't in any building of theirs. If the harbor remembers together—if we put it all in one place, at one time, in front of witnesses who don't work for Meridian—" She faltered, suddenly aware of how young she sounded, how impossible. "I don't know exactly how yet. But I know it starts here. With what you're already doing. With remembering out loud."

The fire ticked in the stove. Down below, the tide turned, and the slap of water against the pilings changed pitch, almost imperceptibly, the way it does when the whole mass of the sea begins, slowly, to move the other way.

Maren Ostrow rose, crossed the room, and did something Tamsin would remember for the rest of her life: she took Tamsin's clenched fists and unfolded them, gently, finger by finger, and held the open hands in her own broad palms.

"Wendell used to say his girl would be the one to read the books straight," she said. "I thought he meant read them. I'm starting to think he meant fix them."

From her apron pocket she drew a key—old, brass, worn smooth—and pressed it into Tamsin's palm, closing her fingers over it.

"Back door of this house," Maren said. "It opens for you now. Whenever you need it, day or night, you come up this stair, and whatever you're carrying, you carry it here."

Tamsin looked down at the key, then up, and found her eyes burning and did not look away from any of them.

Outside, far down the waterfront, a bell began to ring—the late watch change at the Company pier—and beneath its measured tolling, Tamsin heard another sound, fainter: footsteps on the boardwalk outside, quick and uneven, and then a fist knocking hard at Maren's front door, once, twice, urgent.

Maren's eyes met hers over the lamp.

"Now who in God's name," the old woman said, "comes running at this hour?"
