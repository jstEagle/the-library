## 12. Elias Comes Asking

The page had lived in his coat lining for eleven days.

Elias Voss had sewn it there himself with a bent needle and thread borrowed from Mrs. Quill's sewing basket—borrowed meaning taken, though he intended to return both. The ledger fragment was soft as cloth now from handling, its columns of diver names struck through in the old supervisor's brutal shorthand: *lost at depth*, *not recovered*, *debt absorbed*. Twice he had found *W. Pemberton* among them, once marked lost and once—not. That second entry was the splinter he could not dig out. A man could not be drowned twice. Either the clerk who entered it had erred, or someone above him had wanted the name to disappear and done a careless job of making it so.

He told himself, walking down Harbor Street on a grey Thursday that smelled of tar and frying fish, that this was routine. The centenary history required verification of personnel records. Indentured clerks were personnel. It was perfectly ordinary for a junior historian to interview staff about their tenure.

It was not perfectly ordinary, and he knew it. He had chosen her name off the fatality list because she was the only Pemberton still employed by Meridian, and because a man named Callum Reeve had been asking after Wendell Pemberton at three taverns and one counting house in the space of two weeks, and because Elias had learned long ago that when two unusual things happened near each other, they were usually one thing wearing two coats.

The Meridian annex annexed everything, it seemed—the counting house, the warehouse row, the long shed where indentured clerks mended nets of paper the way fishermen mended nets of twine. He found her at the third desk from the window, a small woman with ink-stained cuffs and the particular stillness of someone who has practiced being overlooked until it became an art.

"Tamsin Pemberton?" He set his leather folio on her desk. "Elias Voss, company historian's office. Routine records verification. I'll need perhaps half an hour."

She looked up. Her eyes did the thing he had read about in exactly no manual but had seen twice before in people who survived by wits inside institutions: a flicker of calculation behind perfect blankness, gone before most observers would catch it.

"Of course, sir." Her voice was flat, deferential, bored. She turned to fetch a file that did not exist yet, buying herself time, and he watched her do it with something like admiration.

"Before we begin," he said mildly, "I should tell you I've already spoken to Supervisor Crane about your absences this month. He was very understanding. He said you'd been running errands for him."

Her hands paused over the empty folder. When she turned back, her face was composed, but the composition had changed—a new layer beneath the boredom, alertness dressed up as anxiety.

"He's generous," she said, "to cover for me."

"Mm. He is also," Elias said, keeping his voice level, "the man whose initials appear next to four altered entries in the 1879 debt ledger, which I would have noticed regardless of whether pages were being razored out of my archive, because altered entries leave shadows. Ink sits differently where a hand has scraped and rewritten." He opened the folio, took out nothing at all, and closed it again. "But we aren't here to discuss Crane. We're here because I have a page in my pocket with your father's name on it twice."

The room went very quiet around them—or rather, it went on being loud, the scratching of pens, the cough of the stove, a cart rumbling past the windows, and within all that noise the two of them occupied a silence shaped precisely like a held breath.

"I don't know what you mean, sir," Tamsin said. "My father drowned when I was nine."

"Yes," Elias said. "So the record says. Would you like to see what else the record says?"

* * *

They walked. It was safer than talking in the shed; every wall at Meridian had ears, and some of them reported to Crane. Elias led her along the warehouse row under pretense of showing her archival procedures, and Tamsin fell into step beside him with the ease of someone who had spent her life reading rooms and routes.

At the mouth of Pier Lane, sheltered from the wind by the great salt-stained pilings, he finally stopped and drew the page from his coat.

He watched her read it. He had imagined, on the walk over, what he might feel if she broke down, or denied everything, or tried to snatch it. What actually happened was stranger. She read the way he imagined a surgeon read an X-ray—fast, thorough, cataloguing—and then she folded it back along its original creases with fingers that trembled only slightly, and handed it back to him, and said:

"You kept this out of the fire they're planning."

It was not a question, and it landed on him like cold water. "I don't know what you're—"

"The annex," she said quietly. "The merger. Someone has been pulling ledgers since autumn. Pages razored, boxes re-sorted, whole years of dive contracts missing from the index cards that are supposed to describe them. You're the historian. You've seen it. And instead of writing the pretty book they hired you to write, you cut a page out with a razor of your own and hid it in your coat." Her eyes came up to his, and for the first time there was no performance in them at all, just a fierce, exhausted clarity. "So don't stand there doing your routine-verification voice, Mr. Voss. We're both thieves. Let's at least be honest ones."

The wind pushed through the pilings and made a low organ note of the harbor. Elias found, to his surprise, that he wanted to laugh—a startled, relieved sound he swallowed. Ten years inside Meridian, ten years of learning to speak in the company's careful dialect of implication, and here was a clerk of twenty-two dismantling him in four sentences.

"You could be wrong," he said. "I could be testing you. I could be Crane's man sent to see what you know."

"Are you?"

"No."

"Then it doesn't matter what you could be." She glanced down the lane—two dockhands passing, a gull screaming over the fish market—and lowered her voice further. "You showed me the page first. Before asking me anything. That's either trust or a trap, and traps don't usually start by showing the evidence. So ask your questions, sir, and I'll answer what's safe to answer, and then you can decide whether to walk back to your office and forget me, or listen to what I've spent a month finding out."

* * *

They talked for two hours, walking the long circuit of the harbor that Elias had walked alone a hundred times without ever seeing it.

She told him about the debt ledger—entries in her family's account rewritten in Crane's younger hand, salvage values reduced after the fact, dive dates shifted so that her father's best hauls fell into weeks the Company recorded as losses. She told him about the pattern she'd found: divers whose salvage exceeded their debts died. Not always, not conveniently, but often enough that coincidence wore through like cheap cloth. She told him about Maren Ostrow's boarding house, about letters arriving for drowned men, about widows paid in small sums to stay quiet, about a smuggler named Brannick who had run contraband past Meridian's inspectors for thirty years and could name which shipments of "ballast" were pearls.

And Elias, in exchange—because truce requires payment from both sides, and she never let him forget it—told her what he knew from the other end of the building.

"The destruction order came down six weeks ago," he said. They stood at the rail overlooking Greywater Bay, the water the color of tarnished pewter. "Officially, it's housekeeping ahead of the merger with the Corvane interests. Unofficially—" He hesitated. This was the edge, and they both knew it. "Unofficially, the founder's grandson reviewed my draft history last month and returned it with instructions to remove all references to diving fatalities between 1861 and 1883. All of them. As if the divers hadn't existed."

"How many names?"

"That's the thing. I couldn't count them, because the fatality registers were among the first things removed from my access." He looked out at the water, where the buoys bobbed like patient heads. "Your page is the only list I have. Eleven names. Eleven men struck through in one supervisor's hand, and every strike-through followed the same logic you described—each man died owing less than he'd brought up. Each death settled accounts in Meridian's favor."

"My father's on it twice," Tamsin said. "Once lost. Once not."

"I noticed."

"So did I. For nine years I believed the first one. Now I need to know which entry was true, and why anyone would bother recording him twice." She wrapped her hands around the rail's wet iron. "Callum Reeve won't say. I've asked. There's something he's carrying that he thinks will break me, and he's decided I'm not strong enough for it yet, and the arrogance of that—" She stopped herself, breathed. "Forgive me. You didn't come here for my grievances."

"On the contrary," Elias said. "I'm beginning to think grievances are the only honest records left in that building."

She turned and studied him, and he felt the full weight of that scrutiny—young as she was, indentured as she was, she had the assessing gaze of someone who had survived by judging character accurately and quickly, and he stood in it like a specimen pinned to a card.

"Why?" she asked simply. "Why risk your position? You have a career. A title. In a year you'd be the man who wrote the official history of Meridian Trading, and every door in this city would open to it. Why throw that away for dead divers and a clerk nobody notices?"

The question deserved honesty, and honesty cost him something, and he paid it.

"Because I was hired to write a history," he said, "and I discovered I don't know how to write a lie that large. My mentor warned me curiosity was a career-ending disease. He was right—it's terminal. But I find I'd rather die of it than recover." He paused. "Also, less nobly: my name goes on that book. Whatever it says, I wrote it. I have to live in the same century with myself afterward."

Something in her posture eased—not trust, not yet, but the withdrawal of a weapon held at ready.

"All right, Mr. Voss. Here's my price." She spoke now with the crisp cadence of someone reciting terms she had already rehearsed, perhaps in front of a mirror, perhaps in prayer. "You want the true history. Fine. I have witnesses—divers' widows, a smuggler, paybook stubs, tally marks, testimony that predates every razored page in your archive. Memory doesn't burn, whatever they do to the paper. I'll give you all of it. But none of it goes into a company archive, because company archives get razored, and burned, and 'housekept.' Every statement gets entered into the civic record. The city registry. Where the Company's lawyers can't reach it and the merger can't absorb it. You have standing to do that—historian's certification, witness attestation, whatever your office lets you sign. That's the bargain. Your scholarship, my evidence, the public record."

"And in exchange you want—"

"Nothing." She said it flatly, and he believed her, which frightened him more than any demand would have. "Not freedom papers, not restitution for myself, not protection. I want it written down that eleven men were murdered by arithmetic. After that, they can do what they like with me."

"They'll charge you with theft," Elias said quietly. "If they trace that page to me, they'll charge us both. Crane isn't stupid. He knows the archive is leaking. Sooner or later he'll start looking at the people who touch it, and you—" he gestured at her worn cuffs, her borrowed stillness— "you work forty feet from his office."

"Then we should move fast." A ghost of a smile crossed her face, quick and sharp as a knife coming out of bread. "Do we have terms, Mr. Voss?"

He considered the harbor. The tide was going out, exposing the black ribs of the old wrecks below the pier line—the bones of ships, and beneath them somewhere, the bones of men the Company had written off as losses and profited from anyway. His whole career sat balanced on one side of a scale. On the other side sat eleven names and a woman who wanted nothing for herself.

"We have terms," he said. "One condition of my own. If it comes to a hearing—if any of this ever reaches daylight—I testify. Under oath. Whatever it costs. I won't be the historian who found the truth and filed it politely."

For the first time, Tamsin Pemberton looked at him with something that was almost warmth, and almost pity, and entirely respect.

"Then welcome to the conspiracy, Mr. Voss," she said. "We meet Thursdays at Maren Ostrow's boarding house. Bring your razor. We may need to cut more pages before this is done."

She was turning to go when Elias caught her sleeve—gently, and immediately regretting the liberty, but the thought would not wait.

"Pemberton. The second entry. *W. Pemberton, not recovered.* I've been through the accession logs for that quarter. Three days after your father's recorded loss, a salvage claim was filed against the *Meridian Star*'s last dive site. Filed privately. Paid in full, in cash, by an unnamed party." He watched her face go white and then carefully, deliberately, color itself back to neutral. "Whoever filed that claim believed there was something at that site worth retrieving. Or someone."

Tamsin stood very still. Below them the tide slid out, uncovering more of the harbor's drowned history, inch by inch, whether the Company willed it or not.

"Thursday," she said at last, in a voice that had gone thin and steady all at once. "Maren's. Come at dusk. And Mr. Voss—when you write your history, spell all the names right. Even the ones they struck through."

She walked back toward the counting house, small and straight-backed against the wind, and Elias Voss stood at the rail with an empty pocket where a stolen page used to weigh, understanding that he had just crossed a line he could never cross back—and finding, to his lasting astonishment, that his hands were steadier than they had been in months.
