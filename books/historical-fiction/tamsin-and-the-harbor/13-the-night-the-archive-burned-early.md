## 13. The Night the Archive Burned Early

The fire woke the harbor before it woke the bells.

Tamsin was awake anyway—she had been for hours, lying in her narrow room above Maren's boarding house with the fatality list folded beneath her pillow like a stone she could not put down. When the first orange glow bloomed against her ceiling, she thought at first of dawn, some strange red dawn come early. Then she smelled the smoke, and heard Maren pounding up the stairs two steps at a time, and understood.

"The annex," Maren said, already hauling open drawers, pulling a coat over her nightgown as if coats mattered against fire. "It's the record annex, girl. Get your boots."

Tamsin did not need telling twice. She was down the stairs and out into the street before Maren reached the bottom, barefoot, boots forgotten in her hands, running toward the light that stained the underside of the clouds like a wound seen through skin.

* * *

By the time she reached Customs Row, half the harbor had gathered in the square before the Meridian record annex—a long, squat building of grey brick that had stood, unremarked and unvisited, for sixty years. Tamsin had passed it a thousand times on errands and never once thought of it as anything but what it appeared: a warehouse for old paper nobody wanted.

Now it was a lantern with the sides cut away.

Flames stood in every window, orange and roaring, and the heat reached across the square in waves that pushed the crowd back even as it pulled them forward. The fire brigade was there, buckets passing hand to hand along a line of men, but their efforts looked ceremonial, almost polite—the water hissed and steamed where it struck the walls and the fire ate on regardless, working its way through sixty years of ledgers, contracts, correspondence, payroll books, dive logs. Everything Tamsin had spent three weeks learning to read by lamplight and stolen minutes. Everything Elias Voss had spent months cataloguing while pages vanished under a razored blade.

She found Callum at the edge of the crowd, still dressed, which told her he hadn't been to bed either. His face was lit red and gold, and he was not watching the flames.

He was watching the door.

"Look," he said quietly, without turning. "Third man from the corner. Grey coat. And the one beside him with the lantern—they're not helping."

Tamsin followed his gaze. Two men stood apart from the bucket line, arms crossed, making no move toward the water. As she watched, a third joined them, spoke briefly, and all three turned together to look—not at the fire, but at the crowd. Reading faces. Counting who had come.

"Crane's men," she said.

"Aye." Callum's jaw worked. "Funny thing about fires. They always start convenient." He glanced down at her then, and whatever he saw in her face made him reach out and grip her shoulder hard. "Listen to me. Whatever you're thinking—and I can see you're thinking it—we can't get near that cellar. It'll be flooded by now if they've got any sense, and if they haven't, it'll be an oven."

"Then they haven't got sense," Tamsin said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Callum. Look at them. They're watching the crowd, not fighting the fire. Nobody's gone inside. Which means—" She stopped, because saying it aloud made it real. "Which means nobody knows what's in the cellar."

"Or they know exactly what's in the cellar and they're waiting for it to be ash."

"Paper doesn't burn well underwater," she said. "My father taught me that. Divers keep their contracts oiled cloth and wax seals because salt rot eats ink faster than flame ever could. If the original dive contracts are stored proper—in tin, in the cellar, below the waterline when the brigade floods the foundations—"

"You don't know they're stored proper."

"I know Crane ordered the archives prepared for destruction weeks ago," she said. "I know Elias found razored pages. I know the merger signing is eleven days away and Crane needs those contracts gone before it, because contracts signed in blood-ink don't burn clean—they leave ghosts. Wax seals. Impressions. Signatures pressed through to the board beneath." She was talking fast now, the plan assembling itself even as she spoke it. "And I know that if I'm wrong, we've lost nothing but sleep. But if I'm right—"

The roof groaned. A section of it fell inward with a sound like the harbor swallowing, and a column of sparks rose forty feet into the dark, drifting out over the crowd like dying stars. Somewhere behind Tamsin a woman began to weep, and someone else shouted encouragement to the bucket line, and the whole square seemed to lean toward the burning building the way a crowd leans toward an execution.

"—then everything my father died hiding is sitting in a flooded cellar under that fire," Tamsin finished, "and we have until dawn to decide whether we're brave enough or fools enough to go after it."

Callum stared at her for a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes.

"My God," he said finally. "You sound just like him."

He took off his coat, folded it once, and set it on the cobbles.

* * *

They went around the back, through the alley that ran between the annex and the cooperage, moving fast and low, wrapped in shadows the fire couldn't reach. The rear of the building was less dramatic than the front—just brick and smoke, the flames concentrated in the upper floors where the reading rooms were, where Elias had worked among the shelves. But smoke poured from every seam, and the heat through the iron cellar grate was enough to scorch the air in Tamsin's lungs.

"The coal hatch," Callum whispered. He'd been a diver's partner for years; he knew buildings the way divers knew wrecks, by their service entrances and their weaknesses. "Cellars always have a coal hatch to the street. If the brigade has pumped water into the foundations, it'll be standing deep—but the hatch itself will be above the waterline."

The hatch was a rusted iron flap set flush into the alley cobbles, padlocked. Callum broke the lock with one blow of a pry bar taken from the cooperage scrap pile, and the smell that rose from the dark below nearly turned Tamsin's stomach—smoke and brine and wet ash, the smell of a drowned thing.

"I'll go first," Callum said.

"No." She said it before she'd decided to. "You won't."

"Tamsin—"

"If there's anyone waiting down there, they'll be watching for a big man coming through a hole in the ground. Nobody watches for a clerk." She was already pulling her skirt hem up to knot it at her knees, already testing the ladder rungs with her foot. "Besides. You wouldn't know what you were looking at. Contracts, indenture papers, dive logs—I've mended nets of this paperwork since I was nine years old. I can tell an original from a fair copy blind, by touch, in the dark. You can't."

"That's not—" He stopped. The fire roared beyond the wall between them and the world, and something in his face gave way. "Wendell used to argue like that. Like drowning was just a detail."

"He drowned anyway," Tamsin said, and dropped into the dark.

* * *

The water came to her waist.

It was black and bitterly cold and full of floating debris—charred paper sludge, splinters, something soft and unidentifiable that she refused to think about. Above her, through the broken hatch, the fire's glow filtered down in a dim red murk, enough to see the shapes of shelving rising out of the flood like drowned forest. The cellar was maybe thirty feet long. The water had come in fast—the brigade had pumped the foundations within minutes of arriving, standard practice to keep fire from spreading underground—and it sloshed against her chest in slow, heavy swells whenever she moved.

Somewhere overhead the building groaned and settled, raining soot.

*Tin boxes.* Think. Elias had described the oldest holdings once, in that careful historian's cadence of his, back when they were still circling each other with pretenses: *the pre-consolidation materials are in the northwest corner, galvanized tins, catalogued 1840s through '60s.* Northwest corner. Her father's last contract would have been signed in 1878, but the originals—the master copies, the ones with the wax seals Crane needed destroyed—

There. Against the far wall, a stack of rectangular shapes breaking the surface of the water, lashed to a shelf bracket with cord gone green with age. Tamsin waded toward them, arms cutting the water, breath held against the cold. The first box she hauled toward her was heavier than it should have been, waterlogged, and when she wrestled the lid up her fingers found paper pulp—dissolved, ruined, thirty years of records gone to grey porridge.

Her heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs.

The second box was sealed better. Wax around the lid seams, oilcloth beneath. Inside, when she pried it open under the red murk, the papers were damp but whole—stiff, swollen, the ink blurred at the edges but legible, the wax seals intact like little moons pressed into the page. She fanned them with numb fingers. *Indenture of Service. Articles of Salvage Agreement. Dive Contract—original execution copy.*

Names. Dozens of names. Men she'd known growing up, men whose widows she'd sat with at Maren's table only last week.

"Tamsin." Callum's voice came distorted through the hatch, tight with strain. "Two minutes. The east wall's starting to go—I can hear it."

She found cord in the wreckage of the shelf, lashed the box shut, and hauled it toward the hatch, where Callum's arms reached down into the dark. The box weighed as much as a drowning man. Together they got it up over the lip and onto the alley cobbles, and Tamsin climbed the ladder with her arms shaking and her teeth chattering so hard she bit her tongue.

She was halfway out when the second box caught her eye.

It was smaller. Tin also, but newer, and wedged alone behind a fallen shelf as if someone had hidden it rather than merely stored it. She should have left it. Every instinct of survival said *leave it, climb, live.* But her hands were already reaching, already dragging it free, and when she cracked the lid she saw a single document inside, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as the day it was filed.

She didn't read it. There wasn't time, and the east wall chose that moment to emit a long, tearing groan like something living in pain. She stuffed the document inside her shirt, flat against her chest where the water couldn't reach it, and climbed.

* * *

They got twenty yards down the alley before the wall came down.

The sound was enormous—a whole lifetime of brick and mortar surrendering at once—and the shockwave of hot air and dust rolled over them as they flattened against the cooperage wall, the strongbox between them, Callum's body curved over Tamsin's like a shell. Bricks rained into the alley. Smoke boiled past, thick enough to chew.

Then quiet, except the fire, except Tamsin's own heartbeat filling her ears like surf.

"We can't carry it through the square," Callum gasped. "Every eye in Greywater is on that fire, but Crane's men aren't looking at the fire, remember? They're looking for exactly this."

"The cooperage," Tamsin managed. "Brannick owes us. And no one searches a smuggler's own yard—he's spent forty years making sure of it."

It took them three passes, moving in short bursts between cover, the strongbox passed hand to hand like contraband, which was precisely what it was. Once, crossing the gap behind the sail loft, Tamsin glanced back and saw figures moving at the mouth of the alley—grey coats, a raised lantern, a shout swallowed by the fire's roar. They ran the last stretch bent double, burst through Brannick's gate, and collapsed in the reeking dark among stacked barrels, the strongbox between them, dripping harbor water onto boards that had smuggled worse things than paper.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Tamsin lay on her back among the barrel staves, soaked, scorched, shaking, and laughed—a small, cracked sound she didn't recognize as her own.

"We have it," she whispered. "Callum. We *have* it."

He was kneeling over the box, prying at the lid with the pry bar, and when it came free he lifted out a fistful of contracts and let the fire-glow from beyond the fence fall across them. Water-stained. Swollen. Whole. He turned one toward her and she read the header by that distant light: *Articles of Salvage Agreement, executed this day between Wendell A. Pemberton, diver, and the Meridian Trading Company.*

His name. Her father's name, signed in his unmistakable hand, sealed in wax older than her grief.

Triumph rose in her like the tide—wild, complete, the feeling of a net drawn full after years of empty hauls. They had it. The originals. The signatures, the seals, the terms that showed what the Company promised and what it actually paid. No razored pages, no altered entries, no fair copies in a supervisor's careful hand. The truth, physical and dated and stamped, dripping in her lap.

Then she heard the voices outside the fence, and the triumph turned to ice in her veins.

"—went over the back, I saw them, I'm telling you—a big man and a girl, carrying something heavy—"

Lantern light swung across the top of the fence. Boots crunched on gravel. At least four men, maybe more, and moving methodically now, working their way along the cooperage perimeter the way Crane's men did everything: patiently, thoroughly, as if the outcome were never in doubt.

Callum blew out his breath slowly and met her eyes across the strongbox. In the dark his face was very calm, the way men get when fear goes too deep to show.

"They'll search the yard," he murmured. "Brannick's reputation will hold them ten minutes, no more. We need to move this again—tonight—or hide ourselves, or both."

Tamsin's hand had gone, without her deciding it, to the flat shape pressed against her breastbone—the small dry document she'd pulled from the second box on pure instinct, unread, unknown. Something told her, with the cold certainty of deep water, that Crane's men weren't hunting a strongbox.

They were hunting whoever knew what was worth burning.

She drew the oilcloth packet from her shirt, and in the thin light leaking between the barrels, began to unfold it.
