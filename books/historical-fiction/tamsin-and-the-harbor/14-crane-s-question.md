## 14. Crane's Question

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, before the counting house had properly woken, before the clerks had finished their tea and the ink was still cold in its wells.

A boy in Meridian gray stood at the foot of the stairs to my loft with a folded card in his hand, and he would not give it to Fenwick or to the porter or to anyone but me. I took it and read it standing there on the landing while the harbor fog pressed against the windows like something trying to look over my shoulder.

*Miss Pemberton. Supervisor Crane requests your presence in his office at eight o'clock precisely.*

Eight o'clock precisely. Not *at your convenience*. Not *when you've finished the Corvane accounts*. Crane had never once summoned me by name in six years of service; I had been *the girl*, *you there*, *Pemberton* only when he wanted something mended quickly. Now my whole name, spelled out in a secretary's careful copperplate, as though I had become a document worth filing.

I dressed slowly. I braided my hair the way I did for days when I needed my hands to be steady — tight, plain, nothing loose to fidget with. In the glass above the washstand I looked at myself and practiced the face: pleasant, blank, slightly dull. The face of a girl who mends paperwork and reads nothing. It had kept me alive since I was nine years old. I put it on like an apron.

The strongbox sat under the floorboard three feet from where I stood, wrapped in oilcloth, still smelling faintly of cellar water and char. Twenty-two original dive contracts inside, signed and sealed, going back thirty years. Names of dead men. Numbers that did not match the numbers Meridian showed the world. I had slept one night with it beneath me like a stone, and I had dreamed of drowning, and I had woken knowing that everything had changed — that I was no longer invisible, because a thing hidden is a thing that weighs, and weight leaves tracks.

I stepped off the landing and went down into the fog.

* * *

Crane's office occupied the corner of the third floor where the light was best, which told you everything about how Meridian ranked its people. The supervisors got the sun; the divers got the water; the clerks got whatever leaked through the middle. His windows looked out over the counting floor and past it to the harbor, so that whoever sat behind the desk could watch the whole machine of the company turning below him — the carts, the ledgers, the small bent figures carrying paper from room to room.

I had been in this office twice before. Once, at nine years old, to be measured and entered into the indenture book. Once, at fourteen, to be praised for neatness. Both times I had kept my eyes on the floor and my answers short.

This time Crane was not behind his desk. He was standing at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the harbor, and he let me stand just inside the door for a full minute before he turned. He was a spare man, gray at the temples, with the kind of face that seemed assembled for patience — thin lips, pale eyes, no hurry anywhere in it. Men like Crane did not shout. Shouting was for people who feared they were losing. Crane had spent thirty years making other people afraid, and he wore it the way my father's generation wore calluses.

"Pemberton," he said. "Sit."

There was one chair. I took it. Behind me, softly, the door clicked shut — the secretary withdrawing, or perhaps not withdrawing. I could not tell if we were alone, and I suspected that was deliberate.

"You've been with us nine years," Crane said, settling against the front edge of his desk rather than behind it, which somehow made it worse. It was the posture of a man talking to a friend. "Entered at nine. Father lost at sea — the *Kestrel* incident, 1884. No mother on record. No kin claiming interest." He recited it flatly, without looking at any paper, and I understood that he knew my file the way I knew the debt ledger: by heart, by need. "You've been useful. Quiet. Accurate. I want you to know that has been noticed."

"Thank you, sir." My voice came out level. Small miracle.

"The annex fire." He said it the way another man might say *the weather*. "Terrible business. Old wiring, the fire captain thinks. Or maybe a lamp left burning. These things happen in buildings that have been neglected." A pause, delicate as a razor laid flat. "Of course, the timing is inconvenient. The merger signing is in eleven days, and the Corvane people will want certain historical materials available for review. Materials that are now, regrettably, ash."

"I heard about the fire this morning, sir," I said. "It's a great loss."

"Is it." He turned fully then and looked at me, and his eyes were the color of water over sand — you thought you could see the bottom, and then you couldn't. "You tell me, Pemberton. You're down in the records more than anyone. What did we lose?"

"Ledgers," I said. "Payroll books. Correspondence files. The dive contracts, I'd suppose." I let my shoulders rise and fall, a girl shrugging at the scale of a misfortune too big for her. "Everything, really."

"Mm." He crossed to the desk, opened a drawer, and took out a single sheet of paper, which he laid face-down beside him like a card he wasn't ready to play. "Here's what interests me. The fire burned hot in the upper floors. Paper, shelving, dry timber — all of it gone to nothing. But the cellar flooded. Hoses, mostly, and the tide coming in through the broken coal chute. And do you know what happens to paper in a flooded cellar, Pemberton?"

"It's ruined, sir."

"Some of it." He smiled without warmth. "But a strongbox isn't paper. A strongbox sits in a flooded cellar being wet on the outside and perfectly dry on the inside, waiting for someone with a crowbar and a wheelbarrow and more nerve than sense. And here is the curious thing—" he tapped the face-down sheet once, lightly, "—when my men reached the cellar at four o'clock this morning, the strongbox was gone. Not burned. Not crushed. *Gone.* Along with the crowbar marks on its hasp and the drag-marks in the silt leading toward the delivery door."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us the counting house was waking — a cart rattling on the cobbles, the first clerk calling out a figure — and all of it sounded impossibly far away, as though I were already underwater.

"That's terrible, sir," I said.

"It is." He picked up the sheet and held it, still face-down, considering. "Now. I'm a fair man, Pemberton. I've watched you work for nine years and I don't believe you have more nerve than sense. I believe you have exactly enough sense to stay out of rooms like this one. So I'm going to assume the box walked out of that cellar on its own, and I'm going to offer you a chance to help me find it."

He let that sit. Outside, a gull screamed once over the harbor and was silent.

"If it comes back," Crane said, "before the signing — quietly, no fuss, no questions asked — then this conversation never happened. You go back to your loft and your mending, and in eleven days I'll sign your manumission papers myself. Freedom, Pemberton. Full and clear. Nine years early." He said the word *freedom* gently, almost tenderly, the way a fisherman says *bait*. "And more than freedom. There's a position opening after the merger. Records supervisor. Your own office — a smaller window than mine, but a window. Wages. A name in the company book not as property but as staff. A girl who started at nine years old, ending as an officer of Meridian. That's a story they'd print."

My heart was hammering so hard I was sure he could hear it. I made my face stay soft and grateful and slow. "That's very generous, sir."

"It's practical." He straightened, and the tenderness drained out of him like water out of a cracked jar, leaving only the thing underneath. "Because the alternative, Pemberton, is this." He finally turned the sheet over and slid it across the desk toward me, and I made myself look down at it.

An affidavit. Already drafted. *Statement of Supervisor E. Crane regarding the theft of company property from the Meridian record annex on the night of—* The date was filled in. The description of the strongbox was filled in. The contents were itemized in a secretary's hand: *twenty-two original dive contracts, 1861–1889.* And at the bottom, where a name should go, a blank line with the words *witness for the prosecution* printed neatly beneath it.

They hadn't caught me. They didn't need to. The paper existed whether I had done it or not, and paper, in Greywater, in Meridian, was destiny. I had learned that at nine years old, when a page in a book had made me property. One page had bound me. Another could hang Callum Reeve and bury me in the women's prison on Marsh Island until my indenture was sold to whoever paid the fines.

"Theft of company assets," Crane said mildly, "by an indentured ward of the company. Do you know what happens to an indentured thief? She doesn't go to prison, Pemberton. Prisoners have rights. She gets *sold*. Her contract transfers to the penal contractor, and the penal contractor hires her out to the cannery lines until the debt's worked off. The Corvane people are buying Meridian's paper, not its scandals. I'd have your account settled and forgotten inside a month." He leaned forward, hands flat on the desk, and for the first time his voice dropped into something almost kind, which was the most frightening register he owned. "I don't want that. Genuinely. You're the best clerk this floor has ever produced, and I say that knowing every clerk who's come through it. Which is why I'm asking you plainly, woman to supervisor, no games: *where is the box?*"

Every instinct I had honed across nine years said: lower your eyes. Say you don't know. Be small, be dull, be the girl who mends nets of paperwork and reads nothing.

But he had said *woman to supervisor*. He had stopped pretending I was a child, and some traitorous part of me rose up glad of it even now, even here, with the trap sprung and shining.

"I don't know anything about a strongbox, sir," I said. And it was even true, in the way that mattered — I knew about a strongbox, but I did not know, this morning, sitting in this chair, exactly where Callum had taken it in the dark hours before dawn, and I prayed to a father who might or might not be listening that Callum had been cleverer than both of us.

Crane studied me for a long moment. Then he sighed, and drew the affidavit back, and folded it once along its crease, unhurried, a man putting away a tool he expected to use again soon.

"Eleven days," he said. "The box returns before the signing, and none of this exists. It doesn't return, and this goes to the magistrates with your name on the witness line — or, if you prefer, on the other line. And Pemberton—" I had reached the door; his voice followed me like a hand on my shoulder. "You'll notice I haven't mentioned your friends. The widow Ostrow. The old smuggler. The diver's partner who asks too many questions at the Salt House. I haven't mentioned them because their names are not yet on any paper of mine." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man showing you the empty hook before he baited it. "Paper is patient. So am I. Good morning."

* * *

I made it down two flights before my legs informed me they were done pretending. I stood in the stairwell between the second floor and the first, in the smell of dust and lamp oil, with one hand flat against the wall, and I breathed.

*Their names are not yet on any paper of mine.*

Not yet. He had given me eleven days, but he had also told me, in the same breath, that everyone I loved was already halfway into his ledger — Maren, Brannick, Callum, and God knew who else, Fenwick, the widows at Maren's table, every soul who had ever spoken to me twice. Crane did not need the strongbox to destroy us. He needed only time and ink, and he had both.

And worse — colder, sinking through me like the cellar water — the thought I could not shake: *someone told him about the crowbar marks within hours.* Someone had walked the cellar at dawn, or seen the drag-marks, or counted heads and found one missing from the night watch. The fire had not been accident enough for Crane; he had known what to look for before the ashes cooled. Which meant either Crane was smarter than I feared, or someone close to the fire had talked.

I thought of Fenwick's face yesterday, pale and eager, pressing a cup of tea on me in the records room, asking gentle questions about where I'd been, whether I was well, whether I was sleeping. Fenwick, who had covered my absences, who had laughed at my jokes, who had sworn on his mother's grave he was with me. Fenwick, whose debts I happened to know were three quarters' deep with the company store.

Anyone could be bought. That was the whole architecture of Meridian — not cruelty, not even greed, exactly, but a machine that priced everything, including loyalty, including love, including a girl's freedom, and always found the number at which a person would sell. Crane had just named mine: a window office and a signature. He assumed I had one. He assumed everyone did.

I climbed back up to my loft on legs that shook, locked the door, and knelt and pulled up the floorboard. The oilcloth bundle lay where we'd left it, damp-cornered, heavy with other people's lives. I did not open it. I put my hand flat on it instead, the way you'd check a sleeping child for breath.

Eleven days. The box could not come back — those twenty-two pages were the only true record left on earth of what Meridian had done, and returning them meant feeding them to the same fire that had eaten the rest. But the box could not stay hidden either, because hidden things get found, and found things get owned, and owned things get destroyed.

Which left one place for the truth to live, and it was not a strongbox, and it was not a cellar, and it was not even paper.

It was people. It was Maren's kitchen and Brannick's memory and the widows' tally-marked forearms and the songs the divers sang to count their strokes. It was the harbor itself, which had been keeping these accounts all along, in the only archive Crane could never burn.

I had eleven days to move everything I loved out of his reach and into theirs.

Downstairs, faintly, the counting-house bell rang the hour, and beneath it — softer, from the direction of the water — I could hear the harbor bells answering, the way they always did, the free bells and the company bells ringing out of step, out of time, two cities keeping two different kinds of truth. I blew out the lamp, though it was morning, and began to plan.
