## 16. The Widows' Council

The tide was going out when I climbed the stairs to Maren Ostrow's boarding house, and it seemed to me that half of Greywater was climbing with me.

They came up from the harbor in ones and twos at first, then in strings—a fishwife with a basket on her hip, two riggers still smelling of tar, an old pilot whose face had gone the color and texture of his own charts. By the time I reached Maren's door, the street below looked like a procession. Nobody had called them. That was the thing I kept turning over as I knocked: nobody had called them, and yet they came, because word travels through Greywater faster than any letter can carry it, moving mouth to mouth along the quay the way water moves through sand—silently, invisibly, and finding every low place.

Maren opened the door before my knuckles landed a second time.

"You brought half the harbor," she said. It wasn't quite an accusation. Her eyes went past me to the street, counting, the way she counted everything—loaves, lodgers, losses.

"They weren't mine to bring."

"No," she agreed, stepping back to let me in. "But they're here now, so we'd best make room."

* * *

We made room by pushing the long table against the wall and setting out every chair Maren owned, which was not nearly enough. People stood along the walls, sat on the stairs, perched on the rail of the bed in the corner where a widow named Sefka had slept for eleven years since her husband's lungs filled with harbor water one dive too many. The room above the water filled the way a hull fills—not all at once but steadily, inevitably—and by full dark there were more than forty souls crammed into a space meant for twelve, and the lamplight couldn't reach the far corners, and nobody seemed to mind.

Callum stood by the window with his arms crossed, saying nothing, watching the door the way he always watched doors. Elias Voss sat apart from everyone else on a stool near the hearth, his company-man's coat brushed and buttoned and entirely wrong for the room, holding his hat in his lap like a man at a funeral who isn't sure he's welcome. He wasn't. Not yet. But he'd come when I sent word, and he'd come alone, and that counted for something in a room like this.

Old Brannick arrived last, which was fitting, because Brannick had spent forty years arriving places last—after the inspectors, after the patrols, after anyone who might be asked later what he'd been doing. He filled the doorway for a moment, took the measure of the room with one sweep of those pale smuggler's eyes, and grunted.

"More witnesses than I expected," he said. "Worse for me if I lie, then." And he folded himself onto the floor by the fire like a man settling in for a long night's work, which is exactly what it was.

I stood where the table had been, with the lamp behind me, and looked out at their faces. Divers' widows. Sons of drowned men. The fishwife whose brother's name was struck through twice on a page I had copied until my fingers cramped. They were waiting. That was the terrible part—the patience of it. These were people who had spent years being told to wait. Wait for the boats. Wait for the paymaster. Wait for word that didn't come.

"I'll not make speeches," I said. "You've heard speeches from Meridian men your whole lives and they never fed anyone. I'll say what I know, and then we decide—together—what we do with it."

And I told them. All of it. The ledger entries altered in Crane's hand. The fatality list with names struck through, and the pattern underneath it like a bone under skin: the divers who died were the divers whose salvage had outgrown their debts. The Company did not lose those men to the sea. The sea merely finished what the arithmetic began. I told them about Old Brannick's testimony—the cargoes that came ashore heavier than the manifests allowed, the pearls that entered Meridian's books without ever having left a diver's tally. I told them about the strongbox from the flooded cellar, the original contracts, water-swollen but legible, hidden now in a place only three people knew.

When I finished, the silence was the kind that has weight. Somewhere below us a gull complained, and the house timbers ticked as they cooled, and forty-odd people breathed.

Then Sefka spoke from the corner bed, her voice dry as old rope.

"My Tam died owing the Company four pounds, eleven shillings," she said. "Four pounds, eleven. He dove eleven years." She laughed, short and bitter. "Eleven years, and they kept him poor enough that four pounds could hold him down like ballast. You're telling me that wasn't accident."

"It was design," I said.

"Aye." She nodded slowly. "I always knew it in my body. My head believed the papers. My body knew better."

An old rigger near the wall—Halvard, I learned his name later—cleared his throat. "Say we believe all this. Say it's true as scripture. What do you want from us? Words? Words are cheap. The Company owns the paper the words would go on."

"That," said Elias Voss, "is why I'm here."

Every head turned. He stood, and I will grant him this: his hands did not shake, though I saw him grip his hat hard enough to bend the brim.

"My name is Elias Voss. Until last month I was writing Meridian's official history. A hundred years of the Company, set down in gilt letters, to be read aloud at the merger signing and then locked away where no one could check it." He paused. "I found pages razored out of the ledgers. I asked about them. I was told, very politely, that curiosity is a disease in that building, and that historians who catch it don't stay historians long. Then I was ordered to help prepare the archives for burning. Not archiving—burning. Ahead of schedule. Before anyone could read them."

He reached into his coat and drew out a single sheet, creased and soft with handling, and held it up. Even at a distance I knew it. The fatality list. His page, the one he'd pocketed while the world's paper burned around him.

"There will be a hearing before the merger is signed," he said. "Public. Required by the charter. The Company expects it to be a formality—two hours, some signatures, champagne after." His voice hardened. "It doesn't have to be. The civic record belongs to the city, not to Meridian. Anything sworn before the magistrate enters that record and cannot be razored out, cannot be burned, cannot be bought back. If you testify—if even a handful of you stand up and speak—it goes into the permanent record of Greywater. Forever."

"Forever," repeated Halvard, tasting the word. "And what happens to us tomorrow morning, after forever? Crane's men count heads. They know faces. Some of us have families. Some of us owe money." He spread his hands. "Courage is dear, Mr. Voss, when you're paid in nothing."

Nobody answered him right away, because there was no answer yet, and because the question deserved its silence.

* * *

It was Brannick who broke it, in the end, the way old men break silences—unexpectedly and without apology.

"I've run contraband past Meridian inspectors since before most of you could walk," he said, from his spot by the fire. "Ran silk, ran brandy, ran Chinese tea and French lace, ran anything with a markup. Never once felt shame over it, because the Company I robbed was robbing the whole harbor besides." He levered himself upright, joints cracking like ship's rope. "But what this girl's found—that's different. That's not theft. That's murder done slow, with a ledger instead of a knife, and I've carried enough sins without adding silence to the pile."

He looked around the room. "I'll testify. On one condition. When I stand before that magistrate and name names, I want the whole harbor standing behind me. Not hiding. Standing. Because a witness alone is a target, but forty witnesses are a wall, and even Crane doesn't shoot into a wall of his own townsmen."

Sefka got off the bed.

That was how it started—one widow rising, and then another, and then Halvard pushing off the wall, and the fishwife setting down her basket with a thump like a keel coming home. Nobody made speeches about courage. They simply stood, one after another, the way they'd stood on the quay a hundred times to watch the boats go out, knowing the sea takes and takes, going anyway because the going was theirs.

"I buried my man with lies in his ears," said Sefka. "Told him on his deathbed his tally would clear, that the boys would be free. Lies I half-believed because Meridian wrote them. I'll not tell them again. I'll testify."

"For Anders," said the fishwife quietly. "For Anders, then. For Anders, too," said a young man I didn't know. It moved down the room like fire through dry grass, each voice adding a name, and soon the small room was full of the drowned—names spoken aloud, first time in years for some of them, filling the space above the water the way their owners once filled the water itself. Wendell Pemberton. Tam Ostrow-Sefka. Anders. Jorunn. Little Piet, who was sixteen. I stood among them with my father's name in my own mouth and found, to my astonishment, that I was not grieving alone. Every name came back multiplied.

When the naming quieted, Maren Ostrow rose from her place by the stove. She hadn't spoken all evening; she'd only worked, laying out bread and salt fish and small beer with the economy of a woman feeding an army on a boarding house budget. Now she wiped her hands on her apron and looked at me across the crowded room.

"The vote, then," she said. "All in favor of testifying at the merger hearing, publicly, on the record—stand."

Everyone stood.

Even Callum, by the window—even him, unfolding from the shadows where he'd kept himself all evening, rising to his feet without a word, catching my eye across the room with something in his face I couldn't read and didn't dare name in front of forty people.

"All opposed?" Maren asked.

Silence. The gull outside, the ticking timbers, forty hearts.

"Carried," said Maren, and sat down, and that was that.

Elias remained standing. He looked out at the room—at the widows and riggers and smugglers, at the hat crushed in his hands—and when he spoke again his voice had changed. Less the historian, more the man.

"I'll enter every statement into the civic record myself," he said. "Not the company archive. The city's. And if they dismiss me for it—if they take my position, my wages, my name from their books—" he almost smiled, "—then I'll finally have a name worth keeping."

Brannick snorted. "Poetry. From a Company man."

"Former Company man," said Elias. "As of tonight."

* * *

The room emptied slowly after that, the way harbors empty at slack tide—unhurried, reluctant, everyone lingering over farewells because the next days would not be safe ones. Maren pressed bread into hands. Sefka embraced strangers. Halvard paused beside me at the door.

"My grandfather helped lay Meridian's first warehouse stones," he said. "Thought he was building for the town. Maybe it's not too late for it to be true."

Last of all, when only Maren and Callum and I remained, Callum came to stand before me. Up close, in lamplight, he looked older than he had on the pier—tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix.

"Your father would have hated crowds," he said. "Quiet man, your da. Did his work, kept his counts, loved one person past all sense." His jaw worked. "But he'd have stood in that room tonight. For you."

"Callum—"

"He'd have stood," he repeated, gently, refusing whatever I'd been about to ask, "and so did you. Get some sleep, Tamsin. Hearing's in three days."

He went out into the dark, and I heard Maren bolt the door behind him, and then it was just the two of us and the cooling stove and the smell of bread and salt.

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Then she did a strange thing: she took down a framed sampler from above the mantel—stitched flowers, faded roses, the kind of thing every boarding house keeps and no one looks at—and set it face-down on the table. Behind it, on the bare plaster, hung a small iron nail.

"Husband put that there the day we married," she said. "For a picture we never got money enough to frame." She touched the nail once, lightly, and turned back to me. "Room above the water's yours as long as you need it. That's not charity and it's not debt. That's family, girl, and family's the one thing Meridian never figured how to put in a ledger."

I went up the narrow stair to the little room with the sea in its walls, and I lay down, and I did not sleep. Three days. In three days I would stand before a magistrate and read my father's letter aloud, and Crane would be sitting somewhere in that room doing sums in his head, deciding what I was worth dead or ruined or free.

Near dawn I heard footsteps on the quay below, many footsteps, unhurried, and through the salt-fogged window I watched a line of lanterns move along the waterfront toward the counting house—early, deliberate, wrong for that hour. Men with a purpose, walking in step.

Crane's men. And they were heading, every lantern of them, toward Maren's door.
