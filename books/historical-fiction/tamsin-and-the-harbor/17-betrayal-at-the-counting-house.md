## 17. Betrayal at the Counting House

The morning it all came apart began like any other, and that was the cruelest part of it.

Tamsin rose before the gulls, as she had for eleven years, and stood at the window of her narrow room above Maren Ostrow's boarding house, watching the fog lift off Greywater Harbor in slow gray ribbons. Somewhere out on the water, the dive boats were already moving, their lamps swinging like low stars. She could name each one by the rhythm of its wake. The *Kestrel* rolled heavy to port. The *Sable Ann* cut clean. The *Providence*, old and patched, moved like a tired man who had learned to carry his burdens without complaint.

Her father had dived from the *Providence*. She had learned that only weeks ago, and still the knowledge sat in her chest like a coal she could not put down.

Below her, Maren's kitchen was already breathing out the smell of bread and woodsmoke. Three widows had arrived the night before and slept in the back rooms, and their voices drifted up through the floorboards—low, steady, the voices of women rehearsing. In two days, the merger hearing would convene in the civic hall, and they would stand before the commissioners and say aloud what Meridian had paid them to bury: the rigged debts, the falsified dive logs, the men sent down into cold water to fill company coffers and struck from the record when they drowned.

The strongbox sat beneath Tamsin's bed. She had checked it at midnight and again before dawn, as she did every night and every morning—the original dive contracts, water-stained but legible, Wendell Pemberton's signature among them, the whole drowned architecture of the Company's lie pressed flat in iron.

She checked it once more now. It was there. Cold, heavy, certain.

She should have wondered, later, why she did not think to wonder about the other thing. About who else knew where it slept.

* * *

Fenwick was at his desk when she arrived at the counting house, as he always was, his collar crisp, his ledgers ruled in lines so straight they looked printed. He was twenty-four, quick with figures, and he had been the first person inside Meridian's walls to treat her as more than furniture. He had covered for her absences. He had taught her the filing codes for the old annex. Three weeks ago, when she had finally shown him the fatality list with its struck-through names, he had gone pale and said, *God help us, Tamsin. This has to come out.*

She had believed him. That was the fact she would have to carry for the rest of her life: she had believed him, because she had wanted a friend inside the walls, and wanting had made her careless.

"Morning," he said, not looking up. His pen moved. His inkwell was full. Everything about him was exactly as it always was, and yet some animal part of her, some diver's instinct inherited from a man she barely remembered, registered that the water had gone wrong before her mind knew why.

"Morning," she said. "The hearing's in two days. Maren's expecting the commissioners' clerk at noon to—"

"I heard." Still not looking up. "You should be careful, Tamsin. Talk like that travels."

"It has to travel. That's the entire point of it."

"Yes," Fenwick said, and turned a page. "I suppose it does."

She stood there a moment, waiting for something—a flicker of the eye, a hesitation in the pen. Nothing came. She walked to her own desk in the corner of the clerks' room, the small scarred desk where she had spent a decade being invisible, and began the morning's copying, and the whole time her hands were steady and her mind was screaming.

Supervisor Crane's office door was shut. That was not unusual. What was unusual was the pair of boots she could see beneath the door, planted wide and still, and the fact that the boots had been there when she arrived and were there an hour later and did not shift, as though whoever wore them had nothing else in the world to do but wait.

At half past ten, the door opened.

"Tamsin Pemberton." Crane's voice, mild as milk. "A word."

* * *

The office smelled of ink and bay rum and, faintly, of smoke—the ghost of the annex fire that had gutted the record house weeks ahead of its scheduled burning, a fire everyone in Greywater understood and no one named. Crane sat behind his desk with his hands folded, a man of neat gray habits, and beside the window stood a stranger in a harbor warden's coat with the Company's brass pinned at the collar.

"Miss Pemberton," Crane said. "Do you know Mr. Halloway? Company constable."

"I didn't know the Company kept constables."

"The Company keeps whatever the harbor requires." Crane smiled the way a ledger balanced—satisfying and entirely without warmth. "We've had a theft, Miss Pemberton. From the annex cellar. A strongbox of original dive contracts, salvaged from the flood water by persons unknown on the night of the fire. You wouldn't know anything about that."

The room was very quiet. Through the window she could see the harbor, flat and silver, the *Providence* at her moorings, the whole bright ordinary world going on as if nothing were ending.

"I mend paperwork," Tamsin said. "I don't know anything about strongboxes."

"No," Crane agreed. "You mend paperwork beautifully. That's what made it so strange, you see—when Mr. Fenwick came to me yesterday evening and told me what you'd been doing with your evenings."

The floor did not drop out this time. She had learned that much since Callum's letters. The floor simply went on, and she stood on it, and made her voice stay level, because that was the one skill eleven years of invisibility had perfected.

"What did Fenwick tell you?"

"That you'd been collecting testimony from divers' widows. That you had documents. That there was a plan to enter them into the record at the merger hearing." Crane unfolded his hands and refolded them. "He was quite thorough. He asked, in exchange, for his freedom papers. I granted them. He signed his loyalty oath an hour ago and left on the morning packet for Corvess. You'll not be seeing him again."

She had imagined, in the small hours, many ways it might go wrong. Crane catching her in the loft. The strongbox found. Callum taken. But she had never imagined the specific, ordinary shape of it: a young man at a desk, a pen moving in straight lines, a bargain signed in the same ink he used for columns of figures. Fenwick had covered for her for weeks, and then he had sold her for the same thing the Company had always sold—the promise that a person could buy their way out of the water.

"You gave him papers," she said. "The Company doesn't give papers. The Company sells them."

"The Company is capable of generosity at moments of institutional transition." Crane's eyes were pale and patient. "Here is my generosity, Miss Pemberton. Return the strongbox by tomorrow noon, and this conversation becomes a clerical error. Keep it, and Mr. Halloway will charge you with theft of company property, and no hearing commissioner in this city will hear the testimony of a confessed thief. Your widows will testify to empty chairs. Do you understand the arithmetic?"

She understood it perfectly. It was Meridian arithmetic, the arithmetic her whole life had been computed in: everything weighed, everything owed, and the scales always held in the same gray hands.

"And if I return it?"

"Then you will have shown good sense, and I will forget that a clerk named Pemberton ever learned to read."

* * *

She walked out of the counting house with her spine straight and her mind moving fast and cold, and she had almost reached the pier when she saw them.

Two of Halloway's men, flanking a third figure at the foot of the harbor steps. The third figure was tall, weathered, wearing the salt-bleached coat she had first seen in the counting house doorway the day her world had cracked open.

Callum Reeve did not struggle. That was the thing she would remember—how still he stood, hands loose at his sides, while the constables read the charge. *Smuggling. Conspiracy. Old warrants, dredged up from twenty years of company files.* They had gone digging through whatever the fire hadn't eaten, and they had found his name, or enough of it.

His eyes found her across the distance. He did not call out. He did not make a scene. He looked at her the way a diver looks up from the bottom—measuring the distance to the light, and knowing it is too far, and going calm anyway.

Then one of the men took his arm, and he turned, and they walked him up the harbor road toward the Company lockup, and the crowd on the pier parted around them the way water parts around a hull, closing again behind, leaving no trace.

Tamsin stood at the rail and let the harbor wind push against her, and beneath the grief and the fury, quieter and more dangerous, another voice began to speak.

*Run.*

It would be easy. She had the strongbox. She knew the smugglers' paths along the north bluffs—Brannick had drawn them for her, laughing, on the back of a tally sheet. She could be in Corvess by Friday and gone past any border Meridian's brass could reach. Take the contracts. Bury them. Keep them safe until the world changed, if it ever changed.

*Run, and the widows testify alone. Run, and Fenwick's betrayal wins. Run, and you spend the rest of your life the way you spent the first eleven years—useful, unseen, belonging to no one, carrying the truth like a stone in a bag with no bottom.*

The *Providence* rocked at her moorings. The gulls argued over the fish market. Somewhere in the Company lockup, Callum Reeve sat in a cell because he had kept a promise to a drowned man, and somewhere in Maren's kitchen, three widows were rehearsing their dead husbands' names so they would not stumble on them in front of the commissioners.

Tamsin Pemberton turned from the rail.

She was not going to run. She was going to do something far more frightening.

She was going to walk back to Maren's, take the strongbox from under her bed, and figure out how to testify tomorrow without a single piece of paper—because if Meridian wanted a war between their archive and her memory, then she would show them what the harbor kept in its bones.

Behind her, at the counting house window, Supervisor Crane watched her go, and reached for his pen to write a letter that would arrive too late to matter.
