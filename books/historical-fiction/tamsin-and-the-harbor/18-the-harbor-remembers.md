## 18. The Harbor Remembers

The walk from the counting house to Maren's took eleven minutes if you didn't dawdle. Tamsin had counted them a thousand times, in every weather, and tonight she counted them again because counting was what she did when her hands wanted to shake. Eleven minutes of wet cobblestone, of lamplight bleeding gold across the harbor fog, of gulls arguing over something dead on the pilings. Eleven minutes in which she had to become someone who could do what she'd just promised herself she would do.

She had no paper. That was the arithmetic of it, stripped clean: no strongbox, no contracts, no ledger pages with their damning columns of names and numbers. Crane had taken all of it at dawn—four men and a warrant signed by a magistrate whose house Meridian had built—and Callum was sitting in a cell beneath the customs office on charges old enough to have gray in them. Everything she had gathered through a year of careful, invisible work now sat in an evidence room that answered to Supervisor Crane.

Everything except what lived in other people.

She climbed Maren's steps and let herself in without knocking, because Maren Ostrow's boarding house had stopped requiring Tamsin to knock somewhere around the third week she'd started coming, and neither of them had ever remarked upon it.

The kitchen smelled of barley soup and lamp oil. Maren sat at the head of the table with her mending in her lap—not working it, just holding it, the way a general holds a map of a country already lost. Fenwick's betrayal had reached her hours before Tamsin had; word traveled through Greywater faster than any Company dispatch, moving along the same channels as gossip about storms and births and which inspector took bribes. The widows were there too—Sela Vane with her knuckles wrapped around a cup of chicory she wasn't drinking, old Pettra Hallow with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral, young Ilsa Renn still in her dockwork apron because grief hadn't given her time to change out of it.

They looked up when Tamsin came in, and none of them asked how she was, because they could all see.

"It's gone," Tamsin said. She didn't dress it. "The strongbox. Crane's men took it this morning, before I could move it. And Callum—" Her voice caught on his name and she hated it, hated the crack, pushed through. "Callum's in custody. Old charges. Smuggling."

Silence, except for the harbor breathing against the seawall outside.

Then Sela Vane set down her untouched cup with a click that sounded, in that quiet, like a lock turning.

"So," she said. "It comes down to tomorrow with nothing but our mouths."

"That's not nothing," said Pettra Hallow, and her voice, thin as it was, had iron under it. "My mother told me that once about my father's name, when the Company clerks wrote him down as *deceased, indebted* and she knew he'd died owning nothing but the water in his lungs. She said: they can take the paper, girl, but paper isn't where a thing is kept." The old widow looked around the table. "Where is a thing kept?"

"Here," Maren said quietly. She touched her own breastbone.

"In the arms," said Ilsa, flexing fingers scarred from hauling lines.

"In the songs," said Sela.

Tamsin stood at the edge of the table and felt something shift in her chest—the cold dread that had carried her up Maren's steps beginning, slowly, to warm into something else. Something with teeth.

"I've been thinking like a clerk," she said. "All year, that's been my mistake when it mattered most. I thought the war was over documents. Contracts, ledgers, signatures. I thought whoever held the paper held the truth. But Meridian built its whole fortune on controlling paper—who could read it, who could write it, who could afford to keep it. They burned the annex. They razored pages out of archives. They bought silence with freedom papers and buried men in debt entries. Every crime they committed, they committed *on paper*, because paper was theirs."

"And ours?" Maren asked, though Tamsin could see she already knew.

"Our whole lives," Tamsin said. "That's what they never understood. They think the harbor forgets. They think because we couldn't write things down, we don't keep them. But we've been keeping records for fifty years, gentlemen"—she heard herself say *gentlemen* with a bitterness that startled even her—"fifty years of records, in paybook stubs hidden in mattress ticking. In tally marks cut into doorframes. In songs sung at funerals that nobody at Meridian ever bothered to listen to closely enough to count the verses."

Pettra Hallow began, very softly, to smile.

* * *

They worked until two in the morning, and it was the strangest inventory Tamsin had ever conducted: a census of memory, taken by lamplight in a boarding-house kitchen while the fog pressed white against the windows.

Maren went first. She fetched a tin box from under a floorboard in the back bedroom—the loose board, third from the wall, that every tenant learned within a week—and poured out onto the table a small drift of paper scraps. Paybook stubs, dozens of them, each one a rectangle of flimsy company stock no bigger than a playing card. *W. Ostrow, diver, second-class. Salvage credited: 4 lb 2 oz pearl-shell. Debt balance adjusted.* One stub for every fortnight of her husband Anders's last three years diving.

"They gave these out like they were worthless," Maren said, spreading them flat with both hands. "Little slips you were meant to throw away. But Anders kept every one, because he said someday somebody would want to count. He didn't know who or why. He just said a man should be able to prove what he gave." She looked up at Tamsin. "Forty-one stubs. You know what forty-one fortnights of salvage comes to?"

"No," Tamsin admitted.

"More than his debt. More than double his debt. And he died owing them everything anyway." Maren's voice didn't break. It had passed breaking years ago and come out the far side hard and level as a keel. "You take those to your hearing."

Sela Vane had no stubs—her man, Joss, had drowned in his fourth season, before she'd known to keep anything—but she had the songs. She made them all be quiet, even though there was only the sound of the tide to quiet, and then she sang.

It was a diving song, one Tamsin had heard a hundred times drifting out of taverns and off workboats, so common it was practically the harbor's own anthem. *Down, boys, down, where the cold water's crown—* But Sela sang it slow, and she counted under her breath as she sang, and when she finished she explained what Tamsin had never known: the song had verses for every dive of a season. Real dives, real counts, encoded in the rhythm. The divers had built the ledger into the melody so that no clerk could ever lose track of what they were owed.

"Joss's boat sang thirty-one verses the spring he died," Sela said. "Meridian's payroll recorded nineteen. Ask anyone who was in the Anchor & Chain that season. Ask them to sing it. They'll sing all thirty-one."

Old Pettra rolled back her sleeve.

Tamsin had seen the marks before—everyone in the harbor had seen old divers' forearms, and children assumed they were some kind of sailor's decoration—but she had never *read* them. Now Pettra held her arm under the lamp, and Tamsin saw it: rows of fine scars and inked dots running from wrist to elbow, grouped in fives, the groups themselves grouped in tens.

"Tally," Pettra said simply. "We kept count on ourselves, because ourselves was the one thing they couldn't confiscate. Every dive, one mark. Every salvage weigh-in witnessed by another diver, a dot beside it." She turned her arm slowly. "This is Torv Hallow's arm, girl. He died in '81 and I have been wearing his accounts ever since."

Tamsin reached out and touched the marks—gently, the way you touch scripture—and Pettra let her.

And then Ilsa Renn, who was the youngest and had thought she had nothing to offer, said in a small voice: "I have letters. Not like Mr. Reeve's letters. Just—Torv used to write to my grandmother, they were cousins, and she saved them because she saved everything. They're in Norwegian, mostly, but there are figures in them. Dive depths. Bottom times." She swallowed. "I never thought they mattered. They're just an old woman's correspondence."

"Bring them," Tamsin said. "Tomorrow, everything matters."

* * *

Word went out the way word always went out in Greywater: not by letter, not by crier, but by the harbor itself. Maren sent her boarders to the fish market at dawn. Sela sang at the Anchor & Chain until the tavern understood. Pettra sat on her stoop and told everyone who passed, which was everyone, because Pettra's stoop was on the only dry way to the docks.

By noon, Tamsin, walking to the civic hall to see the chamber where the hearing would be held—to stand in it alone, to learn its echoes—was stopped four times.

A netmender named Gulla caught her arm near the chandlery and pressed a folded scrap into her hand: the inside cover of a paybook, twenty years old, bearing her own father's signature. Wendell Pemberton, signing for a salvage credit dated March of the year he drowned. "Found it when I was re-cording a net chest," Gulla said. "Kept it because the handwriting was pretty. Go make it mean something."

A boy of maybe ten, one of the gull-thin children who baited lines for pennies, ran alongside her for half a block chanting a rhyme about the counting house—*Crane counts crooked, Crane counts sly, count the divers low and dry*—and when she looked at him he grinned with missing teeth and said, "Me da says sing it loud tomorrow."

An old oyster dredger, half-deaf, shouted his testimony at her right there on the public street, unprompted, at a volume that turned heads: "I SAW THE WEIGHMASTER SHORT THE SCALE AT SLIP NINE! FORTY YEARS AGO AND I SAW IT!" A woman selling cockles nodded along as if this were a normal Tuesday.

By evening, Tamsin understood that something had happened that she had not planned and could not control: the harbor had decided. She had spent a year gathering testimony like a smuggler moving contraband, parcel by secret parcel, and now the parcels were walking to the hearing on their own legs. Dozens of them. Perhaps more than dozens. She had gambled on memory, and memory had come back to her multiplied, like bread and fishes, like the tide returning what the sea borrows.

She went to see Callum that evening, because she could not not go.

The customs cell was damp and dim, and Callum Reeve sat on the cot looking older than he had a week ago, his broken-knuckled hands empty in his lap. When she told him—through the bars, quickly, in a low voice—about the stubs and the songs and the tattooed tallies and Gulla's scrap with her father's signature, he was quiet for a long moment.

"You're going to bury them with witnesses," he said finally. "No paper. Just people."

"People are the paper," Tamsin said. "We always were. That was the whole trick—they made us illiterate in their language so we'd never notice we were fluent in our own."

Callum smiled, and there was grief in it, and pride, and something that looked almost like peace. "Wendell would have—" He stopped. Started again. "He used to say the Company kept its books in ink because ink drowns. He kept his in men." His voice roughened. "Tell them tomorrow. All of them. Tell it like he'd have wanted it told—loud, and in order, and with the numbers exact."

"I will."

"Tamsin." He gripped the bars, and she gripped his hands through them, cold iron between their skin. "Whatever happens in that room—whatever Crane does—you walk in there belonging to something. Do you hear me? You spent nine years being useful and unseen. You're done with unseen. Walk in there like a Pemberton."

"I don't know how a Pemberton walks," she said, and her eyes were burning.

"Like your father swam," Callum said. "Straight down, into the dark, toward the thing worth having."

* * *

She barely slept. She lay in the narrow bed in Maren's house—Ilsa had insisted on giving up her own room, and there had been no refusing it—and listened to the harbor breathe, and rehearsed, and lost the thread, and rehearsed again. Somewhere past three in the morning she rose and lit the lamp and took out the day's harvest: Gulla's scrap, Maren's forty-one stubs, the notes she'd made of Sela's verses, the transcription of Pettra's forearm that she'd drawn freehand, line for line, mark for mark.

Not evidence. She knew what Crane's lawyers would call it—hearsay, sentiment, the mutterings of an aggrieved rabble. Paper could be dismissed. Paper had rules.

But a room full of people who stood up one after another after another—that had no rules. That was a flood.

At dawn she stood at Maren's window with her tea going cold and watched the fog lift off the harbor, and saw them coming.

They came up from the docks in twos and threes and tens. Divers with their rolling gait and their scarred forearms bare despite the chill. Widows in their good black, walking together in a silent bloc. Netmenders and caulkers and fishwives, oyster dredgers and line-baiters, the boy with the rhyme riding someone's shoulders, old deaf Brannick's people—Brannick himself absent, still weighing his protection, but his nephews present and standing straight. They converged on the civic hall from every street that led to the water, the way water finds water, and they filled the square in front of it, and they kept coming.

Maren appeared at Tamsin's shoulder with her shawl pinned and her chin set. "Well," she said. "Look at that. Fifty years of their bookkeeping, come to call."

Tamsin watched the crowd gather below the window—a crowd that had arrived unbidden, carrying paybook stubs in their pockets and dive-counts in their skin and thirty-one verses in their throats—and felt the fear in her chest transmute, at last, entirely, into something that could carry her.

"The archive burned," she said quietly, to Maren, to the window, to her father. "But the harbor remembers."

Below, the doors of the civic hall opened, and the bailiff stepped out to call the session, and the crowd moved forward like a tide answering the moon—and Tamsin Pemberton picked up her father's signature, squared her shoulders, and went down to meet it.
