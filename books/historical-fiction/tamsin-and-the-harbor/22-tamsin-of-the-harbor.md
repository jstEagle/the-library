## 22. Tamsin of the Harbor

The bell over the archive door had been Elias's idea, salvaged from a wrecked pilot boat and polished until it sang. Tamsin heard it now, on the first bright morning of spring, and did not look up from her ledger.

"Door's open," she called. "Wipe your boots."

"It's *me*," said Pip Halloway, aged nine, son of a net-mender and a drowned oyster-man, and he wiped his boots with such exaggerated care that she had to turn away to hide her smile.

There were six children in the reading room this morning, seated on benches Maren Ostrow had built from the ribs of a decommissioned skiff. Six was more than the room comfortably held. Last autumn there had been two: Pip, who came because his mother wanted him off the docks before the fish-buyers started drinking, and a silent girl named Corla whose father had been one of the divers whose names Tamsin had entered into the civic record with her own hand. Word had spread the way word always spread along Greywater's waterfront—not spoken so much as *felt*, passed hand to hand like warm bread—and by midwinter there were four, then nine, then thirteen, until Elias had complained cheerfully that his archive was being overrun by illiterate urchins and Tamsin had reminded him that they were, in fact, extremely literate urchins, thank you, and that he'd signed the attendance log himself.

"Right," she said, closing the ledger. "Where were we?"

"The interest clause," said Corla, who was eleven and frighteningly precise.

"The interest clause. Show me where it lives."

Corla turned to the sample contract Tamsin had copied out for them—a real Meridian indenture, deidentified, its cruelties intact because Tamsin refused to soften them. The children had learned to read contracts the way their parents had learned to read weather: as a matter of survival. They could find a penalty clause at forty paces. They could spot an altered figure in a supervisor's hand. Pip had once asked her, with the terrible directness of children, why anyone would sign something that said they belonged to another person, and Tamsin had told him the truth—that hunger signs faster than sense, that paper is patient while people are desperate, and that this was precisely why someone had to be able to read it *for* them.

"That's how they got my da," Corla said quietly, finger on the line. "He couldn't read this part."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "And now you can."

She let the silence sit, because some lessons were better absorbed than explained. Outside the tall windows the harbor went about its morning—the gulls arguing over the fish-market scraps, the ferry coughing across to the far pilings, the cranes of what remained of the Meridian wharf lifting cargo under new ownership and, if the papers were to be believed, new scrutiny. The company still existed. Companies were difficult things to kill entirely; they grew back around their wounds like coral. But the Harbor Trust sat on its board now, three seats strong, and every dive contract since the hearing carried a fatality-benefit clause written in plain language, and the divers' families had received their restitution in installments that arrived, mostly, on time.

It was not everything. Tamsin had learned that no verdict was ever everything. But it was *on the record*, which meant it could not be unmade, only outlived—and records, she had come to believe, outlived nearly everyone.

* *

At noon Callum came, as he did every day, carrying two meat pies wrapped in cloth and pretending, as he did every day, that the second one was surplus.

"Maren sent extra," he said. "She says you're too thin."

"Maren says everyone is too thin. It's her life's philosophy."

"And is she wrong?" He set the pies on the corner of her desk, next to the inkwell and the small brass weight she used to hold down loose pages—a weight cast from a pearl-diver's belt lead, given to her by the widows' council on the anniversary of the hearing.

Callum Reeve had stayed. She had half expected him to go, once his promise was discharged—once Wendell Pemberton's last letter had been read aloud into the public record and the man it was written for had learned, finally and completely, the shape of what her father had done for her. There was nothing left to protect. That was what she'd assumed would send him back to whatever ports had made him.

Instead he'd taken a room above Maren's boarding house, the same one he'd occupied during the worst months, and found work repairing boats, and shown up at the archive every day at noon as though punctuality were a debt he intended to pay forever.

"You're staring at me," he said.

"I'm deciding whether to ask you something."

"You've been deciding whether to ask me something since the equinox. I'm a patient man, but the harbor's taking bets."

Tamsin looked down at the ledger in front of her—the new civic registry, volume one, her handwriting marching across its pages in columns that would outlast them all. Then she looked up.

"There's a house on Lantern Row," she said. "Two rooms and a garden wall. Widow Sennet's old place. She's gone to live with her daughter upcoast and wants it sold to someone who'll keep the roof mended." She smoothed a page that did not need smoothing. "I've saved enough. It wouldn't take much more. A person to split the cost, perhaps. Someone who eats pie."

Callum was quiet for long enough that she heard the ferry bell across the water.

"I'm not a man with much to recommend him," he said at last. "Smuggling charges, pardoned or no. Hands like rope. I snore, according to Maren."

"Maren snores louder."

"And you're certain? You spent a year learning to belong to yourself, Tamsin. I watched you do it. I'd hate to think I was the thing you settled for instead."

She reached across the desk and took his rope-rough hand in hers, and turned it palm-up the way she turned pages, gently, as though it held writing.

"You're not a thing I settled for," she said. "You're a thing I *chose*. There's a difference. Ask any diver—it's the difference between what the water gives you and what you go down after." She closed his fingers over hers. "Lantern Row. Saturday. Bring a roof-mending shirt."

He laughed—the low, startled laugh she'd learned to count among her treasures—and said, "Yes, then," in the voice of a man agreeing to something he'd been agreeing to for a year already.

* *

Saturday, the harbor turned out.

That was the thing about Greywater that Tamsin understood now, the thing she had spent nine years of indenture unable to see from inside the counting house: the harbor kept accounts of its own. Not ledgers—something older than ledgers, kept in casseroles and carpentry and the way a dozen women could descend on a derelict garden wall with trowels before breakfast. By the time she and Callum got the key into Widow Sennet's stubborn lock, Maren Ostrow had already claimed the kitchen, three of her boarders were arguing about the chimney, Old Brannick was on the doorstep with a barrel of nails "found fair and square," and Pip Halloway and Corla were racing each other along the top of the garden wall with the specific energy of children who sensed an afternoon of unsupervised labor ahead.

"You'll want the east window reglazed," Brannick announced. "And there's a damp patch behind the stair. My late wife always said a damp patch is a house asking for attention. Houses are like wives that way." He winked at Tamsin. "Or husbands."

By dusk the garden wall was repointed, the chimney drew, the east window glittered with borrowed putty, and Maren had produced a supper from nowhere that fed fourteen people in two rooms. Tamsin stood in the doorway between kitchen and parlor with a cup of tea going cold in her hands, and let herself look at it: Callum laughing at something Brannick said, his head tipped back; Corla teaching Pip to play cards with the ruthless precision of a future tally-clerk; Maren directing traffic with a ladle like a harbormaster in a storm; the lamplight gold on walls that were, as of this afternoon, partly hers.

A year ago she had belonged to no one. Before that she had belonged to a company, which was a colder thing than belonging to no one, because at least no one leaves you alone in the dark with your grief. Now—

"Tamsin." Maren appeared at her elbow, ladle retired, voice lowered beneath the noise. "You're standing there looking like your father used to look when the catch came in good and he didn't trust it yet."

"Is that what he looked like?"

"Suspicious of happiness. Yes. Exactly that." Maren touched her cheek, briefly, the way she touched everyone—as though checking they were real and warm. "Trust it, girl. It's not a trick. Some years the catch really does come in."

* *

Sunday evening, alone in the archive before locking up, Tamsin climbed the ladder to the high shelf where she kept the documents that were not quite records and not quite relics: the first page of the burned annex's surviving payroll book, water-stained and ghostly; the fatality list with its struck-through names; a child's drawing Pip had left on her desk, depicting her as a tall figure with enormous spectacles guarding a mountain of paper.

And above the desk, framed in glass against the damp, hung the letter.

Her father's final letter. Callum had given it to her formally, on the day of the hearing's anniversary, wrapped in oilcloth as though it might still be contraband. *My Tamsin*, it began, in the unmistakable hand she had known better than her own since she was six years old, and it went on to say everything the record now preserved: the rigged debts, the staged death, the salvage claims set aside to purchase her freedom, the love that had arranged itself, patiently and illegally, into clauses and escrows because love is resourceful when the law gives it nothing else to work with.

*I could not leave you money*, her father had written, near the end, in letters that trembled with the damage the deep water had done to his lungs. *So I have left you proof. Proof is slower than silver but it spends further. Make them write it down, my girl. What is written down belongs to everyone, forever.*

She read it again, as she did most evenings—not because she needed reminding, but because reading it had become a kind of prayer, and Tamsin Pemberton had never been a woman for churches. The harbor was her congregation. Paper was her scripture. And the truth, written plainly, in a hand that trembled or did not, was the only sacrament she had ever trusted.

Below the frame, on the desk, lay tomorrow's work: a fresh petition from the coopers' guild disputing a warehouse assessment, a stack of birth registrations waiting to be copied into the permanent rolls, and a note from Elias asking—again—whether she would consider co-authoring the true centenary history, the one with the razored pages restored and the dead men named. She would say yes eventually. She liked making him wait; it was good for his character.

But first she took out the registry and opened it to the newest page, where yesterday's entries waited in drying ink. At the bottom, in the space reserved for the keeper's certification, she wrote:

*Tamsin Pemberton, Records-Keeper of the Harbor.*

Full. Legible. Hers.

Outside, the tide was turning, and the bell over the door gave one soft ring in the draft—singing, as it always seemed to her, in a voice like her father's: steady, salt-worn, and glad.

She turned down the lamp, and went home.
