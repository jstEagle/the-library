## 1. The Weight of Paper

The copper plate was cold under Paloma's fingers, and she loved it the way one loves anything that has outlived a person.

She had been inking it since before dawn, working by the light of a single oil lamp because the shutters were closed and would stay closed. In the workshop above the Rua da Prata, morning arrived as a gray seam under the door, and that was all the morning anyone could afford. Outside, Lisbon woke in its usual noise—carts, gulls, the fishwives crying the tide—but inside, there was only her breath, the burin's whisper against metal, and the smell of ink and vinegar that had been Master Duarte's smell for thirty years and was now, somehow, hers.

*Vila Serena.* The letters went into the wax ground one stroke at a time, small as a secret, precise as prayer.

It was the last plate. Forty-one of them, each engraved with a village that no longer appeared on any map the Crown acknowledged—villages drowned for reservoirs that served other men's estates, villages emptied to make room for hunting grounds, villages taxed off the face of the earth and then erased from it, so that the families who had lived and buried their dead there had never, officially, existed at all. Master Duarte had spent nine years surveying them. He had walked with his chain and his compass through mud and thorn and official indifference, recording what the government paid him to forget. He called the atlas *A Memória das Terras Perdidas*. The Memory of Lost Lands.

Paloma called it the reason he was dead.

She wiped the plate with a rag dampened in tallow and held it to the lamp, turning it so the light raked across the surface. The incised lines filled with shadow: the river bend, the church with its crooked bell tower, the seventy-one hearths marked with tiny crosses—the census symbol for occupied homes. Seventy-one families. Someone had counted them once. Someone had written them down. And then someone else had drawn a line through the entry, and a third someone had burned the page, and now the only proof that Vila Serena had ever stood beside its river was a rectangle of copper six inches wide, hidden beneath her apron in a workshop that smelled of its dead owner.

"You would have made this line deeper," she said aloud, to no one. Her voice sounded strange in the room, unused. "You always said my lines were too shy."

The workshop answered with silence, but it was a full silence, the kind that comes from furniture and tools still holding the shape of the hands that used them. His coat on its peg. His spectacles folded on the desk beside a half-eaten roll he'd never finish. The wall of drawers, each labeled in his crabbed hand: *Alentejo, norte. Beira, ribeiros. Terras sem nome.* She had not been able to bring herself to touch the spectacles in eleven days.

Eleven days since they told her he had died of a fever. Eleven days since she'd seen the bruising on his wrists that a fever does not make, and said nothing, because saying something would have meant being asked questions, and questions in this city were doors that opened onto cellars.

The bell of the Sé rang seven. Paloma set down the plate, flexed her stiff fingers, and did what she did every hour now: she measured.

This was a habit grief had taught her. When a life must be carried out a door in an armful, you learn to weigh everything twice—once in your hands and once in your heart. The atlas plates: forty-one, wrapped in wool, weighing perhaps four stone together. Impossible to run with, possible to die for. The master's notebooks: three volumes of field notes, coordinates, testimony from old men who remembered where wells had been. Lighter than the plates and heavier than sin. The lantern—she looked at it hanging by the window, the great brass storm lantern with its double-walled frame—and felt again the small wrongness of it, too heavy for its size, that she had noticed only last week when she finally understood what he'd built into it. A hollow between the walls, lined with felt. Big enough for documents. Built, she now saw, years ago, waiting for exactly the day that had come.

He had known. That was the thing she kept turning over like a coin she couldn't spend. He had known it would end this way, and he had built a hiding place into the object he trusted most, and he had died without telling her, leaving her to find it the way one finds a will—by needing it.

Footsteps in the street below, many feet, and a drum.

Paloma froze, then crossed to the shutter and put her eye to the gap between slats. Down the Rua da Prata came a column of soldiers in blue facings, and behind them a town crier mounted on a grey horse, unrolling a parchment with the ceremony of a man who enjoyed his work.

*"Ouvidos!"* the crier bellowed, and the street hushed the way streets do, everyone listening while pretending not to. *"By order of His Most Faithful Majesty and the Secretariat of Overseas Matters: all maps, plans, charts, surveys, and delineations of territory within the realm, whether drawn upon paper, vellum, or plate, which are not entered in the Royal Register, shall be surrendered to the magistrate of the district within seven days—"*

Seven days. She mouthed it. Seven days.

*"—upon pain of fine, imprisonment, and further penalties as befit the crime of concealing intelligence touching the Crown's affairs. All such materials shall be publicly burned. God preserve His Majesty."*

The drum beat twice more and the column moved on toward the next street, and the fishwives resumed crying the price of sardines as though the world hadn't just changed weight.

Paloma let the shutter slat fall back and stood in the gray light with her heart going like a hammer in a mine.

Seven days. Then every drawer in this workshop was evidence. Every plate, every proof pulled from the press, every notebook in her master's hand—fuel for a fire in the Praça, with a crowd invited and a priest presiding. They wouldn't even have to search hard. They knew exactly whose workshop this was. They had questioned him for nine days before they let him die of his "fever," and whatever they learned from him, they had not yet learned everything, or the soldiers would already be climbing her stairs.

But they would come. Seven days was not mercy; it was arithmetic. Time enough for every registered mapmaker in the city to surrender his stock, time enough for informers to earn their bread, and then the knock.

Paloma had imagined this moment often enough in the past week that she expected to feel terror. What she felt instead was something colder and more useful, settling in her chest like ballast: clarity.

Grief was a luxury of people with time. She had none.

She began to work.

First the new plate—Vila Serena, finished at last, the final name in the atlas—wiped dry, wrapped in wool, laid in the chest beneath the floorboards with its forty brothers. She pressed her palm flat over the closed lid for a moment, the way one touches a grave, then pried up the next board: the notebooks, tied in oilcloth. Then the proofs. Then the tools she could not replace, the burins and gravers, because an atlas that survived but could never be printed again was only half saved. Each thing weighed in her hands, then judged, then packed or left. His coat she left on its peg. His spectacles—

His spectacles she took. They weighed almost nothing. They weighed everything.

* * *

By noon she had the measure of her life, and it fit in two loads: the lantern with its hidden core, and a coffin-maker's crate she had bought yesterday from a man too incurious to ask why a young woman wanted a box the size of a small altar. Into the crate went the plates, nested in sawdust. Over the sawdust, a layer of mourning clothes borrowed from the widow downstairs, black wool and black linen, smelling of another woman's grief. If anyone asked—and someone would ask—it was her master's funeral goods. Lisbon was a city that respected funerals; even the tax collectors lowered their voices near a coffin.

She worked steadily, methodically, wiping her inky hands on her apron, eating standing up with the calm of a soldier. Once, passing the window, she heard children in the street singing a skipping rhyme about the burning in the Praça—already a song, already a game—and she stopped with her hand flat against the wall until the verse passed.

When the light began to fail she lit Master Duarte's lamp, and by it she wrote out, in a fair hand she disguised by slanting her letters, a single sheet:

*I, Paloma Almeida, apprentice to Duarte Falcão de Sousa, cartographer, attest that the work herein was surveyed and measured true, and that every village named herein exists, existed, and was extinguished by decree and not by nature.*

She signed it. She blotted it. Then she rolled it tight and slid it into the hollow of the lantern, behind the false back her master had fitted so cleverly that she herself had hung the lantern on its hook for three years without suspecting it carried a second life inside its skin.

The lantern was the plan. She saw it now as clearly as if he'd left her a letter. Everything else—the crate, the mourning clothes, the plates—was cargo that could be lost, replaced, reprinted if even one plate survived. But the lantern was made to be carried openly, innocently, through doors and checkpoints and streets full of soldiers. Who searches a funeral lantern? Who weighs grief?

Her master, dead eleven days, had just handed her the whole of his strategy across the divide, and Paloma laughed—a short, cracked sound, half sob, that startled her in the empty room.

"All right," she told him. "All right. I'll carry it."

She snuffed the lamp. Through the shutters, far off toward the river, she could see torchlight moving along the quay—more criers, more drums, the order rippling outward through the city like a stain through cloth. Somewhere down there was a printer who owed Master Duarte a debt and owned a ship's worth of exile; somewhere beyond the checkpoints was water deep enough to carry truth to other countries. Between here and there lay seven days, three gates she knew of and others she didn't, and every soldier in Lisbon with orders to burn what she carried.

She lifted the lantern. It hung heavy and innocent from her hand, its glass panes dark, its secret sleeping inside the brass.

Below, on the Rua da Prata, boots stopped outside the door.

Not marching. Not passing. Stopped.

Paloma stood motionless in the dark workshop, holding her master's lantern, and listened to a key turn in the lock below.
