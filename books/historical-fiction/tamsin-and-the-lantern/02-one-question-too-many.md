## 2. One Question Too Many

The morning smelled of tar and wet stone, the way Lisbon always smelled near the river when the tide was out and the mud gave up its secrets. Tamsin Reyes climbed the crooked stairs to the first door on his list with his ledger under one arm, its leather cover softened by three years of handling, its pages ruled in the Crown's precise hand: *Name. Trade. Souls in household. Parish of record.*

Four questions. He had asked them so many times they had worn smooth as river stones, and he no longer heard them as words, only as the sound of a lock turning.

The widow's house was the seventh of the morning, a narrow thing wedged between a rope-maker's loft and a shrine to Saint Anthony whose candle smoke had blackened the lintel into a grey beard. The door opened before he knocked twice. She was small and straight-backed, with hands that had clearly done every kind of work and a face that had decided, long ago, not to apologize for any of it.

"Tamsin Reyes," he said, holding up the tin badge that hung from his neck. "Royal census. Four questions only, senhora, if you'll grant me the time."

She looked at the badge, then at his face, and something moved behind her eyes—not fear exactly. Recognition of a kind. "Come in, then. The light's better."

Inside there was one room kept fiercely clean: a loom pushed against the wall, a pot of sardines and onions already perfuming the air, a crucifix above the hearth flanked by two small framed pictures—landscapes, painted by an amateur hand. Hills. A river bend. A cluster of white houses around a church with a bell tower.

He asked the questions. She answered them. Her name was Beatriz Gonçalves. Widow. One soul in the household. Weaver.

"Parish of record?" he said, pen poised over the page.

"Vila Serena."

His pen did not move. He waited for her to correct herself, to laugh, to say *Alfama, forgive me, I'm old.* She said nothing. She watched him with the calm of someone who has rehearsed this moment, or lived it too many times to be surprised by it anymore.

"I don't—" He turned back through his ledger, past the parishes listed in the appendix, the official roll of the district: Alfama, Madragoa, Poço do Borratém, Santos. His thumb stopped at the blank space where his eye had snagged on nothing a hundred times before. There was no Vila Serena. Not in the appendix. Not in the master rolls chained to the desk in the census office. Not anywhere in the four hundred pages of the Kingdom's own account of itself.

"There is no Vila Serena," he said.

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "There isn't."

* * *

He tried again, because procedure demanded it. "Senhora, your parish of birth—"

"Of record," she corrected. "You said *of record*. I told you my parish of record." She folded her hands in her lap like a woman settling in for a long afternoon. "I was born where the church has the bell you see there." She nodded at the little painting. "My husband is buried under an olive tree behind it, because the ground was too stony to dig deeper. I have woven cloth for forty years, and half of it went to the market at Vila Serena, which does not exist, which is why the cloth sold so cheaply, I expect. Invisible cloth. Very convenient for everyone but me."

Tamsin set down his pen. This was the moment, drilled into him during his first year, when he should write *parish unrecorded—subject confused or evasive*, note it for review, close the ledger, move to the next door. The Crown did not pay him to argue with widows. It paid him to count.

But he looked at the painting again. The painter had loved those hills. You could see it in the way the shadows were laid on, patient and fond, stroke after stroke—the work of someone who had walked those paths a thousand times and wanted, desperately, to keep them somewhere.

"How many households?" he heard himself ask. "In Vila Serena."

Her eyebrows rose slightly. For the first time, something in her face shifted toward surprise—or perhaps hope, which is a more dangerous thing.

"Ninety-one," she said. "Before."

"*Before* what?"

She rose, went to the hearth, and lifted down a small clay jar. From it she took a scrap of paper, folded eight times, soft as cloth from handling. She did not give it to him. She only held it.

"Before the surveyors came," she said. "Before the men with the chains and the measuring rods who said the land was needed. Before the paper arrived saying we had never been there." She turned the scrap over in her fingers. "My son is a mason now, in Setúbal. When he writes to me, he writes as if he grew up nowhere. Because according to the Kingdom, he did."

Tamsin felt the ledger in his lap grow heavier, though it had not changed weight at all. It was a strange sensation, one he had no name for yet: the sense that an object he had trusted all his professional life was quietly becoming something else. A weapon, maybe. Or a mask.

"Who signed the order?"

"You know I can't read the seals," she said. "But I know the name everyone whispered. The same name that's on the notice posted at the Chafariz d'El-Rey this week. About the maps."

Something cold walked up Tamsin's spine. He had passed the notice that morning without reading it properly—a crier's bill, ink still sharp, ordering the surrender of all unregistered maps within seven days, on pain of imprisonment. He had thought: *paper business, not mine.* Now the words rearranged themselves into something with teeth.

"Senhora," he said carefully, "if I enter Vila Serena in this ledger, the entry will be struck. And the striking will be noted. And the noting will be traced to this door."

"I'm seventy-three years old," Beatriz Gonçalves said. "Everything traces to this door eventually. Write it or don't. But answer me one thing first, census man, since you're the one who counts us." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "If I don't exist in your book—then who was it you counted just now? Who have you been talking to? What am I, standing here in front of you?"

The pot of sardines bubbled. Outside, a cart rattled over cobbles, and gulls argued over the fish market, and Lisbon went on being loud and alive and entirely indifferent to the question.

Tamsin wrote, in his careful official hand: *Beatriz Gonçalves, widow, weaver. Parish of record: Alfama.* Then, below it, smaller, squeezed into the margin where no clerk would think to look unless he already suspected: *claims Vila Serena.*

It was cowardice and honesty in the same motion, and he knew it, and he closed the ledger anyway.

* * *

The census office occupied the upper floor of a customs house near the Terreiro do Paço, and it smelled of sealing wax, dust, and the particular despair of men who spent their lives reducing souls to columns. Tamsin's superior, Senhor Cordeiro, was a round, pink, punctilious man who kept his snuffbox aligned with the edge of his desk and his conscience aligned with whatever document lay atop it.

"A village that isn't in the rolls," Cordeiro repeated, when Tamsin laid the matter before him. He did not look up from his correspondence.

"Not in any roll, senhor. Ninety-one households, she says. A church. There are records of a market—"

"There are no records of a market, Reyes, because there was no market, because there was no village. The rolls are the Kingdom's memory. If it isn't in the rolls, it didn't happen. That is not a flaw in the system. That is the system working."

"And the people?"

Cordeiro finally raised his eyes. They were mild, and mildness, Tamsin had learned, was the most frightening expression available to him. "Were relocated. Resettled. Assisted in their transition to productive parishes. The language of the decree is quite clear. Would you like me to fetch the decree?"

"The decree references villages by number, not name. Eleven, twelve, fourteen. No names anywhere. How is a man meant to verify—"

"*Verify*." Cordeiro smiled as if at a clever child. "Reyes. You count houses. You count heads. You enter the totals. The totals go to the Treasury, the Treasury levies accordingly, and the Kingdom stands. Where does a village called Vila Serena fit into that? Nowhere. Graciously, mercifully, nowhere." He tapped the stack of papers before him. "And speaking of nowhere—you've seen the proclamation. All private holdings of unregistered maps and surveys to be surrendered within the week. There will be inspections. Some of the old mapmakers' shops are on the list. The Almeida workshop among them."

The name landed strangely. Tamsin had seen the sign a hundred times crossing the hill—*Cartografia e Companhia, J. Almeida*—and had registered it the way one registers weather. "The mapmaker? I understood he'd died."

"He died inconveniently, before surrendering everything, which is why his shop will be searched thoroughly. Paper is a dangerous commodity these days, Reyes. Maps especially. A map tells a man where he is. The Crown prefers to be the one who tells him." Cordeiro returned to his letter, dismissing him with a flick of two fingers. "Stick to counting. Counting is honest work. Don't make it philosophy."

* * *

That night Tamsin sat at his table with a single candle and did something he had never done in three years of service: he reread his own ledger. Not to check figures—to check himself.

Page after page of households, trades, souls. Competent entries. Clean columns. And now, threaded through them, things he had never let himself see: the household entered twice because the family had fled mid-count; the empty room where a fisherman's wife had said *my sons went north for work* with a face that said otherwise; the street corner where an old man had refused to answer at all until Tamsin moved along, and the relief in the man's shoulders when he did. Three years of small wrongnesses he had filed away as noise. As weather.

He turned to Beatriz Gonçalves's entry. *Parish of record: Alfama.* The lie sat there in his own handwriting, neat as prayer.

He thought about the painting on her wall. The hills someone had loved enough to keep.

He thought about the margin, and the small confession hidden inside it—*claims Vila Serena*—and understood suddenly that he had not written it for the record. He had written it for himself. A seed planted in the only ground he controlled. Proof, later, if there ever was a later, that on this one day he had chosen to remember rather than to count.

The candle guttered. Down by the river, a ship's bell counted out the hour, and somewhere in the dark city a dog barked at nothing, or at something no one else could see.

Tamsin closed the ledger. Tomorrow's route took him along the waterfront—past the customs stairs, past the fish market, past the narrow lane where the mapmaker's shop stood dark behind its dusty windows, waiting for the inspectors to come with their warrants and their fire.

He found he had already decided to walk past slowly.

And he found, turning the day over one last time, that the widow's question had followed him home and refused to leave: *What am I, standing here in front of you?*

There was only one way he knew to answer a question like that. You went looking for what the records said wasn't there.
