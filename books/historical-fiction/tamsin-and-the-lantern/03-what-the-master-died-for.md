## 3. What the Master Died For

The knock came at dawn, three blows of an official fist, and Paloma knew before she opened the shutters what it meant. She had known since the criers began their rounds on Sunday—*all unregistered maps to be surrendered within seven days, by decree of His Majesty's most excellent secretary of state*—and she had spent every hour since doing exactly what a loyal subject must not do: hiding them.

She did not answer the door at once. There was no use pretending the workshop stood empty; smoke curled from her chimney like a signature. Instead she moved through the back room with the terrible calm that grief had taught her, the same stillness that had carried her through laying out Senhor Duarte's body six weeks ago, and lifted the great copper plates from beneath the false floor where he had taught her all his secrets lived.

Nine plates. The debts of a kingdom etched in reverse, so that everything printed from them would read true. The drowned village of Vila Serena and its four hundred souls. The granaries emptied into private hands. The roads built on paper only, paid for twice and never laid once. Her master had spent eleven years making this atlas in secret, and it had cost him—she understood now—not his life exactly, but something worse: the long erosion of being watched, of knowing that every visitor might be the last visitor, until his heart simply agreed to stop.

She heard the inspector's voice through the door, thin and patient as a file drawn across iron. "Senhorita Almeida. We know you are awake. Open, or we open it for you."

There was no time for the cellar. The cellar was the first place they would look.

Paloma's eyes went instead to the corner of the workshop, to the thing that had hung there her whole apprenticeship like a piece of furniture too plain to notice: Duarte's surveyor's lantern, its frame of blackened brass, tall as a child, broad as a coffin lid turned upright. As an apprentice she had asked him once why it was so large, so heavy, when any lantern half the size would light a road better. He had smiled in that way of his, folding his hands over his belly, and said, *Because a good mapmaker builds for the day the maps must run.*

She had found the mechanism only after his death, when she went looking for his will and found instead a diagram folded inside his breviary. The base swung open on a hidden hinge; the interior, meant for oil and flame, was a hollow the exact depth of a copper plate. He had built it years ago. He had been waiting, all that time, for the knock she was hearing now.

Her hands shook as she worked. Nine plates, each wrapped in waxed linen against damp and scratching, slid into the lantern's belly one by one. The last one—Vila Serena, always Vila Serena, because he had always loaded it last, as if sending it to bed—caught on the hinge, and outside the door the voice said something sharper to someone else, and boots shifted on stone, and Paloma pressed the plate home with her palm flat and hard, the way she would press down a wound.

The base closed. The lantern looked like a lantern. She draped her master's old traveling cloak over it anyway, because a woman leaving her dead employer's workshop with a lantern made sense, and a woman leaving with anything else did not.

Then she opened the door.

* * *

The inspector was not what she expected. Crown men in Lisbon wore their authority like borrowed clothing, but this one wore his well: grey coat, grey eyes, a face that had been handsome once and had decided around forty that effort was no longer required. Two soldiers flanked him, pikes shouldered, bored as furniture.

"Senhorita Almeida." He consulted a paper without much interest. "Apprentice to the late cartographer Joaquim Duarte. You have continued the workshop?"

"I finish commissions," she said. "Nothing new."

"That is what I am here to confirm." He stepped past her into the workshop as though invited, and the soldiers followed, and Paloma stood in her own doorway like a stranger while they dismantled her master's life with efficient hands.

They were thorough. She gave them that. Drawers came out entire and were upended onto the floor. The press was examined, its screws backed out, its rollers lifted free. The false floor under the flagstones—they found it in less than a quarter hour, and Paloma felt her heartbeat go strange and slow, watching the soldier pry up the board that had sheltered nothing, thank God, nothing but dust and an old boot.

"Empty," the soldier reported.

"Of course it is empty," the inspector murmured. He was standing at Duarte's desk, turning over the tools with long fingers. A graver. A burnisher. The magnifying lens worn smooth at the rim from thirty years against a man's eye socket. "A man does not build a hiding place and leave it empty unless he has moved what it held. Or unless"—and here he looked at her directly, and his eyes were not bored at all—"the thing worth hiding never fit inside."

"My master kept his accounts honestly," Paloma said. "If the crown wants his records of payment, they are in the blue ledger."

"The crown," the inspector said, setting down the lens with a small click, "does not want payment records." He walked toward her slowly, and she made herself stand still, made herself wear the face of a tired apprentice annoyed at a morning's disruption. Fear, she had learned watching Duarte, was best disguised as tedium. No one suspects a bored person. "The crown wants to know what a dying man spent eleven years engraving in a room with one door. Copper is expensive. Plates take months. Where are his plates, senhorita? Melted, I could believe. Sold, certainly. But a craftsman does not destroy nine finished plates. That would be like a mother drowning her children."

"They were sold for scrap," she said. "After the funeral. We needed money."

"To whom?"

"A broker on the Rua Nova. I don't recall the name."

"You don't recall the name." He smiled without warmth, the smile of a man filing something away rather than dismissing it. Then his gaze drifted past her shoulder, to the cloaked shape by the wall, and stayed there one full second longer than curiosity required.

"It lights my way home," Paloma said. "I sleep in the loft. The street is dark."

"Mm." He touched the cloak's hem, just barely, the way a man tests whether bread is fresh, then withdrew his hand. "You may go, senhorita. Take your lantern." And as she bent to lift it—heavier now than any lantern had a right to be, heavy as four hundred erased lives—his voice followed her, mild as milk: "We will speak again. I find that in this city, everyone eventually has something to tell me. It saves everyone a great deal of unpleasantness when they tell me early."

She walked out into the grey morning with the atlas on her hip and did not let herself tremble until the third street corner, and even then only for ten breaths, because ten breaths was all she could afford.

* * *

The docks at first light smelled of tar, fish blood, and rain that hadn't fallen yet. Paloma had chosen the Alfama stairs down to the river because they were steep enough that no soldier climbed them for pleasure, and she came out among the fishwives and the rope-sellers with her hood up and her heart going like a mill wheel.

The *Santa Luzia* lay at anchor beyond the customs quay, an exile ship if ever there was one—her master's last letter, written in the shaky hand of a man who knew how little time remained, had named her captain, a printer by trade who'd fled the Inquisition's softer cousin in Porto and now carried fugitives' goods between Lisbon and London under bills of lading for salt. *Tell him the lantern needs oil,* the letter had said. *He will understand.*

But the ship would not sail until evening tide, and between now and then Paloma had to stand on a public quay holding the most dangerous object in Portugal and look like a widow seeing off a friend.

She had nearly managed it—had found a shadowed stretch of wall near the crane, had set the lantern down and begun rearranging the cloak over it for the fourth useless time—when a shadow fell across her own.

"Citizen."

She looked up into a face she didn't know and would remember forever: young, maybe five years older than her own twenty-three, with ink stains on his fingers that no amount of scrubbing quite removed—the mark of a clerk—and a census ledger under his arm the size of a breviary. His eyes went from her face to the lantern and stopped there, and she watched him do the arithmetic of its weight, its size, its wrongness, the way a man reads a page.

"That is a very large lantern," Tamsin Reyes said.

"It is my master's," Paloma said. "I am bringing it to his grave."

"At the docks."

"There are graves everywhere, senhor. This city buries more than it keeps."

Something flickered across his face at that—recognition, or its cousin—and he crouched, unhurried, and put one ink-stained finger on the cloak's edge where it gapped. Beneath, through the weave of waxed linen, the faint green-black gleam of copper showed like water under ice.

He knew. She saw it land in him, saw the knowledge arrive whole: this was not a lantern, this woman was not a mourner, and the right thing—the thing his office, his oath, his salary demanded—was to stand up, turn around, and call for the harbor watch, whose post stood not fifty paces away, two soldiers dicing in the morning sun.

Paloma did not reach for the knife in her sash. Reaching would confess. Instead she looked at him, and let him see her look, and said quietly, "Whatever you think this is, it is heavier than you imagine, and lighter than you fear. And the man who made it died for less reason than you'd be giving them."

Tamsin Reyes did not move. Out on the water a gull screamed once and thought better of it. Somewhere behind them, the dice rattled, and a soldier laughed at nothing.

"I interviewed a widow yesterday," he said at last, still crouched, voice low, almost to himself. "Vila Serena. She swears her house stands in a village that appears nowhere—in my ledgers, in the parish rolls, in the royal surveys. Nowhere at all. As if four hundred people had been wiped off the world with a wet cloth." He rose slowly, and his hand, when it came up, did not signal the watch. It rested, instead, flat upon the lantern's cold brass roof, the way a man rests his hand on a door he has decided to open. "My superiors told me I had misread my notes. But I don't misread, senhorita. It is the one thing I do perfectly."

Paloma's breath caught. "And what do you intend to do about it?"

He glanced once toward the soldiers, once toward the ship at anchor, and when he looked back at her, the certainty of ten years of honest clerking was cracking visibly down the middle, like ice before the thaw.

"First," he said, "you are going to tell me what else is in that village besides a widow. And then—we need to talk about the checkpoints between here and that ship, because I know exactly how they search, and you are carrying that thing straight into the worst of them."
