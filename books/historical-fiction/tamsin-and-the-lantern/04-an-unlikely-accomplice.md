## 4. An Unlikely Accomplice

*Tamsin*

The widow's cousin kept a room above a rope-walk in the Alfama, and it was there that Tamsin Reyes sat across from a stranger with ink-stained fingers and a lantern she would not let out of her reach.

He had followed her from the docks. He had watched her load the strange chest-shaped frame onto a handcart, had seen the way she glanced over her shoulder at every sound, and he had done nothing. He could have called the guard. It was, in fact, his duty. The royal order was posted on every corner: all unregistered maps to be surrendered within the week. And this woman was fleeing with something that rattled faintly when the cart hit a cobblestone—something that weighed like metal plates.

Instead he had said, "You'll be stopped at the Alfama Gate dressed like that," and then hated himself for saying it, because saying it made him an accomplice.

Now they regarded each other across a table scarred by candle flame, and the woman—Paloma Almeida, she had finally admitted—studied him with the wariness of a cat deciding whether the hand reaching toward it held food or a snare.

"You're a census taker," she said. "You count people for the crown."

"I count people *out* of the crown's ledgers, more often." He pulled his satchel up and set it on the table. "Do you know what I do when a household reports a birth? I write it down. When a family leaves a parish without license? I write them down as departed. Departed to where, no one asks. The ledger only wants a number. It doesn't want to know what happened to them."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because three days ago I interviewed a widow named Inês Cordeira who told me, plainly and without shame, that she lived in Vila Serena. And Vila Serena is not on any list I carry. Not the parish rolls, not the tax districts, not the survey maps. When I asked my superiors, they looked at me the way you look at a man who has asked whether the king sleeps." He opened the satchel. "So I did something improper. I compared my own ledgers against the older ones. The ones from before my office was reorganized."

Paloma went very still.

He drew out two volumes and laid them side by side. The first was new, its binding stiff, its pages dense with figures. The second was fifteen years old, water-stained, its corners soft as cloth.

"Read the eastern district," he said. "Both of them."

She bent over the pages. Her finger moved down the columns of the old book—village names, household counts, tithes assessed—and then moved down the same stretch of the new book, where the names were gone. Where the land itself had been reclassified: *crown reserve, timber rights pending.*

"Vila Serena," she read aloud. "Casa dos Pinheiros. Fonte Alta. Quinta da Sombra—" Her voice caught. She knew those names. He watched her know them.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"Eleven," she whispered. "Eleven villages. My master mapped every one of them. He walked their streets and measured their wells and wrote down the names of everyone who—" She pressed her palm flat against the page, as if she could hold the people down through the paper. "They didn't just erase the villages. They erased them from the *records*. So that when the displaced families came begging in Lisbon, there was nothing to point to. No village. No proof they'd ever been anywhere."

"And my ledgers helped do it," Tamsin said. "Not by lying. By agreeing. Every census after the reorganization simply copied forward the new map. We counted only what we were told existed." He heard his own voice go flat and cold. "I have spent six years believing I served truth because I recorded what I saw. But I only saw what I was permitted to see."

Paloma looked up at him, and in the candlelight her face was fierce and exhausted all at once. "Then why help me? You could walk out that door and report me, and by tomorrow morning you'd be commended."

"Yes," he said. "I've considered it. I consider it roughly once every quarter hour." He almost smiled; she did not. "But here is what stops me. If they burn your atlas, then the lie is complete. There will be no record anywhere—not one—that Vila Serena stood where it stood. The widow Inês will be a madwoman who invented a home. Her dead husband will never have existed. And I will have written the ledger that says so."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below them, the rope-walk creaked, men hauling hemp through the dark.

* * *

*Paloma*

She did not trust him. Trust was a coin her master had taught her to spend carefully, and he had died spending his on exactly this kind of man—a functionary, a pen for the crown—and look where it had left him: interrogated in a cellar, returned home in a box, his death certified as *fever.*

But Tamsin Reyes had put his own ledgers on the table. A man guarding a secret did not show you the evidence against himself.

"The atlas is in pieces," she said finally. "Forty-one copper plates, engraved. Plus the master copy of the survey notes. They're hidden inside a lantern frame my master built—a false chest, double-bottomed, made to pass as grave goods or cargo. I'm taking it to a printer. An exile, Senhor Baptista, who sails on the tide within the fortnight. He can cast type and pull proofs aboard if we get the plates to him. Copies leave Lisbon scattered across Europe, and no burning order in the world can call them back."

Tamsin absorbed this in silence. Then: "Three checkpoints between here and the river landing. I know them all."

"You know them *as a census official*. That may be worse than useless."

"It may be the only useful thing about me." He turned the old ledger toward her and tapped a line of entries. "I process the manifests at the Alfama Gate twice weekly. I know which guards take bribes, which ones don't, and which ones take bribes and search anyway for the pleasure of it. I know the Bridge of Boats closes at vespers and reopens for grain carts at dawn—I know because I've complained in writing about the delay to my own office. I know that the third checkpoint isn't a checkpoint at all anymore. It's the printer's street itself. They've posted men outside Baptista's shop since the surrender order went out. They're watching for exactly what you're carrying."

Her stomach dropped. "Then it's already lost."

"No." He leaned forward, and she saw that beneath the clerk's tiredness there was something harder, something that had been waiting years for a reason to exist. "It's not lost. Because they watch the doors, but they don't think like mapmakers. They think like soldiers: roads, gates, walls. You think like someone who knows that Lisbon is full of ways that aren't roads. Stairs that connect nothing to nothing. Aqueduct galleries. Wash-house tunnels under the Alfama. Am I wrong?"

She stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've spent six years knocking on doors in every quarter of this city, and half the time I enter houses by passages no official map records. Your master's city and mine were always the same city. We just learned it from opposite ends." He paused. "You need someone who knows how the checkpoints breathe. I need someone who knows the ground truth my ledgers erased. Neither of us gets to the ship alone."

Paloma looked at the lantern, propped against the wall where she'd carried it up three flights of stairs on aching arms. Forty-one plates. Eleven villages. Thousands of people rendered into nothing by a stroke of a clerk's pen—and here sat the clerk, offering her his pen.

"If you betray me," she said, "they will hang us both anyway. You understand that. Once you've seen the atlas, you're not a witness anymore. You're evidence."

"I had understood that, yes."

"And if we fail—if we're taken at any gate—you will say you coerced me. You threatened my master's name. Whatever buys me a moment, you say it."

"That's a foolish condition."

"It's the only condition."

He studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face seemed to settle something in his. "Agreed," he said. "Though for the record, I've never coerced anyone in my life. I find people tell me things willingly. It's the one talent my profession requires."

Despite everything—the fear, the grief, the weight of the plates still bruising her shoulders—a laugh escaped her, short and disbelieving. It was the first time she'd laughed since the funeral.

"We move tomorrow night," Tamsin said. "Market day. The Alfama Gate will be crowded with carts, and the guards will be lazy and hot. How good is your disguise?"

"My master's daughter Marisol keeps mourning clothes. Real ones, from her mother's family. And there's a custom in the Alfama—" Paloma hesitated, then committed. "Funeral processions are not searched. Superstition. No guard wants a curse on his family for disturbing the dead."

"A coffin passes, and every man finds urgent business elsewhere." Tamsin nodded slowly. "And the lantern?"

"Goes in the coffin. Grave goods. It's built to look the part—it always was."

He rose, gathered his ledgers, and paused at the door. For a moment he seemed about to say something else. What came out instead was quiet and unadorned:

"Inês Cordeira's village. If we succeed, her grandchildren will be able to find it on a map. That's worth more than my career ever was."

Then he was gone, down the stairs and into the dark, and Paloma sat alone with the candle, the ledgers' ghosts, and the lantern that held everything her master had died for.

Tomorrow night, the Alfama Gate.

She touched the lantern's cold frame once, the way she used to touch her master's shoulder for luck, and began to plan a funeral.
