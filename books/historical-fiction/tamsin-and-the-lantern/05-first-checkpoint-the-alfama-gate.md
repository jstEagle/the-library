## 5. First Checkpoint: The Alfama Gate

The dead man in our procession had no name, and that was the first mercy of the morning.

Marisol had found him through the brotherhood of São Francisco—holy men who buried paupers for coin, who asked no questions of the grieving because grief was a language they had grown tired of hearing. For twelve réis he was ours until sundown: a body already stiff in its shroud, already blessed, already so far past the reach of any clerk's ledger that even the crown's new edicts could not touch him. He would be lowered into consecrated ground by evening, mourned by strangers. In a city where whole villages were being struck from the record, it seemed to me a strange kind of justice that this man would be the only one of us guaranteed remembrance.

We dressed ourselves in the grammar of mourning. Black wool, though the day promised heat. Veils for Marisol and me, wax-stained and smelling of old churches. Tamsin wore the somber coat of a hired mourner—*pranteiro*, they were called, professional weepers who sold their tears by the hour—and I confess there was something fitting in it. A census taker who had spent two years counting the living would now spend one morning pretending to grieve a dead stranger while carrying stolen paper under his arm. If his conscience troubled him, it had learned to keep quiet about it.

The lantern sat at the center of everything.

My master had built its frame with the patience of a man who understood that the best hiding place is not darkness but ordinariness. We had filled the hollow chamber with the atlas plates wrapped in oilcloth, packed tight as sardines, then lined the visible interior with candle stubs, incense cones, a small tin crucifix—the modest treasures a poor family sends with its dead to light the way. A grave-goods chest, the guards would see. A lantern for the tomb, unlit, because you do not burn oil on your way to a burial; you light it when you arrive.

"You'll carry it," Marisol had said to me before dawn, pressing the handle into my hands. "A daughter carries her father's light. Let them read it off your face if they read anything."

So I walked behind the bier with the lantern swinging gently against my hip, feeling every plate shift inside it like a heartbeat, like something alive and impatient to be born.

* * *

Alfama woke around us as we climbed—fishwives hosing blood off their doorstones, cats threading between barrels, an old woman beating a carpet from a balcony so that dust fell like slow snow into the street below. The neighborhood smelled of salt and charcoal and the particular sourness of drains that had been there since the Moors. Above us the castle walls caught the early sun and threw it back pale gold, indifferent as ever to who ruled beneath them.

Nobody looks at a funeral. That is the first law of cities and the second law of grief. We moved at the pace of sorrow—slow, deliberate, heads bowed—and the crowd parted around us the way water parts around a stone, closing again behind without a ripple. A woman crossed herself as we passed. A boy snatched his hand back from a stray dog and went solemn. Even the fishwives lowered their voices, and I felt the strange power of it: we were carrying death through their midst, and death still commanded respect even here, even now, when the men in the palace had begun treating memory itself as a crime.

Then we turned onto the Rua de Santo António and saw the checkpoint.

They had raised it overnight—a barricade of wine casks and planks across the street's narrow throat, flanked by two soldiers with muskets slung and a sergeant with a list. Behind him, a table. On the table, a growing pile of confiscated papers, weighted down with a stone against the river wind. Maps, mostly. I could see the curled edges of them, the ghost of ink lines. Some clerk's life work, surrendered or seized, stacked like firewood waiting for its match.

My mouth went dry. Beside me, Tamsin's breath changed—not faster, but deeper, the way a man breathes when he is forcing himself to be still.

"We knew they'd be here," he murmured without moving his lips. "It's the only road to the eastern gates. Every funeral for three parishes comes through this street."

"Knowing doesn't make my hands stop shaking."

"Then let them shake. Mourners tremble." His eyes stayed fixed on the ground ahead, on the cobblestones, on nothing. "Grief excuses everything except running."

The sergeant looked up as we approached. He was young—younger than Tamsin—with the soft jaw and uncertain eyes of a man promoted beyond his appetite for cruelty. He held up one gloved hand, and our little procession stopped, the bearers shifting the bier's weight with practiced grunts.

"Name of the deceased?"

"José Pereira," Marisol answered, her voice pitched perfectly between exhaustion and dignity, the voice of a woman who had buried too many people to perform it fresh. "Of the parish of Santos-o-Velho. My uncle."

"Santos-o-Velho." He made a mark on his list. "Cause of death?"

"The fever. God's will." She let a beat pass, then added, with just enough edge, "Shall I have him carried back home so you may inspect the fever yourself, Sergeant? He's heavy."

Something flickered across the young man's face—embarrassment, the universal solvent of authority. He waved toward the bier. One of the soldiers lifted the shroud's edge, looked at the gray face beneath, and dropped it quickly, crossing himself. The living are squeamish; it is one of their few reliable virtues.

"And the chest?" The sergeant nodded at the lantern. "The edict requires inspection of all containers. Papers especially."

I stepped forward before my fear could vote against me. I lifted the lantern and opened the little door at its front, tilting it so the contents showed: candle stubs, incense, the tin crucifix catching the light. The plates lay beneath, invisible beneath the false floor my master had built, and I held the sergeant's gaze the way I had seen my master hold difficult clients—not defiantly, which invites challenge, but with the mild patience of someone who has nothing whatever to hide and finds the delay tedious.

"Grave-goods," I said. "For the tomb. Would you take candles from a dead man, Sergeant? His family would curse you from the churchyard."

Behind the veil, Marisol made a sound that might have been grief and might have been a swallowed laugh.

The sergeant peered inside, sniffed at the incense, and was reaching to wave us through—

"No need."

The voice came from behind the table, from a man leaning against the wall with a mug of wine, a soldier's cloak thrown over civilian clothes. He hadn't been part of the inspection; he'd been watching it the way bored men watch anything, with his chin propped on his fist. Now he set down the mug and stood, unhurried, and came around the barricade with the loose-limbed walk of someone who had never once hurried for anyone's convenience.

He stopped in front of Tamsin.

"Well," he said, smiling. "If it isn't the census office's own bloodhound. Reyes, isn't it? Third desk from the window. You interviewed my mother-in-law last month—asked her so many questions she took to her bed for two days." He laughed. "She thought the Inquisition had come back."

Tamsin did not move. I watched him not move with a fascination that bordered on terror—the absolute stillness of him, the veil of dullness dropping over his face like a shutter over a shop window.

"I'm sorry, senhor," he said. "You mistake me for someone else. Today I only weep for hire."

Another man might have pushed. Another man might have grabbed his chin and turned his face to the light. But the soldier laughed instead, clapped Tamsin on the shoulder hard enough to rock him, and said, "Of course, of course. All you black crows look alike." He turned to the sergeant. "Let them through. It's a funeral, for God's sake, and this one—" he jerked a thumb at Tamsin "—looks like he's already dead of boredom."

But before he stepped aside, before he waved us past the casks and the table and the pile of condemned maps, he looked at Tamsin one moment longer. And I saw what passed between them, though no word was spoken.

It was not recognition, exactly. Recognition would have demanded action, and action required certainty, and certainty required effort. What I saw was simpler and far worse. It was a debt being noted in a ledger. A coin placed on a shelf against a future day. *I know you,* that look said. *And someday knowing you will be worth something—to me.*

Tamsin bowed his head slightly, the smallest acknowledgment a mourner could offer, and we moved on.

* * *

We did not speak until we had left Alfama's lanes behind and come out above the eastern terraces, where the city fell away below us in a tumble of roofs and the Tagus spread wide and glittering toward the sea. The bearers carried the bier on toward the cemetery with our nameless dead man, and Marisol peeled away to arrange the burial and pay the gravedigger, leaving the three of us—two fugitives and a lantern—in the shade of an olive tree that had stood there since before there was a Portugal to tax.

Only then did Tamsin exhale, long and slow, as if he had been holding his breath since dawn.

"He knew me," he said quietly. "Not certainly. Not enough to act. But enough to remember."

"Who is he?"

"A soldier named Vasco Cordeira. He drinks at the tavern by the customs house. He plays cards with clerks from my own office." Tamsin stared out at the water, and I watched a man watch his own future go wrong. "He didn't stop us because stopping us cost effort and letting us pass cost nothing. But he banked what he saw, Paloma. Men like that always collect."

I set the lantern down between us, careful as setting down a sleeping child. Through its little glass door I could see the tin crucifix glinting, the candle stubs, all the innocent furniture of grief—and beneath it, silent as sediment, the truth of eleven villages that had never existed and forty thousand souls the crown wished forgotten.

"Then we spend what time he gives us," I said.

Tamsin nodded slowly. Then he frowned, and turned east along the ridge, toward the road that ran down to the river.

"Paloma," he said. "That road passes the Bridge of Boats. And unless my eyes have failed me entirely—there's smoke rising from the far bank. They're burning something down there."

I followed his finger and saw it: a thin black thread rising against the morning sky, exactly where the second checkpoint waited.

Smoke that smelled, even at this distance, like burning paper.
