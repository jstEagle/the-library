## 6. The Cartographer's Daughter

The house at the end of Rua das Flores had a door like every other door on the street, and a knocker like every other knocker, and if Paloma had not stopped before it with the certainty of a woman reading coordinates, Tamsin would have walked past without a second glance. She did not knock. She set the lantern down between her feet, pressed her palm flat against the wood, and waited.

"Someone lives here," Tamsin said. It was not a question. He had learned, in two days of flight, that Paloma answered statements faster than questions.

"My master's daughter." Paloma's thumb moved across the grain of the door. "Marisol Duarte. She married a silk merchant and took his name and swore she wanted nothing more to do with ink." A pause. "That was nine years ago."

"And you're sure she'll take us in?"

"No."

She knocked anyway—three sharp raps, then one, a rhythm that meant something, though Tamsin could not have said what. Footsteps inside. A bolt drawn back an inch. An eye in the gap.

What followed happened too fast for courtesy: the door opening wide, Paloma pulled through by the wrist, Tamsin seized by the collar and hauled after her into a dim hallway that smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, and the door shut behind them with a sound like a full stop.

"You mad girl." The woman who had hold of him was perhaps fifty, silver shot through dark hair pinned hastily, still in her dressing gown at midday. Her grip was astonishingly strong for someone whose hands had presumably spent nine years handling nothing heavier than embroidery. "You come to my door with a crown lantern under your arm and a stranger behind you, in daylight, when half the parish knows my father's name is mud?"

"The lantern isn't crown property," Paloma said. "It's mine now."

"It's *evidence*, is what it is."

"It's everything I have."

Something passed between them then that Tamsin felt he had no right to witness—the old anger in Marisol's face cracking along fault lines of something older and softer, grief perhaps, or its fossil. She let go of his collar. She looked at the lantern as if it were a coffin.

"You'd better come in," she said. "Both of you. And you—" this to Tamsin, up and down, taking the measure of his census office coat, which they had not had time to replace, "—you will explain what a king's counting-man is doing carrying my father's work through Alfama, or you will explain it to the Inquisition instead, because those are your options."

* * *

The front room was a museum of a life deliberately unlived. No instruments, no charts, no paper but a single devotional print above the mantel—and yet Marisol had hung the curtains exactly as a cartographer would have, weighted so they hung straight, hemmed to the inch. Some inheritances go deeper than furniture.

She put wine in front of them and did not sit until Paloma told her everything. Tamsin listened to the story again from its beginning—the master's death eleven days past, the atlas of hidden debts and struck villages, the criers in the praça, the inspector's ransacking hands, the plates sleeping inside the hollowed lantern frame—and he watched Marisol hear it, and he understood that she was hearing two stories at once: the one Paloma told, and the one written in the girl's exhausted face.

When Paloma finished, the silence held for a long moment. Then Marisol said, quietly, "He didn't die of illness."

Tamsin saw Paloma go very still. "The physician said his heart—"

"The physician was paid to say his heart." Marisol rose and went to the window, checked the street out of habit, drew the curtain. When she turned back, her voice had gone flat in the way voices do when feeling has been compressed past all expansion. "I saw him three weeks before the end. They let me visit—his own house, mind, they let me visit *my own father's house*—under escort, because the crown had posted men there. He could barely stand. His fingers were ruined. You know what his fingers were, Paloma. A man who draws lines finer than hair, and they had—" She stopped. Started again. "They questioned him for six days. Not the Inquisition. Worse. Crown men, civil men, the kind who don't wear robes and can't be appealed to. Someone had told them about the atlas."

"Who?" Paloma whispered. "Who knew? Only he and I—"

"Not only." Marisol sat down at last, folding her ruined composure around herself like a shawl. "There was a surveyor who came and went the year before you apprenticed. A friend of the ministry. Your father trusted him because your father trusted everyone who loved maps, and loving maps is not the same as being honest. But that's not the whole of it, child, and you may as well hear the whole of it while your counting-man is here to choke on it." Her eyes cut to Tamsin. "The order to interrogate him came from above. From the office that commissions the surveys. The same office that decides which villages exist and which don't. The same office, if I am not mistaken, that employs you."

The wine in Tamsin's cup had gone warm and tasteless in his mouth. He set it down.

"I file household returns," he said. "Counts of persons, livestock, looms. My office compiles them. What happens above compilation—"

"Above compilation," Marisol repeated, tasting the words with contempt, "is where my father died. Above compilation is where Vila Serena was subtracted from the world. Do you know how a village stops existing, Senhor Reyes? It doesn't burn. It doesn't flood. Someone takes the returns you file and decides the arithmetic is inconvenient, and then the village is *adjusted*, and the people in it become squatters on land they've farmed for two hundred years, and the census men come round the following spring and find no one there worth counting."

"I know," Tamsin said.

It came out of him before he could weigh it, and both women looked at him, and he found he could not take it back, and discovered he did not want to.

"I interviewed a widow," he said. "Three weeks ago. By the river. She gave me her name, her age, her husband dead of fever, and when I asked her parish, she said Vila Serena. I looked it up in my ledger and it wasn't there. Not misspelled. Not crossed out. *Not there.* I asked my superior. He told me I was overworked. I asked again. He took the page." His hands had closed around the memory of that page, the clean tear of it, paper surrendering. "He took the page out of my ledger and told me to recopy the entry without it. And I did. I sat at my desk and I wrote a widow out of Portugal, and my hand didn't shake, and that's the part that keeps me awake. Not the order. The *obedience*."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the street a knife-grinder cried his rounds and faded away toward the river.

Paloma was looking at him with an expression he could not read—or could, and feared to name, because it looked like the beginning of trust and he had done nothing yet to earn it. "Your ledgers," she said slowly. "You kept copies. Off the register. I've seen how you work, Tamsin. You count everything twice."

"I count everything twice," he admitted. "Yes."

"Then somewhere in your coat or your lodging there is a second record of every village the crown erased. Names. Ages. Dead husbands. Livestock." Her voice trembled on the last word and steadied itself, and he marveled at her even as his chest ached. "Do you understand what that is? That's not paper. That's *them*. That's the widow and everyone like her, testifying."

"It's also evidence against me," Marisol said sharply. "And against this house, and against the both of you, and I want it stated plainly, once, that I know this and am choosing it, so that no one may later say the cartographer's daughter was deceived." She pressed her knuckles briefly to her mouth—a gesture so like her father's that Tamsin's throat tightened—then lowered her hand. "He used to say a map is a promise made in ink. That whoever draws the line owes the line truth. They tortured him to make him unsay his promises, and he wouldn't, and his heart gave out on the sixth day, or their hands did the work, and the physician wrote *febre* because fevers are respectable." She looked at the lantern where it stood sentinel by the cold hearth. "So. You'll stay tonight. There's a cellar room behind the wine racks; the merchant I married built it for smuggled brandy and never knew his wife would use it for worse contraband than drink. You leave before first light, down to the boats. And you—" to Tamsin, "—will spend the evening telling me every name in your private ledger, and I will write them in my hand, in my cipher, because paper survives better when it exists in more than one place. My father taught me that much before I ran from him. It took his death to teach me why."

* * *

Night settled over the house in layers. Marisol wrote by candlelight with a speed that betrayed long-buried fluency; Paloma slept sitting upright beside the lantern, one hand resting on its frame the way other people rest a hand on a sleeping child's chest; and Tamsin recited names into the warm dark of a silk merchant's parlor—names of households, names of hamlets, ages and trades and dead husbands—until his voice went hoarse and Marisol's pen kept moving.

It was near midnight when she paused, pen lifted, and asked, without looking up: "The surveyor. The friend of the ministry. You'd be too young to know the name, but I'll say it anyway, because saying things aloud is how they stop being weapons. Cordeiro. António Cordeiro."

Tamsin felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair.

"He sits above compilation now," Marisol said, watching him. "Doesn't he. You know the face."

"I know the face," Tamsin said. "He signs the orders that cross my desk. He signed the order to burn the maps." He swallowed. "And yesterday morning, at the Alfama gate, a guard let us pass who shouldn't have—who looked at me like a man filing something away. Men like Cordeiro keep guards the way spiders keep webs."

Outside, far off toward the river, a bell began to ring—not the hour, but the alarm peal, urgent and arrhythmic, and Marisol blew out the candle in the same motion that had her on her feet.

"They've traced you," she breathed. "Get the lantern. We leave now, ahead of first light, and we pray the bridge of boats is crowded enough to hide what crosses it."
