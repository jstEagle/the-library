## 7. Second Checkpoint: The Bridge of Boats

The river told us before the soldiers did.

We came down through the tanneries where the air hung thick enough to chew, and Paloma—no, I am Paloma; grief has made even my own name strange to me—I heard the Tagus before I saw it: a sound like a congregation murmuring, hundreds of voices gathered at the water's edge. At this hour there should have been only gulls and the groan of moored hulls.

Marisol stopped beneath a dripping archway and pressed two fingers to my shoulder. "Wait."

She had walked ahead of us since dawn without speaking more than necessary, this woman who had learned her father's face from engravings and his habits from other people's stories. She moved like someone who had rehearsed a life she never got to live. Now she stood listening with her head tilted, and I understood that she was hearing what I heard.

"Books," she said. "They're burning books on the bank."

* * *

We saw them from the shadow of a rope-walk: the Bridge of Boats stretched across the Tagus on its spine of linked pontoons, planks rising and falling with the current, and on the near bank a bonfire that was not for warmth. Soldiers fed it from hand-carts—ledgers, pamphlets, folios, their pages catching light in brief orange blossoms before collapsing into ash that the wind carried out over the water like black snow. A sergeant read names from a list as each bundle went in, as though the fire required an inventory, as though paper could be properly killed only if its titles were pronounced aloud.

And among the soldiers, apart from them, stood a man in a grey coat with no insignia worth noticing.

I knew him. Of course I knew him. He had been the third guard at the Alfama Gate, the one who hadn't spoken, the one who had watched Tamsin pass with an expression I had mistaken for boredom. It had not been boredom. It had been recognition, filed away like a specimen in a drawer. He had followed us. Or he had simply known where we would have to go, because there was only one bridge, and everyone must cross it, and the crown's great trick was patience dressed up as procedure.

Tamsin saw him a moment after I did. I felt it in the way his breathing changed beside me—the small hitch of a man discovering that the net has been around him all along.

"That's Ferro," he said quietly. "He audits census offices. He sat two desks down from mine for four years and I never once wondered what he did with the ledgers he took home." His voice had gone flat, which was how I had learned to recognize his fear; when Tamsin Reyes was truly afraid, he sounded like a man reading aloud from a document he despised. "If he sees me here, with you, carrying that—" He didn't finish. The lantern swung gently from my hand between us, its false bottom heavy with engraved copper plates wrapped in oilcloth, forty pounds of a village that officially did not exist.

"We can't go back," I said. "The printer's ship sails on tomorrow's tide whether we reach it or not."

"I know." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, a clerk's gesture, exhausted and precise. "I keep trying to think of it as a route problem. Checkpoint, checkpoint, checkpoint. But they've turned the crossing itself into a pyre, Paloma. They want us to watch. That's part of the procedure. You walk past your own death-in-miniature so you understand what waits if you're caught."

Then Marisol said, "I'll cross first."

* * *

We argued in the rope-walk's stinking dark, in whispers, while the fire roared beyond the wall. She would hear none of it.

"He knows your faces," she said to me and Tamsin. "He doesn't know mine. And I am a woman walking alone toward a bridge, which is the most invisible thing in Lisbon. They'll stop me because stopping women is easy work. And while they're stopped—" She shrugged, elegant, terrible. "You'll be behind me, heads down, and by the time they're finished being thorough with a widow from the tannery district, you'll be mid-river."

"You don't know they'll let you go," I said. "Marisol—"

"No." Her voice cracked open just slightly, and inside it was something I recognized from my master's workshop in the last weeks of his life: the sound of a person who had already done their arithmetic. "Paloma. My father died for these plates. Do you understand what that means? It means I spent thirty-one years believing he abandoned us—that he loved his maps more than his daughter—and then a stranger arrives at my door carrying the proof that he died protecting something, and the something is *this*, is Vila Serena, is every village they scraped off the earth and pretended was never there." She took my wrist. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "I cannot get my father back. I cannot get those years back. But I can put you on that boat. Let me have the one thing grief is actually good for."

Tamsin looked away. When he spoke, it was to the fire. "When they question her, she'll give a name. If it's a real name, they'll check the registers, and if the registers show a woman of that name living where she says—"

"They will," Marisol said. "Ana Furtado, Rua dos Tanqueiros. My mother's name. My mother's street. My mother has been dead nine years and the census still lists her, because no one corrects the records of the poor—they only add to them. Your own office taught me that, census man." She smiled at him without kindness and without cruelty, just the flat honesty of the condemned. "Your empire keeps such beautiful lies. I intend to live inside one for ten minutes."

* * *

She crossed the mudflat to the pontoon-bridge like a woman going to market, shawl over her head, basket on her arm, and I hated how convincing it was—how ordinary. The guards at the bridgehead straightened as she approached, and one of them, young, sunburned, held up a hand, and she stopped, and the pantomime began: questions, gestures, her patient shaking of the head, the slow production of papers from the basket.

Ferro did not move at first. He stood at the edge of the book-fire with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the flames, and my whole body prayed silently, furiously, *don't turn, don't turn*, the way I had once prayed over a plate of etching acid that the lines would hold.

Then he turned. Not toward Marisol. Toward the fire, where a soldier had just heaved on a new cartload—and Ferro raised one finger, and the burning paused, and the sergeant consulted his list, and for ninety seconds the entire apparatus of the checkpoint focused on a single bundle of confiscated maps while a hundred eyes looked nowhere else.

"Now," Tamsin breathed.

We went. Heads down, the lantern disguised as a grave-goods chest between us—we had dressed it in mourning-cloth at Marisol's instruction, a widow's tribute being ferried across the river to a family plot, and grief, like Marisol, was invisible if it walked slowly enough. The pontoon bridge rose and fell beneath our feet, each plank a small surrender to the current, and I counted the boats beneath us the way my master had taught me to count anything frightening: *one, two, three*—the plates against my hip with every step, heavier than lead, lighter than nothing—

A shout from the bank.

Not for us. For Marisol.

"—not on the list, senhora, and this basket will need emptying, all of it, now—"

She was arguing. She was arguing *well*, loudly, invoking a brother-in-law who owed money to somebody's cousin, and the young guard was flushing, and a second guard had come to stand too close to her, and Ferro had begun to walk toward the commotion with the unhurried stride of a man who suspected everything and could prove nothing and enjoyed the suspicion anyway.

Halfway across, Tamsin grabbed my sleeve. "Paloma. Look at me. We keep walking or we turn around, and if we turn around everything she's doing right now is waste. *Keep walking.*"

So I kept walking. It is the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than hiding plates in a dead man's lantern, harder than leaving Lisbon: walking away from the sound of a woman spending herself for strangers, plank by plank, while pretending to be a mourner. The far bank came up to meet us. A bored corporal glanced at the mourning cloth, at my red eyes—which were not performed—at Tamsin's clerk's stoop, and waved us through with two fingers, already turning back to the spectacle on the near shore.

Behind us, I heard Marisol say, clearly, in her mother's proud cadence: "Then arrest me, but you'll write down that Ana Furtado of the Rua dos Tanqueiros asked you a simple question: *what village are you burning today?*"

And I heard Ferro answer, softly, in a voice meant to carry: "Take her in. All of her. Basket and all."

* * *

On the far bank, in the reeds below the customs house, Tamsin was sick. Quietly, thoroughly, a clerk purging himself of arithmetic. I stood over him holding the lantern and did not pretend I was better than he was. My hands shook so badly the plates rattled inside the false bottom like teeth.

"She knew," I said, when he could speak. "She planned it knowing."

"Yes."

"And we let her."

"Yes." He wiped his mouth and stood, and there was something new in his face—not the flatness of fear anymore but something rawer, less manageable. Guilt, maybe. Or its twin, obligation, wearing guilt's clothes. "In my office we had a word for people like her. *Colateral.* Collateral. Damage measured in units. I wrote that word in ledgers, Paloma. I wrote it about villages." He looked at the bridge, where Marisol was being marched away between two soldiers, her chin high, her basket dangling from a guard's fist. "I'm done writing it."

"What does that mean?" I asked. "Concretely. What does 'done' look like for a man like you?"

He turned to me, and the river-light moved across his face, and for the first time since the Alfama Gate I saw not a liability or an ally or a census man, but something I couldn't yet name—a person deciding what he would be when the cost arrived.

"It means at the third checkpoint, I'm not hiding," he said. "It means Ferro gets ledgers. Mine. Every erased village, every corrected line, every lie I ever transcribed—and I sign them. Because she gave herself for copper plates, and the least I owe her is to make sure that when they catch up to us, they can't pretend we were smugglers." His jaw set. "They'll have to call us witnesses."

Downriver, masts swayed above the rooftops. Somewhere among them rode the exile ship, and aboard it a printer waiting for cargo that had never before been printed: an atlas of everything the crown insisted did not exist.

One checkpoint left. One sacrifice already paid.

Tamsin picked up his end of the lantern.
