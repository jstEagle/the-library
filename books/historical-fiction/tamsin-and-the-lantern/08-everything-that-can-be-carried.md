## 8. Everything That Can Be Carried

**Paloma**

The salt cellar smelled of the sea's old ghost—brine soaked into timber so deep that no decade could draw it out. Paloma sat with her back against a barrel stave gone soft with damp, the lantern open in her lap, counting the copper plates by touch because there was no light to count them by. Fourteen. All fourteen. Her master's life pressed against her knees in cold metal, and somewhere above them the tide was turning, and somewhere beyond that the soldiers were turning over every crate and coffin in the Alfama looking for a funeral that had never buried anyone.

Tamsin paced. He was good at pacing. He had the particular restlessness of a man who had spent years walking door to door, asking questions, and now had no doors to knock on except the ones in his own skull.

"We should divide them," he said for the third time. "The plates. Take the six lightest, leave the rest buried under the salt. No one digs through a salt cellar. Come back after the ship—"

"After the ship sails." She said it flatly, not as a question. "And when does it come back, Tamsin? When does any ship come back for fourteen copper plates buried under salt in a city that burns maps?"

"Ships come back."

"Ships come back for cargo worth the return." She lifted one plate into the thin gray light from the grating above—a river, rendered in her master's hand so fine the water seemed to move. "This is the Tagus as it was eleven years ago. Look at the north bank. Look at what is written there."

He looked. He had looked before. She made him look again anyway, because looking was a kind of penance, and she was not the only one who owed one.

"Vila Serena," he said quietly.

"Vila Serena. Eleven years ago there was a village on the north bank with a church and a well and ninety-three souls, and my master drew every roof of it. Now the crown says the bank is empty. Now the census says it. Now *you* say it, in a ledger with your own hand at the bottom of the page." She slid the plate back into the lantern. "You want to leave half of them behind because they are heavy. Tamsin. A village is heavy. That is what no one wants to carry."

He stopped pacing. In the dark she could not see his face well, only the shape of him against the fainter dark, and the sound of his breathing, which had changed—shorter, tighter, the breathing of a man standing at the edge of something.

"Paloma—"

"Don't." She held up a hand he probably couldn't see. "Don't tell me it was orders. Don't tell me you didn't know. You're going to tell me both, and I'm going to sit here in the dark with ninety-three souls in my lap and listen, because there is nowhere else to go until the tide turns, and God forgive me, I want to hear how you say it."

The silence stretched. Above them, footsteps crossed the grating—two sets, unhurried, the walk of men with no reason to hurry—and passed. The salt cellar held its breath with them.

* * *

**Tamsin**

He had rehearsed confessions before. That was part of the work—getting a man to the moment where the truth came out of him like a splinter, all at once, with blood. He had never been on the other side of it. He understood now why men wept at their own doorsteps.

"The census doesn't ask if a place exists," he began. "It asks how many. How many souls, how many hearths, how many head of cattle, how many loaves owed in tithe. You fill in the numbers and you sign, and the numbers go to Lisbon, and Lisbon makes the world out of them." His voice sounded strange to himself, stripped of its office cadence. "Eleven years ago I was newly appointed. My first circuit was the north bank of the Tagus. I stood in Vila Serena. I drank water from their well. A woman gave me figs because she thought the crown's man visiting meant the crown had remembered them."

Paloma said nothing. The dark said nothing. He went on because stopping would have been worse.

"The second year, my instructions changed. The north bank was no longer on my circuit. I asked my superior why, and he showed me the master ledger, and Vila Serena was not in it. Not struck through. Not amended. *Not in it*, as if the ink had never touched the page. And my own first-year returns—mine, in my hand—were not in the archive either. I told myself the records had been reorganized. I told myself a great many things." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry broken sound. "Do you know what I told the widow last month, when she said she lived in Vila Serena? I told her she was confused. I stood in her doorway with my ledger and I told a woman standing in her own house that her house did not exist. And she looked at me the way you look at a man who has been robbed and doesn't know it yet, and she said—"

He stopped. The words had carried him this far and now refused the last step.

"Say it," Paloma said softly. "Say what she said."

"She said: *Then who do you think took it?*"

The tide turned somewhere below them; he felt it in the timbers, a long slow shudder through the floor of the world.

"I went back through eleven years of my own ledgers after that," he said. "Every year, the north bank. Every year, fewer souls. Ninety-three, then eighty-one, then sixty, then the year the numbers simply stopped—because I stopped writing them. Because my instructions said the circuit was changed, and I was a good clerk, and good clerks write what they are told exists." He sat down across from her, finally still. "You asked me once why I followed the widow's trail to the docks. That's why. Because somewhere between the first year and the last, I became one of the instruments that erased her. And an instrument that begins to notice the hand that holds it—"

"—is a dangerous instrument," Paloma finished. "Or a broken one."

"Both. Often both."

* * *

**Paloma**

She had wanted to hate him. She discovered, sitting in the dark with the salt stinging the raw skin of her hands, that she had been carrying the hatred ready-made since the workshop—since the inspector's boots on her master's floor, since the criers in the square, since the word *burn* spoken in the beautiful bored voice of officialdom. It was easier to aim it at a man within reach than at a crown beyond it.

And he had made it easy. He had stood in the widow's doorway with his ledger and his certainty, and Paloma had thought: *there, that face, that is the face of the thing that killed him.* Her master had not died of his cough. She had known it since Marisol's kitchen, since the word *interrogated* fell between them like a dropped plate. Men who drew the truth were interrogated. Men who wrote the lie went home to supper.

But he was here. In a salt cellar, smelling of brine and fear, having walked away from his salary, his office, his name—having confessed the worst of it without being asked twice. Her master used to say that a map was a promise: *this is what is here, and I stood here, and I swear it.* Tamsin Reyes was, in his clumsy, ledger-keeping way, trying to keep a promise eleven years late.

"Give me your hand," she said.

She heard rather than saw his hesitation. Then his hand found hers in the dark—dry, ink-stained at the fingertips, a clerk's hand, trembling slightly.

"Do you feel that?" She pressed his fingers against the lantern's frame, against the plates inside, cold as coins from a dead man's pocket. "Fourteen plates. The weight you want to leave in the salt. Now feel this." She turned his hand over and pressed his palm flat against his own chest. "That is the other weight. The one you've been carrying eleven years. It doesn't get lighter when you set things down, Tamsin. It only gets lighter when you carry what you should have carried all along."

His breath caught. In the dark, she felt him understand it—the whole arithmetic of it, the terrible simple sum.

"So we carry all fourteen," he said. "Even though the third checkpoint—"

"Especially because of the third checkpoint. If we're stopped, if they take the lantern, let them take everything. A half-truth burned is still a half-truth saved. But a whole truth—" she closed the lantern's little door, and the click of it was very loud in the dark, "—a whole truth is worth a whole life. His and ours."

* * *

**Tamsin**

They waited for the tide and the dark together, and the waiting was different now. He noticed it the way a man notices a fever breaking. For three days they had moved together like two animals sharing a den—watchful, economical, every kindness a transaction. Now she was showing him, by the feel of her fingers in the dark, how the plates nested in the lantern's frame, which corner bore her master's maker's mark, how the false bottom opened. She was teaching him the atlas. She was making him a keeper of it, and they both understood what that meant: if one of them was taken, the other would still know what the world had looked like before the burning.

"The printer's ship leaves on the morning tide tomorrow," she murmured. "The cellar door under the shop opens to the water stair. My master drew it once—a map of the printer's own cellar, because the printer was a friend, and because my master trusted no door he hadn't drawn himself."

"Then we go in the last hour of dark. The checkpoint guards change at the fourth bell; the new watch is slow to settle." He heard himself planning it—routes, timings, the small betrayals of procedure he had spent a career learning—and realized he was no longer the liability in their arrangement. Or rather, that he had never been only that. "There's one thing you should know about the third checkpoint, though. It isn't soldiers at the door of the print shop. It's an examiner of records. A crown man with authority to open anything, read anything, arrest anyone."

Paloma was quiet a moment. "You know him."

"I know *of* him. Everyone in the census office knows of him." Tamsin's mouth went dry. "He was my superior's superior. And the last man who stood in his office asking questions about the north bank of the Tagus—"

"—was my master," Paloma said.

Above them, the grating rattled. Not footsteps this time—something dragged across it, heavy, deliberate, and then a voice, muffled by timber and salt, calling down into the dark of the cellar with the beautiful bored voice of officialdom:

"Anyone below? Answer, or we smoke you out."

Tamsin's hand found the lantern in the dark. Paloma's hand found his.

They did not answer. Above them, the dragging began again—closer now, methodical, a man who had all night and a crown behind him—and somewhere beyond the cellar walls, down at the water stair, the black tide of the Tagus was already turning toward morning.
