## 9. Third Checkpoint: The Printer's Door

The dragging stopped directly above them.

Paloma held her breath and listened the way her master had taught her to listen to paper—not for sounds, but for what sounds concealed. The scrape of boots on the grating told her there were at least three men up there; the creak of a chair being set down told her they intended to stay; and the small, wet sound of a wine flask unstoppered told her they were not afraid, which frightened her more than anything else could have.

"They're settling in," Tamsin breathed against her ear. His voice in the dark was barely more than warmth. "They know something is coming through this cellar tonight."

"Then we go now, before—"

"No." His hand tightened on hers. In the blackness she felt him shake his head. "The moment that grate lifts, every lantern on this street turns toward us. We'd be caught between the stairs and the water with nowhere to run but the Tagus, and I've seen how well either of us swims under a load of copper plates."

Above them, one of the soldiers laughed at something—the easy laughter of men who had already won—and the sound came down through the timber like rain through a rotten roof.

Paloma sat back against the cold brick and let the darkness hold her. They had come so far. Through the Alfama Gate inside a coffin-cart with Marisol's borrowed mourning veils scratchy against their faces. Across the Bridge of Boats while the smoke of burning books turned the dawn the color of an old bruise, while Marisol walked back toward the soldiers with her chin lifted and her father's name in her mouth like a blade she meant to use. Three days in a salt cellar eating hard bread and listening to Paloma recite the atlas from memory because saying it aloud was the only way she could bear the silence.

And now the printer's door—Senhor Baptista's print shop, the last door between the atlas and the exile ship *Nossa Senhora das Dores*, which would sail on the morning tide whether its cargo arrived or not.

Through the wall, muffled by brick and salt-rot, she could hear the shop above: the familiar percussion of her trade. The rhythmic groan of the press screw. The slap of dampened paper against the forme. A man's voice counting impressions under his breath. Baptista was printing tonight, working by candlelight as mapmakers and printers always worked when the work mattered, and the soldiers upstairs had let him continue because a busy workshop was an innocent workshop, and because they were waiting for whatever was supposed to come up out of his cellar.

"The ship," Paloma whispered. "How far?"

"Tide stairs are behind the warehouse, maybe sixty paces from the cellar door." She heard him moving in the dark, the soft rustle of him unbuckling the satchel he had carried since the bridge. "Sixty paces across an open yard with soldiers on the street side and a man watching the water stair. I counted four when we came down."

"Five," Paloma said. "There was a fifth asleep against the bollard. He may be awake now."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—flattened into something careful and deliberate, the voice of a census taker reading a column of figures he did not like.

"Paloma. There's a way this ends with you on that ship."

"There is no version of this that ends any other way. That was the whole design of it."

"Listen to me." His hand found her shoulder in the dark, gripped it. "You cannot fight five men carrying a lantern full of copper. You cannot outrun them. But they aren't looking for me."

"They're looking for anyone."

"They're looking for a cartographer and stolen plates. That's what the order says—I've read the circulars, Paloma, I wrote half of them. *One woman, unknown accomplice.*" He drew a breath, and in the dark she heard him doing arithmetic, the terrible kind, the kind that subtracted a person from the world. "I am known here. I'm a crown officer. If I walk up those stairs with my ledgers under my arm and my credentials in my hand and tell them I've been tracking the smuggled atlas myself—that I traced it to this cellar—they will believe me. Why wouldn't they? It's exactly the story their superiors want to hear. It makes them competent. It makes everyone look good. Bureaucracy devours its own lies as happily as it devours villages."

"And then what happens to you?" Her voice cracked on the words, and she hated it. "Tamsin. You saw what they did to my master. He was eighty-one years old and they questioned him until his heart gave out, and they called it illness, and no one—"

"I know what happens." Very quietly. "I helped make the forms for what happens."

* * *

They argued for another hour, there in the dark, while above them the soldiers drank and below them the press groaned out its patient count. Paloma laid out every alternative she could invent—waiting for the changing of the watch, bribing the man at the water stair, swimming the plates across in oilskins—and Tamsin dismantled each one with the same flat patience he used on fraudulent household returns, until she ran out of alternatives and found herself sitting in the dark with nothing left but the truth: that he was going to walk up those stairs and give himself to the thing that had killed her master, so that she could carry the light past it.

"You'll testify," she said finally. "Publicly. Before magistrates, before witnesses. You said it yourself—their testimony too public to disappear. Make yourself impossible to lose."

"I intend to be extremely inconvenient," Tamsin said. "I have been practicing inconvenience my whole career without knowing it. Every ledger entry I ever fudged, every village I rounded down, every widow whose answers didn't suit my superiors—it all goes into the record tomorrow. Vila Serena exists, Paloma. I have the returns. I interviewed the woman myself. My signature is on documents that prove the crown erased eleven hundred people from its own books." A dry, strange laugh. "Do you know what a gift that is? All those years I thought I was complicit. Turns out I was building evidence."

In the darkness, Paloma reached out and touched his face. She felt the stubble, the hollow under his cheekbone where three days of salt-cellar bread had carved him, the wet track at the corner of his eye that he would deny later.

"When the lantern lights," she said, "you'll see it from wherever they keep you. Everyone will."

"That," said Tamsin Reyes, "is rather the point."

* * *

They spent the last hour preparing, and preparation looked like prayer performed by hands. Paloma lifted the plates from their wrappings and checked each one by touch, counting the raised lines of ink the way a mother counts fingers. She rewrapped them in oilcloth, nested them in the lantern frame her master had built—hollowed oak, lead-lined, disguised as a grave-goods chest heavy enough to excuse any mourner's slow pace. The atlas fit the way grief fits a body: perfectly, and with room left over.

Then she took the candle stub from her pocket and lit the lantern itself, just once, cupping the flame with her hands so it made only a coin of light between them. In that small glow Tamsin's face appeared to her—tired, terrified, resolved—and she understood that terror and courage were not opposites after all but the same substance poured into different vessels, and that what he was about to do was both things at once and could not be separated, ever, by anyone.

"For Vila Serena," she said.

He nodded. Then, because he was Tamsin, he added: "For the widow who answered one question too honestly. Write her name in the margin of the first plate. Whatever your real name is. People should be remembered by name."

"Her name is Beatriz Nunes," Paloma said. "And mine is Paloma Almeida. You can put that in your testimony too."

He smiled—a real smile, brief and crooked—and blew out the candle.

The stairs went up twelve steps to a trapdoor. They climbed them together in silence, and at the top they stood a moment in the dark like two people at a church door deciding whether to enter, and then Tamsin pressed his forehead briefly against hers, and then he pushed the trapdoor open and went up into the light, and Paloma was alone with the weight of everything that could be carried.

She heard it all from below.

The soldiers' surprise, turning quickly to satisfaction—one of them actually said *well, well* in the tone of a man finding money in the street. Tamsin's voice, steady and bored and official, reciting his office, his name, his rank, producing papers with the crisp snap of a man who had done ten thousand inspections and resented every one. The word *cellar* spoken by a soldier. The word *ledgers* spoken by Tamsin. And then the beautiful, fatal bait, delivered with the weary contempt of a true bureaucrat: "The woman isn't here yet, gentlemen. She's due within the hour—I've tracked her from the Alfama since Tuesday. If you want the arrest to be yours and not the Intendant's, I suggest you stop drinking and post yourselves properly at the water stair, where she'll have to come from the river side, because I've sealed the street doors. Now do you want my report or don't you?"

Boots moved. Voices argued about credit and rank. And the trapdoor, blessedly, stayed shut—because Tamsin had told them he'd sealed it, and no soldier alive contradicts a crown officer's paperwork.

Paloma waited until the last boot faded toward the water stair. Then she shouldered the lantern, took the candle stub between her teeth, and climbed.

* * *

The cellar door opened onto the print shop's back room, and for one breath Paloma stood in the warm smell of ink and oil and hot metal, the smell of her whole life, and let it fill her. Beyond the partition she could hear the press still working—Baptista, magnificent old man, printing through the siege as though soldiers were weather. She crossed the room in six steps, found the cellar stair to the yard as Tamsin had described it, and paused with her hand on the latch.

Through the partition gap she could see him: Tamsin at the shop counter, ledgers open, surrounded by three soldiers, talking—endlessly, magnificently talking—his finger tracing columns of figures while the soldiers' eyes glazed. Buying her minutes with each page. He did not look toward the partition. He never once looked toward the partition, because looking would have condemned her, and not looking was the bravest thing she had ever watched a man do.

Paloma slipped out into the yard.

The night was huge and cold and smelled of tar and river. Sixty paces of open ground lay between her and the tide stair, and at the far end of it a single soldier stood at the rail, his back to her, watching the black water where the *Nossa Senhora das Dores* rode at anchor with a printer's apprentice waiting aboard. Paloma lowered her head, bent her knees into the shuffle of a laborer under a load, and began to cross—counting the paces as her master had taught her to count everything, one, two, three, the lantern's weight biting into her shoulder, four, five, the soldier at the rail shifting, turning—

Halfway. Twenty-nine paces left. The soldier's lantern swung toward her, light crawling across the cobbles toward her feet—

And behind her, in the print shop, a crash. Shouting. Tamsin's voice rising suddenly, deliberately, pitched to carry across the water: "*There she goes—there! Stop her!*"—and every head on the waterfront turned toward the sound of the cry, including the soldier at the rail, including, God forgive her, Paloma herself for one traitorous heartbeat—

She ran.

Thirty paces became twenty-five became fifteen, the lantern hammering her hip, the tide stair rising to meet her, the boat below with its single silent oarsman, and behind her the yard erupting into torchlight and confusion, Tamsin's false alarm dissolving into real chaos as the soldiers realized they'd been played, as someone shouted *the cellar, check the cellar*, as the whole night came apart around the small stubborn flame she carried.

Hands pulled her into the boat. Oars bit water. The Tagus took them.

From the stern, gasping, Paloma looked back. On the print shop's step, flanked by furious soldiers, stood Tamsin Reyes—alive, upright, hands bound but head high, already arguing with them, already making himself inconvenient. As the boat swept past the warehouse point, he turned his head and found her across forty yards of black water, and though he could not possibly have seen her face in the dark, he smiled.

Then the current took the bend, and Lisbon slid away, and the lantern at Paloma's feet threw its small gold circle onto the boards as the first grey light of morning rose off the sea ahead—where, tied to the mainmast rail, a printed proof sheet snapped and fluttered in the wind like a flag.

On it, in fresh black ink, she could already read the title.

*The Atlas of What Was Taken.*

Baptista had begun without waiting.
