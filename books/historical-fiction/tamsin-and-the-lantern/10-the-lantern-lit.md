## 10. The Lantern Lit

The tide turned at dawn, and with it came the sound of boots.

Paloma stood at the rail of the *Nossa Senhora das Dores*—the exile ship, the printer's ship, the ship that had been a rumor for three years and was now wood beneath her feet—and watched them bring Tamsin Reyes down the quay. Four soldiers, one on either side, one ahead, one behind, as though a census taker with ink-stained fingers required the honors of a general. His ledgers were strapped to his back in a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He had insisted on carrying them himself.

*Let them see what they are confiscating,* he had said. *Let every man on that dock watch a clerk surrender his own arithmetic.*

He walked badly. Three days in a salt cellar will do that to any spine, however principled. But his chin was up, and when the soldiers reached the foot of the gangplank where the harbor master waited with papers, Tamsin stopped—not dragged, *stopped*, on his own legs—and turned once to look out across the water.

Paloma did not know if he could see her. The morning haze lay on the Tagus like breath on glass, and the rail she gripped was wet with it. But he looked directly at the ship, at the place where she stood, for the length of three heartbeats. Then he inclined his head—the smallest bow, the kind a man gives a lady across a crowded church—and let them take him away along the quay toward the customs house, where the Inquisition's men would want to know how a crown census taker came to be carrying evidence of erased villages into exile.

They could not hang him quickly. That was the arithmetic she clung to, the only sum that gave her comfort. There were too many witnesses now. The printers had worked by candlelight through two nights, and the first copies were already loose in the city—in a priest's satchel bound for Coimbra, in an English merchant's trunk bound for London, sewn into the lining of a widow's coat bound for anywhere at all. A hundred and forty sheets, each one a village that officially had never existed, each one signed in the corner with her master's cipher and, beneath it, in smaller type: *compiled from the testimony of T. Reyes, census taker.* You cannot make a man disappear after you have made him famous. The crown understood this. It was why they burned maps quietly, in courtyards, before breakfast. And it was why Tamsin Reyes would be marched through daylight, in view of the whole quay, to a cell rather than a grave—for now.

"For now" is a word sailors use the way other men use "amen."

* * *

The hold smelled of ink and salt and lamp oil. Senhor Baptiste, the exiled printer, moved among his crates like a shepherd among unruly sheep, counting, recounting, muttering in French and Portuguese and a third language Paloma suspected he invented purely for blasphemy. He had lost two fingers to a press in Rouen and gained, he said, a proper appreciation for what paper weighed.

"You'll want to see this," he said, and handed her a copy still warm.

She held the atlas the way she had once held a newborn lamb at her aunt's farm outside Évora—two hands, no grip, all awe. The cover bore no title. Her master had taught her that a forbidden book should look like nothing: plain boards, unbleached linen spine, a book that could sit on a shelf between a psalter and a cookbook and never once be asked for its papers. But inside—

Inside, Vila Serena existed.

There it was, in ink that would not fade: the village on its bluff above the river, forty-one households, the chapel with its crooked bell tower, the olive terraces climbing the hillside in their patient gray-green rows. There was the well. There was the fig tree at the crossroads under which, the widow Domingas had sworn, she had buried her husband's letters because she could not bear to burn them. The map did not say *this village was destroyed by order of the Crown on such a date, and its people scattered, and its name struck from the rolls.* It did not need to. It simply drew the village as it was, in the fine confident line of her master's hand, and beside it, in the margin, in her own smaller script: *Here lived 214 souls. Ask for Domingas Alves. She remembers.*

Memory itself is contraband. She had thought it a bitter joke when they'd hidden the plates in the lantern. Now she understood it as a trade. Every map is a memory pressed flat; every archive is a country refusing to forget itself. The crown could burn paper all day long. It could not burn what a hundred and forty copies would do inside a hundred and forty skulls, each one teaching some stranger in some distant port to ask: *and what else have they struck out?*

Baptiste watched her face. "Your master would have wept," he said.

"He never wept." She smoothed the margin with her thumb. "He measured. He said grief was like surveying—you cannot map the whole territory at once. You take it a chain-length at a time."

"A wise man."

"He died for it." She said it without flinching, which surprised her. Somewhere between the Alfama gate and the Bridge of Boats, between Marisol's diversion and the soldiers' torches on the riverbank, the grief had changed state, the way water changes to ice: same substance, different weight-bearing strength. "I used to think he died of fever. Then I thought he died of betrayal. Now I think—" She turned the page. Another village. Another count of souls. "I think he died the way a seed dies. Which is to say, he doesn't consider it dying at all."

* * *

They sailed on the afternoon tide.

From the quarterdeck she watched Lisbon shrink—not the way travelers say it, sentimentally, the beloved city dwindling like a portrait carried off into distance. It shrank the way a map shrinks when you step back from it: details resolving, proportions correcting. From the river, the Alfama gate was a dark notch in a pale wall. The bridge of boats was a thread. The courtyard where the soldiers burned confiscated books was invisible entirely, though she knew its exact coordinates to the pace. This, she thought, was her master's final lesson, delivered posthumously: distance does not diminish a thing. It makes it legible.

Tamsin was somewhere behind those walls. Alive. Marched in daylight, examined by clerks who would find his ledgers meticulous and damning and impossible to suppress, because he had arranged—as only a census man could arrange—that copies of everything existed in three places, and that the location of the third was known to a notary who hated the governor. Their testimony was too public to bury. He had told her this in the salt cellar, in the dark, with his hand steady on the crate between them: *"They built a machine for making people vanish. So we will make ourselves impossible to vanish."*

She believed him. Belief, she had learned, was also a thing one surveyed a chain-length at a time.

A gull cried overhead, insolent and white. The wind came up the river smelling of tar and pine and the open Atlantic beyond, and the sails took it with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Evening found her alone at the stern rail.

The lantern sat on the deck beside her feet. It looked smaller than it ever had in the workshop—hollowed, disguised, its false bottom empty now, its plates gone to the press and thence to the world. A plain thing. Tin and horn and a stub of candle. Her master had built it for exactly one night such as this, though he could not have known the night, only that such nights come for every honest mapmaker eventually, and that a wise man prepares his escape the way he prepares his margins: carefully, and in advance, and with room for error.

Paloma lifted it onto the rail.

Below, the water ran black and silver. Behind, Lisbon's lamps were coming alive one by one, a constellation pinned to the shore, each flame a household, a ledger, a lie or a truth—she could no longer tell the difference from here, and perhaps there was no difference left to tell. Ahead lay nothing but ocean and weather and whatever ports would take a woman with no country's permission to be anywhere.

She struck the match. The sulfur flared, steadied, and she touched it to the wick.

The lantern lit. Its glow was small—it would always be small; her master had chosen the horn panes precisely so the light would be modest, survivable, the light of a thing meant to last rather than to blaze—and it fell across the deck planks and her own hands and the single copy of the atlas she had kept back from the crates, the first one off the press, the proof sheet with her master's cipher and Tamsin's name and her own marginal hand: *Here lived 214 souls.*

"You told me once," she said to the lantern, to the sea, to the man who had taught her that a map is a promise made to the future, "that a cartographer's work is never finished, only abandoned. That the next hand always takes up the pen." The flame trembled in the evening air. "You didn't tell me the next hand would be mine. I'd have practiced my penmanship."

Somewhere behind her, in a cell above the customs house, Tamsin Reyes was doing what he did best: asking questions, writing answers, making the record true. Somewhere ahead, in London or Coimbra or Amsterdam, a stranger would open a plain-covered book and find a village that officially never existed, and would feel something shift in the floor of their certainty, and would begin—without knowing they had begun—to doubt. That was the cargo. That had always been the cargo. Not the plates, not the paper: the doubt, the question, the lantern passed hand to hand down a darkness no single person could map alone.

A voice at her elbow: Baptiste, come to trim the lamps. He looked at the lantern burning on the rail, at the small stubborn light, and said nothing, because printers know when a page needs no further correction.

Paloma stood watch until full dark, the lantern lit at the rail, guiding no one and everyone—guiding, most of all, herself, out of the harbor of everything she had lost and into the open water of everything she had yet to carry. When at last she took up the atlas and turned below, the flame stayed burning behind her, and the ship ran on into the black Atlantic like a sentence that refuses, even now, even here, to end.

And far astern, faint past all measuring, a second light answered from the customs house window—a candle set in glass, where a census taker sat writing, and would go on writing, for as long as it took.
