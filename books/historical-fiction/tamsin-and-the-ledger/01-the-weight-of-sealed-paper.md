## 1. The Weight of Sealed Paper

The commission came on a Tuesday, in an envelope with no seal, which was how Astrid Nystrom knew it mattered. Sealed envelopes were for people who wanted their business known. Unsealed ones were for people who wanted plausible denial — the sender's, never the courier's.

She read it standing at the window of the Records Annex, fourth floor, where she had spent eleven years alphabetizing other people's decisions. Below her, the Avenue of the Republic was doing its winter impression of a river: grey, slow, choked with debris that used to be certainty. A bread queue had formed outside the bakery on the corner, and half the people in it were carrying luggage, which told you everything about what they expected to find at the end of the queue.

*Courier, First Class,* the letter said. *Assignment: diplomatic materials to the neutral port of Marrow Quay, for onward transmission by sea.* It was signed by a deputy undersecretary whose name she'd have to look up, and countersigned by someone whose signature had been stamped rather than written, which meant he was already gone.

Astrid read it twice and felt nothing she would have called fear. What she felt was closer to relief. Eleven years in the Annex had taught her the one law that governed the capital's final weeks: the important people were dying or fleeing, and the unimportant ones were being handed their work.

She folded the letter into quarters and put it in her coat pocket, over her heart, because there was nowhere else to put anything anymore. The ministry had confiscated the lockers in October when a clerk in Trade was discovered to be keeping more than coats in his.

"Congratulations," said Pell, from the next desk, without looking up. He was sixty, rheumatic, and had survived four governments by the simple method of being too dull to arrest. "You've been promoted to expendable."

"I've been promoted to mobile," Astrid said. "There's a difference."

"Not much of one, these days." Pell blew his nose. Outside, a tram went past with its windows broken out, still running on schedule, because the trams didn't know yet that the republic was ending. "Where are they sending you?"

"Marrow Quay."

Pell did look up then, just for a moment, and his face did something complicated before settling back into its usual blankness. "Long way north."

"That's generally why they call it a journey."

"Mm." He returned to his ledger. Then, as if it cost him nothing, as if it were a remark about weather: "Take the grey coach, not the mail coach. The mail coaches get stopped."

Astrid wrote it down. She wrote everything down; it was the whole of her method. Not in code, not in cipher — in plain hand, in a small notebook, in abbreviations only she could parse, because the safest secret in a collapsing state was one that looked like a grocery list.

*Grey coach. Marrow Quay. Take coat.*

The last item was a joke she made only to herself, and even then she made it carefully.

* * *

They gave her the cylinder on Thursday.

It happened in a room below street level, in the basement of the Foreign Ministry, which had once been a wine cellar for some ambassador's residence and now smelled of mildew and lamp oil. There was a table, two chairs, and a man named Osten whom Astrid had met exactly once before, at a function where he'd spent the evening explaining to anyone who stood still that the war would be over by spring. That had been fourteen months ago. Spring had come and gone twice since.

Osten set the cylinder on the table between them. It was brass, eight inches long, no thicker than her thumb, and it looked like nothing at all — like a piece of a lamp, like a cigar tube, like the kind of object you'd find in a drawer and throw away.

"You'll want to know what's in it," Osten said.

"No," said Astrid.

He blinked. In eleven years of civil service, Astrid had learned that this was the correct answer, delivered at speed. People assumed couriers wanted to know. Couriers who wanted to know ended up knowing, and knowing was a weight, and weight slowed you down, and slow couriers were dead couriers — or worse, slow couriers were caught couriers, and caught couriers talked, and talked couriers started wars that hadn't been scheduled yet.

"It contains diplomatic recognition protocols," Osten said anyway, because men always told you whether you asked or not. "For certain parties overseas. If the government falls —" he paused on the word, testing its weight himself, finding it held — "these documents allow recognized channels to remain open. Continuity. That's all. It is not treasure, Frau Nystrom. It is plumbing."

"Plumbing," Astrid repeated.

"Invisible until it fails." He smiled thinly. "Which is why we chose you."

And there it was — the compliment that was also an insult, the promotion that was also a dismissal. *We chose you because no one will look at you.* Astrid had been no one looked at for so long that she had nearly made peace with it. Nearly.

"What happens to me after delivery?" she asked.

"You take a holiday. Paid." He slid a paper across the table. "Receipt here. Thumbprint here. You'll sew the cylinder into your clothing — we recommend a coat lining, left side, below the arm. Don't carry it loose. Loose things get found."

"And if I'm stopped?"

"You're a widow traveling to visit a sister. Your husband sold haberdashery. Here —" a second paper, a biography, thin as a pamphlet — "memorize it and burn it. You have until Sunday. Coach leaves Monday, dawn, from the Grey Lamb yard off Tanner Street. Grey coach," he added, and Astrid felt the small cold satisfaction of a prediction arriving early. "Not the mail coach."

"Why not?"

"Because," Osten said, gathering his papers, "the mail coaches are carrying people worth stopping, and you are not."

*You are not.* He meant it kindly. That was the terrible thing about the republic in its final season: it insulted you gently, right up until the day it shot you.

Astrid pressed her thumb into the ink pad and onto the receipt, and watched her own print bloom there, whorls and ridges, the most individual thing about her committed to a document that would be filed in a basement and lost within a year. Then she picked up the cylinder. It weighed almost nothing. Four ounces of brass, perhaps less. She was surprised by how heavy it felt in her palm, and understood, distantly, that the weight was not the metal's.

"Frau Nystrom." Osten stopped at the door, his hand on the frame, not turning around. "Whatever happens — whatever you see on the road — deliver it. Nothing else matters. Not the passengers, not the towns, not the news. Deliver it, and go home, and let history do its work around you. Do you understand? You are furniture."

"Furniture," Astrid said.

"Furniture survives," Osten said, and went up the stairs into the noise of the capital, which that afternoon sounded like a market day and a riot having an argument.

* * *

She bought the needle and thread on Friday from a shop that also sold candles, funerary wreaths, and — perched hopefully on a shelf between the black cloth and the beeswax — three tins of sardines priced like jewelry. The shopkeeper took her money without counting it, which told Astrid the man had stopped believing in tomorrow.

Her rooms were on Wren Street, third floor, two windows facing the courtyard and none facing the street, which was why she had chosen them nine years ago and why she blessed the choice now. From the courtyard you could hear the neighbors' lives conducted at full volume: the Voss family arguing about whether to leave, the Voss family arguing about whether staying was braver, the Voss children being told, in the same breath, to hush and to eat.

Astrid laid the coat on her bed. It was a good coat — grey wool, unremarkable, the kind of coat that existed in every wardrobe in the country and therefore in no wardrobe at all. She turned it inside out and examined the lining by lamplight. Left side, below the arm, where a seam already sagged from years of use. A repair, if anyone ever looked. And no one would ever look, because no one looked at widows, and no one looked at grey coats, and no one, in the whole apparatus of the dying republic or the rising one that would replace it, had any reason whatsoever to notice Astrid Nystrom.

That had been the arrangement of her entire adult life. She had entered the Records Annex at twenty-two, a thin girl with a plain face and a talent for being forgotten mid-sentence, and she had built the talent into a career the way other women built trousseaus. She knew which corridors were unwatched. She knew how to walk at a pace that made eyes slide off her. She could stand in a doorway during an argument and leave it afterward with both parties convinced she'd been elsewhere. Her mother had called it shrinking. Astrid called it architecture: you built yourself into the background of every room, load-bearing and invisible, and the building stood because you stood.

She threaded the needle. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and was quietly proud of it.

The first stitch went through the lining below the arm. The cylinder fit along the seam like it had been measured for it — Osten's people knew their work, she'd grant them that. She stitched over it in small, even strokes, closing the brass inside the wool the way you'd close a body inside a shroud, and thought: *this is a strange funeral, and I am both mourner and corpse.*

No. She corrected herself, pulling the thread tight. Not corpse. Cargo.

Outside, the Voss argument had reached its nightly crescendo. *"We are not traitors for leaving." "We are not fools for staying." "The children, Marta. Think of the children."* A door slammed. A child cried briefly and was hushed.

Astrid stitched. Stitching was good for thinking, or for not-thinking, whichever the hour demanded. The lamp guttered once. Down in the courtyard, someone began hauling suitcases across the cobbles, and the sound was exactly the sound of wheels on ice, and she made herself stop listening.

Here was what she believed, and she believed it the way you believe in the floor beneath you — without examining it, because examination was for people with spare courage:

*I am not part of this.*

The republic was falling. Something worse or merely different was coming to replace it. Generals were becoming enemies and ministers were becoming refugees and the Avenue of the Republic would be renamed by whoever won, probably twice. But Astrid Nystrom was none of those things. She was a widow with a sister in Marrow Quay and a haberdasher husband in his grave — a fiction wearing a fiction, a woman made entirely of paperwork. She carried plumbing. She was furniture. When history finished tearing through the capital like fire through a granary, it would find her ash-adjacent, untouched, uninteresting, and it would move on.

Promises, treaties, allegiances — these were for people with faces. Astrid had spent thirty-four years perfecting the art of having none.

The last stitch closed. She bit the thread, turned the coat right-side out, and tried it on. She raised her left arm; nothing showed. She pressed her hand flat against her ribs and felt only the faintest ridge, the kind of ridge a mended seam makes, the kind of ridge a thousand grey coats in a thousand cities were wearing at that very moment.

In the mirror above the washstand, a stranger adjusted her collar. Middle-aged, tired, forgettable. A woman the eye declined.

"There," Astrid said to her reflection. "Nobody at all."

The reflection, being well trained, agreed.

* * *

Saturday she burned the biography in the stove, feeding it in strips, watching the invented haberdasher curl and vanish. Sunday she packed: one bag, light enough to run with, though she did not phrase it to herself that way. She packed it as though she were going to visit a sister. Underclothes, a spare blouse, the notebook, a tin of the expensive sardines because a bribe disguised as a gift was still the most versatile tool she owned. She did not pack photographs, because she owned none. She had removed herself from photographs years ago, the way you'd remove a stain.

Sunday night the city burned something real. She saw it from her window — a glow eastward, over the docks district, and the bells starting up wrong, all of them at once and none of them in rhythm. The Voss children were crying again. Somewhere a whistle blew, and somewhere else a whistle answered it, and the space between the whistles was the sound of a government losing its grip on its own streets.

Astrid sat by the window with the coat over her knees and her hand resting on the place where the cylinder slept against her ribs, and she performed the exercise she had performed every night for eleven years: she made herself small. She folded her future down to a single sentence — *deliver the cylinder, take the ship, take the holiday, become no one again* — and she tucked the sentence away where panic couldn't reach it.

Furniture doesn't think. Furniture doesn't choose. Furniture certainly doesn't remember, which was why she permitted herself, just once, in the dark, before sleep, to think of a garden eleven years ago, and a general's wife laughing, and a promise made carelessly over a cradle — and then she put it away, the way she put everything away, deep in the lining, below the arm, where no one would ever think to look.

Monday came up grey and frozen.

At dawn, Astrid Nystrom put on the coat, took the bag, locked a door she might never unlock, and went down the stairs into the last morning of the world she understood — telling herself, with each step, the lie she was best at:

*I am furniture. Furniture walks through wars. Furniture arrives.*

At the Grey Lamb yard off Tanner Street, the grey coach stood steaming in the cold, and three passengers were already boarding, and one of them — a narrow man clutching a ministry briefcase like a life vest — was looking at her a moment too long.

Astrid memorized his face out of habit, climbed aboard, and did not yet know she would see him again.
