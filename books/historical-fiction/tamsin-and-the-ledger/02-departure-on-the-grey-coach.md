## 2. Departure on the Grey Coach

The Grey Coach left the capital at six in the morning, before the light had properly decided to arrive, and Astrid Nystrom was on it because unimportant people traveled at inconvenient hours.

She took the seat farthest back, by the window, with her coat folded over the satchel in her lap in a way that looked like habit and was actually architecture. The code-cylinder rode against her ribs, sewn into the lining beneath her left arm, and every jolt of the coach reminded her of it the way a tooth reminds you of itself—not painfully, not yet, but insistently, a small metal fact she could not stop touching with her attention.

Four passengers, counting herself. She counted them out of habit, and then, because it was a long road north and habits are the only luggage that never gets stolen, she began to memorize them.

The first was the clerk.

He boarded last, breathless, apologizing to the driver as though the schedule were his personal failing, and wedged himself onto the bench opposite Astrid with the particular posture of a man who has spent twenty years folding himself into chairs built for smaller convictions. He was perhaps forty-five, soft through the middle, with ink-stained fingers—he'd already smudged his cuff—and a leather portfolio clutched to his chest the way other men clutched prayer books or weapons. His coat was good wool but mended at the elbows, the mending done well, which told her he noticed things and could not afford much. His boots told her he walked a great deal within a small radius. His eyes, when they flicked up and found her looking, darted away too fast, and then came back, because men like him always came back; they couldn't help checking whether they'd been caught looking.

Astrid gave him nothing. A tired woman going north to see an aunt, if anyone asked. Boring is a coat you wear over all your other coats.

"Terrible hour," the clerk said, to no one and everyone. "Six o'clock. You'd think the ministry could arrange—well. No. They can't. That's rather the point of everything now, isn't it." He laughed at his own joke, briefly and badly, and then looked out the window as if the capital's darkened streets might contradict him.

They did not. The coach rolled through districts where shopfronts stood shuttered not for the night but for good, their windows papered over with proclamation after proclamation until the paper layers themselves formed a kind of archive—the Republic Endures, grain requisitions postponed, curfew extended, the Republic Endures again, printed larger this time, as if volume were a form of truth. A queue had already formed outside the bakery on Corvin Street, though the bakery would not open for two hours, and the people in it stood with the terrible patience of those who have learned that patience is the only currency still honored at face value.

The second passenger sat beside the clerk: a broad-shouldered man in his fifties with the sunburned neck and cracked hands of someone who worked outdoors even in winter. He said nothing. He had boarded first, paid without haggling, taken his seat, and become furniture, and Astrid noted—with professional respect—that his furniture-ness was better executed than hers. He dozed against the window with his cap pulled low, but she saw that under the brim his eyes opened whenever the coach slowed. A farmer wouldn't do that. A farmer slept when sleeping was offered. This man catalogued stops.

Smuggler, she guessed. Or something adjacent. The roads north were full of them now; sanctions made entrepreneurs of half the countryside and criminals of the other half, depending on which side of the border the goods changed hands.

The third passenger was a woman of about sixty, dressed in black that had once been expensive, with a birdcage covered in cloth balanced on her knees. She held the cage the way one holds an urn. When the coach hit the first rut outside the walls and the cage swung, a small offended chirp came from under the cloth, and the woman soothed it with one finger through the canvas, murmuring, and Astrid understood that whatever was in the cage—a finch, a lark, some scrap of song—was the last living thing this woman intended to save from the capital, and that she knew it, and that she would die before admitting the arithmetic aloud.

Everyone on the coach was carrying something they hadn't declared. The farmer-smuggler carried goods. The widow carried a bird. The clerk carried a portfolio and a terror he mistook for duty. And Astrid carried a cylinder of diplomatic codes worth, depending on the week's fortunes, several lives or several thousand, stitched into her coat like a second spine.

That was the thing about the republic in its final winter, she thought, watching the walls slide past: nobody traveled light anymore. Even the empty-handed were smuggling grief.

---

Outside the city the fog took over, and the world reduced itself to what the coach windows allowed: a strip of frozen ditch, a line of bare trees, telegraph poles marching north with their wires slack and singing in the wind. Twice they passed checkpoints where soldiers in coats too big for them waved the coach down, peered inside with lanterns, and waved it through with the bored contempt of boys playing at power. The youngest of them couldn't have been more than seventeen. He had a chilblain split across his knuckles and he held his rifle the way the widow held her birdcage—as something precious, breakable, and not entirely his.

At the second checkpoint the soldier asked for papers, read Astrid's courier warrant with lips moving slightly, and handed it back without understanding a word of it. That was the system now: documents passed hand to hand like relics, venerated and unread.

"You're government, then," the clerk said, when they were moving again. He said it to her. Of course he did. He'd spent the whole first hour deciding whom to talk to, and she'd lost, because the smuggler radiated *don't* and the widow radiated *grief* and Astrid had merely failed to radiate anything loudly enough.

"I'm a courier," she said. Neutral. True enough to survive inspection, false enough to end there. "Documents between offices."

"The Northern Chancery?" His voice lifted with hope. "I'm Ministry of Records myself. Confiscations division." He said *confiscations* the way other people said a shameful diagnosis—quietly, with a glance around, though no one else was listening. "Archives, mostly. Ledgers. You'd be astonished what people write down and then fail to secure properly." He patted the portfolio. "I'm taking the northern registry files to Marrow Quay for transfer. Offshore storage. Very sensible. Everything valuable should be near water in times like these."

*Everything valuable should be near water.* Astrid filed the sentence away. Her own cargo was bound for exactly that—a ship at Marrow Quay, a neutral flag, codes that would let foreign ministries read the republic's dying mail.

"Times like these," she echoed, offering him the conversational equivalent of a closed door.

He opened it anyway. He talked about the requisitions, and the coal queues, and the minister who'd resigned by simply not coming to work one morning, and the new currency, and the old currency, and how his sister had gotten out through the southern passes in October and written to him twice and then not written, and how silence probably meant the passes were snowed in, almost certainly meant that, definitely didn't mean anything else. He talked the way drowning men swim—not skillfully, but continuously, because stopping was unthinkable.

And Astrid listened, because listening was her trade and because somewhere in the fourth mile of his monologue he said something that mattered:

"—and of course the recovery lists have priority now. Above archives, above everything. General staff directive, sealed classification, which is absurd, because I'm the one who has to file them, so I read them, don't I, I can hardly catalogue what I haven't—" He stopped. For the first time since boarding, the clerk went quiet, and in the quiet his face did something complicated: pride, horror, and the small self-flattering thrill of a man holding a secret, all fighting for the same territory. "Well. Sealed means sealed. Forget I said it."

"Recovery lists," Astrid said, mildly, as one repeats a place name to fix it in memory. "Recovering what?"

"Property. Assets." He smoothed his cuff, smearing the ink further. "In the occupied territories, when the army advances, certain... persons of interest are registered. For repatriation. It sounds worse than it is." He said this with the desperate conviction of a man rehearsing a sentence he'd need later, in a room with harder listeners. "Administrative. It's administrative work. Someone has to keep the lists accurate, that's all, or the wrong families—" He stopped again. The coach swayed. Outside, a burned farmhouse slid past, its roof fallen inward like a ribcage, frost growing in the rafters. The clerk looked at it and did not finish the sentence, and Astrid watched him decide, visibly, to believe that the unfinished part of it was somehow less true than the parts he'd said.

She memorized his face while he wasn't watching: the ink-stained fingers, the mended elbow, the way his jaw tightened around words he swallowed. Someday, she thought, with the flat certainty of long experience, this man will matter to me, and I will wish I remembered more than his elbows. So she fixed him whole—voice, hands, the smell of cheap ink and fear-sweat—and gave him a name in her head. *Ledger.* Men were easier to carry if you named them.

The coach climbed into hills. Snow began, fine and dry, ticking against the windows like static.

---

At the midday halt they stopped at a coaching inn where the stable yard held more soldiers than horses and the innkeeper charged four times the fair price and apologized while doing it, which somehow made it worse. The widow carried her birdcage inside herself, refusing help. The smuggler stayed with the coach, ostensibly guarding luggage, and Astrid noted that he repositioned one particular trunk closer to the driver's box.

Inside, the clerk attached himself to her table with the inevitability of weather.

"They say the fleet at Marrow Quay is being seized," he confided, low, over gray tea that tasted of the barrel it had been stretched with. "Impounded for the war effort. All of it. If your ship hasn't sailed by the time you arrive—" He spread his hands, and the gesture finished the thought: *then we are all of us on land together, and the water belongs to whoever has the guns.*

"My ship sails Thursday," Astrid said, which was true, and felt suddenly, in her mouth, like something from a previous century.

"Thursday." He nodded too many times. "Good. Good. Best to be at sea. My cousin says the coast towns will be occupied within the month—the general staff wants the ports before the ice closes the approaches, and after that the whole northern littoral is—" He caught himself, glanced toward the soldiers in the yard, and lowered his voice further. "It's strange, isn't it. We're the republic. We're supposed to be the ones with the ports."

"We're the ones with the paperwork," Astrid said. "That turns out to be different."

He laughed—startled, real, brief—and then looked stricken, as though laughing at the republic in front of a witness constituted another item requiring cataloguing. Under the table his knee bounced. Astrid realized she was watching a man who had served the machinery his whole life finally hear its gears change, and who could not yet tell whether he was the operator or the ore.

When they returned to the coach, the light had gone the color of pewter, and the snow thickened, and the world shrank to the width of the road.

---

It was in the last hour, with dusk coming down blue and early, that Astrid saw the thing she would remember longest—not the burned farms, not the boyish checkpoint soldiers, but a small procession on the far side of a frozen field: perhaps thirty people walking north along a hedgerow, burdened with bundles and handcarts, a child's red cap bobbing among the dark coats like a struck match. Refugees, moving ahead of the army the way water moves ahead of a plow. Nobody on the coach commented. The widow drew her curtain. The smuggler didn't open his eyes. The clerk watched them until the road bent and the hedgerow took them, and then he said, quietly, to the window:

"There but for the filing system go I."

It was the most honest thing he'd said all day, and Astrid hated him a little for it, and understood him completely, and filed both reactions away with everything else.

Because here was the trade's oldest lesson, the one they never taught openly: *everyone is carrying.* The courier's art was not secrecy—it was the recognition that secrecy is universal, that the widow's cage and the smuggler's trunk and the clerk's sealed lists and the soldier's chilblained hands were all the same cargo wearing different wrapping. You did not hide by being empty. You hid by being one package among packages, by having a weight that explained itself.

Her weight explained itself: a tired woman, a courier's warrant, an aunt in the north. The coach accepted her. The republic accepted her. The war, so far, had not noticed her at all.

She pressed her arm lightly against her ribs, feeling the cylinder's small hard truth, and told herself what she'd told herself since the night she'd sewn it in: *You are furniture. You are the seam in the wall. History is happening to other people, and your job is to carry paper past it untouched.*

The coach rattled north. Behind them the capital sank into the fog like a coin into dark water. Ahead, somewhere past the hills, Marrow Quay waited with its ships and its seizures, its registry files and its sealed lists—and, though no window on that grey coach could show it, a boarding house door against which, in three days' time, a very small knock would fall.

Astrid slept, lightly, the way furniture does.
