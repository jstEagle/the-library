## 3. The Boarding House at Marrow Quay

The coach let her off where the cobbles gave up and the mud began, at the bottom of a hill that Marrow Quay had been built against like a barnacle against a hull. Astrid stood with her one case and felt the fog settle onto her shoulders as if it had been waiting for her personally.

It was the kind of fog that made the town smaller than it was. She could hear gulls somewhere above her, screaming at nothing she could see. A bell buoy clanked out in the harbor, patient as a metronome. Somewhere to her left, water slapped wood — the tide working at the pilings, taking the town apart a fraction at a time and no one objecting.

She had memorized the way from the coach yard to Mrs. Halloway's door before she'd ever left the capital: two streets east, then down Stair Lane, which was not a lane so much as a staircase worn hollow in its centers by three centuries of feet. But in this fog the instructions dissolved. Every street was the same street. Every lamp burned a small defeated orange halo, visible for ten feet and no farther.

Good, she told herself. This is what invisible looks like.

She walked until the houses began leaning over her, their upper stories jutting so far across the narrow lanes that neighbors could have shaken hands across the gaps. Salt crust on every sill. Nets hung like funeral wreaths. A cat watched her pass from a doorway with the flat professional interest of something that had seen every kind of stranger arrive in every kind of weather and had never once been impressed.

Marrow Quay was the sort of place the republic remembered only when it needed something from the sea. There were three things here: herring, salt, and ships willing to ask no questions about cargo. The war had made the third category very profitable and very crowded. Neutral flags everywhere, she'd been told. Ships from Halvard, from the Veltine islands, from ports whose names changed depending on which treaty had most recently collapsed. Captains who took coin in any currency because currency was currency and loyalty was for people who could afford it.

Her contact's letter had named the boarding house: *Mrs. Halloway, Quay Lane, blue door, asks nothing.* The last part was the part that mattered. In the capital, everyone asked everything. Here, presumably, the fog did the asking and found no one worth an answer.

She found the blue door by accident, which is how one finds anything in Marrow Quay — turned down a passage between a chandlery and a shuttered fish-curing shed, and there it was, a door painted the color of deep water, with a brass knocker shaped like a herring that someone had polished so many times its face was featureless.

She knocked.

The wait was long enough that she catalogued the sounds behind it: footsteps, unhurried; the click of a latch being considered before it was opened. Then the door swung inward and Mrs. Halloway looked out at her.

Astrid had expected a certain type — the landlady of spy fiction, all sharp eyes and knowing smiles. What she got instead was a woman of perhaps sixty, broad and slow-moving, with flour on one forearm and an expression like a closed ledger. Her eyes went over Astrid once, thoroughly, the way a customs officer reads a manifest, and arrived at a total she did not share.

"You'll be the one for the back room," she said. "Third floor. Mind the fourth stair from the top, it talks."

"I—"

"Breakfast at seven, supper at six, and I don't cook between meals. The privy's in the yard and the yard floods when the tide's high, so wear the boots you've got and don't buy new ones." She stepped aside. "You'll be wanting the ship."

It wasn't a question. Everyone in Marrow Quay wanted the ship; wanting the ship was the whole economy.

"When does she sail?" Astrid asked.

"When she sails." Mrs. Halloway closed the door behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence. "Captain Orla doesn't post schedules. Schedules are for people with nothing to lose. You'll hear the bell when it's time. Until then you're a boarder, and boarders keep regular hours."

* * *

The back room was exactly what she'd paid for: a bed, a washstand, a window with a view of a brick wall, and a chair that had been repaired with wire in a manner suggesting optimism rather than skill. The room smelled of tallow and old sea air. The wallpaper had once been patterned with birds; now it was patterned with the ghosts of birds, faded to faint brown suggestions, as if the flock had flown off years ago and left only their shadows behind.

Astrid locked the door, sat on the bed, and put her hand inside her coat.

The code-cylinder lay against the lining where she'd sewn it in the capital, a hard cold presence over her ribs like a second heart. Six inches of brass. Inside it, rolled tight as a wasp in a paper nest, the cipher keys for the northern legations — the last workable diplomatic channel the republic had, or believed it had. Without it, the republic spoke to the world and the world heard gibberish. With it, whatever government still answered to the name might beg, borrow, or bargain another month of existence.

And it lived under her ribs, sewn into a coat bought secondhand precisely because it looked like a coat nobody would steal.

She breathed. The cylinder didn't move. It never moved; that was the point of it. For eleven days it had ridden against her body through coach changes and border inspections and one memorable hour in a customs shed where an officer had made her take off her boots while he read her papers upside down. She had smiled the whole time. Smiling was easy. Smiling cost nothing and revealed nothing, and she had been smiling at officials since she was nineteen years old.

*You are furniture*, she reminded herself. *Furniture doesn't sweat. Furniture doesn't check its seams.*

But furniture didn't lie awake either, and she suspected she would.

Supper was at six. Mrs. Halloway served it family-style at a long table in a parlor that appeared to have been decorated by a man fleeing creditors: mismatched chairs, a clock that ran but struck the wrong hours, a painting of a shipwreck hung prominently over the sideboard as though in warning. Two other boarders were already seated when Astrid came down.

The first was an old man called Pell, retired from something maritime and unexplained, who ate with the methodical devotion of a man who had survived famine and intended never to be surprised by it again. He nodded at her, said nothing, and returned to his herring.

The second made up for him entirely.

"Tomas Brandt," he announced, extending a hand damp from the bread. He was maybe fifty, pink-faced, with the soft hands of a desk and the restless eyes of a man recently removed from his desk under circumstances he hadn't yet finished explaining to himself. "Ministry. Records division, formerly. Well — records division, presently, technically, pending reorganization." He laughed, a short bark with no floor under it. "Everything's pending reorganization now, isn't it? You'll forgive me. I talk when I'm nervous. I'm always nervous lately. Are you nervous? Don't answer. Trade?"

"Courier," Astrid said, and gave him the smile. "Commercial papers mostly."

"Couriers!" He brightened. "Then you'll know the roads better than I do. I came up by the coast road and I'll tell you, half the toll stations are just boys with rifles and receipts they've printed themselves. Printed them themselves! One of them charged me a 'documentation surcharge.' I paid it. Of course I paid it. What was I going to do, cite regulations to a boy with a rifle and homemade paperwork?" He shook his head, delighted and horrified in equal measure. "My wife says I should have stayed in the city. My wife says a great many things. She's gone to her sister's in Veltmark until things — well. Until things are things again."

Astrid ate her herring and let him talk. It required no effort. Men like Brandt were weather; you didn't converse with weather, you simply kept your collar up and noted the direction.

But she noted, too, the details underneath the chatter: that he had come north alone, that his wife had gone west, that a records clerk separated from both his household and his ministry in the same season was a man whose institution had stopped needing him — or stopped trusting him. That he wore a good coat gone slightly shabby at the cuffs, the uniform of respectability maintained past the point where it could be afforded. That his eyes flicked, twice during supper, toward the window facing the quay, quick and involuntary, the way a man checks a wound.

"And what brings you to Marrow Quay, if I may?" he asked. "Forgive me. I know it's rude to ask. Mrs. Halloway says asking is discouraged. She said it rather firmly. I find that rather restful actually, don't you? Not being asked. Though I suppose couriers must be asked things constantly—"

"Papers," Astrid said. "Shipping contracts. I wait for Captain Orla like everyone else."

"Orla! The *Vessa*." He pronounced the name with the reverence of a man discussing a saint or a racehorse. "They say she's run the strait nine times since the blockade. Nine! They say she carries anything — tin, wool, persons of quality, persons of no quality whatsoever—" He stopped himself, laughing again, and mopped his face with his napkin. "Listen to me. I haven't even asked your name."

"Astrid Nystrom."

"Astrid Nystrom." He repeated it slowly, tasting it, filing it away somewhere in that soft bureaucratic head where names went and, she hoped, would stay filed and forgotten. "Well. Miss Nystrom. To new acquaintances." He raised his glass of small beer. "May we all be boring to each other."

She touched her glass to his. *May we all be boring to each other.* It was, she thought later, the most honest toast ever proposed in the collapsing republic.

* * *

That night the fog thickened until the harbor bell seemed to come from inside the walls.

Astrid sat by her window — the one facing the brick wall, chosen for her, or assigned, or merely available — with the cylinder's weight against her chest and listened to Marrow Quay close itself for the night. Doors. Bolts. The tide turning somewhere below. A dog barking twice at the end of the street, then deciding against it.

In the capital, the nights had been loud. Even in the last weeks — especially in the last weeks — the city had made noise at itself: arguments through walls, trucks in the boulevards, the printing presses running around the clock because everyone wanted the news first and nobody trusted anyone else's version. Noise meant people, and people meant witnesses, and witnesses were the courier's oldest enemy and oldest protection.

Here there was no noise to hide in. Here the silence itself seemed to listen.

She thought about the ship. Three days, the letter had said, perhaps four, weather permitting. Captain Orla would carry the cylinder north to Halvard, where it would be transferred to a legation pouch, where it would become somebody else's problem, and Astrid Nystrom would dissolve back into the vast undifferentiated population of women with commercial papers and unremarkable coats. Back to furniture. Back to the blessed weightlessness of mattering to no one.

That was the trade. She had understood it for years. The republic took her invisibility and paid her for it in anonymity, and she had signed the contract gladly, because she had seen what visibility cost. She had grown up watching her mother be *someone* — someone with opinions, someone whose name appeared in the wrong lists at the wrong times — and she had learned early that history was a fire and the safest place near a fire was far enough away that you only felt warmth and never flame.

So: furniture. So: fog. So: a room with a view of a wall, and three days of waiting, and then the sea.

Below her window, in the yard, the privy door banged in the wind. She counted the sounds of the house settling the way other people counted sheep. Mrs. Halloway's footsteps below, heavy and unhurried, making her rounds. The clock striking eleven and then, twelve minutes later, striking eleven again, having apparently reconsidered.

Sleep came eventually, shallow and gray.

* * *

She woke once, in the deepest part of the night, to voices below.

Not arguing — murmuring. The particular murmur of two people keeping their voices down not out of secrecy but out of courtesy to sleeping houses. She rose without lighting a lamp and stood at her door, listening.

Mrs. Halloway's voice, low. And beneath it, thinner, higher — a voice she couldn't place, saying something urgent that the fog and the floors swallowed whole. Then Mrs. Halloway again, longer this time, calm, final, the tone of a woman setting down a rule she has set down before. A pause. Footsteps crossing the parlor. The blue door opening and shutting, its latch catching with that same considered click.

Then silence, and after a moment, Mrs. Halloway's tread on the stairs — not coming up, but pausing on the landing below, standing there so long that Astrid wondered if the woman could somehow feel her listening through the boards.

Astrid returned to her bed and lay staring at the ceiling, where the ghost-birds of the wallpaper were invisible in the dark.

Smugglers, she decided. Late arrivals. Money changing hands. Marrow Quay was full of such traffic; it was a port, and ports breathed at night. It was none of her business. Nothing here was her business. Her business was six inches of brass and three days of patience, and she would deliver it and vanish and never think of this gray town again.

She repeated it to herself like a catechism until the fog thinned toward morning.

* * *

Dawn came to Marrow Quay without quite arriving — the fog lifting just enough to reveal more fog, the sky a colorless membrane stretched over the harbor. From her window Astrid could see a slice of the quay now, and the ships: a Halvardish brig flying no colors at all, a salt-smuggling lugger with fresh paint over old scars, and, tied at the deep-water berth, a black-hulled steamer with a white band at her funnel.

The *Vessa*. Waiting. Patient as everything else in this town.

Mrs. Halloway served breakfast at seven sharp — porridge, black bread, tea strong enough to stand a spoon in — and said almost nothing, which suited Astrid perfectly. Pell read a week-old newspaper behind his cup. Brandt talked, of course, about the reorganization, about his former director, about whether the mail boat would run this week (it wouldn't), and Astrid smiled and ate and was furniture.

It was as she carried her plate to the wash basin that Mrs. Halloway spoke to her directly for the first time since the door.

"You sleep light," the landlady said. Not a question. Her eyes were on the teapot she was filling, her voice pitched for Astrid alone under the clatter of Pell's newspaper. "Heard you on the stairs last night, past midnight."

"The house talks," Astrid said. "You told me it would."

"The house talks." Mrs. Halloway set down the pot. For a moment something moved behind her face — not suspicion exactly, and not kindness either, but the flat assessment of a woman deciding what a person was worth and arriving, provisionally, at a figure. "This house hears things, miss, and forgets them by breakfast. That's why it stands. But forgetting's a discipline. Some folk are natural at it. Some folk have to practice." She picked up the teapot again. "Which are you?"

"Furniture," Astrid said.

Something at the corner of Mrs. Halloway's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if it were ever invited. "Furniture," she agreed. "Well. Furniture gets fed at seven and six. And furniture," she added, turning back to her stove, "would do well to remember that the *Vessa*'s captain sends word when she's ready, and not before, and that the last boarder who grew impatient and went asking questions down at the harbor office was asked some questions back. By men who weren't from the harbor office."

The parlor went very quiet. Even Brandt's pen — he'd begun a letter at the table — paused mid-stroke.

"Noted," Astrid said.

"Good." Mrs. Halloway scraped the porridge pan with a violence that ended the conversation permanently.

* * *

The days arranged themselves into a holding pattern. Astrid walked the town in careful circuits — the chandlery, the fish market, the seawall where the wind came off the water hard enough to make thinking impossible, which she suspected was its principal civic function. She memorized faces out of habit: the harbormaster's clerk with ink on his fingers; the pair of men who smoked outside the Anchor & Chain and watched everything and were watched by everyone; a woman selling cockles who had a soldier's bearing under her shawl and eyes that swept the quay on a rhythm too regular to be idle.

She counted exits. Old habit, older than her commission: gates, alleys, the stair-lanes cut into the hillside, the stretch of shingle below the seawall where a determined person might get a boat down to the water at night if she knew whom to pay. She didn't expect to need any of it. She was furniture. Furniture counts doors the way other people count lampposts — idly, without meaning.

But on the second evening, coming back along Quay Lane in the failing light, she saw the thing that unsettled the pattern.

A naval launch had tied up at the foot of the harbor stairs — grey-painted, armed, flying the republic's ensign, which had begun appearing in odd places lately like a rash. An officer stood on the quay talking to the harbormaster, and two sailors were posting something to the notice board by the customhouse. A knot of fishermen had gathered to read it, and the reading had the specific silence of men learning bad news in public.

Astrid didn't stop. She walked past at furniture-speed, eyes forward, and caught only the tail of the officer's voice, carrying flatly across the water:

"—by order of the Admiralty, effective tomorrow, all departures subject to inspection. Neutral tonnage included. No exceptions."

Behind her, she heard one of the fishermen spit, very distinctly, into the harbor.

At the boarding house, the notice had preceded her by rumor, as notices do. Brandt met her in the hall, practically wringing his hat.

"Did you hear? Inspection! All sailings! They can't do that, there are treaties — well, there were treaties, there's a treaty with Halvard specifically, Article Eleven, I drafted correspondence on Article Eleven myself in 'nine—" He caught himself, swallowed. "Miss Nystrom. Your ship. Our arrangements. If they're inspecting everything—"

"Then we wait longer," Astrid said pleasantly. "Waiting is what boarding houses are for."

"Yes," Brandt said. "Yes. Of course. Waiting." But his eyes had gone to the window again, that quick flick toward the quay, and this time Astrid followed the glance far enough to understand it: he wasn't nervous about the inspections.

He was watching for someone.

* * *

That night she checked the cylinder for the first time since arriving — unpicked the seam with a needle borrowed from Mrs. Halloway's mending basket, verified the brass, sewed it shut again in stitches slightly finer than her own. The coat went back on its hook. The candle went out.

She lay in the dark listening to the house breathe: Pell's snore through one wall, the clock striking the wrong hour, the tide knocking patiently at the pilings, Mrs. Halloway's rounds below.

And then, near dawn, through the thinning fog, a sound she hadn't heard before. Small. High. Cut off quickly, as if whoever made it had been taught to stop.

A child, crying, somewhere down the hill toward the water. Crying briefly, then silent — silenced, not comforted.

Astrid lay still and told herself it was the gulls. Marrow Quay was full of gulls. Gulls cried all night over the harbor, and it meant nothing, and in the morning there would be porridge and black bread and the wrong hour struck by the clock, and in a day or two the *Vessa* would sound her bell and Astrid Nystrom would walk down Stair Lane with her commercial papers and her unremarkable coat and disappear into the world's indifference forever.

The gulls cried again. And again. And would not stop.
