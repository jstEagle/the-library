## 4. A Child at the Door

I woke before dawn because the fog had changed. Anyone who has lived a winter in Marrow Quay learns to read the fog the way farmers read clouds, and this one had gone still — no breath of harbor wind in it, no salt, just a flat grey silence pressing against the window like something waiting to be let in.

I lay in the narrow bed at Mrs. Halloway's and did what I always did on waking: took inventory. Code-cylinder, sewn into the left lining of my traveling coat, still there by the weight of it when I lifted the coat from the chair. Papers in my name — one of my names — folded inside my boot. Ship out of Marrow Quay in four days if it came at all. I was Astrid Nystrom, wool merchant's factor, traveling north on dull business, and I told myself the story slowly, the way you count money twice to be sure.

The knock came at first light. Not a knock, exactly. A sound like a knuckle against wood, once, then again, so faint I might have made it up.

I dressed before I answered. That is courier habit: never open a door until you know who could be watching you open it. The landing was empty. The house was silent except for Mrs. Halloway's kettle beginning its low complaint two floors down. I turned the latch and pulled the door open six inches, and there was a child standing on the step.

A girl. Six years old, maybe seven, though small for either. She wore a coat too big for her, a man's coat with the sleeves rolled back three turns and still swallowing her hands, and beneath it a dress that had been good once and had been traveled in hard since. Her face was the color of the fog. Frost had gotten into the fur of her hood and turned it white, and she stood the way children stand when they have been told not to move — not the way they stand when they are cold, which is all motion, but the terrible held-stillness of obedience.

She looked up at me and said nothing.

I looked past her. The street was empty in both directions. Fog to the harbor end, fog to the chapel end, the gas lamp at the corner burning a weak yellow hole in the middle of nothing. No carriage. No porter. No one.

"Are you lost?" I asked.

She shook her head. Then, after a moment, as if remembering that silence was itself an answer someone might punish, she held something up. Both hands, because it was too wide for one.

An envelope.

I should tell you what I thought in that first second, because it matters. I thought: wrong door. I thought: some neighbor's errand, some child sent with a bill or a message, and Mrs. Halloway asleep, and me standing here in my shift with my hair down like any woman woken at dawn. I thought a dozen ordinary things. That is what being invisible does to you — it teaches you to reach first for the ordinary explanation, because the ordinary explanation has never once come through the door with soldiers behind it.

I took the envelope. It was cheap paper, unsealed, unmarked. Inside was a single sheet, and on it, eleven words in a hand I knew better than my own.

**Forget the face. Burn this. She is yours now as promised.**

The fog moved. Somewhere down by the water a halyard slapped a mast, once, and went quiet.

You will want to know how I recognized the handwriting after seven years, and I will tell you honestly: it was the *y*. Most people write a *y* as two strokes. Veyra wrote it as one, a single descending line that crossed its own tail, a flaw learned young and never corrected, the kind of thing a clerk notices because noticing hands is the trade. I had spent a season copying treaty correspondence in that hand's vicinity, and I had kept one letter longer than I should have, and I had looked at that letter more times than a clerk looks at anything. One line, crossing its own tail.

General Sera Veyra. Who had been a colonel then. Whose signature was now worth a province, whose name had been spoken on the wireless that very evening in the capital in the voice governments use for plagues.

And who had left her daughter on my doorstep in the dark.

* * *

"Come in," I said. It came out steady, which surprised me. I stepped back and the girl crossed the threshold without a word, without looking around, walking on the balls of her feet the way you walk in a house where loud floors betray you. Someone had taught her that. Someone had spent these last weeks teaching her everything there was to teach a child about entering rooms quietly, and then put her on a boat or a cart or her own two frozen feet and pointed her at my door.

I closed it and shot the bolt and stood with my hand on the wood.

Up close she smelled of coal smoke and cold wool and, underneath, faintly, of some flower soap that had been washed out of finer clothes than these. Her lips were blue-tinged. Her eyes were dry, which frightened me more than tears would have. Children cry when they are frightened; children who do not cry have been frightened for a long time, long enough to learn that crying costs.

"What's your name?" I asked.

She pressed her lips together.

"You can say it," I said. "Names are safe here."

Another silence. Then, barely above the sound of the kettle below: "Tamsin."

One word, given up like contraband.

"Well, Tamsin." I crouched to her height, which put the frost-white hood between us, and pushed it back off her face. Her hair was dark, cut short and unevenly — someone had done it themselves, quickly, with scissors, the way you cut hair when you don't want it remembered. "How did you get here?"

"A lady." A pause while she decided how much truth was allowed. "On the boat. Then she gave me the paper and said the blue door."

This was the blue door. Mrs. Halloway painted it blue every spring, God knows why, in a town where every other door was black or green, and for seven years it had been nothing but a door. Now it was a landmark in someone else's plan, and I understood that the plan had been drawn long ago, carefully, by the same hand that crossed its own tail.

"Where is the lady now?"

"Back on the boat." Tamsin's eyes went to the door, then to the stairs, cataloguing exits. Six years old. "She said wait on the step and knock soft and don't talk to nobody."

"Did anyone see you? On the street?"

She considered this with a seriousness no child should own. "A man. By the fish shop. But he was only sweeping."

Sweeping, at dawn, outside a fish shop that would not open for hours. I filed it away and hated myself for filing it away, because this was the other thing my trade had taught me: assume the sweeper is paid.

"Come to the fire," I said.

* * *

Mrs. Halloway kept the parlor grate laid but unlit overnight — thrift — and I lit it now, kneeling on the hearth with the match cupped, aware of the girl watching me the whole time with that flat, patient attention. When the kindling caught I sat her in the wing chair nearest it and put a blanket over her knees, and she let me, and accepted the warmth without any change in her face, and I thought: she is in shock. And then I thought: no. She is trained. And I could not decide which was worse.

I went down to the kitchen on the pretext of tea and found Mrs. Halloway already there, standing at the range in her dressing gown, and I saw by her face that she had heard the bolt go and the small feet overhead and had decided, in the space of ninety seconds, to know nothing about it.

"Extra water," she said, and filled the kettle herself, and asked no questions, and I loved her a little and trusted her less, both at once, which I was learning was the standard arrangement between people in that town.

When I got back upstairs Tamsin had not moved except to turn her hands toward the fire. I set the tea down and crouched again.

"Tamsin. Listen carefully. Did your mother give you anything else? Anything besides the letter?"

Something happened in her face then — the first crack in it, a flicker, quickly shut. Her eyes dropped to the coat she was sitting wrapped in. The man's coat. Too big, sleeves rolled, and heavy across the shoulders in a way that a coat needn't be.

I held out my hand, palm up, and waited. She stared at the fire. I waited. This is most of tradecraft, whatever the ministry men think: waiting, and letting silence do the asking. After a long minute she wriggled one arm free of the rolled sleeve and dug into the inner pocket and placed something in my palm, and I closed my fingers around it before I even looked, because the window faced the street and glass has ears.

In the stairwell shadow I opened my hand. A second envelope, sealed this time, and heavier — paper folded over something small and hard. I did not open it. Not yet. Not with the light coming up and the sweeper outside the fish shop and a child downstairs who watched doors.

I tucked it into my bodice and went back and smiled at her, and the smile cost more effort than any document I ever carried.

"Do you like toast?" I asked.

She nodded. Then, as I turned toward the little spirit stove in the corner of my room, she spoke again, unprompted, in a voice so quiet I nearly missed it under the kettle-sound rising through the floor.

"She said you'd keep me." Her eyes stayed on the fire. "She said it like the catechism. She made me say it back."

"And did you believe her?"

That earned me the first real look she'd given me — quick, sideways, appraising, far too old. Children always know when adults are lying about comfort. They can't prove it, but they know.

"I'm here," she said.

Yes, I thought. You're here. On my floor, in a borrowed coat, in a port full of customs men and informers and a republic's police who had begun photographing strangers at the gates, carrying a name that appeared — though neither of us knew it yet — in a ministry ledger under the heading of things to be recovered. I stood at the spirit stove and watched the bread brown and thought about the word *promised*, and about a garden in the capital seven years ago, and a cradle under a pear tree, and a young colonel with ink on her fingers saying *say it*, and me, careless, agreeable, meaning nothing by it, saying *no child of yours will ever be taken while I draw breath.*

Promises have longer lives than treaties. Nobody tells you that. Treaties get torn up every decade; promises sit quietly in a drawer somewhere inside another person, gathering interest, waiting seven years to arrive at your door in the fog with frost in its hood.

I carried the toast over and she ate it in small, controlled bites, and I sat opposite her with my untouched cup and listened to the town wake up below — carts, gulls, the harbor bell — each ordinary sound suddenly a question. Was that a patrol? Was that early even for the fish market?

The letter in my lap said *burn this.* I fed it to the grate instead of the stove's flame, page by page, and watched Veyra's crossing-tailed letters curl and go, and felt no safer, because paper is the smallest part of any secret. The rest of it was standing in a dead man's coat by my fire, warming her hands.

Outside, on the street, boots stopped in front of the house. Two sets. They stood there a long moment — long enough for me to cross the room and put my body between Tamsin and the window — and then moved on, unhurried, toward the quay.

Tamsin looked up at me. She hadn't made a sound.

"It's all right," I said, and for the first time in my life I lied to a child, and found I was good at it.
