## 5. What Was Promised in Peacetime

The child slept in the narrow bed with her fists curled under her chin, and Astrid sat in the one chair by the window and could not stop looking at her.

It was the hands that did it. A six-year-old's hands should be soft, unmarked, hands that had never held anything heavier than a spoon or a doll. Tamsin's were chapped at the knuckles, the nails bitten to nothing, one thumb worrying at the seam of the blanket even in sleep — a habit of someone who had learned early that things could be taken away mid-grasp. Astrid knew that habit. She had it herself.

She should have been sewing the code-cylinder deeper into her coat lining. She should have been memorizing tide tables for the ship that was supposed to arrive in three days, the ship that would carry her north and out of this collapsing country and back into a life where she was no one, which was the only kind of life she had ever trusted. Instead she sat in Mrs. Halloway's cheapest room with a lamp turned low, watching an enemy general's daughter breathe, and the past came up through the floorboards like water through a hull.

* * *

Nine years ago, Astrid Nystrom was twenty-three years old and the most junior clerk attached to the Treaty of Saltmere, which meant she carried paper.

That was all. She carried paper from one pavilion to another across a garden that smelled of cut grass and river mud, and the men who actually wrote history — the ministers, the plenipotentiaries, the colonels with their ribboned chests — passed through her without seeing her, the way people pass through furniture. She had understood by then that this was not a failure. This was a profession. The republic's negotiators needed someone to move documents whose existence they could deny, and Astrid had discovered she had two gifts: a memory that held faces and figures without effort, and a face that held nothing at all. She was a gray woman in a gray coat. Delegates mistook her for a servant, then for a secretary, then for nobody, and every time they made the mistake she filed it away like currency.

The treaty itself was a fiction everyone agreed to believe. Two republics, or a republic and what was still politely called a neighboring state, had spent four years edging toward war over a river delta and its shipping lanes, and now both sides were exhausted enough to sit under canvas in a neutral garden and pretend that signatures could hold back what ambition could not. Astrid carried drafts. She watched clauses die and be resurrected with different punctuation. She learned which delegates drank, which lied about their instructions, which kept mistresses on either side of the border — information she stored without quite knowing why, the way a squirrel stores nuts against a winter it cannot imagine.

General Sera Veyra arrived on the fourth day.

Astrid knew the name before she knew the face, because the name had been in every dispatch for a month: Veyra, the youngest general either delegation had brought, the woman who had ended the border skirmish at Kolvat in eleven days and then, notoriously, fed the prisoners from her own kitchens before releasing them. Half the ministry called her a butcher. The other half called her a politician in uniform, which in the capital was the worse insult.

In person she was smaller than her reputation. That was Astrid's first note, penciled mentally: *smaller than expected.* Dark hair cut practically short, a scar along the jaw that the official portraits had already begun to omit, eyes the color of wet slate that moved over a room the way Astrid's own did — cataloguing exits, cataloguing faces, deciding in seconds what each person wanted and how much of it they'd admit to. Astrid recognized the technique because it was hers. It was like meeting a mirror in a foreign country.

Veyra saw her on the second morning. Actually saw her.

"Your handwriting," the general said, intercepting Astrid between pavilions, holding up one of the draft pages. "You're the one who copies the annexes."

"Yes, General."

"The section on joint river administration. Page forty-one, paragraph three." Veyra's accent was northern, clipped, the vowels of the delta country that the treaty existed to divide. "There's an error carried through from page nine. The tonnage figure. It's been copied wrong four times now, and four delegations are about to initial a number based on a clerical mistake."

Astrid took the page. Checked it against her memory of page nine. The general was right; the error was hers, introduced in the third draft and faithfully reproduced ever since by every copyist including herself, because she trusted her own earlier work too much to re-verify it.

"That's a lesson I'll keep," Astrid said.

"What lesson?"

"That being consistent is more dangerous than being careful." She said it before thinking, which was rare for her, and Veyra laughed — one short bark, surprised out of her, quickly suppressed, as if laughter were contraband she'd forgotten to declare.

"You're the first person in this garden to say something true in four days," Veyra said. "What's your name?"

"Nystrom. Astrid Nystrom."

"Clerk Nystrom." The general handed back the page. Her slate eyes did their quick pass — coat, posture, hands, the way Astrid stood slightly angled so no one could read her face straight-on — and something in them sharpened with recognition, professional courtesy passing between practitioners of the same obscure craft. "You watch doors the way my scouts watch roads. If you ever tire of copying annexes, come north. I can always use people who notice things."

"I'm very good at not being noticed, General. It's the noticing that's the problem."

Another laugh, shorter. Then a delegate's voice called from the pavilion and Veyra was swallowed by the machinery of the negotiation, and Astrid went back to carrying paper, and thought no more about it than she thought about weather. Important people spoke to her perhaps twice a year. She had long ago stopped letting it mean anything.

But Veyra kept noticing her.

It was small things, spread across the three weeks of the conference. The general would take a cup of coffee directly from Astrid's tray when there were servants standing right there whose whole job it was. She'd ask Astrid's opinion of a courier route — *you've walked it, haven't you, you have the look of someone who's counted the kilometers* — and then actually listen to the answer, actually adjust a plan because of it. Once, when a minister publicly berated Astrid for a delay that was entirely the minister's own fault, Veyra, passing, said mildly, "The delay was mine, Minister. I asked for the extra copy. Blame has to land somewhere honest," and kept walking, leaving the minister purple and Astrid standing very still in the middle of the garden, feeling something she hadn't felt since childhood and couldn't immediately name.

It took her until evening to name it. *Fairness.* Someone powerful had spent a coin of their power on her, casually, expecting nothing back.

On the last night but one of the conference, there was a reception at the riverside house belonging to the neutral host — candles, string quartet, delegates drinking each other's healths with expressions of mutual autopsy. Astrid attended in her functionless way, stationed near the doors with dispatch cases, invisible among the visible. And it was there, through an open window onto the terrace, that she heard the argument.

"—will not bring her to the capital, that is final—"

A man's voice, low and furious. And Veyra's, equally low, equally furious, which was somehow worse; Astrid had heard the general angry in negotiations, cold and surgical, and this was different. This was anger with fear underneath it.

"She stays with me or she stays nowhere," Veyra said. "I will not hand my daughter to your mother's house while I sit at a table with these people for another year. You know what the ministries say about me. You know what happens to children of inconvenient officers when the wind shifts."

"She'll be safer in the capital—"

"She will be a hostage in the capital, and you know it, and your mother knows it, and the only reason you're suggesting it is that it's easier for you." A pause. Glass on stone. When Veyra spoke again her voice had dropped to something almost gentle, which was more frightening than the fury. "She's asleep in the east room. Go look at her and then tell me again about safety."

Footsteps. The man came around the corner of the terrace — a tall officer Astrid vaguely recognized from the delta delegation, handsome, weak-chinned, wearing the particular stiffness of a man who has lost an argument and intends to lose it gracefully — and walked past Astrid without seeing her, as everyone did, and went inside toward the east room.

And Astrid, who should have stayed exactly where she was, who had built a whole life on staying exactly where she was, found herself walking out onto the terrace instead. She told herself later it was to retrieve a dispatch case. There was no dispatch case. There never had been.

Veyra stood alone at the balustrade with her back to the garden, shoulders squared the way soldiers square them, which is to say badly, carrying weight in muscles not built for it.

"General," Astrid said.

Veyra turned. For one unguarded half-second her face was open — exhausted, stricken, human — and then it closed like a door. "Clerk Nystrom."

"I wasn't listening. I want to say that first. I was coming out for—" Nothing. There was nothing. Astrid abandoned the lie halfway, which was also unlike her. "I heard the end. I'm sorry. That's all. I'm sorry, and it isn't my business, and I'm going back inside."

"Wait." Veyra looked at her for a long moment. The music drifted out through the window; downriver, a barge sounded its horn in the dark. "Why did you come out?"

Because someone had finally seen her and she had discovered she hated being unseen after all. Because fairness was a debt and debts wanted paying. Because she was twenty-three and stupid in the specific way that good people are stupid, believing that words said kindly bind anyone but the person who says them.

"I don't know," Astrid said honestly.

Veyra nodded slowly, as if honesty were rarer than courage and worth more. "Come," she said. "Since you're here anyway. Since you're apparently the only person in this country who does things without a reason. Come see why I'm being unreasonable."

* * *

The east room was dim. A single lamp burned low beside a wicker cradle — an actual cradle, old-fashioned, painted white, the paint chipped along one rail where generations of teeth had tested it. Astrid stopped three steps inside the door, uncertain, and Veyra crossed to the cradle and lifted the blanket edge with a tenderness so total it seemed to belong to a different woman entirely.

The baby slept the way only very young children sleep: absolutely, completely, as if sleep were a country she held citizenship in and would never be deported from. One fist above the blanket. Breath moving her whole small body.

"She's fourteen months old," Veyra said quietly. "Her father thinks she's luggage. My own government thinks she's leverage. There are men in my ministry — good men, decorated men — who have said to my face that a general's child is a natural guarantee of good behavior, and they smiled when they said it, and no one else in the room objected." She touched the cradle rail, lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking or losing. "If the treaty fails — if any of this comes apart the way these things come apart — do you understand what will happen to her? Not to me. To her. Children don't get courts. They get categories."

Astrid said nothing. What was there to say? She was a clerk. She moved paper.

"I shouldn't have brought you in here," Veyra said, half to herself. "You'll think I'm asking for something."

"Are you?"

"No." And then, terribly, honestly: "Yes. God help me. Not what you think. Not action — you can't act, you're nobody, that's your whole value and I know it. I'm asking for—" The general stopped. Looked down at the sleeping child. Started again, in a voice stripped of rank, of strategy, of everything except the plain frightened animal beneath. "Everyone in my life wants something from me, Clerk Nystrom. My government wants obedience. My husband wants comfort. The enemy wants my signature. Do you know what I would give for one person who owes me nothing and wants nothing, who simply — remembers? Who holds something for me outside the machine?"

The lamp flickered. Downstairs the quartet moved into something slow and foolish, a dance tune played for people who no longer danced.

"I can't promise you anything real," Astrid said. "I'm furniture. You said yourself that's my value. Furniture doesn't hold things."

"Furniture holds things better than people do. People drop them." Veyra looked at her, and the slate eyes were wet and did not apologize for it. "Then promise me something unreal. Promise me the thing that costs you nothing tonight and might cost you everything someday. That's the only kind of promise that's ever worth making — the careless kind. The kind you make before you understand it."

Astrid looked at the child. At the fist above the blanket, curled around nothing, trusting the air. At the chipped paint on the cradle rail, worn by other babies, other wars, other promises made over sleeping heads by adults who would not be there when the bill came due.

She was twenty-three. She believed treaties would hold. She believed the world was mostly paperwork, and paperwork could be filed, and filed things could be found again if you needed them.

"If it ever comes to that," Astrid said — the careless words, the cheap words, spoken over a cradle by candlelight with a quartet playing downstairs — "if the world comes apart and someone comes for her — I'll remember. I'll hold it. Whatever I am by then, whatever side the paper puts me on — no child of yours gets taken while I'm breathing. That's not strategy, General. I just — I promise it. There. An unreal promise."

Veyra closed her eyes. When she opened them she was the general again, armor back in place, and she even managed something like a smile.

"Witnessed," she said softly, looking at the sleeping girl. "By the only party with anything at stake. Sleep well, Tamsin. You have an unlikely guardian. Don't tell her — she'll only worry about it later."

They left the room together. Veyra shook Astrid's hand in the corridor like a colleague, briefly, hard, and said, "Page forty-one, paragraph three. Never trust your own consistency, Clerk," and walked back down into the light and the music and the machinery of peace.

The Treaty of Saltmere was signed eleven days later. Everyone said it would hold for a generation.

It held nine years.

* * *

Promises have longer lives than treaties. No one tells you that. Treaties get signed, sealed, archived, burned; they have ceremonies and expiration dates. A promise is just words absorbed into a stranger's bloodstream, circulating silently year after year, waiting for the moment the body remembers it needs them.

Astrid sat by the window in Marrow Quay and watched Tamsin sleep and understood, finally, with the full weight of nine years settling on her, that she had never been making a gesture that night on the terrace. She had been signing something. And the other signatory — a hunted woman across a border, an enemy of the republic Astrid served, a general whose name was now spoken in dispatches the way storms were spoken of in harbors — had remembered. Had trusted. Had sent her the one thing she could not afford to send anyone else.

*Forget the face,* the note had said. But Veyra had known — must have known — that Astrid wouldn't. That was the whole point. That was why her.

Tamsin stirred. The worried thumb stilled on the blanket seam. In sleep her face went slack and young and utterly undefended, and Astrid saw, for the first time clearly, the shape of Veyra in it — the same stubborn set to the chin, the same architecture of the brow — and felt the promise wake fully in her chest like a muscle long unused, painful with revival.

Downstairs, faintly, the boarding-house door opened and closed. Footsteps in the hall. The particular hesitating knock of the ministry clerk who talked too much, the one from the coach, the one whose nervous eyes had lingered on the girl in the kitchen that afternoon a beat too long — the way a man lingers when a name is assembling itself in his memory.

Astrid rose without a sound. Old trade, old craft: weight on the outer edges of the feet, breath shallow, the gray-woman stillness descending over her like a coat.

But for the first time in nine years, invisibility felt less like armor than like a debt coming due.

She went down to answer the knock.
