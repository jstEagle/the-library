## 6. The Ledger

The knock came again at midmorning, and this time Astrid was the one who opened the door.

She had spent the hours since dawn doing what she knew how to do: nothing, visibly. She had eaten breakfast in the common room with her back to the window. She had mended a seam that did not need mending. She had asked Mrs. Halloway, in a voice pitched for boredom, whether the fog usually lifted by ten — and the landlady, drying cups behind the bar, had answered that it lifted when it lifted, and no amount of staring at it hurried God.

Tamsin was upstairs in the linen closet, which Astrid had chosen over the bedroom because it had no window and because a child hidden among sheets was a child hidden among explanations. The girl had gone without argument. That frightened Astrid more than crying would have. Six years old, and she understood closets.

The man on the doorstep was the clerk from the coach.

Astrid had memorized him out of habit, the way she memorized everything: medium height, thinning brown hair combed with too much care for a man whose coat had worn shiny at the cuffs. Ministry of Records, he'd said on the first day of travel, when nobody asked. He'd talked the whole way north — about requisitions, about the price of coal, about his mother's lungs and the sea air being good for them — and Astrid had catalogued him as *harmless, nervous, talks to fill silence*, which was its own kind of danger, because men who filled silence sometimes shook loose things that should have stayed packed.

"Mrs. — " He consulted a small notebook. "Nystrom. You're Nystrom, yes? We shared the coach."

"We did." She kept her hand on the door. Not closed. Closed doors invited suspicion; half-open doors invited conversation, and conversation could be ended politely.

"I'm Pell," he said. "Oskar Pell. I don't know if you remember—"

"You talked about coal prices."

A flicker of relief went through his face, the relief of a man who wanted above all things to be remembered as unremarkable. "That's me. Look — I'm sorry to trouble you, but I've been asking after you since we arrived. The harbor office said this boarding house took travelers, and the landlady said—" He stopped himself. "May I come in? It's rather cold, and what I have to say isn't for the street."

Everything anyone had to say in Marrow Quay lately wasn't for the street. Astrid weighed him. His breath steamed. His shoes were wrong for the weather — town shoes, leather-soled, soaked through — which meant he'd been walking since early morning, knocking on doors, asking after *her*. A harmless man did not walk a frozen port town at dawn looking for a woman he'd met once on a coach.

But refusing him told him something too.

"Five minutes," she said, and stepped aside.

* * *

The common room was empty at that hour. Mrs. Halloway had vanished into the kitchen with a clatter of pans that announced, clearly enough, that she intended to hear every word through the door while pretending not to. Astrid sat Pell at the table farthest from the kitchen and sat herself where she could see both him and the stairs.

He put his notebook on the table between them, then covered it with his hand, as though it might bolt.

"You'll think this strange," he began.

"I already do."

Something like a laugh escaped him, quickly strangled. "Yes. Well. I'm a records clerk — I told you that. Third grade. It sounds grander than it is. I spend my days copying lists into other lists. Inventories. Seizure manifests. Confiscation schedules." He recited it flatly, the way a man recites his own smallness. "When the northern ministries were evacuated last month, we burned most of what couldn't be carried. But some things were deemed too valuable to burn, so they were boxed and sent south with escorts, and some of those boxes came through my office, and part of my work — my only interesting work, if you can call it that — was verifying contents against manifests before they went into the archive."

"Pell." Astrid let impatience sit in her voice like a stone. "It is cold, and you are wasting my five minutes."

He swallowed. His hand tightened on the notebook. "Yesterday evening I was in the taproom downstairs. I wasn't eavesdropping. I want to say that plainly. But this house has thin walls, and I heard a child's voice, and children are — well." He looked at his own knuckles. "There aren't many children left in Marrow Quay, are there? They were evacuated weeks ago. Or they should have been."

The pan-clatter in the kitchen stopped.

"The child belongs to a relative of the landlady," Astrid said. "Come ahead of the fighting."

"Yes," Pell said. "That's what I would have assumed too. Until I heard her name."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed over the frozen quay, and beneath it, faintly, came the sound Astrid had been hearing all morning and pretending was wind — boots, many boots, moving in step along the seawall.

"Tamsin," Pell said quietly.

Astrid did not move. She had been trained not to move — not by any ministry, but by twenty years of being furniture, of walking through checkpoints carrying other people's secrets in her body and learning that the face was the whole discipline. Stillness was not the absence of fear. Stillness was fear, held very carefully, like a full cup crossing a crowded room.

"That's an old name in these parts," she said. "Half the fishing families—"

"It's not the name alone." He opened the notebook. Under his thumb was a slip of paper — a copy, she realized, made in a clerk's careful hand, the letters upright and joyless. "I verified manifests for a seizure lot designated K-114. Confiscated from the Veyra household staff during the arrests in the capital. Personal papers. Correspondence. And a household ledger — not accounts, nothing so innocent. A listing of dependents. Staff, relatives, and..." He turned the slip toward her, and read, because reading it aloud was easier than letting her read it in silence:

"*Assets to be recovered upon consolidation of the northern territories.*"

"Recovered," Astrid repeated.

"That's the word used." His voice had gone thin. "Children, Mrs. Nystrom. There were eleven names. Children of officers, of staff, of — of people who might someday matter, or who might someday be useful, or who might simply be leverage. The republic calls General Veyra an enemy now, and everything hers became state property, and somewhere a third-grade clerk like me filed her daughter under the same heading as her silver and her horses." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, a man laughing at the bottom of his own profession. "*Tamsin Veyra,* age six. Recovered alive, condition permitting. Do you know what 'condition permitting' means? I know what it means. I wrote marginalia like that for nine years and never once thought about what it meant."

Astrid looked at the slip of paper upside down across the table. She could not read it from here. She did not need to. The words were already inside her, cold and official, the way the worst things always arrive dressed as paperwork.

"And you heard a child called Tamsin," she said, "in a boarding house, in a town where the name runs in half the fishing families, and you decided—" She let the sentence hang.

"I decided nothing." Pell's hands were shaking now. She watched him press them flat against the table to stop it, and fail. "That's why I'm here instead of at the harbor office. Because I keep deciding nothing. All night I've lain in my room deciding nothing. If I go to the authorities and I'm wrong, I've denounced a fisherman's daughter and started a search that turns over every house on this coast looking for a child who doesn't exist — and they'll find her anyway, someone's niece, someone's granddaughter, because searches don't stop when they should. And if I don't go, and I'm right—" He stopped.

"If you're right," Astrid said, "and you stay silent, and it ever comes out that a records clerk recognized the name and said nothing — what happens to a third-grade clerk in wartime?"

"They shoot clerks for less," he said simply. "They shoot everyone for less. That's what wartime is *for*."

The boot-steps on the seawall had stopped. Or perhaps the wind had covered them. Astrid found she was listening past him, past the kitchen door where Mrs. Halloway stood motionless with a pan in each hand, past everything, toward the linen closet two floors up where a six-year-old girl sat among sheets, silent as she'd been taught by no one, silent as if she had been born knowing that stillness was the only country left to her.

"Why tell me?" Astrid asked. It was the question that mattered. "You walked half the town at dawn. Why bring this to a woman you met on a coach?"

"Because you listened," Pell said. "On the coach, everyone else looked out the window when I talked. You looked at me. And because—" He hesitated, and she watched something move behind his face, some small machinery of conscience grinding against the larger machinery he served. "Because eleven names, Mrs. Nystrom. I copied that manifest twice. I checked the seal twice. Eleven children, listed between a set of candlesticks and a case of brandy, and my signature at the bottom attesting the inventory complete. I have been telling myself since yesterday that a clerk verifies paper, that paper isn't people, that the word *assets* is just a word. But you looked at me on that coach like a person looks at a person. So I'm asking you — a traveler, a stranger, a neutral party — am I mad? Is there any version of this where I'm wrong?"

There it was. He didn't want her to confirm the name. He wanted her to absolve him, and either answer would.

Astrid considered lying. She was good at lies the way a carpenter is good at joints — fitted tight, load-bearing. *A relative of the landlady. A common local name. Coincidence.* Three sentences, delivered with mild offense, and Oskar Pell would go away and carry his doubt like a stone in his shoe until the war buried him.

But he had shown her the slip of paper. And whatever else happened next, one thing had become true in this room: the ledger existed. Eleven names. Tamsin Veyra written between candlesticks and brandy, in a clerk's upright hand, under a ministry seal, filed in an archive south of here. The girl upstairs was not hiding from soldiers anymore. She was hiding from a *record*, and records did not sleep, did not forget, did not grow bored. Records waited.

And Pell had already memorized the name. That was the trap he'd built himself into without seeing it: even if he walked away now, even if he chose silence, the name lived in his head alongside his signature, and a man who talked about coal prices to strangers on coaches does not carry a secret well. Someone would ask him, sooner or later, why he looked ill. Someone would buy him a drink. The machinery didn't need him to inform. It only needed him to be weak, and weakness was the one resource the republic still had in abundance.

"Pell," she said. "Look at me."

He looked. His eyes were wet and ordinary and young, younger than his tired face.

"If I tell you what you want to hear," Astrid said slowly, "you'll sleep tonight and hate yourself tomorrow. If I tell you what you're afraid of, you'll do your duty, and a child goes into a file cabinet, and you'll hate yourself forever. Those are your choices. I won't make them for you. But I'll tell you one true thing, and you may weigh it however you like." She leaned forward. "Whatever you decide, decide it alone. Don't report a *question*. Questions start searches, and searches don't check their arithmetic. If you carry this to the harbor office as doubt, they will resolve your doubt for you, and they will not be careful about how."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Astrid agreed. "It's better than one."

Pell sat very still. Outside, the gull cried again, and beneath it — unmistakable now, not wind — the measured beat of boots along the seawall, pausing, resuming. A patrol settling into a rhythm. An occupation learning its streets.

He gathered his notebook. He stood. At the doorway he turned, and for a moment the nervous chatterbox from the coach was gone entirely, and what remained was a gray little man holding the largest thing he had ever held.

"For what it's worth," he said, "on the coach, I talked too much because I was afraid. Everyone thinks I talk because I'm foolish. It's because silence makes me feel the war sitting down next to me." He managed something that was almost a smile. "You never talked at all. I used to wonder what you were afraid of."

Then he was gone, and his footsteps crossed the hall, and the front door opened on the cold, and shut.

* * *

Astrid stood at the window of the common room and watched him go.

He did not head for the harbor office. She tracked him along the far side of the street, past the chandler's shuttered windows, past the customs shed with its new flag, and he walked slowly, a man measuring every step, and at the corner where the lane bent uphill toward the garrison road he stopped. He stood there a long moment, hat in his hands, a small brown figure in the gray light, deciding nothing with all his strength.

Then he turned uphill.

Astrid's stomach went cold and quiet, the way it did before crossings, before checkpoints, before every moment her trade had taught her to recognize as the point where the map ends and the territory begins. Uphill was the garrison. Uphill was the paymaster's office and the provost's desk and a duty officer with a form for exactly this: *possible identification, enemy dependent, request verification and search authorization.*

She counted his steps until the corner took him. Forty-one. She had counted without meaning to. Her mind was already working the way it worked on deliveries — routes, times, weights — except that the weight it was calculating now was forty pounds of sleeping child in a linen closet, and the route was a frozen coast with soldiers on the seawall and ships seized at anchor, and the time was whatever distance lay between this minute and a clerk finding his courage, or his duty officer finding his forms.

"Astrid." Mrs. Halloway stood in the kitchen doorway, still holding her pans, her face gray as the harbor. "I heard."

"Then you heard a man talking nonsense about files and names."

"I heard a man talking about a ledger." The old woman set the pans down with exaggerated care, as though her hands needed a job or they'd shake. "Girl-child's kin of mine, like you said. Fisherman's blood. Common name." She said it loudly, for the walls, for the thin house, for whoever listened. Then, barely above breath: "How long have we got?"

Astrid looked at the stairs. Up there, behind a door with no lock, among sheets that smelled of lavender and other people's better years, Tamsin was waiting the way children wait — absolutely, obediently, believing that the adults downstairs were managing the world.

"Not long enough," Astrid said. "Get her warm things. Not her own coat — anything but her own coat. And Mrs. Halloway — the back stair. Is there a back stair?"

"There's a coal yard," Mrs. Halloway said, "and beyond the coal yard there's a wall, and beyond the wall there's the marsh, and beyond the marsh—" She stopped. Beyond the marsh there was nothing. That was rather the point of Marrow Quay.

Downhill, near the harbor office, a whistle blew — two notes, short and sharp, repeated. Then again from the seawall. Then, faint and answering, from the garrison road above.

Not a drill. Drills had a lazy rhythm to them. This was a message passing hand to hand, a thread being pulled through the town, and Astrid stood at the window and understood, with the clarity that arrives only when it is useless, that Oskar Pell had not walked uphill to decide.

He had walked uphill to report.

She turned for the stairs, taking them two at a time, toward the linen closet and the child inside it, and behind her, through the thin walls of Mrs. Halloway's house, she could hear the boots coming down off the seawall — not marching past this time.

Turning in.
