## 7. Mrs. Halloway's Silence

Edith Halloway had kept a boarding house on Marrow Quay for nineteen years, and in that time she had learned that the most valuable thing she sold was not the beds or the breakfasts but the not-seeing. Fishermen paid for it. Smugglers paid double. The occasional government man paid triple and got less of it than he thought, because Edith Halloway's eyes were old and slow-seeming and missed nothing.

She saw the child on the second morning.

Not the first — the first morning there had only been the courier woman coming down the stairs at half past six with her coat buttoned wrong, which Edith noted and said nothing about, because a guest whose coat buttons wrong is a guest carrying something worth more than her life. That was arithmetic, not curiosity. Edith did sums all day long: how many eggs, how much coal, how many weeks until the herring boats stopped running if the war came north like they said it would.

But on the second morning, when she carried the tea tray up past the landing, she heard — through the door of the corner room, the one with the good lock that the courier woman had asked for by name — a small voice saying, "Again," and then a silence, and then a woman's voice, low and patient, saying, "Again. Slower this time. You are a stone. Stones don't giggle."

And then a child's laugh, quickly strangled, the way you'd smother a candle with your hand.

Edith stood on the landing with her tray going cold and did the sum. A courier who traveled alone did not smuggle children. A woman who taught a child to be still was not a mother showing off; mothers showed off. This was the other thing, the older thing. This was a woman teaching a child how not to be found, which meant someone was looking, which meant trouble, which meant — and here Edith set down the tray on the landing table with a click, deciding — that she had seen nothing at all.

She knocked on the door anyway. Two raps, the landlady's knock, meaning business not friendship.

The door opened the width of a hand. The courier woman — Nystrom, the register said, though Edith doubted every word written in any register these days — filled the gap entirely. Behind her the room looked exactly as a room should look: bed made, window shut, nothing out of place. Too exact. Rooms lived in by one person had a wrinkle somewhere.

"More hot water?" Edith said. "There's a kettle downstairs if you want it."

"Thank you. We're fine."

*We.* Edith let the word sit between them like a coin on a counter. The woman didn't flinch, didn't correct herself. Good, Edith thought. Flinching was how people drowned.

"The ship from Corvane's been delayed," Edith said instead. "Harbor master says three days, maybe four. Fog on the shoals."

"I can wait."

"Most people say that until the third day." Edith turned to go, then stopped, because some things you said and some things you didn't, and she had spent nineteen years learning the difference, and this morning the arithmetic came out the other way. "Miss Nystrom. Whatever you're waiting for — the fog's thick this time of year. Thickest on the quay road after dark. Thick enough that a person could walk from here to the salt sheds without being seen from the water."

The courier woman's face did something very small and very controlled, the way ice does before it decides whether to hold or break.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Edith agreed. "I expect you don't." And she went downstairs and told no one anything, which was, as her mother would have said, the whole of the job.

* * *

Her mother had said other things too, in the years before the fever took her. *A woman survives by the width of what she refuses to look at,* she'd said once, kneading bread in a kitchen in the capital, back when Edith was young enough to think survival was a given rather than a trade. Edith had thought it was cynicism then. She knew better now. It was cartography. You mapped the edges of your own blindness the way a sailor maps the shoals, and you stayed inside them, and you lived.

Edith had two daughters once. Everyone in Marrow Quay who remembered the Halloways knew she'd had two daughters, because grief that old becomes furniture; people stop noticing it the way you stop noticing a stain. Marta, born in spring. Lise, born in the winter the ice broke early. Marta had died at nine days, too small, too soon, the ordinary cruelty of the world. Lise had lived to be eleven.

Lise was the one Edith did not talk about, and therefore the one everyone understood best.

It had been during the last border troubles, twenty years back — everyone called them the last border troubles, as if borders were done troubling anyone, as if history were a tide that had gone out for good. There had been lists then too. Edith hadn't known that at the time. Lists were things that happened in capitals, in ministries, in books with seals on them. In Marrow Quay there were just men who came on a gray afternoon asking questions, and a neighbor girl named Ansa who answered too many of them, and by evening there was a wagon, and by morning Lise was gone, and the men had papers saying that the daughter of Edith Halloway, née Voss, had been *relocated for her own protection*, and that the matter was closed.

Closed. As if a child were a ledger entry that could be ruled off with a line.

Edith had gone to every office that existed. She had walked into the provincial customs house with Lise's christening shawl folded under her arm like evidence, because it was evidence, it was the only thing in the world that proved the child had been real. A clerk had looked at the shawl, and then at her, with the particular pity that powerful men reserve for women without power, and had said, *Madam, I'm sure this is distressing, but these matters are handled at a level above both of us.*

Both of us. As if he and she were in the same boat. As if the boat weren't the whole point.

Lise had never come back. Not in the amnesty, not in the inquiries, not in the mass graves they opened in '61 when the wind shifted and everyone pretended to be horrified by what everyone had known. Somewhere there was a record, Edith supposed. Somewhere in some ministry cellar a line of ink said *Halloway, L., age 11, asset recovered, disposition complete.* Disposition. Like goods. Like herring.

So when Edith Halloway heard a child laughing behind a locked door in her own house — laughing and then strangling the laugh, being taught to strangle it — she did not need to be told whose list that child might be on. There was only ever one kind of list. It grew longer every year and shorter every peace, and the names on it were always eleven years old, or six, or nine days.

She went into her kitchen and began making bread she did not need, because her hands thought better than her head, and while her hands worked she made her decision the way she made all important decisions: slowly, and then all at once, and then never mentioned again.

She would not see the child.

She would feed the child — extra portions went up to the corner room now, an egg where there had been none, the heel of the white loaf instead of the grey — and she would not see her. She would leave the bathwater heating and not ask who it was for. She would mend a coat sleeve that was too short for Miss Nystrom's arms and hang it on the corner room's door without comment, and she would not notice, ever, that it fit somebody smaller. She would learn the sound of small feet on her stairs and file it under *nothing*, under *mice*, under the wide warm category of things Edith Halloway's old eyes had failed to observe.

This was not cowardice. Or rather — she was honest enough, kneading, to admit it might be cowardice, but it was cowardice with load-bearing walls. Because looking away was also a way of standing between. The ministry men, when they came — and they would come; they always came, the republic's decay rolling downhill toward ports like hers, clerks with lists and soldiers behind the clerks — those men would find a deaf old landlady who noticed weather and rents and nothing else. They would find the fog she had been building for nineteen years, room by room, tenant by tenant, silence by silence. And inside the fog, God willing, a child would stay unlisted a little longer.

Ansa had seen everything, that day twenty years ago. Had pointed, had named, had wanted favor. Edith had hated her for thirty years and then forgiven her in year thirty-one, because Ansa had lost a son to the press gangs and Edith understood, finally, that fear makes pointers of us all, and that forgiveness cost nothing and hatred cost sleep.

But understanding Ansa had never brought Lise home. What might have brought Lise home — what Edith lay awake turning over at four in the morning, nineteen years on, the way you turn a stone you cannot lift — was if one person, one, had chosen not to see. If the fishwife on the quay had miscounted wagons. If the harbormaster had misplaced his log. If the neighbor had been blind that one gray afternoon, willfully, generously blind, the way Edith intended to be blind starting now.

You couldn't save a child by looking straight at her. Looking straight at her was how you got her taken.

* * *

On the third day the clerk came back.

Edith watched him from the kitchen window as he picked his way along the quay road — the nervous one from the coach, the one who'd talked too much at dinner his first night, filling silence with ministry gossip the way a leaking boat fills with water. He'd left two days ago saying he had business in Corvane. He came back with different boots. Better boots. Boots bought with new money have a shine to them, and this shine had the specific greasy luster of informer's pay.

He stopped across the street from the boarding house and stood there a full minute, doing his own arithmetic, and Edith, watching through the gap in her curtain, felt the old cold move up her arms. She knew that stance. She had seen a man stand exactly so outside the harbormaster's office twenty years ago, checking a paper against a door.

He crossed the street. Knocked. Landlady's knock — no. Wrong. Three quick raps, official, the knock of a man who has recently learned he is allowed to knock like that.

Edith dried her hands and opened the door and gave him her face, the good one, the vague one, the one that said *old woman, bad hearing, weather, rent.*

"Mrs. Halloway." He smiled. His name was Pell, she remembered. Mr. Pell. He'd had soup twice and complimented the barley. "Good morning. I wonder — your guest, the courier woman. Is she in?"

"She's out," Edith said, which was true; she'd watched Nystrom go down toward the salt sheds an hour past, alone, coat buttoned right this time. "Shopping. These delays try everyone's patience."

"Mm. And she travels alone, does she? Your guest?"

Edith looked at him with the mild blankness of age. "Don't know as it's my business who travels with whom. Boarders pay their rent. The rest is theirs."

"Of course, of course." He was peering past her shoulder into the hall, into the stairwell, and Edith let him peer, because the hall was swept and bare and held nothing, because she had spent yesterday evening removing every trace smaller than a teacup — a wooden horse, painted blue, that had appeared on the windowsill of the corner room and that Edith had wrapped in cloth and buried in the flour bin without letting herself think about whose hands had carved it, or what mother was far away carving horses for a child she'd sent into fog. "Only — there's a matter of registration. New directives. All guests to be listed, ages of dependents noted, that sort of thing."

"Dependents," Edith said.

"Children, mostly." Pell smiled again. It sat badly on him, like boots two sizes too big. "War displaces families. The ministry wants to help. Reunification efforts."

Reunification. Edith turned the word over. Twenty years ago they'd called it relocation. The words changed and the wagon stayed the same. Somewhere in a ministry cellar, she thought, there is a page with my daughter's name on it, and some young man like this one calls it paperwork, and somewhere in this town there may be a page with another child's name, and this young man has better boots today because of it.

"You'll want to come back tomorrow," Edith said. "My ledger's at the bindery. Spine went loose. Hasn't been right since the damp got in." She tapped her temple. "Mine too."

Pell's eyes flickered — suspicion, calculation, the arithmetic of a man deciding whether an old woman was stupid or dangerous and finding the categories inconveniently overlapping. "Tomorrow, then," he said. "I do hope your memory holds out, Mrs. Halloway."

"Memory," Edith said pleasantly, closing the door, "is the first thing the damp gets."

She put her back against the door and listened to his boots recede down the quay road, and then she went to the bottom of the stairs and stood there, hands folded, an old woman in her own hallway, and raised her voice just enough to reach the third floor.

"Kettle's on," she called. "Whoever wants tea should come down before the fog comes in. Fog'll be thick tonight. Thickest of the season."

From upstairs, silence — a careful, listening, child-and-woman-shaped silence. Then, softly, two sets of footsteps, one heavy and one light as a bird's, coming down toward the warmth.

Edith went into her kitchen and set out three cups, and if anyone had asked her why there were three, she would have looked at them with her old slow eyes and said, *Three? I've no idea what you mean.*
