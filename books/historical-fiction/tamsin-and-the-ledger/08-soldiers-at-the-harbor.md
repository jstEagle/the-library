## 8. Soldiers at the Harbor

The drums woke the town before the light did.

Astrid came down the stairs with her coat half-buttoned and found Mrs. Halloway already at the window of the front parlor, a candle unlit in her hand, as if she'd forgotten what it was for. Outside, Marrow Quay was filling with grey. Not fog — she knew fog, had slept three nights inside its wet wool — but men. Columns of them coming down the coast road in the dark, boots striking cobbles in a rhythm too steady to be anything human and ordinary.

"Republic troops," Mrs. Halloway said. Her voice was flat, the voice of a woman reading a bill she'd expected for months. "Regulars, by the look of their kit. They've come by night march from Corvess."

"How many?"

"More than we have streets."

Astrid stood beside her at the glass and counted what could be counted: two companies forming up on the quay itself, an officer's party dismounting near the customs shed, a line of infantry already moving toward the harbor mouth where the fishing fleet lay moored like sleeping animals. Lanterns swung along the breakwater. Someone was hammering something to the customs-house door.

Above her, faint through the ceiling, she heard the small sound she had learned to listen for even in sleep: Tamsin turning over in bed. A child shifting under blankets. Alive. Present. Six years old and asleep in a house that had just become the wrong place to be.

* * *

They posted the proclamation before seven o'clock.

Astrid went out early — deliberately early, deliberately visible, because a woman who hid on the first morning of an occupation looked like a woman with something to hide, and the first rule of her trade was that you gave people exactly as much of yourself as they expected and not one grain more. She buttoned her coat to the throat with the code-cylinder sewn flat against her ribs, heavier than lead this morning, and walked to the customs house with the crowd of shopkeepers and fishwives gathering there like gulls over offal.

A lieutenant read it aloud in a bored parade-ground voice while two soldiers held it flat against the doorframe:

BY ORDER OF THE PROVISIONAL MILITARY GOVERNOR OF THE NORTHERN DISTRICT:

1. The port of Marrow Quay is hereby closed to all maritime traffic until further notice.
2. All vessels in harbor are subject to seizure and requisition.
3. A curfew is imposed from dusk until dawn. Persons abroad after curfew will be shot.
4. All travelers must present papers at the customhouse within forty-eight hours.
5. Any person harboring enemies of the republic, deserters, or enemy dependents will be tried for treason.

The word *dependents* went through the crowd like a fishhook through bait. Astrid heard a woman behind her whisper it back to herself — *dependents* — tasting the shape of it, understanding without being told that the republic's new arithmetic counted children among the spoils.

She did not look at anyone. She memorized everyone.

The lieutenant: young, chin soft, eyes tired, reading orders he hadn't written. The sergeant beside him: older, scarred knuckles, watching the crowd the way a farmer watches weather. Twelve sentries posted along the quay. Two at the harbor mouth already boarding the *Meridian*, the packet ship Astrid had been waiting five days to catch — the ship that was meant to carry her, and her sealed cylinder, onward to the neutral port and the end of her commission.

She watched soldiers carry the *Meridian*'s passenger manifest out of the purser's cabin and hand it to a clerk at the customhouse table. Her name would be on it. Her destination. Her purpose, if the purser had recorded it honestly, which purser's rarely bothered to do — *one lady, passage north* — but still. Somewhere in that building, in ink, was the fact of her.

For nine days she had told herself the same story: I am furniture. Furniture is not seized; furniture is sat upon. But furniture does not have a name on a manifest, and furniture cannot be charged with treason for the company it keeps.

The routes were closing. She could feel them shutting one by one, doors down a long corridor, each thud fainter than the last. Sea: closed. She turned and looked south, down the coast road she'd come up on the grey coach. The coach itself stood at the inn yard, horses unharnessed, driver smoking sullenly against the wheel. A corporal was going through the luggage compartment with a bayonet, prodding parcels open.

Road: watched. And every road out of Marrow Quay ran past a checkpoint now, or soon would.

Which left the marshes, the river, the mudflats where smugglers moved goods that governments pretended not to see. Routes that existed only for people who knew them, and Astrid did not know them, and could not ask after them without becoming interesting.

*Interesting* was the one thing she could not afford to be.

* * *

At Mrs. Halloway's, the morning proceeded with terrible normalcy.

Bread was baked. Kettle boiled. The lodger in the blue room — Mr. Pell, the nervous ministry clerk who talked too much on the coach and had somehow, catastrophically, ended up lodged in the same house as Astrid and her secret cargo of a child — came down pale and chattering about requisition orders and how he'd known, he'd *known*, the northern district would fall to military administration before winter's end, and didn't Mrs. Halloway think the bread was excellent given the circumstances, and had she heard they'd taken the *Meridian*?

"I heard," Astrid said.

"Everything in the harbor. Everything." Pell buttered toast with hands that wouldn't quite steady. "Do you know what that means? It means the navy's mutinied or scattered or sold itself, whichever story you prefer, because if there were still a republican fleet worth the name, they'd never dare close a port this far north. We're beyond the reach of the government now. We're governed by whoever has the most rifles." He laughed, a small cracked sound. "I'm supposed to be in Corvess. I took a holiday. Can you imagine. A holiday. I chose this week to visit my sister."

"When did your sister leave?" Astrid asked mildly.

Pell blinked. "Left?"

"You said *visited*. Past tense."

"She — she went back south in September. Before things got—" He waved the knife vaguely at the window, at the drums, at the century. "Yes. Before."

Astrid filed it away, the way she filed everything: Pell alone, Pell stranded, Pell with no family in town and nothing anchoring him but his salary and his terror. A frightened man with a ministry salary in an occupied town was either a corpse waiting to be robbed or an informer waiting to be useful. Often both.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

Astrid's cup paused halfway to her mouth. Mrs. Halloway, pouring tea, did not so much as glance at the ceiling. "Mice," she said, to no one, in a voice that dared the world to disagree.

"The mice in this house," said Pell, "are remarkably heavy-footed."

"They're old house mice," said Mrs. Halloway. "Old houses, old mice. They know all the boards."

Astrid drank her tea and thought: *I need to teach that child to walk like a mouse.* The thought arrived fully formed, practical, cold at the edges, and beneath it something softer that she refused to examine yet — because examining it would slow her down and slowness killed.

* * *

She spent the day learning the occupation the way she learned everything: by being dull.

Dull women bought herring at the fish stall and heard the fishwife spit that the soldiers had paid in scrip nobody honored. Dull women queued at the well and learned that the garrison had commandeered the customs house cellar for stores, that the officers were billeted at the Anchor and Chain, that the colonel commanding was one Brandt, a name that meant nothing to her and therefore might mean everything. Dull women walked the sea wall at noon and counted, without appearing to count: sentry posts, patrol intervals, the gap of nearly eleven minutes between rounds along the eastern breakwater where the fog came thickest.

Eleven minutes. She turned the number over like a coin, testing its weight.

And everywhere she walked, she saw the other thing, the thing underneath the proclamation's dry language: the machinery warming up. A clerk's table set out on the quay with a ledger open to the sky, registering vessels. Soldiers nailing notices to church doors. A pair of provosts going door to door along Rope Lane with a list, asking each household how many persons resided within.

*How many persons.*

By dusk the answer to that question, asked at Mrs. Halloway's door, would be four: landlady, clerk, courier, and — unless someone lied, unless someone looked away — a six-year-old girl whose surname was on a list somewhere between *assets* and *enemies*.

Astrid climbed the hill road above the town as the light failed — the only person mad enough to be out walking for pleasure, which made her invisible precisely because the sentries wrote her off as a spinster taking her constitutionals. From the ridge she could see the whole harbor laid out below: the seized ships riding at anchor with prize crews aboard, lanterns strung along the quay, the smoke of the garrison cookfires. A town swallowed. A port with its throat closed.

Beyond the harbor mouth, the marshes ran black toward the river country and the border, forty miles of frozen reed and tidal creek where no proclamation reached and no column marched. Somewhere out there was a boat, a man willing to row it, and a price. Everything had a price. She had money — not enough — and she had the cylinder against her ribs, which was worth more than money and cost more than her life.

She had known since the drums began, in the honest cellar of herself where couriers keep their true accounts, that the mission and the girl could not both fit through what remained of this town. She had simply not yet let the knowing climb into the daylight.

From the ridge, the curfew drum sounded. Dusk. In the streets below, doors began closing one by one, and the lamplighter made his rounds flanked by a soldier with a rifle, as though the dark itself now required permission.

Astrid turned back toward the boarding house, walking fast, a dull woman anxious to be indoors before the shooting hour.

* * *

Mrs. Halloway met her in the hall with a finger raised: *wait*. Then she drew Astrid into the parlor, shut the door, and stood with her back against it.

"Two provosts came," she said quietly. "Door-to-door census. Persons residing within, occupations, papers."

"What did you tell them?"

"Four persons. Myself, Mr. Pell, yourself — and my granddaughter." Mrs. Halloway's face did not change at all, which was how Astrid knew what it had cost. "Tamsin. My son's girl, come to stay while her mother's laid up in Corvess. Sickly. The provosts didn't ask much. They wrote it in their book and went on." She folded her hands. "So. As far as the army is concerned, you've a niece."

For a moment Astrid could not speak. This woman — who had taken in a stranger's child with a note that said *forget the face*, who owed Veyra nothing and the republic less, who survived by looking away — had just walked straight into the fire and called the girl her own blood.

"Why?" Astrid managed.

Mrs. Halloway looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Astrid's face seemed to settle some question of her own. "I had daughters," she said. "You'll have noticed I say it in the past tense and don't elaborate. That's all any of us get to keep of certain stories — the tense." She unlocked the door with her thumb on the latch. "Your niece wants her supper. And Courier Nystrom — the girl walks like a grown woman. Heavy heels. Whatever it is you're planning to teach her, teach it fast."

* * *

That night Astrid sat on the edge of Tamsin's narrow bed and listened to the town.

The child was pretending to sleep, breathing in the careful theatrical way children breathe when they want to be left alone with their fear, and Astrid let her pretend for a while. Through the window, muffled by shutter and glass, came the sounds of occupation: boots on the quay, a shouted challenge at some late walker, once — terribly close, up the hill lane — the barked laughter of a patrol outside the Anchor and Chain.

"They're counting everybody," Tamsin whispered at last, into the dark. "The lady downstairs told me. Counting everybody in their book."

"Yes."

"Am I in the book?"

"Not tonight." Astrid smoothed the blanket over the small rigid shoulders. "Tonight you are Mrs. Halloway's granddaughter, and granddaughters are allowed."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then, in the small reasonable voice of six years old: "But books can change."

Astrid's chest tightened around something hot and formless. "Yes," she said. "Books can change. So we're going to make sure that when they change, they don't find you." She leaned closer, keeping her voice low and level, the voice she used for instructions that mattered. "Tomorrow we begin. You'll learn to walk so no one hears you. To stand so no one sees you. When soldiers come to the door, you'll be in the coal cellar, and you'll be silent as stone, and you will not come out until I come for you — not for any voice, not for my voice, do you understand? If you hear me call, you stay. Only my hand on the cellar door opens it. Nothing else."

"In case they make a voice like yours," Tamsin said. Not a question. Children in collapsing republics learned the grammar of terror before they learned their letters.

"In case they make a voice like mine," Astrid agreed. Outside, a dog began barking down by the harbor, then another, then the flat crack of a rifle and silence. Somewhere a bell rang twice and stopped. The corridor of closed doors had run out; the last door was this room, this bed, this child, and behind it only the marsh and the river ice and whatever a smuggler would charge to cross them.

Astrid blew out the candle and lay down on the pallet beside the bed, coat still buttoned, cylinder pressing its cold secret against her ribs, and made her list in the dark the way she'd been trained to make lists: *eleven minutes on the east breakwater; the marsh gate behind Halloway's garden; the tide tables pinned in the fishmonger's window; the price of everything.*

Below in the street, boots passed. Paused. Passed on.

In two days, the census clerk's book would reach the district office in Corvess. In three, perhaps, some thorough captain would cross-check a confiscated ledger against a boarding-house register and find that General Veyra's daughter had been born in a year when Mrs. Halloway's son had been at sea. Paper was patient. Paper always caught up.

Unless, Astrid thought, staring at the ceiling, someone burned it first.
