## 9. Teaching a Child to Be Invisible

The lesson began with a button.

Astrid held it up between finger and thumb — a plain brass button, pried that morning from the sleeve of a coat Mrs. Halloway no longer wore. She set it on the table between them, beside the cold remains of breakfast porridge.

"Pick it up," she said.

Tamsin reached for it at once, small fingers quick and sure, and Astrid caught her wrist before the child's skin touched brass.

"No. Watch first."

She let go. Tamsin sat back, brow furrowed, six years old and already learning that adults in Marrow Quay said *no* more often than they said anything else.

"Before you touch anything," Astrid said, "you look at it. You look at who else might be looking at it. You count the doors. You find the second way out of every room you're ever in. Then — only then — do you reach."

Tamsin looked at the button. Looked at the door to the hall. Looked at the window with its crack in the glass that whistled when the wind came off the water. Then she picked up the button and put it in her pocket without a word.

Astrid's chest did something complicated and painful. She had taught this drill to grown men in the ministry's courier service — men twice her size who still fumbled it, who still reached for things like children. This girl had taken one lesson and folded it away like a letter into its envelope.

"Good," Astrid said, and found her voice was not quite steady. "Again."

* *

The soldiers had come to the harbor three days ago, and since then Astrid had rebuilt their days the way she'd once been taught to build a legend: from the ground up, from nothing, from habit.

Mornings: bread, tea, the button game, which had become the counting game, which had become a dozen small drills disguised as play. Tamsin learned to count windows without moving her eyes toward them. She learned to walk at heel, half a step behind and to the left, so an adult's body would break the sightline of anyone watching from behind. She learned that when Astrid touched her shoulder twice, they changed direction; once meant stop; a squeeze of the hand meant say nothing even if someone speaks to you, even if someone shouts, even if someone says your name.

Especially if someone says your name.

"You're not Tamsin here," Astrid told her, kneeling on the boarding house floor so their eyes were level. "Names are handles. People grab handles. So we take yours off and put it somewhere safe until this is over."

The child considered this with terrible seriousness. "Where?"

"Somewhere only we know."

Tamsin thought about it. "In your coat," she said finally, "with the other paper."

Astrid had not told her about the code-cylinder sewn into the lining. But the girl had seen her check the seam every morning, had watched her press two fingers against the left breast the way other people crossed themselves, and children saw everything. That was the whole problem with children. They were the best observers Astrid had ever met and nobody trained them because everyone assumed they weren't looking.

"Yes," Astrid said. "With the other paper."

"What's my name now?"

Astrid had prepared for this. She had spent a sleepless night preparing for this, lying in the dark listening to the foghorn and inventing legends the way she'd been taught: keep them dull, keep them close to true, give the lie a spine of real bone so it doesn't snap under questioning.

"Mira," she said. "You're my sister's daughter. My sister is dead. There was sickness, last winter, and I've come north to take you because there's no one else. You're sad about your mother but you don't cry in front of strangers, because your mother told you big girls don't, and you promised her."

Each piece was a hook to hang a question on. Grief explained silence. A dead mother explained why no one could be written to for verification. The promise explained the child's discipline better than any training could — because a promise to the dead was the one kind of promise a stranger wouldn't test.

Tamsin repeated it back without stumbling. "I'm Mira. My mama died in the winter. I'm sad but I don't show it." Then, smaller: "Is Mama Veyra really dead?"

"Not yet," Astrid said, before she could stop herself, and then wished the word back. *Not yet* was a spy's answer. A mother's answer would have been *of course not, darling.*

But Tamsin only nodded, accepting it as children accept all the strange arithmetic of war, and asked the next question, which was worse.

"And you? Who are you?"

And Astrid, who had worn a dozen names across nine years of carrying sealed paper through collapsing borders, found she had no ready answer at all.

* *

They practiced in the room, then in the hall, then — carefully, in increments, like a man testing river ice — in the street.

Mrs. Halloway helped in her way, which was to help by not helping. She appeared at the top of the stairs whenever soldiers passed the house and announced loudly that the roof leaked, the pipes knocked, the landlady wanted a word — anything to move Astrid and "her niece" out of sightlines without either of them appearing to hurry. She never asked what they were doing. She never once said the girl's name aloud. Astrid understood this was its own kind of craft, and that the older woman had learned it long ago, in some winter Astrid would probably never hear about.

Out of doors, the lessons sharpened.

"Don't look at uniforms," Astrid murmured as they walked the fish market with its smell of brine and ice. "Everyone looks at uniforms. That's why soldiers watch for people watching them. You look at barrels. At shoes. At the middle distance, like you're bored."

"Bored," Tamsin repeated.

"Bored is invisible. Interested is memorable. Frightened is *very* memorable. Which do you want to be?"

"Bored."

"So be bored."

It worked, mostly. The girl had a gift for blankness — a stillness Astrid had never managed herself at any age. When soldiers swept past on patrol, Tamsin went quiet in a way that wasn't frozen, exactly. It was more like water going flat. Astrid, walking beside her with a market basket she didn't need filled, felt something twist in her chest that was equal parts pride and grief. No six-year-old should know how to go flat.

On the fourth day a sergeant stopped them outside the chandlery.

"Papers."

Astrid produced hers — genuine, valid, useless now for ships but fine for streets — and felt Tamsin's hand slip into hers, performing the grip of a tired child, bored, bored, bored.

"Your kid?" The sergeant glanced down.

"My niece. Sister's girl. Come to live with me since the sickness." The lie came out smooth, warm, boring. She let her shoulders slump just slightly, the posture of a woman worn down by grief and fish prices and nothing interesting at all.

He waved them on. They walked ten steps. Twenty. Fifty. Only at the corner did Astrid permit herself to breathe, and only when they were indoors again did she kneel and take Tamsin by both arms.

"Do you know how good you were?"

The girl's composure cracked, just barely, into something young and blazing. "I counted his buttons. He was missing one."

Astrif laughed — a short, startled sound she didn't recognize coming out of herself — and pulled the child against her coat, against the lining where the cylinder lay over her heart like a second heartbeat.

"That," she said into Tamsin's hair, "is the single most important thing anyone has ever counted."

* *

But tenderness was a luxury with a price, and the bill arrived each evening after Tamsin slept.

Astrid would sit by the window with the lamp turned low and run the ledger of their situation, item by item, the way she ran any inventory. Ships: seized or searched. Curfew: dusk to dawn. The clerk Pellam: gone four days now, surely in some ministry office describing a child he'd seen at Mrs. Halloway's table, a name he'd recognized from a confiscated list. Every day that passed was a day the machinery ground closer. Soldiers at the harbor meant soldiers in the streets soon, and soldiers in the streets meant door-to-door searches, and searches meant questions that *Mira* the orphan could survive but that the truth — General Veyra's daughter, hidden in a courier's keeping — would not.

There was also the matter of the smuggler.

She'd made inquiries, careful ones, through a fishwife who asked nothing and remembered everything. There was a man called Doss who moved goods across the strait at night, no questions, high price. Astrid had enough coin for half his fee. The other half sat against her ribs in a brass cylinder full of codes that would buy passage for two — and cost the republic whatever those codes were worth, which was possibly the war itself.

Every evening she laid it out the same way: deliver the codes and abandon the child to the ledger. Save the child and betray everything she had told herself she was. And every evening the arithmetic came out different than it should have, because somewhere between the button game and the counting of missing buttons, the problem had stopped being a calculation and started being a fact.

She was not furniture. She had never been furniture. Furniture did not lie awake listening to a child breathe in the next bed.

On the sixth night, Tamsin appeared beside her chair without making a sound — a skill Astrid had certainly not taught her — and climbed into her lap as though it were assigned seating.

"I'm being bored," the girl whispered.

"It's past curfew. Bored can wait till morning."

"I had a bad dream. Soldiers took the boat and the boat was made of paper and the paper was on fire."

Astrid held her and looked out at the blacked-out street and thought: *the paper was on fire.* Somewhere in a ministry drawer, a ledger listed this child as an asset, as ink, as a line item to be recovered. And Astrid had begun, quietly, without announcing it to herself, to plan a theft.

"Tamsin," she said, using the true name for the first time in days, savoring it like contraband. "Tomorrow we practice running."

"Running where?"

"Away. Fast, low, and quiet. Can you do fast, low, and quiet?"

Tamsin nodded against her collarbone. Then she lifted her head, and in the lamplight her eyes were Veyra's eyes, level and unafraid in a way no child's should be.

"And after we practice running," she said, "when do we actually go?"

Down in the street below, boots struck cobbles in formation — more boots than the night before, closer than the night before — and Astrid knew the answer had already been decided for them.

"Soon," she said. "Two days. Maybe less."

From the corner of the window she watched lanterns swing along the quay toward Mrs. Halloway's door, and among the uniformed men carrying them she recognized the stooped, apologetic silhouette of Clerk Pellam, come back with soldiers' pay in his pocket and a bargain in his mouth.
