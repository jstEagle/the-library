## 11. Burning the First Page

The customhouse kept its records in the old wool warehouse on Tanner's Lane, and like everything else in Marrow Quay that winter, it ran on habit rather than authority. The clerk who worked evenings had gone home early. The stove in the corner was cold. The ledgers sat on their shelves in ranked rows, spines cracked, dust thick as felt, and nobody had come to count them in weeks.

Astrid knew this because she had spent three days learning it, and learning a building was the first half of every job she'd ever done. You watched the door. You counted the footsteps. You noted when the lamp in the window went dark and how long the darkness lasted before someone remembered to relight it. The republic had taught her this trade without meaning to; a courier was only a woman who paid attention and then pretended she hadn't.

The ledger itself she had seen once, for eleven seconds, two nights ago.

She had been carrying Mrs. Halloway's laundry basket along the quay — a disguise so thin it embarrassed her, but invisibility did not require excellence, only expectation. People saw what they expected: a landlady's drudge, tired, unremarkable. The customhouse yard had been open, the doors propped against the mildew, and inside, under a hanging lantern, a junior officer had been reading aloud from a fat volume to another man whose boots were better than his posture. She had slowed — not deliberately; slowness was invisible where hurry drew eyes — and caught the cadence of names.

*Assets to be recovered.* Children listed between shipping manifests and seized grain accounts, as if they were cargo with legs. She had heard a surname read out — Veyra — and then the page turned, and then a voice said something about the northern districts being cleared by spring, and she had walked on with her basket and her heart going like a trapped bird.

Eleven seconds. Enough.

Now, on the fourth night, she went back through the coal-yard gap behind the warehouse, past the frozen puddles glazed grey as old pewter, and let herself in through the loading door that the watchman chained but never locked properly, because no one had stolen anything from the republic's records in years. Who would bother? Paper was cheap here. Truth was cheaper still. They printed so much of it that no one believed any of it anymore, and a nation that believes nothing can be governed by anything.

Astrid had spent her whole life believing exactly that. She was beginning to suspect it was a lie told by people who wanted the rest of the world to stand still.

* * *

Inside, the warehouse smelled of wet wool and mouse droppings and the ghost of tallow candles. She did not light her shuttered lantern yet. She stood in the doorway and let her eyes adjust, counting shapes: the shelving on the left wall, the reading table with its dead lamp, the stove, the stack of unfiled folders leaning like drunkards against the far post. Somewhere above, ice ticked against the roof tiles.

She moved down the left wall with her fingertips, reading spines by touch the way she'd been taught to read seals by touch — *Harbor Dues, 741–744. Grain Seizures, Southern. Correspondence, Ministry of the Interior.* And there, third shelf from the floor, fatter than the others, its spine marked with a strip of black tape and stenciled letters her fingers found before her eyes could:

**RECOVERABLE PERSONS — NORTHERN REGISTRIES.**

Her hand stayed on it longer than it should have. That was the danger of this work, she thought — not the guards or the dogs or the ice, but the way your own body betrayed you, the way your hand wanted to hold the thing that could destroy you, as if holding it were the same as understanding it.

She took the ledger down, carried it to the table, and lit the lantern behind its shield so the glow fell in a blade no wider than a hand.

Then she opened it, and made herself look.

The entries were neat. That was the worst of it — the neatness. Each name in a fine ministerial hand, each child assigned a number, a district of origin, a disposition. Not murdered, most of them. Worse than murdered, in a way: *relocated for educational integration.* *Placed with loyal households.* As though a child were a language you could forbid. Page after page of small names in beautiful penmanship, and Astrid understood suddenly why the republic had fallen so fast and so quietly — because it had done its cruelest work in handwriting too elegant to shock anyone.

She found the V. section near the end. Her finger walked down the column.

And stopped.

There were three children under Veyra. A son, born in the capital — entry crossed through in red, annotated *deceased, infant, 739.* A daughter, Tamsin Ilse Veyra, age six, last recorded residence: the garrison town of Kesselring. Disposition line blank. Status: *pending recovery.*

Pending. As if the girl asleep right now in Mrs. Halloway's attic room were a letter awaiting delivery, an account awaiting settlement. As if somewhere a clerk had written her name and simply not gotten around to finishing the sentence.

Astrid sat very still in the dark warehouse and listened to the ice ticking on the roof, and felt something settle in her that she recognized from long ago, from the treaty halls, from the moment over a cradle when she had said words she thought were decorative. Promises have longer lives than treaties. She had told herself that was sentiment. It wasn't. It was arithmetic. The promise had compounded, quietly, year upon year, until it came due all at once, in fog, at dawn, at her own door.

She tore the page free.

It resisted more than she expected — the binding was good ministerial work — and the sound of it ripping was enormous in the empty building, a sound like a bone breaking. She froze, lantern shielded, counting ten, twenty, thirty. Nothing moved except her own breath making small clouds.

Then she folded the page into quarters, into eighths, small enough to vanish in her palm, and stood there holding the whole machinery of the state reduced to a scrap of paper — and understood, with a clarity that almost made her laugh, how flimsy it all was. The general's daughter, the ministry's asset, the war's next hostage: a quarter-page of good rag paper, foldable, burnable, *forgettable*, if you were willing to be the one who forgot it.

Paper could be fought with fire. Nobody had ever taught her that. Everyone taught you how to protect documents. No one taught you that documents could be made to die.

* * *

She did not burn it in the warehouse. That was the discipline talking, and for once she listened to it. Burning paper smells — sharp, acrid, unmistakable even through walls, and a cold stove in a records office warming itself at midnight was a confession with a chimney. Instead she folded the ledger back onto its shelf, aligned its spine with its neighbors to the width of a fingernail, and carried the torn page home sewn into the lining of her coat, against her ribs, where the code-cylinder had ridden for six hundred miles.

Two secrets now, riding together. One she had sworn to deliver. One she had just sworn to destroy.

The walk back along the quay took eleven minutes. She used nine of them to plan and two to be afraid, which was roughly her lifetime average. The harbor was quieter than it had been — the troop transports rode at anchor like patient animals, and the customs launches had been commandeered, and the ship she'd been waiting for, the *Meridian*, sat impounded at berth four with a republic flag nailed to its mast and a sentry beneath it who stamped his feet against the cold and looked, to Astrid's practiced eye, about seventeen years old and deeply uncertain what he was guarding.

Curfew bell at ten. She passed the last patrol at the fish-market arch with her head down and her basket of actual laundry on her hip, and the soldier glanced at her the way men glance at weather, and she was through.

Mrs. Halloway's boarding house stood dark and respectable at the end of Marrow Quay, its windows shuttered against both the cold and the occupation. Astrid let herself in through the kitchen, banked the fire low, and climbed the back stairs without letting the third step creak — she had learned the house's voice the way she learned everything, by listening.

Tamsin was awake. Of course she was. Six-year-olds do not sleep through the arrival of the person the whole night has been waiting for.

"You went out," the girl whispered from under the blankets, accusatory and awed at once.

"I went to do an errand."

"At night."

"Some errands keep night hours." Astrid sat on the edge of the bed. In the faint light from the shuttered window the girl's face was all eyes and solemnity, and Astrid felt again the strange double vision that had haunted her since the doorstep — seeing both the child and the name, the girl and the entry, the small warm breathing thing and the line of ministerial ink that said *pending.*

"Tamsin," she said. "Do you remember your mother's writing?"

The girl nodded slowly. "She writes straight. Like fences."

"Like fences," Astrid agreed, and something in her chest pulled tight. "I'm going to show you a page tomorrow. There might be writing on it that looks like fences. I want you to tell me if any of it is hers."

"Why?"

"Because sometimes," Astrid said, choosing each word the way you chose footing on river ice, "people leave messages in places they can't say them out loud. And mothers are very good at that."

Tamsin considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My mama says paper remembers."

"Does she."

"She says you can burn a letter but the burning knows what it says. Is that true?"

Astrid thought of the page against her ribs, already warm from her body, already halfway to ash in her intention. "I think," she said, "that paper remembers what it wants to. That's why we're careful which papers we let live."

The girl frowned, filed this away among the other incomprehensible things adults said, and asked instead the question that mattered: "Are the soldiers coming here?"

"Not tonight."

"Tomorrow?"

"Maybe someday. But not tonight, and I'll be here between now and then. Sleep."

And miracle of miracles — or perhaps only the ordinary exhaustion of a six-year-old living on fear and boiled barley — the girl slept. Astrid sat beside her until her breathing went slow and even, then rose, took the candle stub into the kitchen, and fed the banked stove a single knot of kindling.

* * *

She burned the page standing up.

Not kneeling — she noticed that about herself afterward, and wondered at it. There was no worship in it, no ceremony. She unfolded the ministerial handwriting over the stove door and read it once more, all of it, top to bottom, because erasure deserved honesty: the number, the name, the district, the blank disposition, the word *pending* with its little bureaucratic tail. She read it the way you read a threat, so you'd know precisely what you were refusing.

Then she touched the corner to the flame.

It caught reluctantly — good rag paper, sized against damp, built by its makers to outlast empires — and for one ugly second it flared blue and showed her the whole entry at once, luminous, the name Tamsin Ilse Veyra burning bright enough to read across the kitchen. Then it curled, blackened, and broke apart into flakes that rose up the flue like startled moths.

Astrid fed the fragments in one at a time. She did not stop until every piece was ash, and then she stirred the stove's coals with the poker and ground the ash into them, and raked the lot flat, and sat down on the wood box to shake.

That was the part no one warned you about — that defiance felt like terror from the inside, that courage and panic were twins separated only by what happened next. Her hands would not steady. She pressed them between her knees and breathed, and looked at the stove, and made herself say it aloud in the empty kitchen, barely a whisper:

"She isn't pending."

The words hung in the warm air. Pending meant a clerk would come looking. Pending meant a file that didn't match reality, and files that don't match reality get reconciled — she had watched ministries reconcile discrepancies her whole career, gently, thoroughly, with ink and iron. By tearing the page she hadn't ended the hunt. She'd only made sure that when the record and the world collided, the collision would happen in a room with her in it.

But the page was gone. Whatever else the winter held, whatever the ledger said in whatever hands it landed, one line of it was now so much heat in a stove in a boarding house kitchen, warming tea water for a woman who had chosen, tonight, for the first time in her careful invisible life, to make herself legible to history.

She thought of the boy-sentry stamping his feet under the republic's flag. He didn't know he was guarding an empire of ash.

* * *

She was rinsing the poker when she heard the step on the back stair — the wrong step, the light one, the one that meant Mrs. Halloway, who moved through her own house like a woman walking on a frozen pond. The landlady appeared in the kitchen doorway in shawl and nightcap, took in the scene with one sweep: the stove open, the smell of burnt paper, Astrid standing pale with a fire-iron in her hand like a housemaid caught murdering.

For a long moment neither spoke.

"Burning correspondence?" Mrs. Halloway said at last. "In my stove."

"Yes."

"In wartime. With soldiers billeted at the harbor and informers paid by the head." The old woman's voice was flat, unreadable, and Astrid braced for the sentence that would follow — *you'll bring them to my door, you'll ruin us all* — the sentence any sensible survivor would speak, the sentence Astrid herself would have spoken a month ago.

Instead Mrs. Halloway crossed the kitchen, opened the stove, inspected the ashes with the critical eye of a woman who had burned things before — and Astrid stored that away, filed it, promised herself to ask someday — and closed the door.

"Next time," the landlady said, "burn it in pieces smaller than that. And open the flue fully first. Half the street smelled it." She paused at the doorway, her back to Astrid, a small stiff silhouette against the dark. "I had two daughters," she added, to the shadows. "The registry has them down as relocated. Loyal households, it says." A silence. "I've often thought about what I'd burn, if I could get my hands on the book."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and Astrid stood alone in the kitchen with her heart hammering, understanding that the house around her was full of women keeping silent accounts of their own.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when she finally climbed to the attic to sleep, and she would have slept at once, gratefully, if Tamsin's coat had not been hanging on its nail where Astrid had hung it that evening — damp from the fog, smelling of coal cellar and wool.

Something about it snagged her eye. Habit, again. A courier notices when a garment hangs wrong.

She took it down. Turned it in her hands. The left lapel sat heavier than the right — barely, a weight no one would feel wearing it, but Astrid had spent years feeling exactly these differences, seams and linings and the small dishonest bulges where documents hide. She ran her thumb along the lapel's edge and felt it: a ridge of stitching that didn't belong, fresh thread, tight and small, sewn by hands that knew needlework under pressure.

Someone had hidden something in a child's coat.

Astrid stood in the dark attic holding the little coat, listening to Tamsin breathe, feeling the shape of the secret through the cloth — and understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, that the general had sent more than a daughter across enemy lines.

She reached for the seam ripper in her sewing kit, and began to cut.
