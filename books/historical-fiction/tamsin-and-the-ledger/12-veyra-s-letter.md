## 12. Veyra's Letter

The coat was the problem.

Astrid had known it since the second night — a child's grey wool coat, good quality once, now salt-stiff at the hem and too thin for the northern winter. Tamsin wore it day and night, slept in it, clutched its collar in her sleep the way other children clutched cloth animals. It was the one thing she had arrived with besides silence.

But Mrs. Halloway had found them a trunk of old clothes in the attic — a dead daughter's, though neither woman said so — and among them a proper winter coat, fur-lined at the hood, buttons of real horn. Tamsin had put it on and looked, for one terrible moment, like any other child on Marrow Quay, which was exactly the point and exactly the danger. Children who looked like everyone else got counted. Counted children got listed.

"Keep the grey one," Astrid told her. "Wear this over it when I say."

Tamsin nodded. She had learned to accept instructions without questions, which broke something small in Astrid every time. Six-year-olds should ask *why* until adults beg for mercy.

So the grey coat came off, finally, on the fourth evening after the soldiers took the harbor — laid across the bed while Astrid turned it inside out by lamplight, checking seams the way she'd been taught, because a courier checked everything twice and trusted nothing once.

Her fingers stopped at the left shoulder seam.

There was a thickness there that wool didn't account for. She pressed it. Paper. Folded paper, flat, deliberate, sewn in with stitches so fine they might have been done by a machine — except machines hadn't touched this coat. This had been done by hand, by someone patient, someone who understood that the best hiding place is inside the thing no one will take from a child.

Astrid sat very still for a long moment, listening to the house settle around her. Below, Mrs. Halloway's floorboards creaked their nightly liturgy: kettle, latch, chair, prayer or its equivalent. Down the street, boots on cobbles — the patrol, right on schedule, nine-forty, four men, one limping. The republic had taken the harbor, the ships, the curfew, the future. But it kept schedules. There was comfort in that, of a grim kind.

She took out her sewing kit. Small scissors. And she cut the shoulder seam open with the care of a surgeon who suspects what the wound will show.

* * *

It was not a letter, properly. Letters have salutations and signatures and the comfortable architecture of correspondence. This was a single sheet, folded eight times, written in pencil in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the writer had been moving even while sitting still. No name at the bottom. No name was needed.

Astrid knew the handwriting the way you know a voice through a wall.

*If you are reading this, then she reached you, and you opened the seam instead of burning the coat, which means you are still yourself.*

*I will not insult you with apology. You know me better than that.*

*You will want to know why you. Why not a loyalist, why not one of my own people, why not some stranger paid well enough never to ask questions. I will tell you, because you were always the only person I never lied to, and I refuse to start at the end.*

*Because they will find everyone else.*

*That is not fatalism. That is arithmetic. My staff has been arrested in ones and twos since autumn. My adjutant's family went first, then his clerk, then the man who fed his dog. The lists travel faster than loyalty does. Anyone I could order to carry her, I could also be made to betray — not through weakness, but through the simple fact of being mine, and therefore being findable. A courier with no flag, no patron, no file worth reading — you were invisible, Astrid. I am sorry. I am about to spend your invisibility like coin, and I know what it costs you to hear that, because you once told me invisibility was the only property you'd ever owned outright.*

*Here is the arithmetic of my choice. If the republic wins, she is an asset on a ledger, to be recovered and raised as proof of my treason and a lesson to others. If my side wins, she is a symbol, and symbols get carried on shoulders until the shoulders break. Either way, everyone who loves her becomes a target and everyone who owns her becomes a weapon. There was only one door out of that room, and it was you — the woman who promised me, over a cradle, in peacetime, when promises cost nothing, that no child of mine would ever be taken.*

*You thought I'd forgotten. I have forgotten treaties, troop numbers, the names of men I dined with for a year. I did not forget that.*

*I knew what it would do to you. I knew the codes you carry matter more than you do, to the people you carry them for. I knew that taking her would make you a traitor to your commission if you keep her and an accessory to mine if you deliver her. I chose it anyway. That is the monstrous part — not the battles, whatever they write about those. The monstrous part is that I weighed your life against hers and let the scale tip, and I would do it again before I finished writing this sentence.*

*Do not forgive me. Forgiveness is another debt and you have enough.*

*One instruction only, and then I release you from all the rest: she cannot be raised by anyone who is afraid of her name. Whatever you decide to call her, decide it quickly, because names are armor and she has none. And when she is old enough to ask — and she will ask; she is my daughter — tell her the truth. Not the softened version. Tell her I sent her away to save her and it was also, partly, to save something in myself, and that both things can be true in one breast without canceling out. If she grows up believing I was clean, she'll grow up easy to kill.*

*Burn this. Or don't. I have earned less than your caution.*

*V.*

* * *

Astrid read it three times.

The first time she read it as a courier reads intercepted mail — fast, hungry for operational content, hunting for anything actionable. There was almost none. No routes, no contacts, no cipher keys. He had sent his daughter into the world with no map at all except a promise made seven years ago over a cradle in a treaty tent that smelled of wet canvas and lilies someone had brought to soften the smell of war preparation.

The second time she read it as the young clerk she had been, standing in that tent with her shorthand book, watching General Veyra — not yet general, then, or newly so — hold an infant with hands built for reins and rifle stocks, holding the child like something he was afraid of breaking and more afraid of loving.

The third time she read it as herself, now. And that was the worst reading, because now she could check his arithmetic against her own, and the sums matched.

They had met during the Halvern negotiations — two weeks of it, spring, the river high, everyone pretending the peace would hold longer than the flowers. She had been twenty-three and a second-tier transcription clerk, invisible in the way she'd cultivated since girlhood, present in every room and remembered by none of them. Except him. He had noticed her noticing. On the last night, walking the embankment because neither could sleep, he had said: *You listen the way other people shoot. Nothing wasted.* And later, when the talk turned — as late-night talk between a soldier and a neutral did — to what wars do to children, she had said it lightly, carelessly, the way you say things you don't expect to be held to:

"No child of yours will ever be taken. Not while I'm alive."

And he had looked at her for three full seconds, which felt like three hours, and said, "I'll hold you to that," and she had laughed, because it was peacetime, and peacetime was made of sentences no one meant to honor.

Promises have longer lives than treaties. She had written that herself, years later, in a novel she published under a name that wasn't quite hers. She had thought it was a clever line. It had been a prophecy, and prophecies are only clever until they come due.

Now the bill lay in her lap, folded eight times, smelling faintly of pencil shavings and a child's cold sleep.

* * *

Below, the house ticked and settled. The patrol passed back the other way — eleven o'clock, four men, the limp unchanged. Somewhere beyond the fog the seized ships rode at anchor with republic guns on their decks, and somewhere south the capital she'd fled was finishing its long argument with itself, and somewhere east a ministry clerk named Ossel was spending soldiers' money and sleeping badly, and all of it — the whole collapsing apparatus of a nation — seemed suddenly smaller than one page of pencil-written paper.

Because here was the thing Astrid could not argue with, the thing that sat in her chest like swallowed stone:

He had chosen her *deliberately*.

Not desperately. Deliberately. He had sat down — wherever generals sit when their side is losing and their children are on lists — and he had thought through every door and found them all watched, all bought, all betrayable, and then he had thought of a woman who had once told him she was furniture, and he had bet his daughter's life on a sentence spoken in kindness seven years ago by someone who hadn't meant it yet.

History called Veyra a monster. The broadsheets had decided it months ago; soon the schoolbooks would print it; eventually there would be statues pulled down or erected depending on who won, but either way the word would survive: monster, butcher, traitor to the republic that made him. And maybe the word was true. Maybe the battles deserved it. Astrid had read the dispatches from Corren Field; she knew what "collateral" meant when it was spelled out in column inches.

But monsters did not trust people across enemy lines. Monsters did not send their children into the dark with nothing but a promise. Monsters did not write *do not forgive me* in pencil, knowing pencil fades, hoping perhaps that it would.

Or — and this was the colder thought, the one she made herself look at — monsters did all of those things, and that was precisely how they stayed monstrous: by making decent people carry their weight and call it love. By handing you a child and a choice and letting you believe the choosing was yours.

She looked at the page a long time, turning that over, testing both readings like coins bitten for gold. And in the end she set the question down unfinished, because there was a third reading, simpler than either: it didn't matter whether Veyra was a monster. What mattered was whether *she* was going to behave like one, and the ledger in Ossel's pocket had already answered that for anyone willing to read it. A state that listed children under "assets to be recovered" did not get to define the word monster anymore. It had forfeited the vocabulary.

*Burn this,* he had written. *Or don't. I have earned less than your caution.*

She almost burned it. She held the corner to the lamp flame, saw the paper begin to brown and curl —

— and pulled it back.

No. Not yet. Burning was for documents that had finished their work. This one hadn't. Because Tamsin would ask — Veyra was right, she would ask; the asking was already gathering behind her eyes like weather — and when she asked, Astrid would need the exact words, not a memory of the words. A mother's paraphrase was a kind of lie, and the girl had been lied to enough by history already.

Instead she did what she should have done the night the child arrived: she took up her needle and thread, and she sewed the letter into a new hiding place — not the coat, coats got confiscated, coats got burned — but into the hem of the quilt on the narrow bed where Tamsin slept, stitched flat beneath the binding, undetectable unless you knew to unpick eleven specific stitches. Her own tradecraft, returned to its origin. She had learned concealment from her father, a customs clerk who hid dissident pamphlets in flour sacks; she had refined it for ministries; and now she used it to hide a monster's handwriting under a sleeping child.

The symmetry was not lost on her. Nothing was lost on her anymore. That was the price of the last two weeks: invisibility had ended, and in its place she had been given sight, which was worse and better and irreversible.

* * *

Tamsin stirred when Astrid came back into the room. Not awake — just the small animal adjustment of a child registering a familiar presence, the way dogs shift toward warmth. In the lamplight her face had lost some of its frozen quality these past days. Not softness, exactly. Readiness. She was learning the drills: how to walk without looking at checkpoints, how to answer questions with bored half-truths, how to go silent on command. Astrid hated teaching her and taught her anyway, because tenderness without strategy was just a slower way to lose her.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Astrid whispered.

"I was." Tamsin's voice was hoarse with sleep. Then, without opening her eyes, in the flat tone children use for the questions they've been carrying all day: "Did you find it?"

Astrid's blood went cold and slow. "Find what?"

"In my coat. Mama sewed something in it. She cried while she did it but she didn't make any noise crying. Like you taught me." The girl's eyes opened now, dark and unblinking. "I heard the scissors."

For a moment Astrid couldn't speak. The child had known. Had known for days, said nothing, waited — watching Astrid watch her, running her own small counter-surveillance, and Astrid had thought she was teaching a pupil when in fact she'd been under observation the entire time. Veyra's blood, then. God help them both.

"Yes," Astrid said finally. "I found it."

"Is Mama in trouble?"

The honest answers crowded forward — *your mother is the most wanted woman on the continent, your mother wrote to me that she spent your life like coin, your mother may be dead by spring* — and Astrid set every one of them aside, because there was one truer than all of them.

"She's doing the bravest thing a person can do," Astrid said. "She's keeping a promise."

Tamsin considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she rolled over, pulled the quilt to her chin — the quilt with the letter sleeping in its hem — and within four breaths was genuinely, utterly asleep, the way only children and soldiers can.

Astrid stood over her a long time.

Then she crossed to the window and looked down through the fog at Marrow Quay: the patrol lanterns, the black hulls of seized ships, the customhouse windows still lit where Officer Brandt worked his late files. Three days, maybe four, before the search widened. Two days before Ossel's bargain expired and became a betrayal. One day before Mrs. Halloway's food ran short enough to force questions she couldn't afford to answer.

The harbor was closed. The roads were watched. Every legal door was shut and most illegal ones too.

Which left the boats that weren't in the harbor at all — the ones that came in dark, unloaded in the narrows, and asked no papers of anyone. Astrid had seen their lights from this window, three nights running, low and quick past the breakwater. Smugglers ran contraband through a blockade for one reason: because someone paid them.

And Astrid Nystrom, courier, traitor-in-waiting, keeper of promises, happened to be carrying the single most valuable object to cross the border in either direction this winter — sewn into the lining of a coat hanging on the back of a door.

She had sworn she'd die before surrendering the cylinder.

She hadn't sworn what she'd trade it *for*.
