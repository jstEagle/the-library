## 13. The Smuggler's Price

The smuggler's name was Corragan, and he kept his boat behind a rotting boathouse on the far side of Marrow Quay, where the fog came off the water thick enough to drink.

Astrid found him the way she'd been taught to find anyone: by asking nothing and watching everything. Three days of curfew had taught her the rhythms of the occupied town — when the patrols turned at the fish market, how long the sentry at the customs shed let his lantern gutter while he smoked, which windows along the quay were watched and which were only dark. She had walked past the boathouse twice with Tamsin's hand in hers, the girl dressed as a boy now, hair shorn with Mrs. Halloway's sewing scissors, cap pulled low. On the third pass she had seen a man mending nets who never mended any nets. His hands moved in the wrong rhythm for net work. His eyes counted everything that passed.

She went back alone, after dark, without the child.

The boathouse door opened before she knocked. That was answer enough.

"You've been walking past my door," the man said. He was fifty or so, built like something the sea had given up on and taken back twice, with a beard gone to rust and a voice like gravel poured slow. "Three days running. People who walk past doors three times want something."

"I want passage," Astrid said. "Two seats. South, past the pickets, out to open water where a neutral hull can find us."

"Curfew says nobody moves."

"Curfew says nobody *official* moves."

Something shifted in his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way ice shifts before it breaks. He stepped aside. "Come in, then. Mind the tar."

* * *

Inside, the boathouse smelled of pitch and old fish and lamp oil. A skiff sat half-built on trestles, and beyond it, through a gap in the planking, she could see black water and the shape of a boat moored low in the water — low because it carried no lights and rode quiet, the way boats ride when they're built to be unseen.

Astrid felt something loosen in her chest. Then tighten again, because things built to be unseen are also expensive.

Corragan lit a lamp with a shuttered flame and sat on an upturned crate, studying her. "You're not from here."

"No."

"You're not fishing people, you and your boy." He said *boy* flatly, letting her know he'd seen through the cap and the shorn hair and the walk she'd taught the child, and that it cost him nothing to pretend otherwise. "Fishing people don't ask for open water. Fishing people ask for the next cove down. You want distance."

"I want safety."

"Safety's a country I don't trade with." He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here's what I know. There's a republic dying south of us and a general eating it alive north of us, and this town sits in the mouth between them like a loose tooth. The soldiers took the harbor Tuesday. They took the mail yesterday. Tomorrow they'll start taking people. Anyone who can get a boat out right now is either very brave, very stupid, or very paid." His eyes were pale as herring scales. "Which are you?"

"The last one," Astrid said. "I can pay."

"With what? The banks closed when the troops came in. Coin's worthless until someone wins, and nobody's winning."

"Not coin."

She reached into her coat.

Her fingers knew the seam without looking. Three weeks ago in the capital she had sewn it there herself, stitch by stitch by candlelight, telling herself the weight against her ribs was nothing, a trifle, paper and brass, furniture in the pocket of history. The code-cylinder came free of the lining with a small sound, a whisper of thread giving way, and she set it on the crate between them.

It was smaller than it had ever felt. Six inches of dull brass. Inside it, rolled tight as a wasp's nest, the diplomatic cipher keys of a collapsing republic — the codes that would let whatever government survived read every secret its enemies had written about it. The codes she had sworn to carry. The codes that had been, for twenty years, the entire architecture of her life: carry, deliver, forget, remain invisible.

Corragan looked at it a long time. He did not touch it.

"That's ministry work," he said quietly.

"It's passage work now."

"You know what they do to couriers who lose their cargo?"

"They hang them," Astrid said. "I've seen it done. I helped bury one, once, outside Vellmark. He'd been carrying grain requisitions." She kept her voice level. "I know exactly what they do."

"And you're offering it anyway."

"I'm offering it anyway."

He picked up the cylinder at last, turned it over once, twice, weighing it in his palm like a stone he might skip across the water. "This is worth more than my boat. This is worth ten boats. This is worth a farm in the neutral territories and a name that isn't mine." He set it down again, carefully, the way men handle things that might detonate. "So why does a courier sell her own spine?"

* * *

Because of a child asleep in a boarding house attic under a roof Mrs. Halloway pretended not to look up at. Because of a note in a general's hand that said *forget the face*, and a promise made nine years ago over a cradle, careless as breathing, that had grown teeth. Because somewhere in a confiscated ledger in a ministry office in a town that smelled of tar, a line of ink said TAMSIN VEYRA — ASSET TO BE RECOVERED, and ink did not sleep, and clerks did not forgive, and the machinery of power had already begun to turn its attention toward a boarding house on Marrow Quay like a searchlight beginning its slow arc across the water.

But Astrid said none of that.

"Because I promised," she said.

Corragan studied her for a long moment. Outside, a patrol passed on the quay road, boots crunching frost, lantern light sliding through the gaps in the planks and striping the floor in bars that moved like slow water. Neither of them spoke until the sound died.

"A promise," he said finally. "That's the whole of it."

"The whole of it."

"My wife left me for a man with a promise too," Corragan said. "Hers was a lie, though. Yours sounds like the other kind." He stood, joints cracking like kindling, and walked to the gap in the planking where the dark boat lay. "Weather turns tomorrow night. Fog off the marsh comes down around midnight and doesn't lift till dawn. If we go, we go then. Two hours' rowing to the channel markers, then sail south till we raise a neutral flag or drown. No refunds."

"I don't need a refund. I need certainty."

"Certainty." He laughed, a dry sound with no floor to it. "Woman, I smuggle tobacco and deserters and occasionally priests. Certainty is the one cargo I've never carried." He turned back to face her. "You want my price list? Here it is. Your brass tube buys the boat, my silence, and my hands on the oars. It does not buy my neck if the soldiers stop us. If we're boarded, that cylinder goes over the side and you swim or sink on your own account. And if the girl cries out — if your *boy* makes noise at the wrong moment —"

"He won't."

"She, then." He said it without cruelty, without surprise, just the flat accuracy of a man who has spent his life reading water. "I've eyes, mistress. Eyes are cheap. What I need to know is whether your daughter can be quiet. Because quiet is the whole trade. Quiet is the boat and the oar and the fog all in one. Can she be quiet?"

Astrid thought of the coal cellar drills. Of Tamsin sitting motionless in the dark for an hour, two hours, learning stillness the way other children learned prayers. Of the small cold hand gripping hers under the table while soldiers searched the parlor below, and the girl not trembling, not breathing loud, holding silence around herself like a coat.

"She can be quieter than either of us," Astrid said.

"Then we have terms." Corragan held out his hand, and she took it. His grip was rough as rope. "Midnight tomorrow. The north slip, behind the salt sheds. Don't come early. Early is how people die." He paused. "Bring the child fed. Hungry children make noise."

"One more thing." Astrid nodded at the cylinder on the crate. "If anything happens to me — if I'm taken before tomorrow night — burn it. Don't sell it. Don't trade it. Burn it and scatter the ash in the harbor."

"Why would I do that? It's worth ten boats."

"Because whoever buys it will use it to hunt people," Astrid said. "Children, among others. It's a ledger, essentially. A ledger of everyone the republic wants to find." She heard the words come out of her and understood, with a strange floating clarity, that she was describing herself — describing what she had been carrying against her ribs for three weeks, believing it furniture, believing it nothing. "Burn it."

Corragan looked at her for a long moment, and something in his weathered face moved — recognition, maybe, or respect, or just the tired arithmetic of a man deciding whom to trust in a year when trust was worth more than brass.

"Burn it," he agreed. "Now get out of my boathouse. You've been here eleven minutes and the patrol comes past again in four."

* * *

She walked back through the fog with her coat feeling wrong against her body, the way a tooth feels wrong after it's pulled. For twenty years the cylinder had ridden against her ribs, and she had told herself the weight was nothing. Now it was gone and she could feel the absence pressing there instead, heavier than the brass had ever been.

*You are a traitor now,* she thought, climbing the wet steps from the quay. *Whatever happens tomorrow — whether the boat makes the channel or the soldiers take you on the salt sheds — you have sold your country's secrets to buy one child's life. They will write your name in their books. Not as furniture. As an enemy.*

She waited for fear. She waited for grief, or shame, or the sick vertigo of a woman who had spent her whole life being invisible and had just stepped into the middle of the road with a lantern.

What came instead was something quieter and much more frightening.

Relief.

Mrs. Halloway let her in without a word, as she always did, bolting the door behind her with the soft economy of a woman who had survived three governments and expected to survive a fourth. In the kitchen a single candle burned. Tamsin sat at the table in her boy's clothes, feet not reaching the floor, and when she saw Astrid her whole face changed — the careful blankness falling away for one unguarded second, the six-year-old shining through the disguise like a lamp through cracked glass.

"Did he say yes?" the girl whispered.

"He said yes."

Tamsin slid off the chair and crossed the floor and put her arms around Astrid's waist and pressed her face into her coat, right against the spot where the cylinder used to be, and Astrid put her hands on the small shoulders and looked over the child's head at Mrs. Halloway, who stood by the stove with her arms folded and her eyes bright and hard.

"What did it cost?" Mrs. Halloway asked.

Astrid hesitated. Lying to soldiers was becoming easy; lying to this woman was not.

"Everything I was carrying," she said.

Mrs. Halloway nodded slowly, as if that were the correct price, the fair one, the one she would have named herself. "Then you're lighter now," she said. "Lighter travels faster."

They ate bread and dripping in silence, by the one candle. Tamsin fell asleep at the table halfway through her bread, mid-chew, the exhaustion of a week of drills and curfews and shorn hair finally pulling her under, and Astrid carried her up the attic stairs and laid her in the bed under the eaves and stood a long time in the dark listening to her breathe.

Tomorrow night. Midnight. The north slip behind the salt sheds.

She was almost asleep herself, folded into the chair beside the bed, when she heard the sound.

Boots. Not passing — stopping. Many of them, on the cobbles below, in the street outside Mrs. Halloway's front door, followed by the flat knock of a rifle butt against wood. Once. Twice.

Then a voice, raised for the whole sleeping street to hear:

"Open in the name of the Republic! This house is to be searched!"

Beside her, in the bed, Tamsin's eyes snapped open in the dark — awake instantly, silent instantly, trained — and found Astrid's in the black.

And downstairs, Mrs. Halloway began, slowly, deliberately, to unbolt the door.
