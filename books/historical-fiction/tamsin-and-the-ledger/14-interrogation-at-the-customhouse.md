## 14. Interrogation at the Customhouse

They took her at the fish market, in the grey hour before the stalls opened, when the gulls were louder than the people.

Astrid had gone out for bread because a woman who never left the boarding house was a woman remembered, and being remembered was death. She had counted the soldiers on her route — four at the harbor gate, two at the customs shed, one asleep against a bollard with his cap over his eyes — and she had bought her loaf and turned for home, and that was when the sergeant stepped out of the shadow of the salt warehouse and said, "Madam. The officer will see you now."

Not: you are under arrest. Not: come with me. *The officer will see you.* As if this were a courtesy. As if she were being invited to tea.

She went. There was nothing else to do. Running would have been a confession, and she had not yet decided what to confess to.

* * *

The customhouse smelled of tar, wet wool, and cold tea. Someone had tried to make it an office: a desk, a lamp with a cracked green shade, a map of the coast pinned to the wall with a bayonet. Beyond the desk, through a door left deliberately ajar, she could see the holding room — a bench, a ring bolted to the floor, a bucket. They wanted her to see it. That was part of the method.

The officer sat behind the desk with his gloves off and his hands folded, as though he had been waiting for her all morning and was mildly pleased she'd finally arrived. He was younger than she expected — thirty-five, perhaps forty — with a long face and the kind of neatness that comes from fear of disorder rather than love of order. His collar was buttoned to the throat. His name, stenciled on the door behind him, was VOSSE.

"Sit down, madam."

She sat. She put the bread on her knee like a woman who intended to eat it later and saw his eyes flick to it, cataloguing it, and understood that everything in this room would be catalogued, including her.

"Name?"

"Astrid Nystrom."

"Residence?"

"Mrs. Halloway's, Marrow Quay. Room at the back, second floor."

"Purpose in town?"

"I'm waiting for a ship." She let a little weariness into it, the weariness of every traveler stranded by the occupation. "I was told there'd be sailings twice a week. I've been told many things lately that aren't true."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that she had scored a point, filed away for later use against her.

"Where are you traveling from?"

"The capital."

"And before that?"

"Before that I lived in the capital. Before that I was born in Halden, which is why I sound like this." She touched her own accent lightly, the way one touches a scar to show it doesn't hurt. "Officer, may I ask what this is about? I have bread going stale and a landlady who counts her spoons."

He ignored the question with the ease of long practice. "You came north on the Grey Coach. The ninth of the month."

"Yes."

"You were one of four passengers."

"Three others and myself, yes. A wool factor, a farmer's widow, and a ministry clerk whose name I never caught because he talked enough for all of us."

Vosse opened a folder on the desk. She did not look at it. Looking at it would have told him how much she cared what was in it. Instead she looked at the map of the coast, at the little inked arrows showing patrol routes, and memorized two of them without appearing to.

"The clerk," Vosse said, "has made a statement."

"Has he," said Astrid. "Did he also tell you the weather? He seemed to have opinions on everything."

"He says he recognized someone in Marrow Quay. A child. A child of particular interest to the ministry." Vosse folded his hands again. "He says the child is staying at a boarding house. He says the woman keeping the house has a lodger who arrived on the same coach, who keeps to herself, who watches everything and speaks of nothing, and who went out at dawn yesterday and again today carrying nothing and returning carrying nothing — except this morning, bread."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, water slapped against pilings.

"That's a great deal of saying," Astrid said, "for a man who talked too much."

"It is." Vosse leaned back. "Here is my difficulty, madam. Everything he says is possible. None of it is provable. A boarding house has six rooms and eleven souls in it; any of them could be hiding an army. A woman buys bread; women buy bread. And yet." He tilted his head. "You have not asked me the obvious question."

"Which is?"

"Whether there is a child in your house."

And there it was — the trap laid flat in the open, so obvious it was almost insulting. Deny the child existed and she lied about something checkable; admit knowledge and she explained why a stranger knew a stranger's boarders. Astrid let three seconds pass, the length of time a person takes when genuinely puzzled, and then said:

"There are children everywhere in this town, Officer. Half of them are hungry and all of them are loud. If you mean a specific child, you'll have to be more specific than a man who can't remember his own name being worth telling me."

"Tamsin," Vosse said.

He watched her say it. He watched her the way a man watches water for the ripple where a fish rises.

Astrid frowned — a small frown, the frown of a foreigner wrestling with an unfamiliar name. "Tamsin. Is that a name or a disease?" She shook her head. "No. I don't know it. Should I?"

"Perhaps not." He turned a page in the folder. "Then tell me about yourself instead. You're a courier."

Her heart did not stop. She had spent years training it not to stop, and it obeyed her now, beating on, steady as a clock in an empty house. But her hands — she kept them still on the loaf.

"A courier," she repeated. "Of what, letters? I wish someone had told me. I've been waiting a fortnight for word from my sister in Halden."

"You were seen at the ministry annex in the capital. Twice. In September."

"Everyone was seen at the ministry annex in September. It was the only building still issuing travel papers. I queued for nine hours between a priest and a man selling forged ones, and I'd take the priest's word over the ministry's at this stage." She heard her own voice go on, smooth and easy, and marveled at it from a small cold distance — *that's me, that's my voice, lying* — the way one marvels at ice bearing weight. "What I am, Officer, is a seamstress with a bad knee and a married sister who writes to me when the post still runs. If that makes me a courier, then half this country is carrying dispatches in its knitting."

Vosse studied her for a long moment. Then he did something worse than shouting. He closed the folder.

"You understand my position," he said, almost gently. "The republic is dying. Everyone knows it, including, I suspect, the republic. When a thing dies, its last act is usually to sign papers, and those papers name names. Children's names, even. Especially children's names — children are the only assets that appreciate." He stood, took his gloves from the desk, and began working them onto his fingers, one seam at a time. "I could hold you. I could hold everyone in that house until someone talks, because someone always talks eventually. But I am a busy man in a dying country, and so instead I am going to do something I rarely do."

"Release me?"

"I'm going to believe you." He smiled then, fully, and it was the most frightening thing in the room. "Provisionally. You'll remain in Marrow Quay. You'll report to this office daily — tomorrow, the day after, each morning at eight — until either a ship sails that can carry you, or I find the thing I'm looking for. Whichever comes first. Do you find that fair?"

"I find it tedious," Astrid said, rising, "but I've survived tedium before."

"One more thing." He did not move toward the door. "The clerk who made the statement. He's been paid. He'll be paid again if his information proves out, and charged if it doesn't, which gives him a powerful incentive to prove it. Men like that don't forget faces, madam. It's the only capital they have." He opened the door for her, courteous as a hotel manager. "If a child should appear at your boarding house — lost, frozen, forgotten by whoever left her — you would bring her to me. Wouldn't you?"

"To the customhouse," Astrid said. "Of course."

"Liar," said Vosse pleasantly, and held the door.

* * *

Outside, the fog had thickened until the masts along the quay were suggestions rather than objects. Astrid walked home at a normal pace, past the sentries, past the shuttered market, the loaf under her arm, her face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman thinking about dinner.

But inside, where no one could catalogue it, she was screaming.

*Liar,* he had said, smiling, and meant: *I know, and I cannot touch you yet, and when I can, I will.* He had given her the daily reports not because he believed her innocence but because a watched woman cannot run. Every morning at eight she would stand in front of him and be counted, and while she was being counted, someone else could be searching Mrs. Halloway's house.

They would search it tomorrow. Perhaps tonight. She had until the next dawn, maybe less, and Tamsin was upstairs in the linen cupboard behind the folded sheets, breathing through her mouth the way Astrid had taught her, small and silent as a held note.

At the boarding house door Astrid stopped, set down the loaf, and pressed her palm flat against the wood — one second, two — gathering herself the way you gather a dropped stitch before the whole row runs.

Mrs. Halloway opened it before she knocked. The old woman's face said everything her voice would not: *they came already.* Two soldiers, an hour ago. They had walked through the kitchen, tapped the walls, looked under beds, and left a printed notice nailed to the doorpost: ALL PERSONS SHELTERING ENEMY DEPENDENTS WILL BE PROSECUTED UNDER ARTICLE NINE.

"They didn't look up," Mrs. Halloway whispered. "The cupboard. They didn't look up."

"They'll come back," Astrid said. "Tomorrow they'll bring a man who knows how to look."

Above them, faintly, from the second floor, came the smallest sound — a cough strangled to nothing, a child doing exactly as she'd been taught. Invisible. Silent. Safe, for one more night.

Astrid picked up her bread and climbed the stairs, already counting hours, already planning the lie she would tell at eight o'clock — and knowing, with a certainty colder than the fog, that the lie would need to be her best one yet, because Vosse had stopped believing her the moment he opened the door.

Eight o'clock. One night. One chance.

She went up to teach Tamsin a new lesson: how to disappear entirely, before the man who sees everything came back to look.
