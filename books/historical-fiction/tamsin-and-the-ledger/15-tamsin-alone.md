## 15. Tamsin Alone

The coal cellar smelled like the inside of a black thing.

Tamsin sat on an upturned crate and counted her breaths, because Astrid had told her to. *When you are frightened, count your breaths. When you reach two hundred, you will have forgotten why you were frightened.* Tamsin had reached forty-one and she still remembered exactly why.

*Don't come out,* Astrid had said. *No matter what you hear. No matter what you see. If I'm not back by the time the church bell rings six, stay until morning, then go up the stairs, turn left, and knock on the door with the blue paint. A woman named Mrs. Halloway — no. Not Mrs. Halloway. She's gone now. Knock on the door with the fish painted on it. Say the word "herring" and nothing else.*

Tamsin had asked what would happen at six o'clock.

Astrid had crouched down so their faces were level, the way she always did, as if being tall were a coat she could take off when it mattered. "Nothing is going to happen," she'd said. "I'm going to buy bread."

But her hands had done the thing they did sometimes, smoothing the front of her coat over and over, like a cat washing a spot that wasn't dirty. Tamsin had learned to read hands before she could read words. Her mother's hands did that too, in the last winter, before the car came with the flags on it.

So now Tamsin counted breaths in the dark, and the coal shifted somewhere behind her, settling, or maybe not settling, and she decided it was mice. Mice were fine. Mice were small and busy and nobody's enemy.

Forty-two.

She could hear the town through the little grate near the ceiling. Boots. Lots of boots, more than boots should ever be, going past in the street above. Men shouting a word over and over — *curfew, curfew* — which was a grown-up word that meant *go inside and be afraid where we can't see you doing it.*

Tamsin went inside. She was already inside. That felt like winning something.

Fifty-five.

*

The trouble with counting breaths was that eventually you ran out of things to count them *against*. The church bell rang four. Then five. Astrid had left when the light was still grey and low, the color of dishwater, and had said she would be back well before dark. But the grate showed only a thin stripe of sky and the stripe had gone from grey to nothing, and the bell rang six, and the cellar door at the top of the stairs stayed shut.

Seven o'clock. Eight. Tamsin knew because the bells kept going, patient as sheep, and each one made her chest feel smaller.

She thought about the bread. Astrid had gone for bread. Bread did not take four hours. Even in the capital, where the queues bent around corners like snakes, bread took one hour, maybe two if the baker was a liar about his stock. Marrow Quay was a small place. There were maybe three shops in the whole town and Tamsin had seen all of them from the coach window, hunched under the fog like old men waiting for a bus that never came.

So either Astrid was lost — impossible; Astrid knew every street the way Tamsin knew the cracks in her own bedroom ceiling — or Astrid was hiding, or Astrid was caught.

Or Astrid was dead.

Tamsin made herself think the word all the way through, because Mama had taught her that too. *If you refuse to look at a thing, it grows teeth in the dark.* Dead. Astrid dead in the street somewhere with her bread scattered, and the soldiers stepping over the loaves, and nobody in the whole world knowing where Tamsin was except a woman named Mrs. Halloway who had gone away, and a smuggler whose face Tamsin couldn't quite remember, and a general who was on the other side of a river.

That was the whole list. That was everyone.

Ninety-one breaths. She'd lost her place twice.

The coal behind her shifted again, and this time it didn't sound like mice. It sounded like a person trying very hard to sound like mice, which was worse, and Tamsin turned around slowly with her fists up the way Astrid had shown her — thumbs outside, weight low, *you're not fighting, you're buying three seconds, that's all anyone ever needs* — and found nothing there but a slope of black lumps and her own imagination grinning back at her.

She put her fists down. Her eyes were hot. She was six years old and she knew that crying made noise and noise made soldiers, so she cried the way Astrid had taught her, silently, with her mouth open and her jaw locked, tears running straight off her chin into the dark, and it worked, it worked fine, and she hated that she knew how to do it.

Mama had taught her that one too. Not on purpose. Just by example, in the last winter, sitting upright in bed with the flags-on-the-car men downstairs, smiling at Tamsin while her eyes did something else entirely.

*Forget my face,* the note had said. In Mama's handwriting. Tamsin had read it eleven times. She could not forget the face. The face was the last face. Forgetting it would be like forgetting which way was up.

*

The bell rang nine.

Tamsin stood up on the crate and looked out the grate. From down here the street was a slot of lamplight and fog, and once, twice, a boot crossed the slot. Slow boots. Patrolling boots. The kind that wanted to be heard.

She got down. She walked the length of the cellar, twelve steps one way, twelve steps back, because Astrid said a body that stays still too long forgets how to move, and a body that paces wears a groove in its own worry. Twelve steps. Twelve steps.

At some point she realized she was pacing toward the stairs.

She stopped at the bottom of them and looked up at the door. It was a plain door, wood, with a latch that stuck unless you lifted it just so — Astrid had made her practice lifting it just so, four times, saying *again, again* in that flat patient voice until Tamsin wanted to scream. Now the sticking latch seemed like the most beautiful thing in the world, because it meant the door was meant to be opened by someone who knew the trick, and Tamsin knew the trick, and Astrid was on the other side of it, or ought to be, or had been this morning, and the distance between the bottom of the stairs and the top of the stairs was thirteen steps and the distance between Tamsin and Astrid was suddenly unbearable.

*No matter what you hear. No matter what you see.*

But here was the thing about instructions: they were written by people who assumed the other person would keep existing. Astrid had given orders the way you leave food out for a cat, confident there'd be a cat. And the bell had rung nine, and the bread was four hours cold wherever it was, and Tamsin had spent her whole short life learning that the people who told you to wait were the people who didn't come back.

Mama had said *wait in the carriage, darling, I'll be ten minutes.* Tamsin had waited in the carriage for three hours until a footman with a strange accent opened the door and looked at her the way you look at furniture you've been sent to move.

She was not waiting in any more carriages.

She climbed the stairs.

*

The latch stuck. She lifted it just so. It gave with a click that sounded, in the silent house, like a rifle shot, and Tamsin froze on the top step with her heart slamming, counting to fifty, sixty, seventy, listening to the house argue with itself — beams ticking, fog pressing on the windows, somewhere far off the boots going by again, unhurried, bored.

The boarding house kitchen was dark. Embers in the stove, orange as eyes. Mrs. Halloway's chair sat empty by the window with a shawl folded over its arm, neat as a made bed, and seeing the shawl folded made Tamsin's throat hurt, because it meant Mrs. Halloway hadn't planned to be gone long either, and now the chair was empty too, and the whole world was chairs with shawls and nobody in them.

There was bread on the table. Half a loaf, cut ragged, with butter gone stiff beside it.

Tamsin stared at it. Her stomach clenched so hard it almost doubled her over. Because Astrid had been eating breakfast here — this morning, yesterday, whenever — and had torn the loaf and never finished it, and unfinished bread was a sentence with no end of it, and Tamsin stood in the dark kitchen with her hand hovering over the loaf and understood, in the way children understand things suddenly and completely, like a lamp coming on, that something had gone wrong in this room and the wrongness was still in the air, stale as the butter.

There was a scuff mark by the back door. Two of them. Boot-shaped, pointing out.

And pressed into the soft dirt of the yard beyond the glass, half-hidden by frost, a third print — bigger, ridged, a soldier's nailed sole, planted right where a person would stand to knock.

Tamsin's hand closed on the bread without her deciding anything. Her body had voted. Her body wanted to run back down to the cellar and pull the door shut and count breaths until spring. But her feet carried her instead to the window, up on her toes, chin just clearing the sill, and she looked out into the frozen yard and down the lane toward the harbor.

Lanterns. A knot of them, down by the water, bobbing. Voices carrying flat across the cold — not shouting now, that low official murmur of men doing paperwork to other men. And between two lanterns, held by the arms, walking but not really walking, being moved the way sacks are moved —

Blonde hair coming loose from its coil. A coat with the lining hanging open where someone had cut it, threads trailing, looking for something sewn inside.

Tamsin knew that coat better than she knew the note. She'd slept under it. She'd hidden inside it on the coach, buttoned up against Astrid's side like a second shirt.

They had Astrid.

The bread fell out of her hand onto the floor and she didn't hear it land. Everything in her went narrow and bright and simple, the way it had gone the day the flags-on-the-car men took Mama — that terrible childhood clarity where the whole world shrinks to one fact and the fact is *they're taking her* — and Tamsin was at the back door with her hand on the latch before the thinking part of her caught up and grabbed the rest of her by the collar.

*You're not fighting,* Astrid's voice said in her head. *You're buying three seconds.*

Three seconds for what? Three seconds wouldn't get Astrid out of soldier's hands. Three seconds wouldn't do anything except add a small girl to the sack-count, and Astrid had spent — how long? days? weeks? — teaching her, drilling her, making her practice stillness and silence and the flat gray walk, all of it for this exact moment, this exact lane, these exact lanterns.

Tamsin let go of the latch. She stood with her forehead against the cold door and breathed, one, two, three, and the brightness drained out of her and left something colder and older behind, something that felt like it belonged to a much larger child.

Down the lane, the lanterns were moving toward the customhouse. Toward the big stone building with the iron door. She could still see the blonde hair. She fixed it in her mind the way she'd fixed Mama's face, refusing the order in the note, refusing to forget, filing it away where the important things lived.

Then she heard the front of the house — the street side — the rattle of the latch, heavy fingers, a voice low and bored: *"In here. Check the rooms."*

Boots on the boarding house floorboards. Two sets. Coming through the hall.

Tamsin turned and looked at the kitchen — the bread on the floor, the scuffed marks, the open cellar door at the top of the stairs, breathing darkness into the room like a mouth.

Too far. The boots were faster.

She dropped flat behind Mrs. Halloway's chair, pulling the folded shawl down over herself, becoming a shadow among shadows, a lump among lumps, small and gray and still — *stillness is a skill, Tamsin, and skills can't be confiscated* — her heart going so hard she was sure it would give her away, sure they'd hear it, sure the whole town could hear it.

The kitchen door swung open. Lantern light swept the floor, crossed the fallen bread, stopped.

"Huh," said one of the soldiers. "Somebody's been at the loaf."

And then, from the direction of the harbor, cutting through the fog and the murmur and the boom of the customhouse door closing, Tamsin heard a voice she knew — raised, angry, unmistakably alive — and every plan, every drill, every careful gray silence she owned went up like paper.
