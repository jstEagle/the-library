## 16. The Officer's Doubt

Captain Emeric Voss had been awake for thirty-one hours when the fragment found him.

He was sitting in the back room of the Marrow Quay customhouse with a cup of tea gone cold beside him and a pencil worn down to a stub, and on the table before him lay the things they had taken from the courier woman's room: a hairbrush, a spare collar, a tin of tooth powder, a prayer card to no god anyone in the capital still admitted to worshipping, and a scrap of paper the size of his palm that smelled faintly of smoke.

The scrap was the problem.

Everything else about Astrid Nystrom — and he did not believe for one hour that her name was Astrid Nystrom, though it was a good name, a plain name, the kind of name a person chooses precisely because it sounds like furniture — everything else fit. The boarding-house register signed in a steady hand. The passage papers stamped by an office that no longer existed to confirm them. The coat with its mended lining, which his sergeant had turned inside out twice looking for the code-cylinder every courier out of the capital was rumored to carry, and found nothing but honest wear and a button hanging by a thread.

She had sat across from him for four hours and lied to him beautifully, and the beauty of it was what convinced him. Liars who are frightened lie badly. Liars who are calm have usually decided that the truth is more dangerous than whatever happens to liars caught lying well. She had told him she was a shipping clerk's widow waiting on a berth to Halvard, and she had told him this while looking at the bridge of his nose rather than his eyes, which is what trained people do, and she had not once touched the locket at her throat, which is what people carrying something precious do without knowing they're doing it.

But the fragment didn't fit.

It had come from the grate in her room at Mrs. Halloway's house, fished out by a corporal with a poker because Voss had ordered every hearth in the quarter searched after the second report came in from the ministry clerk — a soft, damp-handed man named Pell who sweated through two shirts a day and who had seen, he swore, a child. A girl of six or so. At the courier's door. Before dawn.

Children were not Voss's business. Children were never anyone's business until the ledger made them business, and the ledger —

The ledger. That was the other thing that didn't fit.

* * *

The requisition had taken him most of the day. The confiscated materials from General Veyra's ministry offices had been crated and shipped south in November, all except the indexes, which remained in the harbor master's strongroom under three locks and the guard of two men who spent most of their shift drinking. Voss had signed himself in at dusk and stood among the shelves with a lantern, pulling index boxes marked with the old classification stamps — ASSETS, RECOVERY PRIORITY, the bureaucratic language that made abduction sound like inventory — and he had found the volume within twenty minutes because the republic, even dying, kept excellent records.

It was a slim book. Grey binding. Numbered pages in a clerk's immaculate hand. Names, ages, last known locations, recovery status. He had opened it expecting to feel nothing, because he had learned long ago that feeling nothing was a professional skill like marksmanship, and he had been good at both.

Then he had turned to page forty-one.

*VOSS, MAREN.* Age seven. Last recorded address: the sisters' school at Cold Harbour. Recovery status: pending transfer, northern route.

His sister's daughter. His sister who had written to him in October asking — begging, the word had been *begging*, and he had read it three times and answered with a form letter — whether there was any way to get Maren out ahead of the advance, because the general's men had been through Cold Harbour once already asking questions at the school, polite questions, the kind with paperwork behind them.

He had sent the form letter. There was a procedure, he had written. Applications were processed in order. He had been careful to use the impersonal phrasing, the kind that could be shown to a superior without shame, and he had been ashamed anyway, and then he had put the letter away and gone to a review and drunk too much and forgotten it, the way you forget anything if you work at forgetting hard enough.

Now here was her name in a grey book between a boy from the fishing villages and a pair of twins from somewhere in the hill country, listed as an asset. Pending transfer. Northern route.

Northern route. Through Marrow Quay. Through his own jurisdiction, his own docks, his own custody chain.

Voss stood in the strongroom for a long time with the lantern burning down and the cold coming up through the stone floor into his boots, and he thought about the word *asset*, and he thought about Maren at seven, gap-toothed, who had once asked him whether soldiers were allowed to be afraid and he had said no and she had said *then you must be very tired*, and he had laughed, and now the word *pending* sat next to her name in a dead government's handwriting.

He copied the page. He told himself it was evidence. He knew it wasn't evidence. Evidence went into reports, and reports went upward, and upward was where the orders came from, and the orders would say *recover*, and the recovering would be done by men like his sergeant, efficient men, tired men, men who had stopped asking what the paperwork meant because asking was a luxury the war had taxed beyond anyone's wages.

He pocketed the copy instead.

* * *

Back at the customhouse, past midnight, he laid the burned fragment beside his copy of page forty-one and made himself look at both.

The fragment was small. Corner of a page, charred along one edge. But the hand was the same hand — the same immaculate clerk's numbering visible in the margin, the same column ruling — and enough letters survived to make the line legible if you worked at it, and Voss had spent eleven years working at fragments for a living.

*—SIN,* it began. Then a burn scar. Then: *age six. Marrow Quay. Priority.*

Tamsin. Tamsin Veyra. The general's daughter, the child whose face was on half the warrants in the province and whose name alone was worth a promotion to whoever delivered her. The courier woman hadn't just been hiding a child. She had been burning the record of the child — tearing the girl out of the machinery's memory, feeding the page to the grate the way you'd feed anything you wanted gone.

And the question that had been circling Voss all evening finally landed, and it landed hard:

Why?

Not why hide the girl — fear explained that, or money, or some connection to Veyra's faction. But why burn the page first? A woman fleeing with a stolen asset doesn't pause to destroy paperwork. Destruction serves no escape. It serves only one purpose: to make the thing unfindable. To take the girl out of the category of *asset* entirely. To declare, in the only language the state respects, that this child is not property.

You did that for someone you loved. Or someone you'd promised.

Voss looked at the fragment a long time. His tea had gone fully cold. Somewhere above him a soldier's boots crossed the floorboards, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be until morning.

And underneath the professional question another question rose, the kind he usually drowned before it could breathe: *What is the difference, Captain, between the woman in your cell and yourself?*

She had torn out one page. He had copied one page. She had used fire; he had used a lantern and a steady hand. Both of them had stood in front of the state's records and decided that one name in them was a lie the state had no right to tell.

The difference was that she'd been arrested for it and he was still holding the pencil.

* * *

At two in the morning he went down to the holding room.

She was awake. Of course she was awake; she had probably been awake the whole time, sitting straight-backed on the bench with her hands folded, conserving herself the way couriers conserve themselves, spending nothing. When the lamp light reached her she looked up with an expression of mild, patient inconvenience — the expression of a woman delayed at a ferry crossing — and Voss felt again the strange admiration that had been dogging him since the first hour of her interrogation, and felt also how dangerous admiration was, and stood in the doorway anyway.

"Madam Nystrom," he said.

"Captain." Her voice was level. Slightly hoarse. A woman who had been crying quietly and stopped before anyone could see, or a woman who had not cried at all. He couldn't tell, and the not-telling was itself an answer of a kind.

"I have some questions," he said. "They will not take long."

"I'm glad to help however I can." Perfectly flat. Perfectly cooperative. A performance so seamless it had stopped being a performance somewhere around midnight, or perhaps it had never been one; perhaps this was simply what she was, a person built entirely of composure, and the woman underneath had been left behind in some other town years ago.

"The child," Voss said. "Pell says he saw a child at your door."

"There was no child."

"He described her. Small, dark-haired, about six. Wearing a man's greatcoat cut down."

"People leave things at doors in a port town," she said. "Bundles. Packages. I open nothing that isn't mine."

"You'd swear to it?"

"I'd swear to anything true," she said, and for the first time something moved behind her eyes — not fear, he realized. Warning. As if she were telling him, in the only channel available to two people being watched by their own consciences: *be careful what you ask me to swear, Captain, because I will keep my word, and then where will we both be.*

Voss looked at her. She looked back. Between them, unspoken, lay a grey ledger and a burned page and a girl named Maren and a girl named Tamsin and the whole rotten apparatus of a republic that had begun eating its children and calling it policy.

"Your ship won't sail," he said instead. "The harbor's closed. You understand that."

"I understood it when your soldiers took the piers."

"Then you'll be here some days yet." He paused. "The cells are cold. The upper rooms are warmer. I'll have you moved up. No charge — a courtesy. Detained persons awaiting transport."

Something flickered across her face then, quick as a fish under ice. Gratitude, maybe. Or the recognition of a bargain whose terms neither of them would ever say aloud.

"That's kind of you," she said.

"It isn't kindness," Voss said. "It's delay. Learn the difference; it may save you." And he turned and gave the order to the sergeant on duty, and heard the woman's door unlocked and relocked on the upper floor, and walked back down the stairs with his heart going harder than it had gone during any battle he'd ever stood in, because battles at least had the decency to tell you which side you were on.

* * *

The search order was drafted at four o'clock.

It was a simple document. He had drafted fifty like it: *house-to-house sweep, Marrow Quay district, priority target juvenile female, description attached, all exits watched, all vessels held.* His signature at the bottom would set forty men moving through the fog before dawn with lanterns and lists, and the arithmetic was simple and terrible: forty men against one small girl in a town with maybe sixty cellars, and the odds were not the girl's.

He wrote it. He read it. He held the pen over the signature line.

Maren's name sat in his breast pocket, copied in his own hand, and it weighed more than the pistol on his hip.

He thought about his sister's letter. *Begging.* He thought about the form letter. He thought about the corporal who had found the fragment, and how the corporal had looked at it and said, "Burned, sir. Someone wanted that gone," with the toneless voice of a man reporting weather. He thought about the courier woman upstairs in the warm room, keeping her silence like a soldier keeps a position, and about the soft clerk Pell, who would report again tomorrow, and the day after, until someone higher than Voss took the matter out of his hands.

Delay. He had promised her delay. But delay was a currency that devalued hourly, and Voss knew — had always known, since the moment he'd pulled that index box from the shelf — exactly what he was doing and what it would cost. Not his career; careers were cheap this winter, going cheap as everything else. Something worse. The comfortable fiction that he was a man who enforced the law rather than a man who chose, daily, which parts of it to survive.

He signed the order.

Then he dated it wrong.

Not obviously — nothing obvious, nothing a tribunal could hang him with. He advanced the date by one day, the way a tired man might, the way a man working his thirty-second hour might plausibly err, and he initialed the correction with a flourish that obscured the figure, and he routed the order through the standard channels, which in Marrow Quay in the third week of December meant it would sit in the harbor master's tray until the morning clerk came on, and the morning clerk was a boy of nineteen who did not start before eight, and eight was —

Eight was hours. Hours was a boat. Hours was frozen marshland and a river and whatever desperate plan the invisible woman had been building all these long nights behind her perfectly composed face.

Voss set the order in the tray. He squared its edges. He stood over it for a moment like a man standing over a grave he had dug for himself and finding, unexpectedly, that the digging had felt like breathing.

Then he took his coat and went out into the fog, and did the last thing, the thing he had known he would do since page forty-one: he walked the long way round, past the coal yard on Tanner's Lane, and paused there in the dark to light a pipe he did not smoke, and while his hands were busy with the match he let his eyes do one slow, careless pass along the yard wall — the cellar grate, the coal chute, the low window with its broken latch.

Nothing moved. Nothing was supposed to move. But the latch on that window had been broken for a week, according to the yard's own complaint log, and tonight it hung closed, and freshly closed, and there was a smear in the coal dust below it the size of a small hand.

Voss knocked out his pipe, and walked on, and said nothing to anyone.

Behind him the fog thickened toward dawn, and somewhere in the marshes north of the town a smuggler's boat waited on a falling tide, and somewhere in a warm room above the customhouse a woman lay listening to the bells count five, and the search order slept in its tray like a loaded gun no one had remembered to pick up — yet.

By eight, everyone would remember at once.
