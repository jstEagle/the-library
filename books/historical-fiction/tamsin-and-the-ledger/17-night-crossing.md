## 17. Night Crossing

The bells woke her at four.

Not the customhouse bells — those she had stopped hearing days ago — but the harbor bells, rung hard and out of rhythm, the way a man rings them when he is not marking time but warning of it. Astrid was out of bed before the third stroke, hands already moving through the dark with the economy of ten years' practice: coat, boots, the little canvas bag packed since Tuesday and never once unpacked for comfort.

Tamsin was already awake. Of course she was. The child slept like a cat in wartime, one ear always open to the world.

"Is it today?" Tamsin whispered.

"It's tonight," Astrid said, "and it starts now."

She had rehearsed this hour so many times that it arrived almost gently, almost familiar, like a train pulling into a station she had studied on a map. Coat. Boots. Bag. She knelt and buttoned Tamsin into the boy's clothes they'd bought off a fisherman's widow — wool too big at the wrists, sleeves pinned twice — and pulled the cap down over the girl's ears until only the sharp small face showed, all eyes and resolve.

"You remember the rules?"

"Silence is a door," Tamsin recited. "I hold it shut from the inside."

"And if we're separated—"

"I go flat, I go still, I count to three hundred, and then I go where the water goes." The girl's breath fogged in the cold room. "Downstream, past the broken pier, under the black boat."

"Good." Astrid's throat tightened on something larger than *good*, and she swallowed it. There would be time — God willing there would be time — for everything larger than *good*.

* * *

They went out through the coal cellar, because Mrs. Halloway had shown her the loose board behind the coke bin weeks ago without ever once acknowledging what it might be for. That was the landlady's whole grammar: she communicated entirely in things she declined to see.

The night beyond the wall was the kind of cold that makes a lie of the word *night*. It wasn't dark so much as dense, fog frozen halfway to frost, every surface furred white, the cobbles slick with rime that cracked under their boots like thin sugar. Astrid kept them off the main streets, through the tannery yard, along the drainage cut where the ice made its own road. Behind them, up the hill, the town was waking wrong — too many lamps lit at once, lanterns moving in the streets in pairs and threes, and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the sound of dogs.

"They'll follow the cellar smell first," Astrid murmured. "Coal covers us. Keep your hands out of the snow — skin shows like a lamp."

Tamsin nodded and buried her fists in her sleeves.

Astrid had planned the route in her head eleven times and walked it twice by day, wearing different coats, carrying different excuses. North out of the tanyard, across the salt marsh where nothing grew tall enough to hide a dog but the ground itself did the work — channels, hummocks, reed-beds stiff with frost — two miles of it, to the creek where Brann's boat lay under netting among the eel-stakes. The tide turned at seven. Brann sailed with it or not at all.

Two miles. In summer it was a stroll. Tonight it was a country.

* * *

The marsh took them the way the sea takes a swimmer — gradually, then completely.

Within half a mile the town lights were a smudge behind them and the fog closed ahead like a held breath, and Astrid learned the night's first lesson: that frozen marsh is not silent. The ice in the channels groaned and ticked as the tide pulled underneath it. The reeds clicked against each other like knitting needles. Every sound was doubled — the real one and the echo of it off the fog — so that the world seemed full of footsteps that were not theirs.

Or were not only theirs.

Behind, maybe half a mile back, maybe less, a dog gave voice. Not the belling of a hound on scent — not yet — just one sharp interrogative bark, the sound of an animal asking its handler a question.

Then silence. Then two more barks, closer together.

"They have something," Tamsin whispered.

"They have our cellar," Astrid said. "That's all they have. We are smoke now. Walk."

But the girl was right, and Astrid knew it, and knowing it changed the texture of the air. They crossed the second channel on the ice, Astrid testing each step with her heel before committing her weight, Tamsin following exactly in her prints because that was the rule — *one set of tracks looks like a woman; two sets look like a fugitive* — and on the far bank Astrid allowed herself thirty seconds of rest behind a hummock while she listened.

The dogs were organized now. There were two voices she could distinguish, one high and one with a bell-like note, and they were working a line — east to west, methodical, the way soldiers work when they are not chasing but sweeping. Somewhere a whistle sounded, short-short-long, and was answered.

"They're not behind us," she said softly. "They're beside us. They think we went north along the coast road."

"Are they wrong?"

"Not yet."

* * *

It was somewhere past the third channel that the cold stopped being weather and became a participant.

Astrid felt it arrive in stages. First her fingers went from painful to absent, which she understood was worse. Then her feet announced themselves as objects she was operating rather than limbs she possessed. Her breath came in white flags that hung in the dead air long after she'd exhaled, marking their passage more honestly than any tracks. Beside her, Tamsin had gone quiet in the dangerous way — not disciplined-quiet but diminishing-quiet, her steps shorter, her answers shrinking to nods.

Astrid stopped them inside a stand of blackened reeds and crouched to the girl's level.

"Talk to me."

"I'm fine."

"That was slow. Slow costs us. Say something true."

Tamsin's chin trembled — from cold, mostly, but not entirely. "My toes went away a little."

"Stamp them. Hard. Now, before you decide they're gone forever." Astrid took the girl's hands and chafed them between her own, rough and quick, then pressed them under her arms. "Cold is a liar. It tells you to stop, and stopping is the only thing that kills you out here. So we make a bargain: it lies, and we keep walking. Yes?"

"Yes."

"And here is another truth, free of charge." She held the girl's face between her mittened hands. "Whatever happens tonight, you do exactly as I said. Flat, still, three hundred, downstream. Because I will find you. Do you understand? Whatever happens, my whole job in this life, starting tonight, is finding you. Everything else is decoration."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment, six years old and older than that, and nodded once, the way soldiers nod.

They kept walking.

* * *

The sweep found them at the fourth channel.

There was no dramatic moment — no shout in the dark, no lantern flaring over a hummock. What happened was smaller and more terrible than that: the dog-voices changed key. All night they had been asking questions, scattered, speculative. Now, suddenly, they were answering one. Both voices converged south of the reeds, and a human voice joined them, low and carrying across the ice: *"Line! Line on the channels!"*

Lanterns bloomed in the fog, three of them, then five, strung out in a ragged arc that was, unmistakably, tightening.

Astrid did the arithmetic without wanting to. The arc ran from the coast road to the sea-wall. The creek with Brann's boat lay north-northwest, a mile and a quarter. The arc was closing at walking pace; the dogs would close it faster. Between the arc and the creek lay the widest channel of the marsh, forty feet of black water under ice that had been groaning all night about how thin it was.

"We're going to be early," she whispered, and turned north, and broke the rule she herself had made: she stopped caring about tracks.

They ran. Or moved at the shuffling, lurching gait that running becomes in the dark on broken ground, Astrid hauling Tamsin by the wrist over tussocks and through frozen reed, breath sawing, the bag slamming her hip, the girl's hand a live coal of grip in hers. Behind them the lanterns multiplied — someone had seen movement, or thought they had, and hope is enough for a soldier with orders. A whistle blew the pursuit pattern now, no pretense of a sweep, and a rifle shot cracked the fog open, aimed at nothing, meant for everything: *we know you're here.*

The channel appeared out of the murk like a wound.

Forty feet of it, black water visible through seams in the ice, the far bank a pale suggestion. Astrid went out onto it low, weight spread, sliding rather than stepping, dragging Tamsin after her, and the ice spoke under them — a long rising creak, then a crack that ran away sideways like a whip being flexed.

"Mama—" Tamsin began, and caught herself, because the rule was no names in the open, even that one, *especially* that one.

"I know," Astrid breathed. "I know. Almost."

They were two-thirds across when the ice ended the argument. A pane the size of a door let go beneath them with a sound like a slammed cathedral, and Astrid's leg went through to the thigh, the cold hitting her like a fall from height, total and electric. She didn't scream, because screaming spends heat. She got her arms flat on the sound ice, took Tamsin's collar in both fists, and heaved — once, twice, a third time powered by nothing she could name — and they came out on the far bank soaked to the knee and hip, steaming faintly in the frost-light, alive.

Behind them, at the near edge, the lanterns stopped. Dogs don't like thin ice either. Voices argued. A lantern swung, probing, calculating.

"Flat," Astrid gasped. "Reeds. Down."

They flattened into a frozen reed-bed, side by side, cheek to frozen mud, Astrid's soaked leg already burning as the wet began to freeze in her boot. Thirty feet away a lantern beam swept the reeds overhead, close enough to count the stalks, and passed on. Passed on. Came back. Held.

In the beam's edge, Astrid watched her own breath rise above the reeds in little white ghosts and understood, with terrible clarity, that the fog that had hidden them all night was now betraying them, that warmth itself had become evidence, that there was no version of invisibility that survived a lantern held steady—

And then the tide turned.

She heard it before she understood it: a deep subterranean shifting under the marsh, the whole drowned landscape exhaling, the channels filling from the seaward end. And with it, from the north, faint and impossible and beautiful, a single bell — Brann's bell, rung twice, the signal that meant *now or never*.

The lantern lifted away from their patch of reeds. Voices called, urgent, from the direction of the creek — because of course, of course the searchers had realized it too, that the tide was the whole game, that whatever the runners wanted was leaving on the water, and every light in the marsh wheeled north like a flock startled off a field.

Astrid rose out of the mud with her soaked leg screaming and her heart doing the opposite of screaming, which was worse.

"Now," she whispered. "Run anyway. Run ugly. The boat won't wait and neither will the tide."

They ran the last quarter mile on legs that had stopped belonging to them, past the eel-stakes, through the net-hung skeletons of old boats, down the slick mud of the creek bank where Brann stood in the shallows holding his craft against the current, his face a gray smear under his hood, his mouth shaping words the wind tore apart — and Astrid read them rather than heard them:

*You're late. And you're followed.*

She turned.

Up the creek, along the ridge of the sea-wall, the lanterns had found the horizon line — a dozen of them now, maybe more, strung out against the paling sky like a rosary of small fires, coming on fast. And ahead of them, where the creek met the river and the river ran wide and black to the sea, the water was beginning, at its edges, to move.

Between the boat and the sea lay the last channel — broad, tidal, its ice groaning like a living thing under the new push of the tide.

Brann shoved his bow toward the bank and shouted the only words the wind let through whole:

"She'll take the ice or she won't. Choose!"
