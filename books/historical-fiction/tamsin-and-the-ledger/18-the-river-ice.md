## 18. The River Ice

The smuggler's name was Corven, and he had lied about the ice.

Astrid understood it the moment they reached the reed bank and saw the river — not the grey lace of early winter she'd been promised, but a black seam of open water running down the channel's spine like a wound that refused to close. The cold had come late and hard this year; the shallows had frozen first, then the margins, but the deep current in the middle still moved, dark and muscular, shrugging off every night's frost.

"That's a week of cold away from holding," Astrid said.

Corven spat into his glove. "It held for me Tuesday."

"It held *you*," Astrid said. "You weigh as much as one of my boots."

Behind her, Tamsin made no sound at all. That was the thing that frightened Astrid most now, more than the dogs or the lanterns or the searchlights sweeping the far hills: how good the child had gotten at silence. Six years old, and she could stand motionless in freezing reeds with her breath cupped inside her coat like something stolen. Astrid had taught her that. She tried not to think about what else she was teaching her.

Corven studied the river with the flat patience of a man pricing fish. "We cross where the current bends. Ice is older there. We keep twenty paces apart, we don't stop, and if it talks to you—" he tapped his ear "—you answer by going flat and spreading your weight."

"If it talks?"

"Everything talks before it breaks. You'll hear it. Like a woman clearing her throat under the floor." He looked at Tamsin then, really looked, for the first time since the coal cellar. Something crossed his face — not kindness exactly. Recognition. "How much does she weigh?"

"Enough."

"Then carry enough rope."

* * *

They went at the hour when the patrol lamps on the quay changed hands, when the soldiers were coldest and least willing to look at anything farther than their own boots. Astrid carried Tamsin on her back, the girl's arms locked around her throat, her chin tucked into Astrid's collar so her breath wouldn't rise as steam and hang in the air like a small white flag. Astrid had wrapped the child's boots in sacking to muffle any sound against the ice. Her own feet she left bare in the wet — she couldn't say why, except that she wanted to feel what the river was doing, and trust between her skin and the world had become the only instrument she had left.

The code-cylinder was gone. She had paid it to Corven two nights ago along with every coin she owned, and the thought of it still arrived sometimes like a slap — the weight missing from her coat lining, the mission dissolved into a smuggler's strongbox somewhere under the planks of a boat named *Mercy*, which was either an irony or an instruction. Let them call her traitor. Traitor was a word men used when a woman chose. She had been called worse things this month in silence.

The marsh gave way to mud, the mud to frozen gravel, and then the gravel rang hollow under her feet and she knew they stood at the edge of it.

The river lay before them like a road built by something that hated travelers. Frost smoke drifted low across the surface, moving without wind. On the far bank, maybe four hundred yards of darkness away, the pine line stood black and absolute. Somewhere beyond it, Veyra. Somewhere behind them, lanterns already bobbing along the quay road — too many, moving too fast, in the wrong pattern for a change of watch.

"They know," Astrid said quietly. Not a question.

Corven had gone very still. "They know someone crosses tonight. Maybe not who. Maybe not where." He knelt and drove a spike of iron into the bank ice, tying off one end of a coil of rope. "That buys the first ten yards. After that the river keeps its own accounts."

Tamsin's arms tightened around Astrid's neck. She didn't speak. They had an agreement about speaking on the move: three taps meant *I'm here*. Two meant *put me down, I can walk*. One meant nothing yet, because Astrid had forbidden it, because one tap was the tap you saved for *I'm afraid* — and she hadn't needed it yet, and some animal part of Astrid believed that if the child never used it, they would live.

Three taps now. *I'm here.*

Astrid stepped onto the river.

* * *

For the first fifty yards the ice behaved. It groaned beneath them — long, shifting complaints, the sound of a giant turning over in sleep — but it held. The frost smoke thinned as they moved out, and the sky opened above them, enormous and starless, clouded to the horizon, which meant the searchlights would have nothing to reflect against except fog and water, which meant they would be visible as blades rather than beams.

Corven moved ahead, bent low, testing each stride with his pole before committing his weight. Astrid followed his exact line. Behind them, back on the southern shore, the lanterns had stopped. Gathered. She could see them massing at the point where the marsh path met the bank, and she could see, among them, the taller shape that didn't sway the way tired men swayed. An officer who stood straight. The officer from the customhouse, probably. The one who had looked at her for a full minute and let her go, and whose face she would remember longer than her own name.

A voice called across the water. It carried strangely over the ice, flattened and doubled by frost, arriving from everywhere and nowhere:

"*Corven!*"

The smuggler didn't break stride. But his shoulders said everything.

"You're done, Corven! The boat's taken! Your boys are in irons and telling stories! Come back and it's a cell! Stay out there and it's the river!"

Astrid did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do all night. If the boat was taken, there was no boat. If there was no boat, the far bank was not safety but merely the other side of the same trap, unless Veyra's people were where Veyra's letter had sworn they would be — and letters were paper, and paper had lied to her before, though never with this handwriting.

The searchlights came on.

Two of them, from the battery on the customs ridge, sweeping down through the frost smoke in slow crossing arcs, and where the beams hit the river the ice lit up like the inside of a pearl — every crack, every frozen gas bubble, every hairline fault suddenly legible, a map of everything that could kill them drawn in light. Astrid dropped flat without deciding to, pulling Tamsin off her back and under her own body, cheek against ice so cold it burned, and watched the beam crawl toward them across a hundred yards of shining river.

"Under me," she breathed. "Don't move. Don't breathe if you can manage it."

The light swept past. Darkness slammed down, and in the afterimage Astrid saw the map anyway, printed on the inside of her eyelids: the safe lane was gone. Their route ran straight through a lattice of cracks wider than her arm, branching like lightning burned into glass.

Corven's whisper came out of the dark, closer than she expected. "New line. Thirty degrees left, toward the bend. There's old ice under the shadow of the bluff — the lights can't hold it steady there."

"And after the bend?"

"After the bend is the middle channel." He paused. "After the bend, we find out what I promised you was worth."

One tap against Astrid's shoulder blade. Small fingers through her coat. *I'm afraid.*

Astrid pressed her hand flat over the child's and held it there for the length of one breath. Then she lifted Tamsin onto her back again, found the new line with her bare feet, and went.

* * *

The bend bought them ninety seconds and cost them everything else.

The shadow of the bluff did hide them — the searchlights raked past overhead, unable to drop their angle, and for a few strides Astrid dared to believe the night might simply let them go. Then the ice began to talk.

Not groan. Talk. A crisp, articulate sound, like knuckles cracking under the floorboards of a vast house, ahead of them and to both sides, a conversation conducted entirely in warnings. Corven stopped so abruptly that Astrid nearly walked into his pole.

"What is it?" she whispered.

He didn't answer. He crouched, laid his palm flat on the ice, and listened the way Mrs. Halloway listened at doors — with his whole body, with whatever old griefs he kept under his coat. When he raised his head, Astrid saw the truth in his face before he spoke it, and she hated him gently, the way you hate a doctor.

"Current took the old ice out from under the new," he said. "There's nothing under us but water and a wish. Go back and they take the girl. Keep going and—" He shrugged. "The river doesn't negotiate."

"Then we spread out and go fast."

"We go *light*." He uncoiled the rope from around his shoulders and tied one end around his own waist, the other end he held out to her. "Twenty feet apart. If I go through, don't be a hero, don't come to me, just keep the line and pull when I shout. If she goes through—" he looked at Tamsin, and his voice roughened "—you go in after her. Rope's for whoever's left."

Astrid tied the rope around her waist, above Tamsin's locked legs, knotting it twice. "Corven."

"What."

"When this is done — if this is done — I'm going to tell someone your real name."

Something moved through his face, quick as a fish under ice. "Don't bother," he said. "I sold it years ago. It wasn't worth what you paid for mine." And he turned and ran.

* * *

Running on river ice is not running. It is a controlled fall repeated at speed, a prayer performed with the whole skeleton. Astrid ran it carrying a child, barefoot, in the dark, while the world beneath her spoke in its cracking language and the searchlights wheeled above like the eyes of something patient and enormous.

Forty yards. Fifty. The ice under her feet thinned in sensation even where it didn't thin in fact — she could feel the water's presence through it, the way you feel a stranger standing behind a door. Tamsin's breath was hot against her neck, fast, small, controlled — *she's using what I taught her, God forgive me, she's six and she's using what I taught her* —

Seventy yards. The bend opened ahead, and beyond it the middle channel showed itself as a long black tongue of open water, steaming faintly, thirty feet wide, edged in ice that shone white and treacherous in the sweep of a distant lamp.

Then the shot went up.

Not aimed — a flare, rising from the southern bank, blooming red above the river, and in its falling light the whole world turned the color of a wound. Astrid saw Corven silhouetted at the channel edge, pole planted. Saw the soldiers pouring onto the near shore, a dozen of them, lanterns swinging. Saw the officer standing apart from them, not shouting, watching the river with his head tilted, listening — listening for the ice the way Corven had listened, and Astrid understood in one cold flash that he knew exactly what the river was doing to them, and was simply waiting for it to finish.

"HOLD THE BANK!" someone bellowed. "NOBODY CROSSES! LET THE RIVER HAVE THEM!"

And the river answered.

The crack came from below and ahead at once — a sound Astrid felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears, a single colossal syllable spoken by the whole river at once, and then the world tilted. Not broke — *tilted*, a sheet of ice the size of a chapel floor lifting at one end like a lid, and Corven went with it, sliding backward down the incline toward the open water, pole skidding, rope snapping taut and hauling Astrid forward onto her knees, Tamsin screaming — actually screaming, finally, the one tap spent at last —

"LET GO OF THE ROPE!" Corven bellowed. "CUT IT OR SHE'S IN WITH ME!"

Astrid had no knife free, no time, and made the only choice the arithmetic allowed: she unwound the knots with fingers that no longer belonged to her, raw and stupid, while the rope dragged her toward the tilting edge and Tamsin clung to her neck and the flare-light died and the searchlights swung back and found them — found them both, pinned in white light on a breaking river, as visible as they had ever been in their entire invisible lives.

The rope came loose. Corven went down the incline, into the channel, under — and came up once, gasping, in the black water, and shouted something that the wind took apart before it arrived. Then the current folded him under, and the river closed over him like a page.

Astrid rolled away from the edge, hauled Tamsin up against her chest, and got her feet under her on ice that was still shivering from what it had done.

The channel was thirty feet wide. The far ice began where the black water ended, and beyond it, two hundred yards of broken, ridged, groaning floes to the pine line and whatever waited there.

There was no boat. There was no smuggler. There was no route.

There was a woman, a child, thirty feet of open black water at a temperature that killed in minutes, and behind them the sound of soldiers being ordered not to fire — not out of mercy, she understood, but because the officer wanted them alive, or wanted the girl alive, or simply preferred that paperwork stay simple.

"Mama." Tamsin's voice, tiny, wrecked, the first time she had ever used the word, and Astrid felt it go through her like a spear through water. "Mama, I'm cold."

"I know, little bird." Astrid was already tearing strips from the hem of her coat, wrapping the child's hands, binding Tamsin to her own body with the smuggler's rope, chest to chest, knot after knot, until they were one creature with four limbs and one set of lungs. "Listen to me. Once only, so listen well. We swim. You do not help. You do not kick. You are cargo, and I am the courier, and I have never once lost what I was carrying. Do you hear me? I have *never once*."

Three taps against Astrid's shoulder. Faint. *I'm here.*

"On my word. Breathe."

She stepped to the edge of the broken ice. The searchlights held her in a column of white. Across the water, on the far bank, a lantern appeared among the pines — one lantern, swinging in a rhythm that was not military, not panicked, but deliberate, three swings and a pause, three swings and a pause.

Someone was signaling. Someone was waiting.

Astrid filled her lungs with air that felt like swallowing knives.

"Now."
