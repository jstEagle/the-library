## 20. The Choice of Names

The far bank was a country of black pines and no lanterns.

Astrid knelt in the shallows with Tamsin pressed against her chest, both of them soaked to the ribs, the river still roaring its complaint behind them. Somewhere out on the water a man was shouting — the smuggler, Brech, being pulled under by soldiers' hands or by the current, she couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her throat like a stone. She had sold her mission to that man. He had bought it with his boat, and perhaps with his life, and the ledger of what she owed had grown another page she could never repay.

Tamsin's teeth chattered against her collarbone. Small hands, blue at the knuckles, gripped the front of Astrid's coat with the ferocity of someone who had learned in one winter that everything could be taken.

"We're across," Astrid whispered. "We're across, little one. That's the whole battle."

It wasn't. It never was. But the child needed the lie shaped like truth, so Astrid said it again, softer, and felt Tamsin's grip ease by a single degree.

She got them up the muddy shelf of the bank and into the tree line before she let herself shake. Her hands wouldn't work. She tried to wring water from her coat hem and her fingers simply refused the instruction, curling uselessly, so she stopped trying and crouched there breathing fog into the dark, listening for dogs.

No dogs. The searchlights behind them had gone to sweeping the near shore, the marsh, the place where any sane fugitive would have been caught. No sane fugitive crossed breaking ice at night carrying a six-year-old. Invisibility had failed her all winter; absurdity, it seemed, might succeed where craft could not.

"Miss Nystrom." The voice came from the trees — low, worn, unmistakable even now, even after years, even stripped of the parade-ground authority that had once made junior ministers spill their tea. "Don't run. I've watched you cross a frozen river. You haven't the strength left to run from me."

Astrid turned slowly, putting her body between the voice and the child, which was ridiculous — the voice belonged to the one person on earth who would die before touching Tamsin — but the body keeps its own ledgers too.

General Elara Veyra stepped out from between two black pines like something the forest had decided to return. She was thinner than the woman from the treaty photographs, thinner than the figure Astrid had last seen across a negotiation table when the republic still pretended to have seasons and patience. She wore a fisherman's oilskin two sizes too large and a wool cap pulled low, and without the grey uniform she looked less like a general than like every tired woman who had ever waited out a storm in a harbor tavern. Only the eyes were unchanged: pale, patient, and absolutely awake.

"You," Astrid said. It came out as an accusation. She supposed it was one.

"Me." Veyra came closer, stopping three paces off, close enough to see the child, far enough to be courteous about it. Her gaze moved to Tamsin and something happened in her face — a fracture, quickly mortared over, but Astrid saw it, because Astrid had spent her whole life learning to see what powerful people tried not to show. "Is she—"

"She's frozen, exhausted, and alive. Which is more than I can say for your courier arrangements." Astrid heard her own voice sharpen and did nothing to blunt it. "A note on a doorstep. Forget the face. Do you know what I thought when I read that? I thought: this is how monsters write."

"Yes," Veyra said simply. "I know. I wrote it that way on purpose. If the note fell into other hands, it needed to be nothing — a cruel abandonment, a stranger's errand, no thread leading back to me. And if it fell into yours—" She paused. "Then you'd already understand why I couldn't sign it."

Wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea deciding things. Tamsin stirred against Astrid's shoulder and Veyra's whole body tilted toward the child, an inch, no more, and then corrected itself with visible discipline.

"There's a farmhouse," Veyra said. "Two miles north. A widow named Osk who owes me more than she can count and hates me almost as much. She'll take us in until dawn. After dawn—" She spread her hands, oilskin sleeves flapping. "After dawn we decide what we are."

"We," Astrid repeated. "There's no *we*, General. There's me, a woman who has lost everything she owned in one winter, and you, a woman whose name is on execution orders in three capitals. And between us there's a child who belongs to neither of us and both of us, and who I am not handing over to anyone, including you. If that was the price of the rescue, you should have stayed on your side of the river."

Something flickered across Veyra's face that was almost — almost — a smile. "You always talked like a clerk who'd read one too many subversive pamphlets."

"I *was* a clerk. That was the entire point of me." Astrid shifted Tamsin's weight to her other hip. The girl had gone heavy with sleep, dead-weight trusting, and Astrid's arms ached and she would not have put her down for a promotion to empress. "Take us to the farmhouse. Then we talk."

They walked. Two miles through frozen mud and pine dark, Veyra setting a pace that respected the child's sleep, and Astrid cataloguing her out of habit — the slight hitch in the general's left stride, an old wound; the way she checked the treeline at intervals without turning her head; the way she kept herself always downwind of the child, as if even her smell might be evidence. A hunted animal's grammar. Astrid recognized it because she'd been speaking it fluently since autumn.

* * *

The widow Osk's farmhouse was a low stone thing with a turf roof and a stove that had been fed recently enough to still hold heat. The widow herself took one look at Veyra, spat — with feeling rather than hatred, Astrid judged — and pointed at the back room without a word. Whatever history lived between those two women, it had been settled long ago and at a price neither wished to discuss before strangers.

Astrid stripped Tamsin out of the wet coat, wrapped her in blankets that smelled of lanolin and woodsmoke, and stood over her until the small breathing went long and even. Only then did she come back to the main room, where Veyra sat at the table with her cap off and her cropped grey hair steaming faintly in the stove-light, watching Astrid with the expression of a woman reading a document she already knew was going to cost her.

Osk put down bread, hard cheese, and a jug of something spirituous, then withdrew to the loft with the dignity of a woman who had survived three regimes by knowing when stairs were useful.

"Eat," Veyra said.

"You first." Astrid broke the bread anyway, tore it, handed half across the table. Their fingers didn't touch. Some old formality held between them, some protocol from a peacetime negotiation room neither had ever formally left. "And then you'll explain to me why the most wanted woman in the republic was waiting on the far bank of an unguarded river crossing at midnight, in February, for a courier she hasn't seen in seven years."

"Because I knew you'd bring her here." Veyra ate her half of the bread in four efficient bites, like a woman refueling rather than dining. "Not to this farm. To this coast. To Marrow Quay. You're Nystrom. You follow procedure the way other people follow religion. The codes go north, the ship goes out of Marrow Quay, therefore the child goes wherever you go, because you promised me, seven years ago, over a cradle, that no child of mine would ever be taken." She set down the crust. "I built my entire last campaign around the assumption that you keep your promises. It was the only assumption left worth building on."

"That's not strategy. That's madness."

"It's both. Most surviving strategies are." Veyra leaned back, and the chair creaked under a frame that had once commanded armies and now commanded, at best, a widow's spare room. "You want the full accounting. Fine. When the purges started, they didn't come for me first — they came for my staff. My aides. The women who ran my household. And the lists included dependents. Children. There's a ministry ledger — you may have heard of it — in which my daughter appears as an asset. Recoverable. Like a debt or a chair."

"I've seen it," Astrid said quietly. "Page forty-one. I burned her page."

Veyra went very still. For a long moment the only sound was the stove ticking and the wind working at the turf roof, and Astrid watched a general absorb the news that a clerk had done more for her daughter with a match than an army could do with artillery.

"Burned it," Veyra repeated.

"The original page was already gone — I stole a copy from the clerk who found her name. But paper remembers, so I burned the memory too. It won't hold. Copies exist. Men exist who've memorized worse lists than that one." Astrid wrapped her blistered hands around the jug and poured, not because she wanted the drink but because her hands wanted an occupation. "But for one night, somewhere, your daughter does not officially exist. I recommend savoring it. It's the only victory I've managed all winter."

"And your codes?"

"Gone. Sold to a smuggler for a boat he no longer possesses." She drank. The liquor was harsh and honest, like everything else on this coast. "Somewhere a neutral port is waiting for diplomatic instructions that will never arrive, and the republic's last chance at a negotiated surrender died in a coal cellar in Marrow Quay, and I am the traitor who killed it. Write that in your memoirs, General. Assuming anyone lets you keep memoirs."

Veyra was quiet for a while. When she spoke again, the parade-ground voice was gone entirely, and what remained was smaller and older and much more dangerous. "You think I called you here to thank you. Or to apologize. I'm going to offer you something instead, and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to understand it, in that order."

"Go on."

"There are people beyond the border. Not armies — those are finished, or soon will be. People. Archivists, exiles, a few officers who kept their consciences and lost their commissions. They're gathering what the republic destroyed. Testimony. Records. Names. Including the names in that ledger — because burning one page doesn't burn the machine, Nystrom, and when this regime falls, and it will fall, the men who ran it will scatter like cockroaches into neutral ports and comfortable exile, and someone will need to remember exactly which faces go with which crimes." She tapped the table once, a gesture that had once ended meetings. "You're a courier. The best I ever cleared. You memorize faces out of habit — I've known that about you since the treaty talks, you used to stand at the window and watch the delegations arrive and I could see you filing them away. Faces, routes, names. You are, whether you admit it or not, a walking archive. And I am offering you a place in the one work left that matters."

Astrid set down her cup. "Which is?"

"Sanctuary. Under a new name. New papers — good ones, I've spent a year arranging them. A town on the northern coast where nobody asks questions because everybody fled there from somewhere. A trade, if you want one; your hands look like they could learn bookbinding. And Tamsin raised in safety, under that new name, with no ledger anywhere in the world containing her." Veyra's pale eyes held steady. "That's the offer. Disappear properly this time. Not the courier's invisibility — the real kind. The kind that lasts."

The stove ticked. Wind worried the roof. In the back room, Tamsin breathed in the small metronome rhythm of genuine sleep, and Astrid found she could not stop listening to it, the way a drowning woman cannot stop looking at shore.

"No," she said.

Veyra blinked. In seven years, Astrid suspected, few people had said no to this woman, and none of them had meant it.

"Say the rest of it," Veyra said.

"You want to hand me a new name like a coat. Wear this one instead, it fits better, nobody will ever look twice." Astrid stood, and her knees protested, and she ignored them. "I had a name that fit perfectly for thirty-one years. Astrid Nystrom, courier, third-class commission, furniture. Invisible. I told myself it was safety. Do you know what I learned this winter? Invisibility is just a name other people give you so they don't have to see what you're doing. Mrs. Halloway was invisible. Brech was invisible. Every clerk who ever carried a list of children to a filing cabinet was invisible, right up until the moment the list was used, and then it turned out we'd been load-bearing the whole time." She heard her own voice rise and let it. "I spent one winter becoming a person, General. An actual person, with a debt to a drowned smuggler and a promise to a landlady and a burned page and a child who calls me by a name she invented because I was too cowardly to offer my own. I am not putting that person in a drawer so I can be furniture in some northern fishing village until I die."

"Tamsin needs you alive. Dead heroes don't raise children."

"Tamsin doesn't need a ghost either." Astrid came around the table, and up close she could see the exhaustion carved into Veyra's face, the cost of a year of running, and she made herself say the next part gently, because it deserved gentleness. "You offered me a new name. Here's my counteroffer. I keep mine. Astrid Nystrom, openly, on whatever documents will have it. If your archivists need a courier who can carry testimony across borders, they can hire one under her true name, and when the day comes that someone reads that name aloud in a courtroom or a history book or a schoolroom, there'll be a woman standing behind it who chose to be seen. That's the only sanctuary I'm interested in. The kind I don't have to vanish to enter."

Veyra looked at her for a long time. Outside, the wind dropped suddenly, the way it does on this coast, all at once, like a held breath released, and in the silence Astrid could hear the river they'd crossed still muttering to itself in the distance.

"You understand what you're refusing," Veyra said at last. "Safety. Real safety. The kind I'd have given anything for at your age."

"I understand exactly." Astrid sat back down, and to her own surprise, smiled — a real one, crooked and tired. "I spent my whole life believing I was a bystander history hadn't noticed yet. Turns out history noticed. Turns out I was in the ledger all along, I just couldn't read the entry. Well. Now I can. And I find I'd rather be written down than erased."

Veyra exhaled slowly. Then she did something Astrid had never seen her do, not in negotiations, not in photographs, not in the fever-dreams of a nervous young clerk: she laughed. Quietly, briefly, but genuinely, shaking her head at the ceiling beams like a woman conceding a point to fate.

"A courier who refuses a false name. A traitor who wants credit. You realize you've become the least employable person on this continent?"

"I've become the most *findable* person on this continent. There's a difference."

"There is," Veyra agreed. "And God help whoever finds you." She reached into the oilskin coat and drew out a folded paper — travel-worn, sealed with plain wax, handled often. She set it on the table between them but kept two fingers on it, an old negotiator's habit, holding terms hostage to attention. "Then hear this, Astrid Nystrom, under your true name, since names matter so much to you tonight. There's a copy of that ledger. A complete one. Not the ministry draft you burned — the master record. It left the capital eleven days ago in a diplomatic pouch, bound for a safe deposit house in Corvane, where certain gentlemen intend to wait out the collapse and then sell it back to whichever government rises next. Every name. Every asset. Every child they didn't manage to recover — and every witness who signed against them. Mine is in it. So is yours, as of Tuesday, when a customs officer filed a report describing a woman who lied fluently."

Astrid felt the floor tilt slightly under the farmhouse. "Where's the pouch now?"

"On a ship called the *Meridian*, out of Corvane harbor, six days from now. And before you ask — yes, that's the ship your codes were meant to meet. The world has a sense of humor. It's just a very bad one." Veyra slid the paper across. "I can't cross that border. My face is on walls in every port town from here to the capital. But a courier with her own name, her own face, and a talent for being underestimated—" She shrugged, oilskin whispering. "Well. You said you'd rather be written down than erased. Here's your pen."

Astrid looked down at the paper. A route. A harbor. A name she'd been carrying sewn into her coat lining since the first cold week of winter, when she'd still believed she was furniture.

In the back room, Tamsin murmured in her sleep — a word, half-formed, and Astrid caught it clearly across the small dark room.

"Mama."

Neither woman moved. Neither woman looked at the other. The word hung in the stove-lit air, belonging to no one and to both of them, and Astrid understood that whatever name she chose — the old one, the true one, the one she'd just refused to surrender — she would carry that syllable with it for the rest of her life, and she found, examining herself honestly in the stove-light, that she did not want to give it back.

She picked up the paper.

"All right," said Astrid Nystrom, aloud, to a general, in her own name, in a room where three people could hear her. "Tell me about Corvane."
