## 22. Tamsin and the Ledger

The house on the hill above Vessengard had a view of the harbor, and Astrid had chosen it for exactly that reason — so she could watch the ships come in every morning and know, with the certainty of a woman who had once lived by schedules she never wrote down, that the world was still moving.

She was sixty-one now. Her hands had begun to ache in cold weather, an old debt from the marsh crossing that no doctor could quite name. She kept bees, badly. She kept her own name, perfectly. *Astrid Nystrom* sat in the parish register, in the ferry manifests, in the ledgers of the seed merchant who came up from the valley twice a year. After thirty years of practicing invisibility, visibility had turned out to be a discipline of its own: you had to maintain it daily, like a fire, like a promise.

The letter came on a Tuesday in October, carried up from the village by the postman's boy, who complained the whole way about the steepness of the path. Astrid read it standing at the kitchen window while the kettle went ignored behind her.

It was written in Tamsin's hand — which was to say, it was written in a hand that had learned to be careful. Even at twenty-eight, even after university, even after everything, Tamsin still wrote as though the paper might be confiscated. The loops were small and closed. Nothing leaked off the line.

*Aunt Astrid. I have found it. I will explain when I come, but I am coming tomorrow, and I am bringing it with me. Do not be afraid. I know what to do now. I think I have always known.*

Astrid set the letter down on the table and looked at it for a long time.

*I know what to do now.*

There was only one thing, in all their shared history, that remained undone.

* * *

They had parted badly, or rather they had parted the way people part when neither of them believes in endings. That was eleven years ago, after the trial — after Tamsin, nineteen and furious, had stood up in the hearing room in Vessengard and said, in a voice that did not shake, "I am listed in that document by name. I would like to see it." And the magistrate, an old man who had spent his whole career pretending the Republic's last years had been someone else's fault, had refused, and Tamsin had refused to sit down, and the room had learned her name the way rooms learn names: reluctantly, and then completely.

Afterward, in the corridor, Astrid had tried to take her arm and Tamsin had pulled away.

"You burned my page," Tamsin said. "You decided what I got to forget."

"I decided what *they* got to keep."

"It's not yours to decide. It was never yours. You carried me across ice and I will love you until I die for it, but the ledger isn't finished just because we escaped it. There are other pages. Other children. Some of them grew up. Some of them are asking questions. And you want us all to be quiet because quiet is how you survived."

"Tamsin—"

"No." She had stood there in the corridor, tall and straight-backed, with Veyra's jaw and Astrid's stubbornness and something else entirely that belonged to no one but herself. "You taught me to be invisible. Thank you. It saved my life. But I'm done being invisible, and one day I'm going to find every page of that ledger that exists, and I'm going to decide what happens to them. Not you. Me."

And then she had gone, into the corridor crowd, and Astrid had stood there feeling the strange double weight of having succeeded and failed at the same time.

They had written, since then. Careful letters, closing their loops. Tamsin had studied law in the south, then history, then archival law, which made Astrid laugh out loud alone in her kitchen when she first read it — the girl had gone and made a profession of documents, of paper, of the exact machinery that had once been aimed at her throat. Of course she had. Astrid should have seen it coming the day the child first learned to write her own name and wrote it forty times in a row, checking each one against the last.

But this letter was different from the others. This letter said *I found it.*

* * *

Tamsin arrived the next afternoon on the late coach, carrying a single leather satchel and wearing a grey coat that made Astrid's chest tighten — the same cut, nearly the same color, as the coat Astrid had worn on the Grey Coach all those years ago, the coat with the code-cylinder sewn into its lining. Whether Tamsin knew this, whether she had chosen it deliberately, Astrid couldn't tell. With Tamsin you never could. That was Veyra in her too: the general's habit of doing significant things without announcing them.

"You look well," Astrid said on the doorstep, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

"You look old," Tamsin said, and then, before Astrid could decide whether to be wounded, she put down the satchel and hugged her — hard, brief, complete, the way she had hugged her once at the far edge of a frozen river with soldiers' lanterns swinging behind them. "I missed you. Don't make it strange."

"I would never make anything strange," said Astrid, making it immediately strange, and Tamsin laughed, and the sound filled the kitchen the way it hadn't filled any room in eleven years.

They ate late. They talked about bees and about the trial records and about a man named Ostrick whom Tamsin had married and divorced with such efficiency that Astrid only learned of the marriage two years after it ended. And then the dishes were washed, and the lamp was trimmed, and Tamsin went to the satchel by the door and stood with her hand on the buckle for a moment, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet before a confession.

"Before I show you," Tamsin said, "you should understand how I found it. Because you'll want to know if anyone else can."

"All right."

"The original ledger — the one the clerk showed me at Mrs. Halloway's, the one he copied his report from — burned with the ministry archive when the Republic fell. Everyone assumed that was the end of it. But archives are like rivers. When you dam one, the water doesn't disappear. It goes somewhere else." She unbuckled the satchel slowly. "In the fifties, a provincial court in the east requested certified copies of certain asset registries for a property dispute. Nobody thought about what else was in the packet. The copies were filed, cross-indexed, and forgotten. Forgotten for fifty years, until a junior archivist — a woman named Pell, who is going to be famous someday — noticed that one registry had been recopied in a different hand than the rest, and started asking why."

"And?" Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "Why?"

"Because the clerk who reported us — Brenner, the nervous one from the coach — made his own copy before he handed the original up the chain. Insurance, probably. Men like him always keep insurance." Tamsin drew out a folder wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth, a bound sheaf of brittle paper, foxed with age, its edges soft as cloth. "This is Brenner's copy. Page for page. And page nine—" her voice did not waver; Astrid noted that, noted the discipline of it, and grieved that a child should ever have needed such discipline— "page nine is mine. *Asset 114. Tamsin Veyra, age six. To be recovered.* Recovered. As though I'd been mislaid. As though I were a coat left at a boarding house."

The lamp hissed. Outside, wind came up the hill from the harbor and pressed against the windows.

"There are forty-three names," Tamsin said. "Forty-three children. Eleven died during the war — Pell confirmed it from parish registers. Nine emigrated; three of those are alive and have agreed to testify. The rest—" she looked up, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady— "the rest are scattered, some don't know they're listed, some do and have built their lives around not knowing. And here is the question I have carried across four hundred miles to ask you."

"Ask it."

"A lawyer says the courts will seal it. A judge has already hinted as much — the state prefers its embarrassments unpublished. The archivist wants it preserved intact; historians will fight her for it; they'll call it evidence, they'll call it heritage, they'll put it behind glass and charge admission." Tamsin laid the ledger on the kitchen table, open, and turned back the cover with two fingers, gently, the way you touch a wound. "And I keep thinking about a woman who taught a six-year-old that paper is not fate. That a page can burn. That what's written about you is not the same as what's true of you."

Astrid looked at the page. Thirty-some years, and the clerk's neat hand had faded to brown, but the words were legible as the day they were inked. *Tamsin Veyra, age six. To be recovered.* Below it, a column of administrative notes, dates, initials. A whole life reduced to inventory. Somewhere a man had written these words over supper, breathing, ordinary, alive — and had gone on being alive for decades while the girl he inventoried grew into a woman standing over the list of herself.

"You want to burn it," Astrid said. It wasn't a question.

"I want to ask you first." Tamsin's hands flattened on the table on either side of the page. "Because you burned my page without asking, and you were right to, and you were wrong to, and I've spent half my life deciding which. So I'm asking. This copy belongs to the law, to the historians, to the archive, to everyone who says memory must be preserved even when memory is a knife. Or it belongs to us — to the forty-three, living and dead — and we get to say what it was. I know what the responsible answer is. I've given lectures on the responsible answer. I stood in a courtroom at nineteen and demanded to see this document because I believed it should exist and be faced." She stopped. Breathed. "But I've faced it. And facing it hasn't freed anyone. And I keep thinking: justice outside the law was the only justice anyone ever gave either of us. Maybe it's the only kind there is."

For a long moment Astrid said nothing. She thought about Mrs. Halloway, dead forty years now, who had survived by not seeing and had saved a child by choosing, once, finally, to see. She thought about the interrogating officer — Sorvad, his name had been — who had found a burned fragment of this same ledger in a grate and hesitated, because his niece's name was on another page, and whose delay had bought them the night that mattered. She thought about fire: the code-cylinder's papers curling in a stove, the first page of the ledger going up in a boarding-house grate while Tamsin slept upstairs, Veyra's second letter — *I chose you because you keep promises you shouldn't have to* — turning to ash in her palm on a frozen riverbank.

Every time, the fire had felt like theft. Every time, it had felt like the truest thing she'd ever done.

"When your mother gave you to me," Astrid said, "she told me to forget your face. That was the instruction. Forget the face, and the child survives, because a thing unrecorded cannot be hunted." She touched the edge of the brittle page, not reading it, just touching. "I disobeyed her within ten seconds. I have been looking at your face for thirty years. And here is what I learned, and it took me most of that time: the record is not the wound. The record is only where the wound was *written down*. Burning the writing doesn't heal the wound. But sometimes—" her voice caught, and she let it— "sometimes burning the writing tells the wound it's over."

Tamsin was crying now, silently, the way she had cried at six, in silence, trained out of sound before she could walk. Astrid reached across the table and took her hand.

"So yes," Astrid said. "Burn it. Burn it yourself. I burned your page once and it wasn't mine to burn. This one is yours. It was always going to be yours. That's why you found it — not the archivist, not the lawyers. You."

* * *

They went out to the yard behind the house, past the silent hives, to the iron fire-ring where Astrid burned brush in the spring. The night was clear and cold, early-winter cold, the kind that sharpened every star. Tamsin knelt and arranged the ledger in the ring — not crumpled, not thrown, but laid flat, respectfully, the way you lay out a body.

"Do you have matches?" she asked, and her voice was almost amused. "Thirty years ago you'd have had six kinds of fire on you. Flint, matches, a little lens for focusing sunlight—"

"I retired." Astrid crouched beside her anyway, and produced, from her coat pocket, a box of matches, because some habits do not retire. "One kind. That's enough."

Tamsin struck the match. The flame stood up straight in the still air, and for a heartbeat neither of them moved.

"For the eleven," Tamsin said. "Who didn't grow up."

She touched the flame to the corner of the first page. The old paper caught slowly, then all at once, the way paper does — reluctant and then eager, as if it too had waited a long time for permission. Fire walked across the columns of names. *To be recovered*, the clerk had written, and now the recovering was happening at last, in orange and black, in the only direction that had ever been right.

Tamsin fed each page in singly, watching each one go. She did not rush it. Astrid understood: this was not destruction. This was testimony, delivered to the oldest witness there is. Somewhere in the smoke were forty-three children, and a general stripped of rank waiting on a riverbank, and a landlady who chose not to see, and an officer whose doubt cracked the machine from inside, and a young courier who had sewed secrets into her coat lining because she believed she was furniture — and who had learned, one frozen mile at a time, that no one is furniture, that invisibility was always an illusion, that identity is not what history assigns you but what you refuse to surrender.

When the last page curled and went, Tamsin sat back on her heels and watched the sparks climb toward the stars.

"They'll ask what happened to it," she said after a while. "Pell. The historians. There'll be questions, maybe inquiries. I'll tell them the truth — that the document was destroyed by consent of the surviving parties. Let them argue about it for fifty years." She glanced sideways, and there, briefly, was the six-year-old again, the girl from the coal cellar with ash on her cheeks. "Do you regret it? Any of it? The codes, the mission, the border? You paid everything. You were a traitor to your own side for me, and nobody ever thanked you properly, and you never got your old life back."

Astrid considered the question honestly, because Tamsin deserved honesty more than comfort.

"I delivered nothing," she said. "I betrayed my commission, my government, and my trade. And the republic fell anyway, within the year, and the codes would have burned with everyone else's secrets. History didn't need my errand. It needed—" she gestured at the dying fire, at the woman kneeling beside it, at the whole improbable chain of choices that had run from a boarding-house door to this cold hilltop— "it needed this. Someone to refuse. That's all justice ever is, Tamsin. Someone refusing, at the exact moment refusal costs the most. Everything else is administration."

Tamsin reached over and took her hand, and they stayed like that until the fire burned down to a red heart and then to grey.

Then Tamsin stood, brushed the ash from her knees, and looked out past the dark shapes of the hives toward the distant lights of the harbor.

"There's one more thing," she said quietly. "Pell found something else in the archive. Another registry — different war, different regime, but the same handwriting on the cover, the same format. Children. Listed as assets. Still open, Astrid. Still being added to." She turned, and her face was lit from below by the last coals, and she was no longer the child from the coal cellar at all; she was the inheritor. "I start work with her commission next month. And I thought — I hoped — you might teach me the other things. Not the archives. The other things. How to be invisible when it's needed, and how to be seen when it matters, and how to carry what can't be carried openly." A pause. "How to keep promises you shouldn't have to."

Above them the stars were hard and ancient and indifferent, and below them the harbor lights burned, and Astrid Nystrom — who had once told herself she was furniture, who had spent a lifetime learning otherwise — felt the old cold ache in her hands and the warmer ache under it, the one that had never once healed and never once needed to.

"Well," she said. "We'd better start with the coat linings."
