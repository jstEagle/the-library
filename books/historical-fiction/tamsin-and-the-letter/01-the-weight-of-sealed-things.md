## 1. The Weight of Sealed Things

There is a particular pride that belongs to a man who carries other people's secrets without ever once being tempted to open them. I know it well. I had worn it for eleven years the way another man might wear a medal — polished, displayed, and entirely undeserved.

My name is Cyrus Yarrow, and in the winter of the Republic's last breath I was a courier of the second rank, which meant I carried sealed diplomatic correspondence and was expected to be neither curious nor clever. I was very good at both expectations.

The letter I carried that morning rode against my chest in a oilcloth wallet, and it was, I told myself for the hundredth time, beneath my notice. That is not quite accurate. Nothing marked with the Chancery's black-and-gold seal is beneath anyone's notice; it is merely above one's permission. There is a difference, and the difference keeps men like me employed and occasionally keeps us alive.

I boarded the *Vesperine* at Quayside Stair in Corvess with my collar up against a rain that could not decide whether to be rain or sleet, and behind me the city did what burning cities do, which is pretend very loudly that they are not burning. Smoke rose from the Ministry quarter in three gray columns, and the papers called them "controlled reductions of sensitive archives." I had delivered enough letters to know what a government means when it burns its own paperwork: it means the war is lost and the archivists have been conscripted.

But none of that was my business. My business was Marrowgate, six days by sea, one letter, one signature, one receipt. Neutrality, I liked to say, is not cowardice — it is craftsmanship.

The purser gave me a cabin barely wider than my shoulders and told me, with the cheerlessness of his profession, that we'd sail on the morning tide if the tide cooperated and the harbor master didn't hang anyone important enough to delay departures.

"And if he does?" I asked.

"Then we sail anyway," he said. "Hanging's quick. Paperwork isn't."

I spent the first evening in the cramped wardroom rehearsing my delivery speech. This is a courier's vanity, and I confess it freely: we compose little ceremonies for ourselves because the work itself is so anonymous. *In the name of the Provisional Chancery of the Republic of Vessany, I present this correspondence to the Honorable—* except I didn't yet know who was honorable in Marrowgate, which was rather the point of the seal. So I practiced the part I knew, the bow that is neither servile nor familiar, the presentation of the wallet, the recitation of the receipt-number. I practiced it against the porthole glass, where my reflection practiced it back, and we agreed it went beautifully.

A steward brought me tea that tasted of iron and tallow, and asked me if I'd seen much of the fighting.

"I've seen nothing," I said, "and I intend to keep it that way."

"Smart," he said, in the voice of a man who did not think it smart at all, and took the cup away.

That night the wind came up from the northeast like something owed money. By midnight the *Vesperine* was rolling in troughs deep enough to make a believer of anyone, and by dawn the captain had made the decision every captain dreads: we would put in at Sennet Hole, an unscheduled stop, a fishing anchorage with a breakwater shaped like a broken tooth, until the storm spent itself.

"Unscheduled" is a word that should frighten couriers more than it frightened me. I sat in the wardroom watching the gray water heave and told myself that delays were simply the sea's own bureaucracy, slower than the Chancery's but no less inevitable.

*

We made Sennet Hole near dusk, riding a swell that lifted the whole ship and set her down again like a hand testing fruit. From the deck I could see the town: a scatter of slate roofs hunched under the headland, one church spire leaning as if eavesdropping on the sea, and below it, the long stone pier where lamps were already lit.

There were too many people on the pier.

That was my first thought — the small, stupid arithmetic of a man who has never been displaced from anything. A pier built for forty fishermen held what looked like four hundred souls, and more were coming down the coast road in a straggling line, carts and bundles and children carried on hips, their shapes jerking oddly in the lamplight because of the wind.

"Refugees from Vell," said the boatswain beside me. He said it the way you'd report weather. "Been coming through for a fortnight. Vell fell Tuesday."

Vell had not fallen. Vell had been *reduced*, according to the bulletins — its garrison honorably withdrawn, its civilian administration transferred. I had carried two letters concerning Vell myself and had read neither, and I remember feeling a flicker of something at the word *Tuesday*, because Tuesday I had been in Corvess complaining about the tea, and while I complained a city had ended, and I had filed my receipts and gone to bed.

"They'll want passage," the boatswain said. "Captain won't take them. Mail contract."

"Of course," I said. "Mail contract." And I heard my own voice do the thing it always did — slide smoothly over the fact, the way wax slides over paper, sealing it shut.

We lowered a boat for fresh water and bread, and I went ashore because eleven years of discipline had curdled into restlessness and I wanted, absurdly, to walk on ground that wasn't moving. The wind hit me at the gangway like a wall with opinions.

The pier smelled of fish, tar, wet wool, and woodsmoke — and under all of it, faintly, the smell I would come to know too well in the weeks after: the smell of people who have burned their own furniture to stay warm. A woman was selling strips of dried herring off a plank laid across two barrels, and children stood around her not begging, just standing, the way children do when begging has stopped working. An old man sat against the bollard nearest the lamp with a carpet bag on his knees and his hands folded on top of it, patient as luggage. He wore a clerk's coat, good cloth gone green at the cuffs, and he watched the *Vesperine* with an expression I recognized because I had seen it in mirrors: the expression of a man waiting for a ship that will not stop for him.

"You from the mail packet?" he asked when I passed. His accent was inland, educated, Vellish.

"I am."

"Is she going on to Marrowgate?"

"After the storm."

He nodded slowly, and I saw him doing sums — how many days, how much food left in the carpet bag, how many coins under the lining. Then he said, carefully, politely, the way one asks about the weather: "Do you know if there's any word? Of the eastern provinces. Whether the amnesty lists are posted."

I didn't know what amnesty lists were. I said so.

"No," he said, almost gently. "You wouldn't. You carry the letters. You don't read them." He smiled at me, and there was no cruelty in it, only a tiredness so complete it had gone translucent. "That must be a comfortable trade, while it lasts."

I gave him some coin — not generosity, I told myself on the walk back, just ballast, just keeping my hands busy — and returned aboard with the bread and water and the clerk's smile stuck somewhere under my ribs where it wouldn't come loose.

*

That night I took out the wallet and held it. Not to open it — I want that understood, though understanding came late — but because it was warm against my chest all day and cold now, and I wanted to feel that it was real. Black wax, gold thread through the seal, the Chancery's triple tower pressed deep. Heavy. Heavier than paper should be.

I lay in my narrow bunk listening to the storm knock at the hull and rehearsed my speech one more time, and my reflection in the dark porthole mouthed it along with me, and for the first time in eleven years I heard how it sounded: a man reciting neutrality to himself in an empty room while the world outside filled up with people who had run out of rooms entirely.

*Beneath my notice.* I turned the phrase over like a coin and found the edge worn smooth with handling.

The wind dropped sometime before midnight. The sea went flat and silent in the way that sailors say is worse than any gale, and the *Vesperine* sat becalmed a mile off the coast with her sails slack, her lanterns lit, and her cargo of sealed things sleeping soundly.

I woke once, briefly, to a sound I took for rigging creaking. It was not rigging.
