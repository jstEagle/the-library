## 2. What the Water Gave Back

The sea went still at dusk, and I have distrusted quiet ever since.

A becalmed ship is a strange thing. The *Vesperine* lay on water flat as poured pewter, sails slack, the whole vessel creaking in that idle, conversational way ships have when they have nothing better to do than remind you of their age. Captain Ossler chewed his beard and blamed the weather on everything from the moon to the republic's tariffs, which I took as my cue to retire to my berth with the mail satchel chained to my wrist and my dignity intact.

I should explain something about myself: I am a courier of the Third Rank, which means I carry letters I am not permitted to read, to recipients I am not permitted to know, for reasons I am not permitted to wonder about. Some men find this maddening. I found it restful. Ignorance, properly maintained, is a kind of armor. The war had been going badly for three years, but it was going badly at a comfortable distance — in dispatches, in rumors, in the faces of men who had been closer to it than I. My part was to walk through it untouched, like a man crossing a ballroom during someone else's quarrel.

The letter rode against my ribs in its oilskin sleeve. Republic seal, black wax, the kind that meant someone important had been awake late writing it. I rehearsed my delivery speech out loud, because I am vain in small private ways and there was no one aboard to mock me. *"By authority of the Provisional Secretariat, I present—" No. "I bear a communication of the highest urgency—"* Better. Marrowgate's harbor office would want gravity. Gravity was free; I carried plenty.

Then the ship went silent in a way ships shouldn't, and I learned what the captain already knew: the calm wasn't weather. It was an escort.

They came up the hull without lanterns, six or seven of them, and the first I knew of it was a deckhand's scream cut short in the middle — not stopped, *removed*, like a word struck from a sentence by a careful editor. Then boots on the deck above, and Ossler shouting the only prayer merchants know: that cargo mattered more than blood.

I did the cowardly thing, which in hindsight was also the wise one: I stayed below with the lamp doused and listened. There were thumps, negotiations conducted in the language of threats, the particular wet sounds I will not describe because you can imagine them well enough and I'd rather not join you in the imagining. Someone kicked open the strongbox. Someone else laughed at how little was in it.

And then footsteps came down toward the berths.

I pressed into the corner with the satchel under my coat, and a pirate — young, scarred at the jaw, holding a lantern he clearly considered optional — swung the light across me and said, "Courier."

"Mail service," I agreed, hands visible, voice admirably steady considering. "You've no quarrel with the mail."

"We've no quarrel with anything that isn't worth stealing." He held out his hand. Behind him, two more filled the doorway with the patience of men who had heard every speech before.

Here is where I must confess to a failure of nerve so complete it borders on philosophy. I thought about arguing. I thought about the oath I'd sworn, the little brass badge of the Third Rank, the speech I'd rehearsed. And then I thought about the wet sounds upstairs, and I handed over the satchel like a man paying a toll.

The young one slit the oilskin with a knife, broke the strongbox of the satchel, and pulled out the letter. He turned it over once, reading the black seal, and something crossed his face — recognition, or fear, hard to say which at lantern distance. He weighed it in his palm the way you'd weigh a purse.

"Worth more than the ship," he murmured, mostly to himself. Then he looked at me, and whatever mercy lived in him was clearly on a budget. "Sealed orders go to the bottom. Nothing personal."

He snapped it in half.

I want to tell you I leapt to defend the secrets of the Provisional Secretariat. What actually happened is that I made a sound — some undignified noise between protest and grief — and lunged, and caught the halves as they came apart, because the wax had split along the fold rather than shattered, and paper is faster than principle.

The pirates laughed. One of them patted my head, which I resented then and resent still. They took the silver from Ossler's cabin and left us rocking on a dead sea with two dead crewmen, and I sat on the floor of a berth that smelled of tar and vomit, holding a letter I was now legally obligated to destroy and personally incapable of putting down.

Because the break in the wax had opened the fold just enough.

*By order of the Provisional Secretariat of Vessany:* the hand was cramped, urgent, the handwriting of a man writing past midnight. *The household of ALDERY, Marrowgate, is convicted of treason against the Republic. Let all officers and informants take note: Maren Aldery, matriarch; Ilsa Aldery, child of the line. Execution upon identification. Reward for confirmation of identity: four hundred crowns.*

Four hundred crowns. I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt like illness, that they had priced my family the same as a decent horse.

Aldery. My mother's name, the one she never used after she left — fled, I was told; the stories disagreed and everyone telling them had been drinking. I had grown up Yarrow, my father's name, my father's silence, a boy taught not to ask about the woman who'd gone back east to people we did not speak of. I had assumed, in the vague way children assume, that those people simply didn't exist, the way the far side of the moon doesn't exist.

Now the republic had issued their death warrant, and I was the courier.

I read it eleven times. I know because I counted, the way a man counts when counting is the only thing keeping him upright. The letter named a grandmother I had never met and a cousin who might be eight years old or eighteen; it offered coin for their heads; and it was addressed to the garrison commander at Marrowgate, whose office stood four streets from wherever they were hiding, if they were hiding, if they were alive.

Somewhere out on that flat black water, a gull cried once and gave up.

I am proud of my neutrality. That has been the joke of my life, hasn't it — a man proud of standing in no one's fire, who discovers at forty-one that the fire was always his own house. I sat in the dark with half a letter in each hand and understood that I had never been neutral at all. I had merely been uninformed.

In the morning, the wind returned, as winds do, indifferent to what they carry. I gave the captain the story we agreed on — pirates took everything, seals and all — and he looked at me a moment too long before nodding, because captains know which lies keep a voyage insured. The *Vesperine* turned her nose toward Marrowgate.

I spent the passage deciding nothing, which is itself a decision, and arrived in harbor with the halves of the letter sewn into my coat lining and a delivery speech I would never give.

* * *

Maren Aldery counted her grandson's arrival before she ever saw him.

She had developed, over two years of hiding, the habits of a woman twice her age and half her size: ears tuned to the rhythm of boots on cobbles, a memory for uniforms, a gift for vanishing kitchens. She knew the sweep pattern of the harbor patrol the way other women knew psalms. She knew which neighbors could be trusted with bread and which could be trusted with nothing, and that these were almost entirely the same list, ending quickly.

So when the knock came that evening — three soft raps, pause, two more, the smuggler Brannoc's signal, updated that very afternoon — she put her hand over Ilsa's mouth before the girl could answer it, and felt her heart do something old and stubborn against her ribs.

Through the shutter crack: a courier's coat, travel-worn. A face.

Her daughter's chin. Her father's brow. Fifty years folded up and handed back to her across a doorstep, wearing the republic's colors.

"Well?" whispered Ilsa, eight years old and furious at being muzzled.

Maren didn't answer. She was looking at the young man's eyes, at the way they kept moving — checking the street, checking the windows, checking himself — a hunted animal's eyes in a face that had her mother's bones. Couriers came to doors all the time in Marrowgate now, and most of them were exactly what they appeared.

But this one hesitated. And in the hesitation, his hand drifted to his chest, to his coat lining, in the manner of a man guarding something he hadn't decided to surrender.

Maren Aldery had buried a husband, a son, and a republic, in that order of grief if not of date. She had promised herself she would hope for nothing further, as hope was expensive and she was nearly out of coin.

She opened the door anyway.

"Cyrus," she said — not asking, because grandmothers do not guess.

The courier went white as sailcloth. And behind him, at the end of the lane, a patrol lantern swung its light slowly toward the house.
