## 3. A House of Borrowed Time

I delivered the letter.

I want that on the record, in whatever ledger the gods keep for fools and couriers. I walked into the Chancery Annex at Marrowgate at half past nine in the morning, salt still crusted in my collar from the pirate night, and I handed a sealed — well, *resealed*, if we're being honest, with candle wax and a coin for pressure and a prayer I didn't know I knew how to say — envelope to a clerk with ink-stained fingers and dead eyes. He logged it. He stamped it. He did not look up.

"Name?" he asked.

"Cyrus Yarrow," I said, and my voice held, which surprised me more than anything else that had happened in the past three days.

"Courier's mark?"

I showed him. He copied it into his book, and somewhere in those columns of ink my name became a small, permanent thing: proof that Cyrus Yarrow had done his duty, delivered the death warrant, and gone about his business.

The clerk filed the letter in a tray marked *PRIORITY — INTERNAL SECURITY* and returned to whatever he'd been doing before, which appeared to be nothing.

That was it. That was the whole ceremony. Eleven years of service, two shipwrecks' worth of luck, one act of piracy, and my great career came down to a stamp thumping against wet paper while a man behind me sneezed.

I walked out into the gray light of Marrowgate feeling like a man who has paid off a debt and discovered the creditor is still following him down the street.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about doing the right thing by the rules: it doesn't wash off.

I spent the afternoon in a dockside tavern called the Drowned Bell, drinking thin wine and rehearsing arguments with myself. *You're a courier. You carry things. What's inside them was never your business.* That was the speech I'd given myself a hundred times on the deck of the Vesperine, and it had always worked, because I'd never actually known what was inside. Ignorance had been my neutrality. Now I knew, and knowledge turned out to be a stain you couldn't scrub with wine or logic.

*Aldery.* My mother's name. The name she never spoke, the family she fled or was taken from — she died when I was four, and my grandmother Maren raised me on stories with all the names sanded out. A woman who told me fairy tales with the villains removed and thought I wouldn't notice the shape of the holes.

And now the republic wanted them all dead. Not arrested. The word in the letter — I could still see it, the clerk's neat bureaucratic hand — was *extirpated*. Households. Whole households. As if treason were something that grew in the walls and had to be burned out with the house.

By dusk I had stopped arguing with myself, which is how I knew I'd lost.

* * *

Finding the Alderys took less skill than I expected, which frightened me. If I could find them in an evening, so could the men whose job it was.

The address in the letter was false, but the letter also named a fishmonger's stall on Cordwainer Row — a *contact point*, in the regime's language — and I stood across the street from it for an hour watching a broad-shouldered woman gut mackerel and pretend very hard not to notice me. Eventually she got tired of the game, wiped her hands, and said, "Either buy something or get hanged somewhere else."

"I'm looking for the Widow Corvane," I said, using the false name I'd pried out of the letter's margins.

"And what do you want with her?"

"My name is Yarrow," I said. "Cyrus Yarrow. My mother was Aldery."

The knife stopped moving. It started again. "There's no such name anymore," she said, but she jerked her chin toward the alley behind the stall, and I understood I was being invited to disappear.

* * *

The house was behind the tanneries, which meant it smelled like a secret ought to smell: sharp enough to keep casual noses away. Two rooms above a cooper's shop, low beams, one window painted over. And there, in the doorway between the rooms, a small old woman with white hair pinned up like she'd dressed for a formal occasion the world had failed to attend.

Maren Aldery looked at me for a long time.

"You have her chin," she said finally. "Your mother's. I used to tell her it would get her in trouble." Her voice didn't shake. Her hands did. She folded them into her apron to stop it. "You're late. I've been expecting a courier since autumn."

"You knew someone would come?"

"I knew the letters would catch up eventually. They always do." She stepped aside. "Come in before the street remembers your face. And you — " this to a girl of perhaps ten who was peering around the doorframe with her grandmother's exact eyes — "Ilsa, this is your cousin. Say hello and then go back to your sums."

Ilsa said hello the way children say hello to strangers who might be furniture. But she looked at me a beat too long, cataloguing me, the way you look at weather coming in.

* * *

They were living as the Corvanes, tanners' kin, quiet people who owed no one and were owed by no one. Maren sewed. Ilsa studied — real sums, real letters, smuggled books under the floorboard. It might have been a life, if lives weren't made of names.

I stayed the night because the tide was out and the streets belonged to patrols. I told myself that too. The tide. Convenient thing, the tide; it excuses everything.

Near midnight, boots in the street below.

Not passing boots. *Stopping* boots. The particular rhythm of a sweep — doors tested along the row, the cooper's first, then working toward us. Maren was already awake, already moving, blowing out the lamp without a wasted motion, and I learned in that moment where I got my habit of acting first and feeling after.

"The floor," she whispered. "Under the east boards. Take Ilsa."

"There's room for—"

"*Take Ilsa.*"

I took Ilsa. She went down into the dark without a sound, which told me she'd done it before. I followed, pulling the board over us, and lay in blackness smelling of sawdust and lamp oil with the child's breath quick against my neck while boots crossed the room above and a fist hammered the door.

"Security inspection. Open."

Maren opened it herself, unhurried, an old widow roused from bed, indignant in the way of respectable women everywhere. Papers. Questions. The soldier's lantern swinging slow across the room. Through a knothole I watched the light crawl toward the floorboards, toward the corner where I had left —

Where I had left, on the table, in plain sight, my courier's satchel.

The soldier's hand went out for it.

And Maren said, loudly, in the voice of a woman whose patience has limits, "That's mine, young man, and if you've finished frightening old women I'd like to sleep." She stepped directly between him and the bag, small and immovable as a doorpost. He hesitated. Somewhere down the row another door opened, another household being bled slowly. He took her papers, glanced at them, handed them back.

"Corvane," he read. "Tanners' kin."

"Tanners' kin," she agreed.

He left. The boots moved on. I lay in the dark with my heart going like a drum in a parade and understood that I had just hidden behind an eighty-year-old woman, and that she had done it without breaking stride, and that she had been doing it for years.

* * *

We didn't sleep after that. Maren lit the lamp again and put water on to heat, because that's what her generation did with terror — made tea over its corpse.

"They'll come back," she said. "Sweeps repeat. Third visit they take the whole house. That's how it works now. You don't get arrested, you get *erased*." She said it like a recipe. "So. Courier. Tell me what you brought them."

I told her. All of it. The seal splitting, the names, the bounty, the word *extirpated*, and then the worst part, the part I'd carried up the stairs with me like a stone in my boot: that I had resealed it and delivered it anyway.

She listened without interrupting. When I finished she poured the tea and said, "Good."

"*Good?*"

"Duty kept you alive long enough to find us. Sentiment would have drowned you at sea." She pushed the cup into my hands. "Now the question is what you do with the part of you that isn't duty. Because I can see it sitting there. It's been sitting there all evening, chewing on you."

"It's not that simple. There's a copy in the archive. Burning my copy changes nothing."

"No," she agreed. "But burning it changes *you*, and I'm given to understand you're the only family I've got in the room." She nodded at the hearth. "Half of it. The half with our names. Keep the rest — the routing marks, the officer's signature. Proof cuts both ways someday."

So I fed the page to the fire, the one with the Alderys listed like vermin, and watched my mother's name curl and go bright and become nothing, and felt — I had expected grief, or shame. What I felt was lighter. Lighter and more dangerous.

* * *

At dawn I went looking for passage, and found it in the person of Dree Halloway, a smuggler with a face like a closed fist and a brig called the *Kindly Tern* loading at the coal quay under paperwork that would not survive close reading.

"Two berths, morning tide," I said. "Quiet passengers."

"Two berths is four hundred marks and a lie I'll need to practice."

I put three hundred on the barrel between us. "And the rest in silence about who asks after you tomorrow."

Halloway counted it twice, then looked at me with something that wasn't quite pity. "One berth," she said. "Cargo manifest changed overnight. Happens. One berth, and I sail whether you're aboard or not."

"One berth."

"One berth." She spat over the side, professionally. "Choose fast, courier. Tide doesn't argue."

I walked back through the waking streets, past shuttered stalls and the first gray light coming up off the water, doing arithmetic I already knew the answer to. One berth. A child who carries the name forward, or the woman who gave me every name I have.

When I reached the tannery road, I stopped.

Soldiers at both ends of the street. Lanterns still lit against the dawn. A sergeant checking doors against a list, working toward the cooper's shop, and above the painted window, a thin line of lamplight where Maren sat up waiting, because of course she was awake. Of course she knew.

I had maybe five minutes, one berth, and the rest of my life to decide what it was for.

I ran.
