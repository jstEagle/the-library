## 4. One Berth Before the Tide

There is a particular cruelty in a dawn that arrives on schedule. All night the harbor quarter had held its breath, lamps burning low, shutters drawn against a darkness that everyone knew was temporary. And then the sky went grey at the appointed hour, indifferent as a clerk, and the boots began.

I heard them before I saw them — not marching, worse than marching: walking. A cordon moves at an unhurried pace because it does not need to hurry. It has numbers, and numbers do not tire. From the upstairs window of the Aldery house — the Voss house, as the street knew it, borrowed names over borrowed blood — I watched soldiers take the corners of the square like men setting a table. Two at the fishmonger's. Two at the well. One idling by the customs shed with the studied boredom of a man who has been told to be seen.

"They're early," I said, which was a stupid thing to say, since they were precisely on time and we were the ones who had run out of hours.

Behind me, Maren Aldery was braiding her granddaughter's hair. Her hands did not shake. Mine had been shaking since midnight, when Tam Verrin the smuggler had pressed two fingers to his cap and delivered the arithmetic of our ruin: *One berth. The *Kestrel* rides high with cargo, and the harbor watch will count heads at the mole. One, courier, or none.*

One berth. Three of us who could use it — though Maren had made it plain from the first hour that she was never going anywhere, in the tone other people reserve for declaring they will not eat shellfish. That left me and a girl of eleven with her grandmother's stubbornness and her mother's eyes I had never seen except in Maren's face, like a letter written in a hand I almost recognized.

* * *

The negotiation had happened downstairs, by candle stub, in the voice people use when the walls have ears. Ilsa had wanted to give up her place. Of course she had. Children are natural martyrs; they haven't yet learned that dying nobly is easier than living inconveniently, and someone has to carry the family forward whether the family approves or not.

"I'm small," she'd argued. "I can hide in the cargo hold. You're big and useless and you know how to talk to officials."

"Flattery noted," I'd said, "and rejected."

"You're the coward," she'd said, with the directness of children and the accuracy too, and Maren had said, "Enough," in a voice that had once, presumably, governed households larger than this one, and the argument was over before it had properly begun.

So now I knelt on the floor of a stranger's bedroom and lied to a child, which is a skill I had always prided myself on lacking.

"The tide turns in forty minutes," I said. "You'll go down to the mole with Tam's man, wearing this—" I handed her a fisherman's oilskin that smelled of tar and herring, "—and you'll not look back, and you'll not cry, because crying draws the eye. In Brannock Harbor there's a woman named Sef who owes your grandmother more than she'll admit. You say the word *kestrel* and she'll keep you until it's safe. Do you understand?"

"No," said Ilsa. "Why won't you come?"

Because the ship takes one, and one was never going to be me. Because someone must stand in the doorway when they break it, so that what they find looks like surrender instead of escape. Because your grandmother will not leave and I will not leave her, and if I pretend otherwise you'll see through it, because you are eleven and terrible.

What I said was: "Someone has to finish burning the letter."

* * *

The letter. Half of it, rather. The night before, kneeling at the hearth with Maren watching, I had torn the diplomatic dispatch down its crease and fed half to the fire — the half bearing the seal, the signatures, the order for the execution of the household of Aldery. It had gone up in a bloom of orange light that seemed far too festive for what it was. What remained I had kept: the pages naming the family, but also the pages proving their innocence — trade ledgers, correspondence, the dry bones of a case that only looked like treason to men who needed it to. Proof, I'd told myself, for a future court. For history, which is a slow clerk but a fair one, eventually.

Now I folded those pages into a packet no bigger than a prayer book and wrapped it in oilcloth, and I put it into Ilsa's hands along with the braid Maren had finished.

"This is yours," I said. "Not to read. Not to show anyone, ever, unless the day comes when showing it saves lives. Hide it where even you can't find it easily. When you're grown, if the world has improved — and it will, I refuse to believe otherwise out of sheer professional stubbornness — you'll know what to do."

"What is it?"

"The truth," said Maren from the doorway, "which weighs less than it should and costs more than it ought. Kiss your grandfather's ghost goodbye, child, and mind the gangplank."

Ilsa kissed Maren. Then, to my considerable surprise, she kissed me — a fierce, brief collision, the kind of kiss given by people who have decided grief is a waste of the remaining minutes.

"When this is over," she said, "you'll find me. That's an order. Grandmother says you're bad at orders, so practice."

And then she was gone down the stairs, a herring-smelling ghost in an oilskin too big for her, and I stood at the window and watched a small figure cross the square through the gaps between soldiers who were looking for fugitives, not fishwives' children, and slip down the mole into the grey morning, and I did not breathe again until the *Kestrel*'s sails caught the offshore wind and the harbor took her, and then I breathed, and it hurt, and it was the best pain I have ever felt.

* * *

They came at the seventh bell, which showed a certain respect for convention. Maren and I sat in the front room like a pair of portraits, tea going cold between us, because if you are going to be arrested you may as well be arrested with dignity and lukewarm tea.

Maren had refused to run for the last time at moonset. "I am seventy-one years old, Cyrus," she'd said. "I have buried a husband, a son, a daughter-in-law, and every illusion I ever owned. I will not die running through fish guts so that a lieutenant can write *fugitives shot resisting* in his report. They will find me in my chair, in my own house, under my own name — the real one, finally — and let them choke on it." She'd paused, and her voice had softened into something I hadn't earned yet. "Besides. Someone should stay with you. Arrests are terrible alone. You forget which jokes are funny."

The door broke inward on the second blow. Four soldiers, then an officer — Captain Ellery Vance, though I didn't know his name yet, only his face, which I had seen two days before at the customs shed, watching a refugee family with an expression that had not matched his uniform. He had looked then like a man reading his own orders and disliking the author.

"Cyrus Yarrow," he read from a paper he clearly did not need. "Courier, third class. Charged with theft of state correspondence."

"Theft," I said. "That's generous. Usually I'm charged with nothing at all. Couriers are furniture, Captain. No one arrests a chair."

"The chair burned half a diplomatic dispatch," said Vance. "We found the ashes."

Ah. So they had searched the hearth while we sat drinking our tea. My stomach performed a slow roll. The intact half was a mile out to sea inside a child's oilskin, and the burnt half was ash in a grate, and both facts hung in the cold air of the front room waiting to see which way the morning would fall.

Vance crouched at the hearth. He lifted a pinch of ash between finger and thumb, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I had done: paper burns to nothing, but wax burns differently, and the seal had been crimson and thick, and somewhere in that grey there might be a fleck that a diligent man could call evidence of anything he liked.

He could call it proof that I had destroyed orders. He could call it proof that I had received them. He could dig in that grate until he found enough to hang the whole story on, and no one in the Republic of Vessany, in its final winter, would ever ask him why.

Maren's hand found mine across the cold tea. Her grip said: whatever happens, we are still Alderys, and that word means what we decide it means.

Vance let the ash fall back into the grate. He brushed his fingers on his coat, and when he straightened, something had gone out of his face — or rather, something had gone behind it, filed away in the place where men keep the things they cannot afford to believe yet.

"Ashes," he said, to his sergeant, in a voice pitched just loud enough. "Nothing identifiable. Courier claims the wind scattered the draft; the old woman confirms it." He looked at me then, one long moment, and I understood that he was not sparing us. He was enlisting us — quietly, without paperwork, in the only rebellion available to a man still wearing the uniform of a state he no longer served. "Take them in. Gently. They'll want statements."

* * *

They marched us through the square as the sun finally cleared the harbor mouth, gilding the water, the masts, the empty space on the horizon where a ship with one passenger was making for open sea. Maren walked like a queen reviewing troops she intended to dismiss. I walked like a courier carrying nothing, which was, at last, the truth.

At the corner of the customs shed, Vance fell into step beside me, close enough that his words reached only me.

"The wind," he said, "scattered the draft."

"It did," I said.

"And there's nothing left to find."

"Nothing," I agreed, "that anyone alive knows how to look for."

He nodded once, and glanced at the sea, and I understood that somewhere in Brannock Harbor, years from now, a woman named Sef would receive a visitor, or a letter, or a rumor carried on the tide — and that the world improves the way tides turn: slowly, invisibly, and all at once.

They put us in a cell with a window, which I chose to regard as a professional courtesy. Maren sat beside me and rested her head on my shoulder, seventy-one years old and lighter than I had ever known her.

"Well, Cyrus," she said. "Tell me a joke. We've got time, apparently."

And because the tide was turning, and because somewhere out past the harbor bar a girl with her grandmother's stubbornness was carrying the future in an oilskin bundle, I found — to my own considerable surprise — that I had one.
