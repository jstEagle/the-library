## 1. The Last Plate

The cold in the workshop had a texture to it, Tamsin thought. Not the blunt cold of the street, which shoved and howled and was done with you, but a patient cold, one that had settled into the stone floor and the iron press and the very grain of the boxwood over the months since Master Hollis had died, and meant to stay. It was the cold of a room that had stopped being lived in and had begun, instead, to be kept.

She had been keeping it since October. Keeping the fire small, keeping the tools oiled, keeping the shutters latched against the wind that came down the river valley from the north and pressed its face against the windows like a beggar. And now, on the last night of January 1847, with the lamp turned up high and her breath ghosting silver above the plate, she was keeping the last promise too.

The Marlow Bend County Atlas. Forty-one plates, thirty-nine finished under Hollis's hand, and the fortieth — the great folding map of the river town itself, the one every purchaser would open first and study longest — left on his bench with only the coastline of it done: the river's western bank traced in his fine, sure line for six inches before the burin had been set down forever.

Tamsin had not touched the plate for eleven days after the funeral. She had told herself it was respect. It was fear, though she would not have used that word. The plate lay there under its cloth like a body, and every time she lifted the cloth to look, she heard — or thought she heard, which amounted to the same thing in that silent room — the small wet sound of his breathing at the end, the rattle that the doctor had called *the chest filling* and that Tamsin had known even then was something else entirely: the sound of a man being unmade, stitch by stitch, out of the world.

But tonight the fear had finally lost its argument with duty, and she had swept the shavings from the bench, laid the plate on the leather cushion, and taken up the burin he had held last.

It fit her hand differently than it had fit his. That was the first thing. His grip had been low and loose, an old man's economy, the tool moving more than the hand; hers was higher, tighter, a young woman's strength spent where his had been conserved. For the first inch of line she felt like a thief wearing his coat. Then the line went true, and the coat fit, and she stopped thinking about it at all.

Engraving is not drawing. Drawing forgives. A drawn line can be smudged, erased, argued with, redrawn. An engraved line is a wound in the wood that you have decided to keep. Every stroke commits. The burin goes into the boxwood and takes away material that no power on earth can put back, and what remains — the raised line that will take the ink, that will press itself onto damp paper, onto a hundred copies, five hundred copies, each one carrying that decision forward into years Tamsin would never see — what remains is not a picture. It is a verdict.

*Maps record the world,* Hollis had told her, her first week in the workshop, nine years ago, when she was fourteen and all elbows and hunger and grief herself, though she hadn't known the word for that either. He had set a magnifier in her palm and closed her fingers around it. *They don't make anything. They don't change anything. A map is a mirror, girl, and mirrors tell no lies. Remember that when your hands shake.*

She had remembered it. She had built nine years of her life on it, plank by plank, the way a house is built on a foundation nobody looks at. The world happened, and the map recorded it, and between those two facts stood the engraver — necessary, skilled, and neutral as glass. There was comfort in that neutrality. Comfort, and shelter, and a kind of cleanliness. Whatever else in Marlow Bend was crooked — and the town had its crookednesses, every river town did, debts and grudges and the slow silt of old favors — the atlas would be true. The atlas would be the thing in Marlow Bend that could not lie.

She cut the line of the west bank now, working outward from where Hollis had stopped, matching his stroke by eye and then by touch, until her line and his were one line and no living person could say where the dead man's work ended and hers began. The lamp threw her shadow huge against the whitewashed wall, arms enormous, head small, some giant bent over a county.

Outside, the river ice boomed. A long, deep report, like cannon fired under the water, followed by a cracking whisper that ran the length of the valley. The January thaw had come early this year — three weeks early, the ferry-women said, and they should know — and the ice was talking to itself in the dark, shifting its weight, remembering that it was water.

Tamsin did not look up. Later, much later, she would remember that she had not looked up, and wonder at it.

* * *

She worked through the town first, because the town was the heart of the plate and her hands knew it best. The grid of Front Street and Mill Lane, the courthouse square with its four corners marked by the founders' elms, planted, so the story went, the same spring the town was surveyed. St. Brannoc's church and churchyard. The tannery row. The bridge, drawn with the care Hollis had always given bridges — he had loved them, had called bridges *the only honest architecture, because they admit what they're for.*

Then the outlying holdings, parcel by parcel, and here her pace slowed, because the parcels required the deeds, and the deeds required the ledger, and the ledger sat in the drawer of Hollis's desk where he had kept it for twenty years, and opening that drawer was still — eleven weeks on — like opening a door into a room where someone had just been.

The brass handle was cold enough to bite. Inside: the deed abstracts in their tin box, the surveyor's field notes tied in bundles with string gone soft and grey, the stub pencils, the tin of tobacco he had sworn off in '39 and never actually surrendered. And beneath the tin box, flat, wrapped in oilcloth, the provenance notes — the record of who had held what land before whom, going back past the founding families to the first surveys, the spine of the whole atlas, the thing that made a collection of pretty pictures into a legal document.

Tamsin took the notes back to the bench and worked with them open beside her, copying lot numbers, checking boundaries, her burin resting between strokes. This was the part of engraving nobody saw and everybody trusted: the hour upon hour of verification, the cross-reading of documents, the quiet assumption that paper agreed with paper, that the world kept its own accounts honestly and the mapmaker merely transcribed them.

The laborers' row came next in the sequence.

Her pen stopped above the note.

There was no laborers' row.

Not in the notes. Not in the field-book bundle for the east bank survey. Not in the index of the forty finished plates. She turned back through the provenance pages, thinking she must have missed it — the row of cottages that had stood below the mill race on the eastern shore, the long low houses where the mill workers and their families had lived, smoke-stained, close-set, loud with children in summer. She had walked past it a thousand times. Everyone in Marlow Bend had walked past it. It had been *there.*

And the atlas — the atlas she had helped build for nine years, plate by plate — showed nothing on that stretch of bank but willows and a disused landing, annotated in Hollis's own hand: *unimproved ground.*

She sat with that for a long moment, the lamp hissing, the ice booming faintly out on the black water. Then she shook her head at herself, almost smiled, and reached for the older surveys to check the discrepancy. Of course there was an explanation. Grounds changed names. Rows were torn down, tenants moved on, parcels consolidated. Paper disagreed with paper all the time; that was why the cross-reading existed.

Except that when she pulled the earliest survey — the founding survey, the one done the spring the elms were planted — that stretch of the east bank was blank too. Blank from the first. As if the row had never been built at all. As if the people who had lived there, worked the mill, paid their rents, christened their children at St. Brannoc's, had stepped off the face of the earth without disturbing so much as an ink-line.

*Unimproved ground.* In his handwriting. In the hand she knew better than her own, the hand she had just spent four hours imitating stroke by stroke so perfectly that the difference was invisible.

Tamsin looked at the plate on the cushion, half-finished, the town glowing under the lamp in its web of committed lines, and felt for the first time in nine years the small, cold, precise sensation of a foundation shifting underfoot — not collapsing, nothing so dramatic, just settling, the way ice settles, the way a thing announces quietly in the night that it has begun to move.

She wrote *unimproved ground* into the plate anyway. What else was there to write? The documents said it. All of them said it, unanimously, which should have been reassuring and instead felt, tonight, for the first time, like a choir singing a little too perfectly in tune.

She engraved the east bank. Willows. Landing. Nothing.

* * *

Dawn found the plate finished. She cleaned it, inked a proof on the hand-press — the good proofing paper, the last full sheet in the drawer — and pulled the print while the windows were still grey, and hung it to dry on the line by the stove where proofs had hung for twenty years.

Forty plates. The atlas complete. Somewhere upstairs in the cold loft, crated in straw, waited the copper of the title page and the bound dummies awaiting the printer's sheets. By spring, by the thaw, by the first boats upriver, the Marlow Bend County Atlas would be in the shops in the capital, in the hands of purchasers who would never know the name of the woman who had finished it — purchasers who would open the great folding map and see a town without tragedy, tidy and surveyed and agreed upon from its first elm to its last lot line, every fact resting on every other fact like stones in a wall.

Hollis had wanted that. She believed it the way she believed the river ran north — absolutely, without examining it. He had worked on the atlas for nineteen years. He had refused two offers from the capital cartographers because, he said, *a county ought to be mapped by someone who drinks its water.* He had bled into this book — she had watched him cough blood onto a blotter in November and wipe it away and go on cutting, saying the plate wouldn't wait and neither would he. Finishing it was not work. It was a debt. The last duty owed to the dead, and she had paid it, alone, in the cold, and now she could grieve properly, having earned her grief like wages.

She stood before the drying proof a long time. Her hands ached. There was a cut on her thumb she didn't remember getting, welling slowly, and she pressed it absently against her apron and left a small rust-colored mark there, and on the hem, and thought nothing of it.

The light came up over the eastern hills and caught the river, and the river was wrong.

She saw it plainly, standing right there at the window with the proof hanging behind her: the ice was breaking up three weeks early, moving in great grinding pans toward the sea, and the channel was already open enough in the middle that the dark water showed through like a vein. The ferry would run soon. Boats would come. Everything upstream of this moment was about to arrive in Marlow Bend at once — the buyers, the historians, the capital men with their capital opinions — and the atlas would meet them finished, sealed, agreed.

Tamsin turned back to the workshop, to the forty plates lying in their tissue, to the ledger still open on the bench where she had left it, its pages fanned, its ribbon marker fallen out and lying on the floor like something dropped in haste.

She knelt and picked up the ribbon. And beneath where the drawer's false bottom had shifted — shifted, she would think later, when she moved the ledger, or perhaps it had been shifting a long time, waiting — she saw the corner of an envelope. Sealed. Yellowed. In her master's hand, cramped and hurried, unlike any hand he ever used on a plate:

*To be opened only by the eleventh child.*

The workshop was very quiet. Out on the river, the ice groaned like something waking.

Tamsin Ashgrove stood holding a letter addressed to someone who did not exist, and understood that the atlas was finished, and that nothing — nothing — was finished at all.
