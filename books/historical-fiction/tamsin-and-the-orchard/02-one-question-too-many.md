## 2. One Question Too Many

The knock came at half past nine on a Tuesday in late January, when the frost had grown so thick on the workshop windows that the daylight came through gray as dishwater. Tamsin heard it through the door of the engraving room, where she sat hunched over the final plate with her graver poised, and for one foolish heartbeat she did not answer at all — because to lift the tool from the copper was to break the line she'd been carrying in her mind since dawn, a river bend that had taken three days to get right.

Then the knock came again, patient and official, the kind of knock that would not be argued with.

She set the graver down in its groove of wax, wiped her fingers on her apron out of habit rather than necessity, and went through the cold passage to the front of Master Hollis's house, where the shop sign still hung — *HOLLIS & ASHGROVE, ENGRAVERS AND SURVEYORS* — though there was only an Ashgrove behind it now, and only just.

The man on the step was not what she expected. Census men, in her experience, were either clerical ghosts who wanted nothing but your name spelled correctly or officious little tyrants who counted your chickens through the window. This one was neither young nor old, perhaps five-and-thirty, with a face like an unremarkable ledger page — fair hair going thin at the temples, a coat brushed clean but worn shiny at the cuffs, and eyes the color of rainwater that looked at her with an attention she found immediately uncomfortable, the way one feels watched by a portrait.

"Miss Ashgrove?" He held up a leather folio stamped with the county seal. "Elias Vane. Enumerator, district four. I'm verifying households for the crown's return." He said it without flourish, as though reciting something he'd said four hundred times and meant perhaps forty of those times. "May I come in? It's bitter enough out here to crack brass."

"It is my master's house," Tamsin said, stepping aside before she had quite decided to. "Was," she corrected, and hated how the correction sounded. "I keep it now."

"So I understand." He crossed the threshold, ducking slightly under the lintel even though he had inches to spare, and stood in the passage warming his hands without being invited to do anything else. His gaze went up the stairs, along the row of survey instruments mounted like hunting trophies, and stopped — just for a moment — on the small framed map above the coat pegs: Marlow Bend as it had been drawn in 1811, when Hollis was an apprentice himself. Then it moved on, and she could not have said why she noticed the stopping at all.

"The schedule will take ten minutes if you're cooperative," Vane said. "Twenty if you're precise. I'm told you're both."

"Who told you that?"

"The postmaster. The ironmonger. Half the town, when I asked after you." He opened his folio against the wall and produced a pencil, which surprised her; she had expected a pen, ink, the ceremony of record. "Names first. Who sleeps under this roof?"

"I do."

"Alone?"

"Alone."

He wrote it. She watched the pencil move — quick, unlovely strokes, no art in them at all — and felt the strange vertigo she always felt when her life became someone else's handwriting. *Tamsin Ashgrove*, reduced to a mark on a government page. It occurred to her, not for the first time, that this was what maps did too, what all paper did: it took the living world and pressed it flat, and whatever didn't survive the pressing simply ceased to be carried forward. She had never thought of this as a violence. Paper was neutral. Paper forgot nothing and invented nothing; it only held what was given to it, faithfully, like water holds a boat.

That belief had been drilled into her by a dead man, and she wore it like the apron she hadn't needed to wipe.

"Occupation?"

"Engraver and surveyor. I've completed the county atlas begun by Josiah Hollis. It goes to the binder in March."

Something shifted in Vane's face — not much, a millimeter of movement around the mouth — and he wrote again. "The atlas. That's been long expected."

"Long delayed," Tamsin said. "Master Hollis was ill two years. Illness does not consult publication schedules."

"No. It rarely does." He glanced toward the back of the passage, toward the workshop door, from beneath which came the faint mineral smell of acid and wax that lived in the whole house now, soaked into the plaster. "Might I see it? The workshop. I like to note outbuildings and their uses. It helps the agricultural returns."

"There are no agricultural returns here."

"There are always returns, Miss Ashgrove. The question is whether anyone writes them down." He smiled when he said it, a dry, apologetic smile, and she understood it was meant as wit and not warning, and understood also that she could not tell the difference, and disliked him a little for that.

* * *

The workshop was warm relative to the rest of the house because the stove had been fed at six, but Vane walked it the way a man walks a church — slowly, hands clasped behind him, reading. And that was the word for it, Tamsin realized with growing discomfort: he was *reading* the room. The presses. The racks of finished plates wrapped in flannel. The great table where the atlas lay open under weights, showing the parish of Marlow Bend rendered in her best hand, every field boundary and ferry crossing and mill race true to the last chain surveyed.

"This is fine work," he said. "Better than fine." He bent close to the plate without touching it. "This bend in the river — most surveyors would have generalized it. You haven't."

"Generalizing is lying with extra steps," Tamsin said. "My master taught me that."

"A man of principle."

"The most principled man I ever knew." She heard her own voice harden and did not know why, except that something in Vane's mildness made her want to defend Hollis preemptively, against an accusation that had not yet been made.

Vane straightened and consulted his folio. "Now. The schedules list this property as a dwelling and workshop, held by Josiah Hollis, deceased last November. Is that correct?"

"Correct."

"And you hold it how? Lease? Deed?"

"Lease. From the Corven estate. Seven years, four remaining." She said it easily, because it was simple and true and required nothing of her.

"Mm." He turned a page. "And the orchard cottage?"

Tamsin's hand, which had been resting on the edge of the great table, went still.

The orchard cottage stood at the far end of the Ashgrove land, past the rows of bare apple trees, a low stone building with a slate roof and a garden gone wild at its edges. It had been empty as long as she could remember — longer than her memory ran, anyway. Hollis had kept it locked. When she was small she'd believed a widow lived there, then that no one lived there, then that it was simply part of the land, the way the well was part of the land, requiring no explanation. She used it now for storing apples in autumn and, in truth, for very little else. It appeared in her mind the way familiar things do: entirely, and without any history attached.

"It's on the same parcel," she said. "Same lease. It's disused. I store fruit there."

Vane nodded and wrote something. Then he did not write something else, and the silence stretched just long enough to become deliberate.

"You'll want its details for the schedule," Tamsin prompted, because silence, in her experience, was a thing you filled the way you filled a misprint.

"Details are already noted," Vane said mildly. "Stone-built, two rooms, disused, on the Hollis lease. What interests me isn't the schedule." He looked up at her, and those rain-colored eyes were not mild at all. "It's provenance. Who owned the cottage before the Ashgroves took their lease? Before Hollis, I mean. The estate papers show the parcel changing hands in '32, but they show it oddly — the earlier conveyance is referenced but not copied. There should be names attached to the ground under your feet, Miss Ashgrove. There usually are."

"There were laborers' cottages along the river once, I suppose. Before the flood." She said it lightly, tossing it out the way one tosses out a fact learned and unexamined, a coin spent without counting it. "Everyone knows the '31 flood took the riverside properties. The founders rebuilt the town on higher ground. That's the whole story of Marlow Bend — flood, fire, rebuilding. It's in the parish histories."

"It is in the parish histories," Vane agreed. "All of them. In the same words, nearly." He tapped his pencil once against the folio, a small dry sound like a beetle against a window. "Which is itself interesting, if one reads closely. But never mind that. The cottage specifically. Who held it before '32?"

"I don't know."

"Did you ask Hollis?"

The question landed somewhere below her ribs, in the place where grief keeps its accounts. *Did you ask Hollis.* How many questions had she not asked him, in eleven years, because the answers seemed guaranteed to wait? A man dying slowly leaves his questions scattered around him like tools he means to pick up tomorrow. She had stepped over them for years.

"He was a busy man," she said. "A dying man, these last two years. I didn't trouble him with antiquities."

"No," Vane said softly. "I imagine you didn't."

He wrote again, and Tamsin watched the pencil and felt the first wrongness arrive — not a thought yet, not a suspicion, just a physical unease, the sensation of standing on ice and hearing, far off and faint, the sound of the river moving underneath. Vane wrote for a long time. Longer than the question deserved. She could not see what he put on the page, and the not-seeing bothered her more than seeing would have.

"Is there a difficulty?" she asked.

"No difficulty." He closed the folio. Then, as though the closing had loosened something in him, he added, almost conversationally: "It's only that I've done this work nine years, Miss Ashgrove. Towns, villages, hamlets. And towns are honest in aggregate even when they lie individually. Ask ten people who owned a field and you'll get eight answers and two shrugs, and the truth sits somewhere among them like a stone in a sieve. But some questions—" he paused, choosing, she thought, with unusual care— "some questions make people go quiet in a particular way. Not thinking-quiet. Deciding-quiet. They decide what they're permitted to remember." He met her eyes. "You went deciding-quiet just now."

"I went remembering-quiet," Tamsin said, with a chill in her voice she did not altogether control. "There's a difference, Mr. Vane, though I don't expect a census man to know it."

"No," he said. "Probably not." He tucked the pencil into the folio's spine and inclined his head, courteous to the point of mockery or humility — again she could not tell which, and suspected he engineered it so. "Thank you for your time. One household, one occupant, one disused cottage of unclear provenance. The crown thanks you too, though the crown has poor manners and never says so."

He saw himself to the passage. At the door he paused with his hand on the latch and looked back — not at her, but past her, down the corridor toward the workshop, toward the atlas under its weights, and then further, as if his gaze could travel the frozen garden and the black rows of apple trees to the orchard cottage at its far end, locked, silent, full of stored apples and stored years.

"March, you said," he remarked. "When the atlas goes to the binder."

"The first week."

"Before the thaw, then." Something crossed his face — she would think about that something for days afterward and fail to name it; relief, or regret, or the expression of a man setting down a weight he'd intended to carry further. "Good day, Miss Ashgrove. Mind the ice on your steps. It's treacherous this year especially. Things that look solid from the kitchen window rarely are, once you're standing on them."

The door shut. His footsteps crunched away down the lane, unhurried, and faded.

* * *

She went back to the plate. That was the discipline of her life: the work waits for no feeling, and feeling, unworked, ferments into something worse. She picked up the graver, found her line where she had left it, and pushed the tool into the copper, and the burr curled up silver and bright, exactly as it had ten thousand times before.

But the line wavered. Not visibly — anyone else looking at the plate would have seen nothing but mastery — but she felt it waver, in her wrist, in the attention behind it, the way a singer hears a false note no audience can catch. The river bend under her graver was the same river bend she had drawn a hundred times. The river bend was true. The river bend had been surveyed by Hollis in 1838 with a chain and a theodolite and recorded in his own hand, and paper did not invent, paper did not forget, paper held what was given to it.

*Who owned the cottage before the Ashgroves.*

She cut the next curve and thought: it doesn't matter. Some farmer. Some family that moved on, the way families do, west or south or into the ground, and their deeds passed to the estate and the estate leased to Hollis, and Hollis to me, and the chain of custody is a chain of indifference, and there is no stone in this sieve.

*Deciding-quiet,* Vane had called it. She had decided nothing. She had merely failed to know.

She cut another inch and stopped, because she caught herself doing something she had never done at the table: listening. Listening past the tick of the cooling stove, past the wind worrying the apple trees, past her own breath. Listening for footsteps that had already faded, as though the house might tell her what the man in the rain-colored eyes had declined to.

Then she did a thing that shamed her even as she did it, a small thing, a coward's thing. She took a scrap of proof paper, and on it, in a disguised hand — a clerk's hand, not her own — she wrote the words *orchard cottage, prior holding unknown,* and burned it in the stove, and watched the curl of flame take it, and felt, absurdly, safer.

Paper held what was given to it. She had just given paper a lie by omission, and burned the evidence, and called the burning prudence.

* * *

The afternoon light was failing blue-gray across the snow when she finally gave up the pretense of work and pulled on her cloak and boots and walked out through the orchard. She told herself it was to check the stored fruit against the frost, which was even true, in the way that many lies are built around a true beam.

The cottage crouched at the end of its own long shadow. Up close it was only a building: stone walls furred with lichen, a slate roof missing two courses at the north slope, a door whose lock she had oiled herself each autumn without knowing why she bothered. The garden was a wilderness of last year's stalks under snow. An old pear tree leaned over the east gable, older than any tree in the orchard proper, planted by someone with a different idea of the place than any she possessed.

She stood in the doorway with her key in the lock and did not turn it.

Who owned the cottage before the Ashgroves. It was nothing. It was a question about a deed, the dullest species of question, the kind that got answered in solicitors' offices by men who charged by the hour. It was not worth the heat leaving her body in this wind.

And yet.

Eleven years in this house, and Hollis had never once mentioned the cottage. Eleven years of maps, and she could not recall — she tried now, walking the memory backward through the atlas plates, the drafts, the fair copies, the surveys — she could not recall the cottage appearing on any map she had ever drawn or traced or studied of this parish. Buildings appeared on maps. Disused mills appeared. Ruined chapels appeared. Even collapsed wells appeared, with the notation *dry.* A stone cottage two rooms strong, standing intact inside a leased parcel, absent from every plate of a master cartographer who generalized nothing—

*Generalizing is lying with extra steps.*

The wind moved through the bare trees with a sound like paper turning, thousands of pages turning at once, and Tamsin Ashgrove stood in the snow with a key in a lock and understood, in the way one understands a fever coming on — gradually, then all at once — that she had lied that morning. Not by omission. By commission. When Vane had asked who held the ground before, some voice older than her memory had risen in her throat and answered *laborers' cottages, before the flood,* smooth as oil, and she had believed in that moment that she knew it, and she did not know it. She had never known it. No one had ever told her the history of her own garden.

The paper was not neutral. The paper had never been neutral. Somewhere between the world and the map, in the space where the graver meets the copper, someone chose what to give the paper — and she was beginning to suspect, with the orchard wind rising and the light dying and the cottage waiting behind its oiled lock, that the choice had been made long before she ever picked up a tool.

She turned the key. The lock gave its familiar click, and the door swung inward onto darkness that smelled of apples and dust and, beneath both, faintly, of old smoke —

and she stood on the threshold, unwilling, for reasons she could not name, to strike a light.
