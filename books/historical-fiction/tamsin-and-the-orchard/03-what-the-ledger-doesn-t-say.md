## 3. What the Ledger Doesn't Say

The snow came down in the particular sideways way of Marlow Bend winters, flung against the workshop windows as though the river itself were trying to get inside. Tamsin Ashgrove let it rattle the glass and kept her eyes on the ledger.

Master Hollis's desk had three drawers that locked and one that did not. In life he had kept the unlocked drawer full of nothing — pencil stubs, a tin of licorice drops, a broken magnifying lens wrapped in flannel. She had opened it a hundred times looking for erasers, for twine, for the small brass protractor with the worn thumb-screw. It had never once occurred to her to look *beneath* the nothing.

But Elias Vane's pen had hovered over his census page like a hawk over water, and hawks do not hover unless they see something below.

*Who owned the cottage before the Ashgroves?*

She had lied. The lie still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, cold and smooth and wrong. Twenty-six years of telling the truth about distances and boundaries and bearings — because a map that lies kills people, Master Hollis used to say, drowned them on sandbars and marched them off cliffs — and she had spent her first falsehood on a question about a cottage she had lived in since before she could remember.

She did not know who owned it before the Ashgroves. That was the thing. She had never thought to ask, any more than she had thought to ask why an orphan girl was raised in a cottage at the edge of an orchard by a childless mapmaker instead of in the parish house with the other foundlings, or apprenticed out at twelve like every other fatherless child in Marlow Bend. Some questions are so large they stand in front of you your whole life, and you mistake them for scenery.

She pulled the drawer out entirely, set it on her knees, and tipped it into the lamplight.

Pencils rolled. Licorice drops scattered, gray with age. And there, wedged flat against the bottom of the drawer where a false seam ran — a seam she had never noticed because the drawer's lining paper was identical front to back — lay a thin packet of papers tied with the green waxed thread Hollis used for bundling survey notes.

Her hands went still around the drawer's edge. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind worried the shutters of the orchard cottage, half a field away, where she would sleep tonight in the bed she had slept in all her life.

*Provenance notes.* That was what she had told herself she was looking for when she sat down at his desk an hour ago. The atlas needed its final plate finished by spring, and the county histories demanded citations — deeds, grants, dates of incorporation. Hollis had been fastidious about provenance. Every boundary line in his maps could be traced to a document, and every document to a date. It was the discipline she loved most in him: the belief that the world, properly recorded, could not be argued with.

So she told herself it was discipline that made her fingers pull the green thread loose. Discipline, and not the hovering pen of Elias Vane.

The packet held perhaps thirty sheets. Survey fragments, mostly — contour sketches of the bend in the river, calculations in Hollis's upright hand. Then a folded newspaper clipping, brittle as autumn leaf. Then two things that stopped her breath.

The first was a page of vellum, cut cleanly from a bound register. She knew the format instantly; she had transcribed a hundred such pages for Hollis over the years. Parish registers were printed in ruled columns — date, name, parents, officiant — and this fragment carried the top margin and the first ruled line and then nothing, because someone had taken a razor to the rest. The cut was expert. It followed the gutter so precisely that only a close examination of the stitching holes along the left edge betrayed that anything had been removed at all. Whoever cut it had wanted the removal invisible.

At the top of the surviving strip, in the register's printed header, she could read the year.

**1831.**

And beneath it, the first entry, whole and untouched:

*Baptisms solemnized in the Parish of Marlow Bend in the Year of Our Lord 1831.*

*January the ninth. Child of —*

And there the line ended, because the razor had fallen exactly there, severing the record at the word *of*. Not one baptism. All of them. Whatever names had followed — however many children of whatever parents had been christened in the first weeks of 1831 — they existed now only as a clean absence, a white stripe of missing vellum running down the page like a scar.

Tamsin set the fragment down on the blotter very carefully, the way one sets down something that might go off.

People did not cut pages from parish registers. Registers were the spine of the law. A man might forge a deed, might bribe a clerk, but the parish book was sacrosanct — Hollis had drilled that into her alongside geometry. *You can argue with a memory,* he said. *You cannot argue with ink.* And yet here was ink argued with, amputated, vanished. Here was proof that somebody in Marlow Bend believed there were words in the parish book worth destroying the book itself to be rid of.

January 1831. The winter she had been born — if the story she'd been given was true. *Your mother died of the fever, child, and your father before her, and old Hollis took pity on a friendless babe.* That was the story. She had never asked for details, because orphans learn early which questions make kind faces go careful.

The second thing was a letter.

It was sealed. That alone made her heart behave strangely — a slow, heavy percussion, once per beat too many. Nobody sealed letters to Tamsin Ashgrove. Nobody wrote letters to her at all, save the paper merchant in Reading and twice, absurdly, a map collector in Boston. This letter was written in Hollis's hand — she knew his hand better than her own, had spent ten thousand hours beside it, had learned to read his intentions through the pressure of his nib before he spoke them aloud. The address was short. Five words.

*To the eleventh child.*

Not her name. Not *Tamsin*, not *Miss Ashgrove*, not even *my apprentice*, which is how he had referred to her in the two formal documents of her life — her indenture papers and his will, both of which named her heir to the workshop and its contents. *To the eleventh child.*

Eleven. The number sat oddly. Marlow Bend had no tradition of eleven of anything. Eleven bells, eleven fields, eleven children — the figure meant nothing, and precisely because it meant nothing it felt loaded, deliberate, a key cut for a lock she hadn't known her house contained.

She turned the letter over. The wax seal was unbroken, deep red, stamped with Hollis's mark — the crossed dividers he used on every plate he ever engraved. He had sealed it and addressed it and put it in a false-bottomed drawer behind pencil stubs and licorice, which was to say he had hidden it from everyone including, perhaps, himself.

He had died in November without a word of it. Four months ago. She had held his hand while the coughing took him apart, had fetched the doctor, had sat through the long last night listening to the sound of a life running down like a clock nobody wound anymore — and he had never said *there is a letter*, never said *look in the drawer*, never said *eleventh*. His last coherent words had been about the atlas. *Finish the north plate. The hatching on the flood plain, mind the weight of it.* Duty to the work, to the end.

Or — she thought now, with a sensation like the floor tilting a degree — his last coherent words had been about exactly this, and she simply didn't yet know how to read them.

*Flood plain.* The hatching on the flood plain. Why would her mind hand her that phrase now, of all phrases? Because it was January, and the letter was dated — no, the letter wasn't dated, but the register fragment was. January 1831. The winter of the big ice, the old men called it, the winter the river froze bank to bank and stayed frozen till March. She had heard it mentioned the way one mentions weather from another decade — a curiosity, a hard winter, nothing more. There had been no flood in the winter of '31. A frozen river doesn't flood.

Doesn't it?

Hollis had taught her hydrology the way he taught everything, from evidence. Ice dams, he said, were rivers holding their breath. When a river froze in great slabs that jammed at a narrows, the water behind the jam kept rising, silent, under the ice, until — he had shown her engravings of the Alps, of dammed valleys bursting in spring — until the breath came out all at once.

She looked at the register fragment again. *Baptisms, January 1831.* And the razor falling just below the first line, taking every name after it.

Children. Baptized children, plural, whose names someone had needed gone. Not one child. A column of them.

Tamsin became aware that she was standing. She had no memory of standing. The lamp threw her shadow huge across the shelves of plates, and somewhere in that wall of copper and steel hung the county atlas — nearly finished now, forty-one plates, the work of nine years, the monument she had sworn over Hollis's grave to complete. Two of those plates covered the river bend. Plate nineteen, the lower bend, drawn from surveys of 1838. Plate twenty-two, the orchard district, surveyed 1841. Both of them showed the land as it was when Hollis walked it with his chain and his theodolite.

Both of them showed the orchard cottage standing alone in empty ground.

Hadn't it always been alone? She tried to picture the ground between the cottage and the mill road as it must have looked in 1831 — seven years before Hollis's survey, seventeen before tonight. Orchard on the rise, river flats below. Her mind produced a picture and the picture was empty, and the emptiness bothered her the way a misprinted coordinate bothers a cartographer: not visibly wrong, but *wrong*, the way silence in a house is wrong when you're used to footsteps.

Because cottages don't stand alone. Cottages cluster. Laborers' rows, ferry lanes, mill streets — poor folk build poor houses cheek by jowl near the water and the work, because poor legs are tired legs. An isolated cottage on good flat land near a mill was a peculiarity. Peculiarities have histories.

She sat back down. She made herself sit, actually — pressed her palms to the desk and lowered herself into Hollis's chair, which still smelled faintly of his tobacco and the camphor he rubbed into his chest each winter. Around her the workshop held its breath: the gravers racked in their roll, the acid bath lidded and sullen, the great press under its sheet, the plates gleaming dully in their slots like a congregation of grey faces.

"Talk to me," she said quietly, to the room, to the dead man in it. "You hid this from me for fifteen years and then you died and left me the key. Talk to me."

The room offered her the lamp flame and the wind and nothing else. Dead men talk in documents or not at all.

So she searched.

She told herself it was provenance. The fiction lasted maybe twenty minutes. She went through the locked drawers with the key from his watch fob — the key she had worn on her own neck since the funeral, because leaving it in a drawer had felt like burying him twice. Deed abstracts. Correspondence with the Ordnance office. Invoices for copper and nitric acid, itemized in his tidy hand down to the farthing. Nothing about 1831. Nothing about baptisms. Nothing about children.

Then she went to the shelf above the acid bath, where he kept his working notebooks — forty years of them, cloth-bound, numbered in Roman numerals on the spines. Volume XXXI covered 1830 to 1833. She had consulted it before, years ago, for a note on the ferry crossing. Tonight she opened it to January 1831 and found the pages — she counted them twice, ran her thumbnail along the gutter — trimmed. Not razored, as the register had been; these pages were cut shorter than their neighbors, sliced flush with the binding so that the loss looked almost natural, the way old books wear. But the sewing holes were there. Six leaves gone. Six days of a mapmaker's daily record, removed from the middle of the hardest winter in living memory.

A man cuts one page in passion. A man cuts six leaves in deliberation, and stitches the wound closed, and lives with it for sixteen years.

Tamsin pressed the notebook flat under her hands and stared at the wall, at nothing, at the shape of a thing assembling itself in the dark of her understanding. Someone had come to Hollis — or Hollis had gone to someone — and together they had decided that six days of January 1831 must not exist. And Hollis, who believed ink was sacred, who taught her that the whole moral architecture of mapmaking stood on the inviolability of the record — Hollis had done the cutting himself. She was sure of it. The trim was too skilled, the deception too craftsmanlike. Only a man who had spent his life making things exact could make an absence this exact.

And then he had kept the register page. Kept it, sealed a letter to *the eleventh child*, and hidden the lot in the drawer where he kept his smallest, most personal things — the licorice he sucked while thinking, the lens he used for fine work. Hidden them among the tools of seeing.

Why keep the evidence of a destruction you performed? Unless you meant someday to undo it. Unless the cutting had been done under compulsion — threat, debt, loyalty — and some part of him had refused, quietly, across sixteen years, to let the destruction be total. A man can be forced to burn a bridge and still pocket one plank.

*Eleventh.* She said the word aloud and it sounded strange in the empty workshop, syllables hanging in the cold air like breath. Eleventh child. Of what count? Whose count? If the register page held baptisms — say, a season of baptisms, say a dozen, say eleven plus one — then the eleventh child was simply the last name on a list. The last of a row. The last of—

Of the laborers' row.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and it fit so perfectly she went cold from the scalp down. The laborers' row: the phrase surfaced from nowhere, from some overheard scrap of old Brida Ferris's ferry-talk, from a song the fishwives sang, from the sediment of a childhood she had assumed was ordinary. There had been a row of cottages on the river flats below the orchard. She was almost sure of it. Almost sure the way you are sure of a dream an hour after waking — certain of the feeling, unable to produce a single detail.

An isolated cottage stands alone only after its neighbors are gone.

She looked at the letter again. Red wax, crossed dividers, five words in a dead man's hand. Her name wasn't on it. *The eleventh child* wasn't necessarily her at all — the reasoning part of her, the part Hollis had trained, laid that out coolly. The letter could belong to anyone. It could be a formality, an apology, a bequest to some other family entirely. Fifteen years in this workshop and she had never once seen him seal a letter; he distrusted ceremony. For him to seal one, address it so strangely, and hide it — this was either a message of enormous importance or the private ritual of a guilty man, and she could not tell which possibility frightened her more.

There was a knife in the tool rack for trimming paper edges — a slim bone-handled blade, honed monthly, kept sharp enough to split a hair. She had used it a thousand times. She took it down now and held it, and the blade trembled slightly in the lamplight, or her hand did; at this distance the difference hardly mattered.

To break a seal is a small violence. She had been taught that too, in her way — that the sealed thing has a dignity, that opening it uninvited is a trespass no apology fully repairs. And this seal was not uninvited, exactly. The letter was addressed to someone. Perhaps to her. Perhaps to whoever found it, which after tonight was her. But the address frightened her worse than anonymity would have, because *the eleventh child* presumed a list, and lists presume order, and order presumes that someone, at some point, had counted children — counted them the way you count stock, or losses.

Losses. The word landed and stayed.

Outside, the snow had thickened; the windows had gone blind white, and the workshop felt like the inside of a closed fist. The stove was low. She should feed it, sleep, take the letter home to the cottage and open it by her own fire in daylight, like a sensible woman, like a woman who had lied to a census taker and wanted only to get back to her engraving and her grief and her orderly, mapped life.

She fed the stove instead. She put another log on and watched the flames climb, and she took up the bone-handled knife, and she fitted its edge under the lip of red wax the way she would fit a graver to a plate — finding the line, honoring the material, letting the tool do what it was made for.

"One question," she said to Hollis, to the dark, to herself. "That's all I want. One answer, and I'll finish your atlas and never ask again."

The wax parted with a soft crack, loud as a shot in the silent room.

Inside: four pages, closely written, the script smaller and more cramped than any hand of his she knew, as though the writing itself had been squeezed through a narrow place. The salutation stopped her heart mid-beat, because it was not *Dear child* or *If you are reading this* — it began without greeting at all, straight into confession, the first words already halfway to damning:

*If you have cut this seal, then I am past explaining myself, and you deserve better than the story I told you.*

Below the salutation, the second line, and the third — and Tamsin Ashgrove, standing alone in her dead master's workshop with the snow sealing the windows and the river muttering under its ice a quarter mile away, read the first sentence entire, and then sat down slowly on the floor, the letter shaking in her hands, because the first sentence contained a word she had heard only once in her life, spoken by a dying woman years ago, and a number, and the number was eleven:

*I took you in the spring of '31, the last of eleven, because I helped make the other ten disappear.*
