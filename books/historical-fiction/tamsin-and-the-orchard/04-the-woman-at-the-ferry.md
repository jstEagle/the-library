## 4. The Woman at the Ferry

The river did not freeze so much as it pretended to.

That was the first thing Tamsin learned about the Marlow in winter, in the years after Hollis took her in and set her to grinding ink before she could reach the second shelf. Ice formed along the margins in white lace, thickened over the slack water by the willows, and then — where the current ran strongest, out past the pilings of the old mill — it thinned to a grey skin that shivered when the wind crossed it. A map showed the river as a single confident line. The river itself was a hundred opinions about whether it was water or stone.

She thought of this as she stood on the north landing on the last morning of January, her breath pluming, the letter from Hollis's desk folded against her breastbone like something stolen. Which, she supposed, it was. She had taken it from his drawer without leave, though he was nine months dead and could not object, and though the direction on it — *the eleventh child* — had nothing to do with her. Nothing to do with her. She made herself think the words plainly, the way one checks a bearing twice.

The ferry was not running. Of course it was not running; the ferry never ran in deep cold, because Brida Ferris ran it alone and even Brida Ferris had limits. But smoke rose from the ferry house on the far bank, a thin grey thread against the white sky, and where there was smoke there was a stove, and where there was a stove there was a woman who had lived on this river for sixty years and knew every lie it told.

Tamsin pulled her collar up and knocked on the ferry house door until her knuckles ached.

"Door's not locked." The voice inside was gravel poured slow. "If you've come about the license, tell Corven I'll pay him when the river pays me."

"I haven't come about the license."

"Then you're either lost or trouble." A pause, and the creak of a chair. "Come in, then. Trouble keeps a body warmer than being lost."

* * *

The interior of the ferry house was small and dense as a ship's cabin: a stove black as a beet, a cot, a table scarred with ring-shaped burns from sixty years of mugs, nets hung from the rafters like shed skins, and everywhere — everywhere — the smell of tar and woodsmoke and fish. Brida Ferris sat with her feet propped on an upturned crate, a woman built like a mooring post, grey hair cut blunt at the jaw, hands so mapped with scars and rope-burns that Tamsin's professional eye kept trying to read them. She held a clay pipe, unlit, turning it over and over in her fingers as if counting the years by its bowl.

"You're Hollis's girl," Brida said. It was not a question. "The engraver's apprentice. I've seen you cross twice last summer, always looking down at your little book, drawing my river like it owed you money."

"Tamsin Ashgrove." She hesitated over the name. It felt strange in her mouth this morning, like a coat that might belong to someone else. "Master Hollis is dead. I'm finishing the atlas."

"I heard he died. River hears everything; that's why I live on it." Brida gestured at the stool across the table. "Sit. You didn't row yourself across half-frozen water at dawn to tell me about an atlas."

"No." Tamsin sat. The stool wobbled; she steadied it with one hand and felt foolish. She had rehearsed this crossing a dozen times in her head, standing at her window above the workshop, watching the ice. In every rehearsal she began calmly: *Madam, I am verifying provenance notes for the county atlas, and certain records regarding the laborers' row—*

But now, sitting opposite eyes the colour of river-water over slate, she found the calm would not come. So she did what she had learned to do in fifteen years at a master's elbow: she worked from evidence.

She drew from her satchel the razored stub of page from the parish register, wrapped in oilcloth, and laid it on the table between them. Then the census slip with Elias Vane's hovering question mark. Then, last, the sealed letter, and watched Brida Ferris go very still.

Stillness, Tamsin thought, looked different on different people. On Hollis it had been a held breath, a pen stopped mid-stroke. On Merrit Corven, two nights ago across the dinner table, it had been a blade sheathed slowly. On Brida Ferris it was the stillness of the river under ice: everything still moving underneath, only frozen at the surface.

"Where'd you get that," Brida said quietly. Not the letter — the register page. Her pipe had stopped turning.

"It was already cut out when I found it. Someone razored eleven entries from the baptismal register, 1831, and left nothing but the stubs. Master Hollis kept the stubs in his drawer with a letter addressed—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a letter. It says *the eleventh child*, and I don't know who that is. And yesterday a census taker asked me who owned our cottage before the Ashgroves, and when I said no one, he wrote something in his book that I don't believe was *no one*. And I have spent three days going through every paper in that workshop"—her voice climbed before she mastered it—"and there are gaps. Gaps in deeds, gaps in registers, gaps in the plates themselves, places where names were burnished off the copper like they'd never been struck. And everyone I ask looks at me the way you're looking at me now."

"And how's that?"

"As if I'm holding something that isn't mine to hold."

Brida leaned forward and tapped the register stub once with a cracked fingernail. "You're right. It isn't yours." She sat back. "Yet."

* * *

The stove ticked. Out on the river, wind worried the ice, and somewhere beneath it the current spoke in its low continuous syllables. Tamsin waited, because waiting was a tool too; Hollis had taught her that a plate tells you when it's ready for acid, and people were no different, only slower.

Brida lit her pipe at last, cupping the match against a draught that wasn't there, and smoked for a long moment before speaking.

"How old are you, girl?"

"Nineteen. Twenty in the autumn."

"Nineteen." She said it the way a surveyor reads a depth sounding — measuring, not commenting. "And Hollis took you in when?"

"I was four. He told me my parents drowned in the flood of '31 and left no kin, and he was a friend of — of someone. He never said who. There was a paper. I signed nothing; I was four. But there was a paper."

"There was a paper." Brida exhaled smoke toward the rafters. "There's always a paper. That's the whole trick of this town, do you know that? Marlow Bend runs on paper the way my ferry runs on rope. Paper makes a man own land he never walked. Paper makes a child nobody's. Paper can drown a whole street of houses so thoroughly that fifty years on, folk walk past the empty ground and feel nothing but a draft." She turned the pipe over again. "You say you're making a book of maps."

"The county atlas. Every parcel, every boundary, every road and bridge and mill. Master Hollis worked on it nineteen years. I mean to finish it by spring."

"Every parcel," Brida repeated softly. "Then you'll want to know what belongs where. And who owned what before who. That's honest work. Dangerous work, in this particular town, but honest." She fixed Tamsin with those slate-water eyes. "So here's what I'll give you, free, since you froze your fingers knocking on my door. Ask about the winter of '31, girl. Ask it proper — of the church, of the deeds office, of old men in the taproom, anyone fool enough to answer. Ask what happened between the first hard frost and the thaw. Then decide if you want the answer."

"The winter of '31." Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "The flood. My parents died in the flood. Everyone knows about the flood."

"Do they?" Brida smiled without any warmth in it at all. "What do they know? Say it. Tell me what everyone knows."

"That the ice dammed above Harker's Bend, and when it broke the water took the low ground by the mill, and — and there were losses. Drownings. The parish buried—" She faltered. Buried whom? Where? She had stood in St. Aldhelm's churchyard a hundred times; she could draw its stones from memory, had drawn them, for the atlas. There was no flood ground. No mass grave, no marker, no row of small stones. She had never noticed. She had never once, in fifteen years of noticing everything, noticed that the dead of '31 had no graves.

Brida watched the realization move across her face like weather and nodded slowly, as if confirming a chart.

"Aye," she said. "Now you see the shape of the hole. Go home and look for the rest of it. Look for the street that fed the mill. Look for the fire that burned the same night the ice broke — fires don't start themselves in a flood, girl, whatever the founders' grandfathers wrote. Look for the widows. Look for the children." Her voice dropped, and for a moment the gravel in it smoothed into something older and wearier. "Look for eleven children."

The number went through Tamsin like a burin slipping.

"Why eleven?"

Brida rose, joints cracking, and went to the window that faced the river. Grey light fell across her broad back. When she turned around, her face had closed again, the way ice closes over open water overnight — completely, and as if it had never been otherwise.

"Because I counted," she said. "In the winter of '31 I was twenty-two years old and strong as a barge horse, and I carried two of them across this river on my back with the ice groaning like a damned soul under us, and I counted them onto the far bank, and I have counted them every night since in my head like a woman telling beads. Eleven." She jabbed the pipe stem toward the register stub. "And that letter you're sitting on like a hen — you take it home and put it back where you found it, and you stop opening drawers that aren't yours. Because the people who razored that page are still alive, girl, most of them, and their sons sit on the magistrate's bench, and they did not cut that page for fun. They cut it because eleven children were a loose thread, and loose threads unravel claims. Land claims. Do you understand me? This town's grandest families are standing on ground that was paid for with a drowned street, and the deed is only good so long as nobody remembers the price."

The stove ticked. The river muttered. Tamsin found that her hands, resting flat on the table, were trembling, and she pressed them down as if smoothing a warped sheet of copper.

"If it's all been erased," she said slowly, "how would asking change anything? If there's no record—"

"There's record." For the first time, something flickered behind Brida's stillness — quickly hidden, but Tamsin saw it, the way you see a fish turn under ice. "There's more record than they'd sleep easy knowing. Paper burns. Stone holds. And memory—" she touched her own temple briefly, roughly, as if silencing it, "—memory's stubborn as a burdock root. But none of that does you any good, or me either, unless you're sure. Sure past the point where it's comfortable. So: ask about the winter of '31. Everywhere. Politely. Let them watch you asking. And when you know enough to be frightened — properly frightened, not the little shiver you've got now — then you decide."

"Decide what?"

"What kind of mapmaker you're going to be." Brida came back to the table and looked down at her, and her voice gentled into something almost like pity. "Hollis was a good man. I liked him. But he was a careful man, and care has a cost, and he paid it late in life and alone, and I'll say no more than that. You finish his atlas, you'll have to choose what he couldn't. Now get out. River's talking, and I don't cross fools or children in January."

"I'm neither."

"No," Brida agreed, and there was, unmistakably, the ghost of approval in it. "Not anymore, I'd say."

* * *

She did not take Tamsin across. Instead she walked her to the downstream point where the ice lay firmest — "walk where the reeds froze standing; the current's lazy there; test each step like a widow testing a suitor" — and stood with her arms folded, watching, while Tamsin picked her way out onto the white plain of the frozen Marlow.

Halfway across, Tamsin stopped.

It was not fear, though fear was there, humming in her knees. It was the strangeness of standing on the thing she had drawn all her life. She had rendered this river a dozen times — its course, its bends, its bridges, the ferry crossing marked with a neat dotted line and a tiny anchor symbol. From above, from the engraver's bench, from the godlike distance of the map, it had been simple. A line. A fact. Now she stood on it and it sang and shifted and held her weight grudgingly, and beneath her boots, somewhere in the dark water, lay the drowned street, the burned mill, the unmarked dead, the eleven.

*A map is a promise that the world stays still,* Hollis used to say, bending over the plate. *That's why we make them. Everything else moves.*

He had said it like comfort. Today it sounded like a confession.

Tamsin knelt, chipped a hole in the ice with her heel, and looked down into the black moving water for a long moment. Then she stood, marked her bearing on the far bank, and crossed the rest of the way without falling.

* * *

Back at the workshop, the afternoon light came low and copper-coloured through the frosted panes, and the unfinished atlas lay open on the bench where she had left it — Plate Forty-Seven, the eastern parishes, half of it inked, half of it still bare metal waiting for her hand. Her hand. Nineteen years of Hollis, compressed into a girl with a burin.

She should work. She always worked. Work had been the answer to grief, to doubt, to loneliness; work was the religion of the workshop and she was its devoutest creature.

Instead she set down her satchel, took out the razored stubs, the census slip, the unopened letter, and laid them in a row on the bench beside the map. Evidence. Provenance. The beginnings of a plate no one had commissioned.

Then she fetched the parish records she'd borrowed — legitimately, under the atlas's authority, the one power the project still gave her — and spread them wide, and dipped a pen, and began at the top of a fresh sheet of paper:

*Marlow Bend, winter 1831–32: persons resident, persons deceased, persons departed.*

By lamplight, working methodically backward through baptisms, marriages, burials, tithes, militia rolls, school ledgers, she built the list the way she'd build any map: from converging lines. And the lines converged. Street by street, name by name, she reconstructed the laborers' row that no map admitted to — fourteen households, forty-one souls, and among them, in the school ledger, in a teacher's cramped hand, a list of pupils transferred out in March of '32 with no destination recorded.

Eleven names.

Eleven children, aged between three years and twelve, struck from the school roll the same week the deeds office redrew the river parcels, the same week the parish register lost its page.

Tamsin sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs, snow slid off the eaves.

She looked at the list for a long time. Then, with a steadiness she did not entirely feel, she copied the eleven names onto the same sheet as her other notes, folded everything together with the census slip and the stubs and the letter, and locked them in the bottom of her trunk under her spare linens — the place where a person keeps the things that frighten them.

Tomorrow she would ask about the winter of '31, politely, everywhere, exactly as Brida Ferris had instructed. She would let the town watch her asking.

But tonight, before sleep, she allowed herself one question of her own, whispered to the dark workshop like a bearing taken in fog:

*Which one of the eleven am I?*
