## 5. Eleven Names

The desk had a smell all its own.

I had known it since I was nine years old — beeswax and pipe tobacco and the faint iron tang of the engraver's ink that never quite came out of anything it touched. Master Hollis had kept the big oak desk locked for as long as I could remember, not out of secrecy but habit, the way some men lock their tools against thieves who will never come. He kept the key on a ribbon around his neck. When he died in October — quietly, between one breath of coughing and the next, with his hand still resting on the proof of the river map — they had buried him with his coat on. Nobody thought to take the ribbon off.

Nobody but me.

I had worn the key at my own throat now for three months, and until this morning I had used it only once: to open the drawer where he kept his burnishing stones, because a plate would not take ink true without them and the atlas could not wait on sentiment. The atlas was nearly done. That was the thing I told myself each dawn as I lit the workshop stove alone, each evening as I wiped down the rollers and banked the fire. *Nearly done.* Fourteen plates finished to his standard or near enough; two more cut but unproofed; the title page drawn in fair copy awaiting transfer. When it was bound and delivered to the subscribers — Corven's name first on the list, of course, Corven's money under half of it — then I could grieve properly. Then the last duty owed to the dead would be discharged, and paper would be paper again, and I could stop waking at three in the morning with the sensation of having forgotten something enormous.

But there was still the question of provenance.

Every respectable county atlas carried notes on sources — deeds consulted, registers copied, surveys dated. Master Hollis had been rigorous about such things to the point of pedantry, and if I sent the book to press without them, the first scholar who opened it would know something was missing. So on the third Tuesday of February, with sleet ticking against the workshop windows like fingernails, I unlocked the desk for the second time and began going through his papers properly, drawer by drawer, looking for his source memoranda.

The upper drawers held what I expected. Surveyor's receipts. Correspondence with the London lithographers, stiff and formal. A bundle of letters from subscribers, including one from Magistrate Corven written in a hand so controlled it looked printed, thanking Hollis for his "service to the permanent memory of Marlow Bend." I set that one aside with an odd feeling in my stomach that I did not examine. Permanent memory. It was a strange phrase for a man to use about a map of streets he owned most of.

The middle drawer stuck.

It had always stuck, I realized — I remembered Master Hollis cursing at it gently, thumping it with the flat of his hand the way one thumps a mule. I pulled until the runners shrieked and gave, and the drawer slid out fat with paper, and the smell changed. Underneath the tobacco and wax there was something older. Camphor, maybe. Or just time.

On top lay a folded document sealed with red wax, cracked with age but unbroken. The superscription was in Master Hollis's hand, but shakier than I had ever seen it, the letters leaning like fence posts after a storm:

*For the eleventh child.*

Eleventh child. I sat with the letter flat on my palm for a long moment while the stove ticked and the sleet wrote its grey shorthand on the glass. There were no children attached to this workshop. There never had been. I was his apprentice, taken in at nine years old, but apprentice was not child, and I was certainly not the eleventh of anything. Perhaps it referred to some order of engravers — a guild numbering, a lodge. Master Hollis had been a private man about his past; he had come to Marlow Bend already grown, already skilled, already carrying whatever weight a man carries when he chooses a town at the far end of a river and stays thirty years without once speaking of before.

I turned the letter over twice. The seal held. And here is the truth I must write plainly, because everything afterward grew from it: I wanted to open it. My thumbnail was actually pressed into the seam of the wax before I stopped myself. Not from virtue — let me be honest, since honesty is the whole labor of these pages — but from fear. A letter addressed to someone who does not exist is either a joke, a mistake, or a door. And doors, once opened, do not care whether you are ready to walk through them.

I set it aside. I told myself I would ask Brida Ferris about it at the ferry crossing on Thursday; the old woman had known Hollis longer than anyone, longer even than me. I told myself the provenance notes might be in the bottom drawer, and reached for it, and found it locked — a small brass lock set into the wood, the kind meant for shopkeepers' cash boxes, nothing grand.

The keyhole was smaller than the one on the ribbon at my throat.

I should have stopped there. I want that recorded too. But the drawer had stuck, and the seal had said *child*, and somewhere beneath my ribs a wire had begun to hum, low and persistent, like the ice on the river does in late February when you stand very still on the bank and listen. I went to the wall of small drawers above the bench — the ones holding gravers and scrapers and the jeweler's screwdrivers Hollis used for fine hatching — and tried them against the little lock the way any apprentice tries any key, half hoping, half not caring.

The fourth one fit. Of course it fit. He had been a careful man, but careful men hide things in places their own hands can find blind.

The bottom drawer opened onto a shallow tin box, and the tin box opened onto paper that did not belong in any engraver's desk.

A copybook. A schoolchild's copybook, the cheap sort sold at the general store for a penny-three, marbled covers gone soft at the corners, the spine cocked from being held by small hands. My hands, forty years short of existing, knew the shape of it before my mind did. I have handled ten thousand sheets of paper in my life and I am not a fanciful woman, but when I lifted that copybook out of the tin, the hair on my forearms stood up as though the room's air had shifted a degree toward winter.

The cover bore no name. Inside, the first page was ruled for practice lines and mostly blank, except at the top, where a childish hand — round-bellied letters, uneven pressure, the pencil dug in hard enough to emboss the next leaf — had written a single line:

*Mrs. Quill says we must learn our names proper or the river will take them back.*

Below it, down the rest of the page and across eleven more, ran a list.

Not a practice list. Names. Real names, each written out carefully, each given a full line as though the writer had understood that a name deserves its own territory. I read them slowly, aloud, because reading them silently felt like a discourtesy:

Joss Renner.
Ada Quill.
Merrit.
Brida Ferris.
Samuel Pike.
Tobias Vane.
Lettie Marsh.
Caleb Marsh.
Nan Oakes.
Jem Oakes.
Ruthie Oakes.

Eleven names. I counted them twice, then a third time with my finger under each line, the way you count a thing you are afraid has changed since your last counting.

And every name was struck through.

Struck through neatly — that was the terrible part, the part that made my skin crawl even before I understood why. Not scribbled out in a child's anger but ruled through with a straightedge, a single confident line apiece, in ink that was darker than the pencil, in a hand I knew better than my own heartbeat. Master Hollis's hand. Each of the eleven names crossed out with a draftsman's precision, the kind of line you draw when you are deleting an error from a map. Deleting. That was the word that rose in me, unbidden, unwelcome. These were not children struck from a list. These were features struck from a survey.

Except one.

At the foot of the final page, below Ruthie Oakes, there was a twelfth line. No full name. Only two letters, penciled, in the childish hand — the same hand that had written Mrs. Quill's proverb about the river:

*T. A.*

No strike through them. Nothing else. The page ended there, and the remaining leaves of the copybook were blank, and the blankness roared.

I sat down on Hollis's stool without deciding to. My legs simply informed me they were finished. The tin box sat on my knees, the copybook open across it, and the workshop went on being a workshop around me — the stove breathing, the sleet ticking, the finished plates stacked in their tissue like sleeping things — but none of it reached me. I was looking at two pencil letters the way a woman looks into a well she has drunk from all her life and sees, for the first time, the depth below the bucket.

*T. A.* Tamsin Ashgrove.

I told myself it was coincidence. I heard my own voice say it inside my head, calm and reasonable, in the tone Master Hollis had taught me to use when a plate seemed ruined: *step back, look again, the eye deceives.* Children write initials. Children write all sorts of things. Some other T.A., some other child entirely, some pupil of this Mrs. Quill whoever she was — the list proved nothing, connected to nothing, except that Master Hollis had inked out eleven names with the steadiness of a man correcting a ledger, and had left two letters standing like a door left open on purpose.

But my hands were shaking, and hands do not shake at coincidences.

Because here is what I had not told anyone, what I had barely told myself: I did not remember arriving at Marlow Bend. This is not remarkable in itself — I was nine, and grief blurs, and the Ashgroves' story was simple enough that I had never needed to question it. The Ashgroves, distant cousins of my late father, had taken me in after a fever took my parents upriver; old Mistress Ashgrove had died my second winter with them, and old Master Ashgrove the spring after, and Master Hollis, who had been their lodger, had kept me on as apprentice rather than see me sent to the parish. A tidy story. A story with clean edges, the kind of story that fits itself to a gap without rattling. I had recited it so many times — to the vicar, to the census men, to gossips — that it had worn smooth as river stone, and a stone worn smooth tells you nothing about the mountain it broke from.

And now a penny copybook, hidden in a locked box in a locked drawer beneath the desk of the one man who had raised me, held eleven struck-through names and my own initials standing free at the bottom of the list like a survivor on a shore.

Nine years old. The year I came. The year Hollis took me in. If the copybook was what it appeared to be — a child's record, a schoolroom exercise grown suddenly serious — then it was from the winter of '31, the winter before I arrived, and every name in it belonged to a child of Marlow Bend, and Master Hollis had crossed them out one by one sometime after, in ink, with a straightedge, deliberately, and kept the book anyway. Kept it locked. Kept it the way a man keeps a wound: covered, but not removed.

Why strike the names and keep the list? Why not burn it? A man destroys what he wants destroyed. He locks away what he cannot bear to lose and cannot bear to look at. I knew that duality — I had lived in it for three months, keeping the workshop exactly as he left it while telling myself daily that finishing the atlas was a duty and not a haunting. Hollis had been unable to erase those eleven children completely. Something in him had refused. But he had struck them through, all the same. All but one.

All but me.

The thought arrived whole and cold, and I pushed it away, and it came back, and I pushed it away again, and by the fourth return I had stopped pushing and simply sat with it, the way you sit with a fever, letting it burn what it burns.

There was a twelfth name missing from this list. Eleven struck through, and one represented by initials only — as though the child who kept the book had never learned her own name properly, or had lost it, or had been given a new one and did not yet trust it. *Mrs. Quill says we must learn our names proper or the river will take them back.* Ada Quill. Second on the list. Struck through. Was she the schoolmistress? Had she taught these children? Had she written the proverb, and had the child copied it down as a shield?

I closed the copybook. I opened it again immediately, unable to bear the blindness of the cover. This is the honest record of that hour: I read the eleven names perhaps twenty times. I said them aloud until the stove needed feeding and I ignored it. I traced the unstruck initials with one fingertip, lightly, as though pressing harder might smudge them out of the world — or might confirm them, I could not say which I feared more.

Tamsin. Where had my name come from? I had asked once, years ago, sitting on this very floor watching him cut a coastline. He had paused — I remembered the pause now with horrible clarity, the graver lifting from the copper, the long moment before he answered — and said his mother's name had been Tamsin, a north-country name, and he had liked the sound of it for me. I had accepted it. Children accept. A pause of three seconds is nothing to a child and everything to a man who is inventing.

And Ashgrove? The orchard cottage, the apple trees gone woody and half-wild, the cellar I had been forbidden as a child — *damp, unstable, nothing down there but brick and spiders* — the cottage whose ownership Elias Vane's hovering pen had made suddenly visible to me only days ago, the way a splinter becomes visible only once it begins to fester. Who owned it before the Ashgroves? Vane had asked. I had lied. First lie of my life, smooth as a strike-through, and I had lain awake after wondering why it had come so easily.

Now I wondered a different thing. Now, with the copybook open on my knees, I wondered whether the Ashgroves had ever existed at all.

The bell over the workshop door rang, and I flinched hard enough to rattle the tin box.

Elias Vane stood in the doorway, stamped with sleet, his census satchel dark with wet, and for one irrational instant I shoved the copybook behind my back like a girl caught stealing sugar. He noticed — of course he noticed; noticing was his profession — but he only inclined his head and said the roads were turning to porridge and he'd hoped to warm himself a moment, if the mistress of the workshop permitted.

"The mistress permits," I said, and heard how strange the phrase sounded coming out of me, out of an apprentice, out of a *T. A.*, and covered it by clearing the desk — sweeping Hollis's correspondence over the tin box, over the copybook, hiding eleven struck-through children under a magistrate's thank-you letter.

Vane warmed his hands at my stove and talked of nothing — the thaw, the price of coal, the way the ice boomed at night now, deep down, like something turning over in its sleep. I answered in monosyllables and watched his eyes drift, professionally, to the stacks of finished plates, to the press, to the desk I was standing slightly in front of.

"Nearly finished, I'd guess," he said. "The atlas."

"By spring," I said. "Before the boats come up."

"Everyone's eager for the boats." He smiled, mild as milk. "New faces, new money. Though I'll confess a census man looks at a fresh influx differently than a merchant does. Every new arrival is a name to be entered. And every entry—" he tapped his satchel, "—is either true or it isn't. There's no third thing. That's the whole discipline, really. Paper doesn't get to have opinions."

"No," I said. "Paper is neutral."

He looked at me a moment too long, the way he had looked at the orchard cottage entry, pen hovering. Then he thanked me for the warmth and went out into the sleet, and the bell rang behind him, and I stood alone in the workshop with a hidden copybook burning through the magistrate's letter as though the paper beneath were heated.

I waited until his footsteps faded on the lane. Then I took the copybook out and looked at it again, because I could not stop looking, because it was a mirror and I had seen something in it and mirrors do not release you by being covered.

Eleven names struck through. Two letters standing. A letter sealed in wax upstairs in my rooms, addressed to a child who supposedly did not exist. A razored register page I had not yet found but now knew, with the certainty of the feverish, must exist somewhere in these papers. And beneath it all, humming louder than the ice: *the eleventh child.* Hollis had written it shaky-armed, dying, or near dying. He had known who would read it. He had known who was left.

Thursday I would cross the river. Brida Ferris — fifth on the list, struck through in her turn — would tell me what these names were, and why they had been crossed out, and what happened in the winter of '31 that a schoolchild thought the river might take names back.

Until then, there was only the copybook, and the stove, and the long grey afternoon, and the two letters at the bottom of the list that were mine — that had perhaps always been mine, that I had perhaps never owned at all.

I fed the stove. I banked the fire. I put the copybook in my bodice, against my heart, which was pounding like someone knocking at a door from the inside, and went upstairs to sit with a sealed letter I was no longer sure I had the right to leave unopened — or the right to open.

Outside, far down the valley, the river spoke once, deeply, in its sleep.
