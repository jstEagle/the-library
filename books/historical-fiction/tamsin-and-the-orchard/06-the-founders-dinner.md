## 6. The Founders' Dinner

The invitation came on card stock heavy as a gravestone, edged in gilt, smelling faintly of the Corven house's beeswax.

*Mr. Magistrate Corven and the Founding Families of Marlow Bend request the pleasure of Miss Tamsin Ashgrove's company, Thursday the eleventh of February, at seven o'clock, to celebrate the completion of the County Atlas.*

Miss. Not *Mistress*, not *Engraver*. The word was chosen; Tamsin could feel the choice in it the way she felt a burr in paper stock. They had made her small on purpose, folded her down to fit inside their hospitality like a map creased into a pocket.

She read it three times by lamplight in the workshop, then set it beside the finished plate — the last plate, the river bend rendered so finely that the ink of memory still seemed wet on the copper. Master Hollis would have framed such an invitation and never answered it. He had distrusted dinners the way other men distrusted ice: sound enough in the middle, treacherous at every edge.

But Hollis was eleven months in the churchyard, and the atlas needed more than completion now. It needed *patrons*. Paper cost money, presses cost money, and the subscription list for the county folio had been drafted with room at the top for exactly the names that would be seated at this table tonight. She could refuse and keep her pride whole and her purse empty. Or she could go, and smile, and let them believe they were buying her while she learned what they wanted bought.

She went.

* * *

The Corven house stood at the highest point of Marlow Bend, which was itself a kind of argument. Every other building in town crouched near the water where the trade was; only this one had climbed the hill, as though its owners had decided early that proximity to the river was for people who needed the river. From its windows you looked *down* at the bend, at the mills, at the ferry crossing, at the whole working spine of the town laid out below like something owned rather than inhabited.

A footman took her coat. A second footman took her name, pronounced it wrong — *Ashgrove* flattened into two bored syllables — and announced her to a drawing room full of candlelight and conversation that did not so much pause when she entered as politely make room for her, the way water makes room for a dropped stone before closing over it.

She knew some of the faces from plates she had engraved. Alderman Pike, silver-haired, amiable, his hands folded atop his belly in the attitude of a man perpetually about to agree with someone. The Harrow brothers, whose tanneries stank up the eastern bank and whose money smelled of nothing at all. Mrs. Latchford, widow of the mill owner, wearing black silk that must have cost more than Tamsin's entire workshop. And at the center, holding court beside the fire with a glass of madeira he was not drinking, Magistrate Edmund Corven.

He was not what she had expected, though she could not have said what she had expected. She had imagined a blunt man, a bully built like his authority. Instead he was spare and grey and courteous, with the mild eyes of a schoolmaster, and when he turned that gaze upon her it carried no menace at all — only attention, complete and patient, like a lens focusing. It was worse than menace. Menace at least admitted what it wanted.

"Miss Ashgrove." He crossed the room to her himself, which she understood instantly was a performance staged for the benefit of everyone watching. "The town owes you a debt. Your master began a great work. You have had the grace and the skill to finish it."

"Master Hollis began it," she said. "I only cut what he drew."

"And yet the hand that finishes is always remembered longer than the hand that begins." He smiled. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it less trustworthy. "Come. Let me show you off properly."

He led her through the room, naming her to clusters of founding family the way a man presents a horse he means to buy — *the engraver herself, the finest copperplate work between here and the capital, trained by old Hollis, who kept his craft better than his books, ha.* The laughter that followed was polite and slightly puzzled, and Tamsin realized Corven had made a joke about her master's finances that only these people were positioned to understand, and that she had just been gently reminded, in front of all of them, that she was a tradeswoman among gentry. A tool with talent. Welcome, but not *kin*.

Dinner was served at a table long enough to require its own geography. She was seated at Corven's right hand, which she recognized as both honor and containment — the seat closest to the host was also the seat most watched. Soup was a clear consommé. Fish followed. Between courses, the talk flowed around her like the river around a mooring post.

They talked of the new turnpike. Of wool prices. Of a bill in Parliament concerning navigable rivers, which Corven dismissed with a flick of two fingers — "London will discover the Bend when London finds it profitable, and not one year sooner." They talked of the church roof, and of young Mr. Harrow's marriage, and of the quality of this winter's ice, which was thick, someone observed, thicker than any winter since—

Since.

There was a fractional silence. Not a pause; silences that small are engineered, not accidental. Mrs. Latchford set down her wine glass with exaggerated care. Pike said something about the herring run, too loudly, and the table gratefully swerved after him.

Tamsin kept her eyes on her plate and catalogued the moment the way she'd catalogue a smudge on a plate proof: *here, at this word, the line breaks.* She had spent her life reading documents. Tonight, for the first time, she read a dinner.

Corven leaned toward her, voice pitched for her alone beneath the general conversation. "You'll forgive them. Old families are like old maps — there are regions we prefer not to survey twice."

"I find," Tamsin said, "that a region unsurveyed is usually a region someone has paid to keep blank."

Something moved behind the magistrate's mild eyes — appreciation, she thought, genuine and chilling. He raised his glass to her slightly. "Then your atlas will be all the more valuable for having avoided such payments."

Before she could answer, he rose, tapped his glass, and the table fell obediently silent.

"My friends. We are gathered to honor a book." He gestured to her without looking, a small proprietary motion, as if she were part of the place setting. "For three years, the late Mr. Hollis labored on an atlas of our county — its rivers, its roads, its boundaries, its honest acres. And when God called him home, his apprentice — *his daughter in craft*, I should say — took up his burin and did not put it down until the work was done."

Applause, measured and warm. Tamsin inclined her head and thought: *he has rehearsed this.*

"And I will tell you what this atlas means." Corven's voice softened into the register of a man sharing something precious. "It means that Marlow Bend finally stands recorded. Surveyed. Set down in copper and ink for the capital to see. And what will the capital see?" He let the question breathe. "It will see a town without tragedy. A town that suffered no scandal, lost no lawsuits, nursed no grievances. A town that rose from wilderness by honest labor and lawful dealing, and asks no one's pardon for its prosperity." He lifted his glass higher. "A clean map, my friends, for a clean history. To Marlow Bend."

"To Marlow Bend," the table echoed, glasses rising around her like a field of small fires.

*A town without tragedy.*

Tamsin sat very still, her own glass untouched. She had heard lies before — clumsy lies, panicked lies, the lies of debtors and apprentices caught with broken tools. This was none of those. This was a lie delivered as a toast, wrapped in warmth, offered to a room full of people who would each carry it out of this house and repeat it until it hardened into fact. That was how a lie became history, she understood suddenly. Not through argument but through *dinner*. Through repetition in warm rooms by people whose voices carried weight because their land did.

And she had brought the instrument. She had finished the very plate that would carry their story to the capital, believing a map simply recorded the world, never once imagining that the world might be arranged to suit the map.

Her hand, resting on the tablecloth, had gone cold. She willed it still.

* * *

It was during the removal of the covers, in the shuffle of servants and shifting chairs, that she noticed Merrit Corven.

She had marked her earlier, distantly — a tall woman of perhaps six-and-twenty at the table's far end, dark-haired, dressed in green so dark it was nearly black, who had eaten steadily and spoken little and laughed at none of her father's jokes. Now, as the ladies withdrew and the gentlemen lingered over port (an arrangement Corven had waived in Tamsin's favor with a gracious gesture that kept her, conveniently, under his eye), Merrit remained standing by the window, apart from the withdrawing ladies, watching her.

Not glancing. *Watching.* With the fixed, unblinking attention of someone counting another person's breaths.

When their eyes met, Merrit did not look away, as courtesy required. She held the look, and something passed across her face that Tamsin had no name for — not hatred exactly, or not only hatred. Recognition, perhaps, of a kind. The way one animal recognizes another across a clearing.

Later, in the drawing room, Tamsin found herself cornered near the pianoforte with Mrs. Latchford, who patted her wrist and told her what a comfort it must be, dear, to have such a connection to the Corvens now, and wasn't it remarkable how well some people rose, given application and a good master. Tamsin answered in monosyllables and searched the room for the dark-green dress.

She found Merrit at her elbow so abruptly that she started.

"You are the engraver." Merrit's voice was low and even. Up close she was handsome in a hard, unspent way, with her father's grey eyes and none of his warmth. "You finished the atlas."

"I did."

"All of it? Every plate?"

"Every plate."

"According to his drawings." Merrit said it flatly, a statement, but it landed like a question. "You cut what was on the drawings. Nothing else."

"That is what engraving is, Miss Corven."

"Is it." Something flickered in her face — disappointment, or its twin, relief. "My father speaks highly of you. He does not speak highly of many people. You should understand what that costs."

"I begin to."

"Do you?" Merrit stepped closer, and now her voice dropped to almost nothing, beneath the piano being opened across the room, beneath Mrs. Latchford's cheerful narration of her son-in-law's prospects. "Do you know what you've made? Have you read every line of it? Every boundary, every parcel, every—"

"Merrit." Corven's voice, mild as ever, from across the room. He had not raised it. He had not needed to. "You'll frighten Miss Ashgrove, and I need her steady. The atlas reflects upon us all."

The silence that followed had a texture Tamsin felt physically, like cold air through a cracked pane. Merrit's jaw tightened. When she spoke again it was aloud, pleasant, and utterly false. "Of course, Papa. I was only admiring the craftsmanship." Her eyes never left Tamsin's face. "We are all so proud of what this town has accomplished. All of it. Everything."

It sounded, to anyone listening, like loyalty. To Tamsin, standing close enough to see the pulse jumping in the younger woman's throat, it sounded like a warning shouted through a locked door.

* * *

The carriage Corven ordered for her — his own carriage, the gesture noted and admired by the whole room — carried her down the hill through streets glazed with refrozen snow. Through the window, Marlow Bend lay blue and quiet under a rising moon, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold. The river ran black between its banks, sealed now under a foot of ice, holding whatever it held.

She thought about the evening the whole way down the hill, replaying it like proof sheets, hunting errors.

*A town without tragedy.* Said to a table of people who had gone silent, all together, at a single half-spoken phrase about thick ice. Said while a woman in dark green watched Tamsin with an anger that predated this dinner, that seemed almost personal, as though Tamsin had done something to her merely by existing — or merely by finishing a certain book.

And Corven. Corven, who had praised the clean map, who had toasted the honest acres, who had already ordered his carriage and his patronage as if the transaction were settled — as if she had been invited here not to present the atlas but to be shown its owner.

In the workshop, past midnight, she lit the lamp and pulled the county plate from its rack and studied it under the magnifier, looking for what she did not yet know to look for. The river. The bend. The mill seats. The ferry crossing. The orchard road, running up from the east bank. All correct. All beautiful. All, she now suspected, *edited* — though by whose hand, and against what original, she could not say.

Hollis's drawings were still in the flat drawers, hundreds of them, in their careful sequence. She had filed them herself after his death, believing she was preserving a finished thing.

Now she opened the first drawer and stood looking down at the stacked papers, at her dead master's pencil lines, and understood that she was about to begin comparing a map against itself.

Somewhere outside, far off, the river boomed once under its ice — a deep, tectonic sound, like a door closing in an empty house.

Tamsin pulled the topmost drawing into the lamplight and began.
