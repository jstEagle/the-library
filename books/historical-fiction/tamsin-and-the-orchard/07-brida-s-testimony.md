## 7. Brida's Testimony

*Dictated by Brida Ferris, ferry-woman of Marlow Bend, to Tamsin Ashgrove, engraver, on the fourth day of February, 1847. Taken down in her own words as spoken, at her kitchen table above the ferry landing, with the river groaning under its ice beyond the window.*

---

You'll want me to start at the beginning, and I will, but you should know that I've started this story a hundred times in my head and never once finished it aloud. Not to my husband, God rest him, not to the priest, not to myself in the dark when the river makes that sound it made that night. So if my voice shakes, girl, you write the shaking down too. It's part of the record.

The beginning is this: there was a row of houses where the willows are now. You won't find it on any map, and that's the whole reason you're sitting in my kitchen with your pen scratching like a mouse in a wall. But it was there. Fourteen houses, timber-framed, built by the mill company for the families that worked the wheel and the kilns and the sorting floors. We called it the laborers' row, plain as bread, because nobody who lived there had time for prettier names. Good roofs. Bad drainage. A well at each end and a shared oven in the middle, and children everywhere, always, like sparrows after threshing.

I wasn't one of them. My man kept the ferry even then, and we lived up on the bank where you're looking now through my window. But I knew every soul in that row. I'd ferried them across to market and back for years. I ferried their dead, too, when the fever took the little ones, which it did too often, and I can tell you that a ferry-woman knows a community better than any parson, because people talk on the water. They tell you things on the water they'd never say on land. Something about being between shores loosens the tongue.

So when I say I knew them, understand what I mean. I knew Marta Kessling sang to her babies in a language her husband didn't speak, some old country tongue from before either of them crossed over. I knew Tam Renner drank but never missed a shift and could fix anything with wire and stubbornness. I knew the Quill woman — no relation to your schoolmistress, or maybe every relation, ask Ada yourself — I knew she taught letters to any child who'd sit still, charging a button a lesson. And I knew there were eleven children that winter who'd lost one parent or both to the fever two years running, and the row had folded them in like dough around raisins. Nobody called them orphans. They were just everybody's. That's what a row is. That's what a row *was*.

Now. The winter of '31.

You'll have heard the official version, because the official version is all there is anymore. Ice jam upstream, thaw flood, some damage to the low fields, regrettable loss of property. That's the version in the county book. I've read it. It's four sentences long and every one of them is a lie by omission, which is the most respectable kind of lie there is.

Here's what actually happened.

That December the cold came down like a lid. Three weeks without a break, hard enough that the river froze bank to bank, which it does here maybe once in ten years, and when it does, the old men watch the bend above town and mutter. Because the river doesn't freeze smooth. It freezes in plates, and the plates shove and stack against each other where the channel narrows past the mill race, and if the stacking goes on long enough you get an ice dam — a wall of it, higher than a house, holding back everything upstream. And everything upstream keeps coming. The river doesn't stop for anyone's convenience. It just waits, and grows heavier, and waits.

By the second week of January the dam stood better than twenty feet and the water behind it had backed up into the low pasture, drowning fences, drowning the woodpile, creeping toward the row. People moved their stock uphill. The mill kept running, because the company wanted its winter cut milled and Mr. Corven's father — yes, *that* Corven, the magistrate's grandfather, though the family would rather you said the elder Corven — the elder Corven sent word that the wheel would turn until it couldn't. There were men in that mill working double shifts right up to the end. Remember that. It matters to what came after.

The night it broke was the ninth of January. I know the date the way you know your own birthday, because I've said it to myself so many times it's worn a groove. Moonless. Bitter clear. I remember the stars were out like nails hammered into black tin, and I remember thinking, standing in my yard with the bucket half-frozen in my hands, that the river had gone quiet. Wrong quiet. A held-breath quiet.

Then the sound came.

I've heard a tree fall, I've heard the millstones crack, I've heard ice go out in ordinary spring breakup, which sounds like God dragging furniture upstairs. None of it is anything. This was the earth itself tearing. The dam didn't melt or crumble — it *failed*, all at once, the way a rope fails, and the water behind it came down the valley in a single body, a moving hill of it, carrying the broken ice ahead of it like teeth.

I ran for the bank. Everyone who could walk ran for the bank, and I wish to God none of us had, because what you saw from there you carried for life. The water hit the row first. Fourteen houses, and the river took the downstream six whole — lifted them off their sills and rolled them, and I heard screaming from inside them, and then I heard nothing, because the water closed over like a mouth. The mill went next. The flood hit the wheelhouse and the wheel shrieked and then the lamps — they worked by lamplight, those double shifts, oil lamps everywhere, and the flood smashed them all at once into the oil store —

It burned on top of the water. I need you to write that down exactly. The mill burned while it floated. Fire riding the flood, a mile of burning river, and the light of it turned the ice fields downstream orange, and the smoke went straight up in the still air like a black pillar, and the noise — the fire roared and hissed where it met the water and underneath it the screaming went on and on and then stopped in ones and twos, the way candles go out.

Fourteen houses. Six gone outright in the first minute. The rest stood, because the flood split around the rise where the oven was, but they stood flooded to the eaves and freezing, and the people who survived the first wall of water were climbing onto roofs in the dark with the temperature falling and the burning mill lighting everything like judgment day.

We got forty-one souls off those roofs and out of the water that night. Forty-one. I counted them into my kitchen and the church and the tavern, wrapped in anything dry, and I counted them again at dawn because I couldn't make the number sit still in my head. Forty-one living. And the dead — we found thirty-three bodies in the weeks after, in the willows, in the driftwood, in the mill ruins when the water dropped. Some we never found at all. The river kept them.

And the children. You want the children, so here are the children.

Eleven of them lost a parent that night, and seven of those lost both. The youngest was two years old — she was found at dawn in a driftwood tangle near the ferry landing, lashed into a feather mattress ticking that someone, God bless whoever it was, had knotted around her like a sling. She grew up. You know her. She teaches school now and keeps a slate nobody's allowed to read. But I'm ahead of myself.

For three days, the town did what towns do. It grieved loudly and fed people and buried the dead in a common grave because the ground was iron and graves had to be blasted. The founders paid for coffins — I'll grant them that, and mark that I only grant it because refusing to grant it would be its own kind of lie. Coffins, bread, blankets. For three days Marlow Bend was decent.

On the fourth day the gentlemen met at the Corven house, and on the fifth day the decisions came down, and I have spent sixteen years trying to decide whether those decisions were made in malice or in cowardice, and I have concluded it doesn't matter, because the row is equally dead either way.

They ruled the land unsafe. Floodplain, they called it. Unfit for habitation, and the remaining eight houses of the row were condemned and torn down inside the month — torn down, mind, with the salvage sold, sold by the company, which owned the houses and therefore owned the wreckage of the lives inside them. And the eleven children, who had aunts and uncles and neighbors and godparents among the survivors, who had *us*, who had me — the eleven children were dispersed. That's the word the overseer used. Dispersed. Like seed. Like ash.

For their own good, they said. Write it down, girl, write it exactly: *for their own good.* Because a child raised by grieving neighbors in poverty, they said, would fare worse than a child apprenticed out, bound out, taken in by a respectable household that could use small hands. The Kessling girl to the Corvens' own household, as ward — charity, and everyone to remark on the charity. Two boys bound to farmers upriver. One to the tannery. The Renner boy, ten years old, judged unruly within the year and shipped to the colony before he was twelve — unruly, meaning he bit the man who told him his mother's death was God's will. The baby to the Quills' cousin downriver. And so on, and so on, eleven times, each placement dressed in kindness and each one a door closing.

And the parents' names — the drowned, the burned — went into the parish register same as anyone, at first. Burials are burials; even the founders couldn't stop God's paperwork. But then the claims started. The company claimed flood losses. The heirs claimed the row's leases voided. And somewhere in the tangle of claims and counterclaims, it became inconvenient that fourteen households of laborers had ever held rights to that land, or improvements on it, or gardens, or fences, or a common oven built by their own hands over twenty years. Improvements mean compensation. Compensation means the founders pay. And so the row began to disappear.

First the tax rolls. Then the lease books. Then the parish register itself — and here, girl, put your pen down a moment, because this is the part I've never said to another living soul, and I need to see you hear it.

I saw the page cut out.

Not the whole register. One leaf, razored close to the spine, clean as surgery. I was at the vestry the following spring, some errand about the ferry license, and the clerk had the register open on the counter, and I saw the stubs of the cut page and the ghost of ink transferred to the leaf facing it — reversed writing, like a mirror, the way ink bleeds through paper and leaves its shadow behind. And the clerk saw me see it, and he closed the book, and he said, *misprint, damaged leaf, replaced,* and his hands were shaking, and I let it lie. God forgive me, I let it lie, because my man's license hung on the corporation's goodwill and we had no one else to be.

But I remembered the shadow of the ink. Reversed, it read — I've had sixteen years to work it out — it read *baptisms and burials, laborers' row, January 1831.* Thirty-three burials and a scatter of baptisms, the children the row had sheltered, all of it, gone in one razor stroke.

And the maps. You're the map woman, so listen closest here. Old surveyor Hask drew the bend in '29, before the flood, and his plate showed the row plain as day — fourteen little rectangles along the south bank, labeled *Mill Row.* When the new county atlas was commissioned, Hask's plate was returned to him for correction, and he corrected it, because he had grandchildren and a mortgage, and the corrected plate shows willows. Trees. Green trees where forty-one survivors watched thirty-three neighbors drown. I know because Hask told me himself, drunk, two years before he died, weeping into my husband's whiskey and saying the trees were the best trees he ever drew, that he'd given them roots deeper than any real tree has, out of spite, so that anyone who looked close would see they were planted by hand.

He said something else that night, and I want you to carry it carefully. He said the atlas man — your master, Hollis — had argued with the corporation over that plate. Argued hard, they said. Lost, the way everyone lost. But Hask said Hollis never threw anything away, that the man kept proofs and drafts and misprints the way other men keep sins, boxed and sealed, and that if the truth of that bend ever needed finding, it would be found in Hollis's own workshop, in his own hand, filed under his own guilt.

I've waited sixteen years for somebody to go look.

Why did I wait? You have the right to ask it, sitting there with your pen. Why did I keep silent while children were scattered and a street was murdered twice — once by water, once by paper? I'll tell you plainly. Fear first, and shame after, and then habit, which is fear grown old. What could a ferry-woman do against men who owned the mill and the land and the law and the ledger? Speak, and lose the ferry, and then what good was I to anybody? And the longer I waited, the more my silence became its own crime, and the heavier it grew, until carrying it felt safer than setting it down, the way a pack feels part of your spine.

But then you came across my ice in January, asking after a cottage that isn't on any map either, and I watched your face when I told you to ask about the winter of '31, and I saw you weren't going to let it lie. And I thought: I am sixty-one years old. The river took my hearing in the left ear that night and gave me this tremor in the hands, and it can take the rest whenever it likes, but it will not take this last thing. The dead of the row have been nameless long enough. Paper killed them twice. Let paper raise them, then, since paper's what we've got.

So take it all down. Every word. The dam, the fire, the forty-one, the thirty-three, the eleven, the razored leaf, the trees with spite-roots. Swear it however oaths are sworn these days — on the book, on the river, on my mother's grave, I don't care. I, Brida Ferris, ferry-woman of Marlow Bend, saw what I saw, and I say it now for the record of anyone who will ever read:

There was a row. There were families. There were eleven children. And the town that calls itself without tragedy built that innocence out of their bones.

Now, girl, put down your pen and warm your hands, because what I have next isn't testimony. It's names. All eleven, as best a memory can hold them after sixteen winters — and one of them, when I say it, you're going to want to stand up and leave this kitchen, and I'm asking you now, before I say it, to stay seated and let me finish.

* * *

She said the names slowly, one by one, watching my face across the table, and I wrote them all, and my hand stayed steady until the eleventh.

Then she reached across and covered my writing hand with her trembling one, and said: "Stay seated, like I asked. Because the eleventh name on my list, girl, is the name your master carved into the corner of every proof he ever pulled — and if you don't know why yet, you will, and God help us both when you do."

---

*End of dictation, taken down verbatim. The remainder of the evening's conversation is recorded separately, under seal, in the engraver's own hand.*
