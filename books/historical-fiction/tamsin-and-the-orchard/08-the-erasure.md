## 8. The Erasure

I did not sleep.

I told myself I was working — that a cartographer's answer to any question worth asking was more paper, not less — but the truth was simpler and uglier: if I stopped moving my hands, Brida Ferris's voice would catch up with me, and the eleventh name on her list would have nowhere to hide except inside my own skull.

So I worked. The workshop stove had gone to ash around midnight and I did not feed it, because the cold kept my thoughts in rows the way a case keeps plates. I lit four lamps instead, and laid out everything Master Hollis had left me across the long bench like a surgeon laying out a body: the county atlas proofs, forty-one of them, each pulled on his own press in his own ink; the deed abstracts he'd copied for reference; the parish registers he'd consulted for boundary disputes going back to the town's founding; his field notebooks, eleven of them, their spines cracked from use.

And I began at the beginning, which for Marlow Bend meant 1824, when the founders had divided the river flats among themselves.

I want to be honest about what I expected to find, because honesty is the only thing I have left that no one can alter. I expected to find nothing. I expected to spend the night confirming that old Brida's memory had softened and swelled with age, the way memory does, turning a hard winter into a catastrophe because catastrophe makes better story than hardship. I expected to feel ashamed of myself by dawn, and to carry that shame quietly, the way one carries most things.

That is not what I found.

* * *

The laborers' row appears first — last, rather, working backward — in Hollis's field notebook for the autumn of 1845, two years before his death. He'd been surveying the flood line for the insurance abstract Corven & Sons required, and his notes are immaculate as always, every measurement triple-checked, every bearing reduced twice.

On the third page, in the margin, in his small severe hand:

*Ridge of foundation stones visible at low water, N bank, approx. 40 rods below the mill race. Nine hearths? Ten? Ask at the parish.*

Ask at the parish. I turned the page. There was no follow-up. In twenty years of notebooks, Hollis never once left a question unanswered — I had copied enough of his methods to know that unanswered questions physically pained him, that he would walk ten miles to resolve an ambiguity of three feet. But here was a ridge of foundation stones on the north bank, ten possible hearths where the official map of 1846 showed open meadow, and the matter was simply... closed. Dropped like a stone into deep water.

Except it wasn't dropped. Because I remembered the proofs.

I went to the proof cabinet — the tall one against the east wall, the one whose key hung on the same nail it had hung on for thirty years — and pulled the drawer marked *NORTH BANK / RIVER FLATS*, and there it was. The 1846 proof of Plate Fourteen, the one showing the north bank below the mill race.

There were two versions in the drawer.

I had catalogued these proofs myself, three years ago, when Master Hollis's hands had begun to fail him and he'd asked me to organize the flat files. Two pulls of the same plate, dated a week apart. At the time I'd assumed the second corrected some drafting error — a mislabeled elevation, a slipped hachure. Draftsmen pull corrections constantly. It had never once occurred to me to compare them.

I laid them side by side under the lamp.

They were identical. Identical in every line, every label, every contour — identical everywhere except a patch roughly four inches square, just below the mill race, on the north bank.

In the first proof, that four-inch square contained fine engraved detail: a row of small rectangles, ten of them, tightly spaced, each with a tiny garden plot behind it, rendered in the abbreviated convention Hollis used for worker housing throughout the atlas. Beside them, a label so small you'd need a loupe to read it: *LABORERS' ROW — ERECTED 1827.*

In the second proof, the square was empty. Clean meadow. The engraving was flawless — burnished down so skillfully that unless you held the two proofs together, you would never know anything had ever been there. Someone had taken a burnisher to a finished copperplate and erased ten houses, ten gardens, and eleven families, and then re-polished the plate until the metal forgot what it had been made to say.

I sat down on the stool without meaning to.

Because I knew that burnishing. I knew that hand. You spend seven years grinding your thumb raw on someone else's plates, cleaning their acid, pulling their proofs, and you learn their touch the way a daughter learns a mother's footsteps on stairs. The erasure was done conservatively, patiently, by someone who understood exactly how much pressure removes ink without gouging copper.

It was Master Hollis's hand. No one else had ever touched those plates. No one else had ever been permitted to.

* * *

I should stop here and tell you what I did, because what I did matters more than what I felt, and what I felt I will not be able to describe accurately anyway.

What I did was keep checking.

Some part of me — the part trained in this very room, by that very hand — understood that a single altered proof proves nothing except that a proof was altered. Errors happen. Second thoughts happen. A patron withdraws funding for a detail and the detail comes off the plate; it has happened to engravers since Gutenberg. If I wanted the truth, I could not look at one document. Documents lie individually all the time. It is only in aggregate, in the terrible arithmetic of many papers agreeing or failing to agree, that they confess.

So I checked everything.

I started with the deeds, Hollis's copies of the original land divisions of 1824–1831. The laborers' row had stood, per its own label, from 1827. Workers' housing on the river flats belonged either to the mill concern or to whichever founder had sold or leased the parcel. I traced the parcels. Parcel forty-one, north bank, forty rods below the mill race: granted 1824 to Josiah Corven, senior — the magistrate's grandfather — leased onward in 1826, per a marginal note, "to the Mill Company for the erection of dwellings for its hands."

The lease itself was not in Hollis's files. I searched for an hour. Every other lease, every other transaction, every scrap of paper touching every other parcel along both banks — present, copied, annotated. Parcel forty-one's lease existed in the record as a marginal note referring to a document that was nowhere. Not missing the way papers go missing — misfiled, water-damaged, eaten by mice. Missing the way a tooth is missing when it has been pulled.

Then the tax rolls. Hollis had copied the assessment rolls of 1828 through 1833 for a boundary dispute with the ferry interest — Brida Ferris's late husband's interest, though I didn't know that yet. In the 1829 roll, parcel forty-one was assessed. I have the figures in front of me now and I will copy them here so that they exist in more than one hand in this world: *Parcel 41, improvements thereon, ten dwelling houses, assessed value £140.* Ten dwelling houses. Assessed. Taxed. Which means inspected, which means seen, which means real — because assessors do not tax houses that do not exist, however agreeable they may find magistrates.

In the 1831 roll, parcel forty-one appeared again — assessed at £12. *Vacant ground.*

Between the spring of 1830 and the spring of 1831, ten houses worth £128 of assessed improvement had ceased, on paper, to exist. And I already knew, from Brida, what had happened between the spring of 1830 and the spring of 1831. The ice dam. The flood. The fire at the mill, the same night, the same wind.

Disasters unmake houses. That is what disasters do; I am not so naive as to think paper should have said otherwise. But a disaster does not unmake the *record of* the houses. When the mill itself burned in '31 — rebuilt within two years, insured, litigated over, present in every subsequent roll and map and memory — its burning was documented six ways to Sunday. The laborers' row burned the same night, killed eleven parents and orphaned eleven children, and left behind in the written world precisely one trace: a tax assessor's cold little word, *vacant*, and a ridge of foundation stones that a surveyor noticed in 1845 and was never allowed to ask about again.

* * *

It was past three in the morning when I finally forced myself to do the thing I had been circling for hours.

The parish register.

Hollis kept his own transcripts of the St. Aldhelm's registers — births, deaths, marriages from the town's founding — because the vicarage flooded twice a decade and the originals were rotting. His transcripts were famous for their fidelity. Vicar after vicar had relied on them. I had relied on them.

Brida had given me names. Eleven parents. I took the first name — Daniel Renner, laborer, mill-hand — and looked for his children's baptisms, because baptisms were recorded whether parents lived or died, and children born between 1820 and 1830 would appear.

There was no Daniel Renner in the transcript at all. Not his marriage, not his children, not his burial. A man who had lived ten years in a house on parcel forty-one, paid taxes through his landlord's roll, worked the mill whose bell rang the hours over the whole town — absent. As if he had never drawn breath.

I checked the second name. Absent. Third. Absent. My hands had begun to shake by the fourth and I made them stop, because shaking hands smear ink, and if I was going to do this then I was going to do it properly, with clean evidence, the way he taught me.

Nine families. Twenty-two adults and children between them, counting the drowned. Not one entry. Not one baptism, not one burial record for the drowned themselves — and there had been a flood with bodies, Brida had said so, bodies buried fast in frozen ground in the churchyard's north corner. A mass burial leaves a register entry even in the worst-kept parish in England. It is practically the definition of a register's purpose.

I sat looking at the transcripts for a long time.

And then I saw it. I nearly missed it, and I want it recorded that I nearly missed it, because the people who did this were good at what they did, and we should not comfort ourselves otherwise.

Page ninety-one of the deaths transcript. Hollis numbered his pages himself, in the corner, in his own hand — he numbered *everything*, it was almost a mania with him. Pages eighty-nine, ninety: present. Page ninety-two: present.

Page ninety-one was numbered in a different ink.

A paler ink. Newer. The numeral matched the style perfectly — he was too careful to let a forgery differ in form — but the iron-gall had oxidized differently, browned at a different rate, and once you saw it you could not unsee it. One page of the death register transcript had been recopied. Recopied, not replaced — Hollis never discarded paper — and somewhere in this workshop, in some file I had organized with my own hands, the original page ninety-one might still exist.

I found it at dawn, in the third place I looked: filed under *MISCELLANEOUS PARISH PAPERS, 1829–33*, a folder so blandly labeled it could only have been deliberate. Inside, a single loose sheet, older paper, creased as if it had been folded and carried in a pocket for years.

Deaths, December 1830 – March 1831. In the vicar's own transmitted text, faithfully transcribed: eleven entries in quick succession, all dated within nine days of each other. *Drowned by the breaking of the ice.* Nine adults, and — my eye went down the list and my whole body went cold — two infants, listed with their mothers.

Eleven entries. Eleven dead. And beneath them, in the vicar's closing note for the season, one line Hollis had preserved because he preserved everything:

*Buried in the north corner, ground being froze. Also eleven children of the above dispersed amongst the town by order of the Bench, it being judged best for their welfare.*

By order of the Bench.

Corven. The word was practically a signature.

* * *

I stood in the grey light coming through the frost-furred windows, holding a dead man's secret in both hands, and I made myself think about the plates again, because the plates were the thing I could not explain away.

Here is what I know about copperplates: they do not change their minds. Paper can be razored, recopied, refiled, lost. Ink can be matched. But a finished engraved plate is a physical commitment — weeks of work cut into metal — and to erase work from a plate requires a decision, a burnisher, an evening of patient destructive labor, and a reason strong enough to make a proud craftsman mutilate his own art.

Two plates. I had found two proofs altered, and I went back through the drawer and found the second pair while the light came up: Plate Nine, the town plan itself, the heart of the atlas, the sheet every buyer would study longest. Same story. A four-inch patch below the mill race, burnished clean. On the earlier proof, faint as breath: the row, and the label, and something else I hadn't noticed on Plate Fourteen — eleven small crosses in the churchyard's north corner, the cartographer's mark for graves.

Someone had erased a graveyard. Someone had erased children's graves from the town map, and polished the copper smooth, and pulled fresh proofs, and filed the old ones away in a drawer rather than destroy them.

He couldn't destroy them. That was the thing I kept arriving at, the thing that hurt worse than the betrayal itself. Hollis — precise, severe, incorruptible Hollis, who had taught me that a map is a promise made in copper — had complied. Under pressure, from the Bench, from Corven, from whatever threat had been laid on his table in that week between the two pulls, he had picked up the burnisher and erased ten houses and eleven graves and eleven orphaned children from the finest atlas this county would ever see.

But he had kept the proofs. He had kept the original register page. He had written *ask at the parish* in a notebook he must have known might be searched. He had hidden the truth in the one way available to a man whose every instrument of truth-telling had been taken from him: he had buried it, carefully, in his own files, and labeled the grave *Miscellaneous*.

Why?

If he had simply surrendered, he'd have destroyed the old proofs — any careful man would, to protect himself. Keeping them served no purpose except one: so that someday, someone, comparing, would find the seam. He had not merely complied. He had complied *and left a door open*, and then he had died — worn out, his hands ruined — before he could walk through it himself.

Or before he dared to.

I thought about the last year of his life, the year I had shared it. The way he had grown silent in his final months, checking the windows when the knock came, the way he had pressed the unfinished atlas into my hands — *finish it, girl, it wants finishing* — with an urgency I had read as grief and pride and had never once thought to read as fear. I had believed I was completing his life's work.

I had been completing his surrender. Unless—

Unless finishing it honestly was the completion he actually intended, and he had lacked the courage or the years to do it himself, and had gambled everything on whether the person he raised would prove braver than he was.

I did not know which. I sat in the cold workshop with the two proofs side by side — the town as it was, and the town as it was purchased into being — and I understood that I could not know which, not from paper. Paper had gotten me this far and paper had reached its limit. Everything now depended on people: on a ferry-woman's testimony, on survivors scattered and silenced, on whatever remained of eleven lives in eleven hiding places.

And it depended on the founding families, who had spent seventeen years maintaining a beautiful lie, and who had, I now understood with total clarity, every reason in the world to learn whether a census taker's questions had opened my eyes.

Which was when I heard the knock.

Three measured raps at the workshop door, formal as a summons, and through the frosted glass a silhouette I recognized — broad-hatted, upright, patient. Magistrate Corven did not make calls at dawn. Magistrate Corven sent cards.

But there he stood, and in his gloved hand I could see the white rectangle of an invitation, and I knew — with the certainty of a woman reading contours on a map she has just learned to distrust — that whatever was written on that card had been decided the moment old Brida Ferris opened her mouth.

I hid the original page ninety-one in my apron pocket, laid the altered proofs face-down, and went to answer the door.
