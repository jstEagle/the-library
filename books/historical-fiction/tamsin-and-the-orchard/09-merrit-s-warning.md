## 9. Merrit's Warning

Merrit Corven had learned early that a town could be read the way her father read ledgers — not by what was written, but by what had been ruled out. Marlow Bend's streets were full of ruled-out things. The gap where the laborers' row had stood, now a tidy green called Founders' Walk. The absence of any grave marker for the eleven families the river took in '31. The way old women went quiet when children asked about the winter the mill burned.

She had been raised inside those absences, taught to call them order.

So it galled her — truly galled, in a way she could not put a polite word to — to watch Tamsin Ashgrove walk through that ordered silence like a woman reading a letter addressed to someone else.

* * *

Her father had summoned her at dawn. He received her in his study with the shutters half-closed against the pale light, as though winter itself were something to be managed, and he sat behind his desk with his hands folded and his voice pitched low, which was how she knew he was afraid.

"The engraver girl has been asking questions," he said.

"Everyone asks questions."

"Not these." He slid a paper across the desk. It was one of Vane's census returns, copied out in the clerk's neat hand — the Ashgrove entry, with the orchard cottage circled twice in red ink. "Vane is thorough. Too thorough for his station. And Hollis's workshop has been lit past midnight three nights running. Burning oil costs money, Merrit. Grief does not keep such hours. Work does."

"What work?"

"That," said Magistrate Corven, "is precisely what I have sent you to find out."

He told her then — plainly, without flourish, because her father never flourished; he simply stated things until they became true — that the ice on the river would break within the fortnight, perhaps sooner with this warm wind coming up from the south. And when it broke, the first steamer of the season would come up from the capital carrying men hired at considerable expense: historians, archivists, a man from the Crown Land Office who owed Corven a favor older than Merrit herself. They would arrive, they would examine the town's records, and they would find them immaculate. A town without tragedy. A founding written clean.

"They will find it so," Corven said, "provided nothing new is added between now and then."

"You want me to watch her."

"I want you to *talk* to her." Her father's eyes came up from the desk, and there was something in them Merrit rarely saw — not anger, but a kind of tired patience, the patience of a man who had spent thirty years maintaining a wall and resented every hour of it. "You were both girls of an age once. Before she went to live with Hollis. You remember her."

Merrit remembered. She remembered a thin child with ink-stained cuffs who had appeared at the Ashgrove cottage one spring and been spoken of thereafter as if she'd always existed, the way the town spoke of many things. She remembered that the other children had not quite known what to make of her, and neither, in truth, had Merrit.

"She is nobody," Merrit said. "An apprentice finishing a dead man's book."

"Dead men leave dangerous books." Corven rose and went to the window, looking out at the frozen river with the expression of a man watching an enemy he could not arrest. "There was a copybook in Hollis's desk. I have reason to believe it survived him. If that girl has found it — if she has shown it to anyone —" He did not finish. Her father never finished threats; he let the silence do the work, and the silence always did.

"And if she hasn't?"

"Then this conversation need never have happened, and you may tell her the magistrate sends his compliments on the atlas. Go at midday. Take the carriage as far as the lane and walk the rest. I don't want the sight of our wheels outside her door giving the ferry-woman ideas."

Brida Ferris. Of course. Her father kept accounts even of people who thought themselves beneath accounting.

Merrit took her coat and gloves and went out into the cold, and only when she was halfway down the stairs did she admit to herself what she had not said aloud: that some part of her had wanted this errand. That Tamsin Ashgrove, with her ink-stained hands and her dead master and her midnight lamps, had begun to feel less like a nuisance and more like a question — and Merrit Corven had spent her whole life being forbidden answers.

* * *

The orchard cottage sat back from the lane behind a hedge gone wild with frost, its windows fogged from the forge-heat of the workshop beyond. Merrit dismissed the driver at the corner, walked the last quarter mile as instructed, and stood a moment at the gate gathering herself, because whatever she was about to do here, it would not be done twice.

Tamsin answered the door with a rag in one hand and turpentine on her breath, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her forearms ghosted with acid scars that caught the light like old watermarks. Up close she was thinner than Merrit remembered, and sharper — not pretty in the way the founders' daughters were pretty, but cut somehow, the way a plate is cut, all her softness engraved away into lines.

"Merrit Corven." Tamsin did not sound surprised. That was the first wrong thing. People were always surprised to see a Corven at their door.

"You know why I've come."

"I imagine we'll establish that between us." Tamsin stepped back from the doorway — not an invitation exactly, more the concession of a woman who had decided arguing on a doorstep undignified. "I'm cleaning plates. You'll forgive the smell."

The workshop was colder than Merrit expected, the furnace banked low, the long engraving table littered with copper squares and burins and little drifts of metal shavings that glittered like frost. On the wall hung proofs of the atlas — Marlow Bend rendered in Hollis's fine obsessive hand, every field numbered, every bend of the river named. Merrit had grown up under that map's authority. Looking at it now, she felt the peculiar vertigo of seeing a familiar face and wondering what it had looked like before the scar.

"My father sends his compliments," she said. "On the atlas."

"How kind."

"He means to present it himself. At the assembly rooms. With the historians' names printed in the program." She watched Tamsin's hands go still over the plate she was wiping. "You didn't know about the historians."

"I knew the ice would break eventually. Ice does."

"The steamship *Meridian*, out of the capital. Professor Maren's people — no, not Maren herself yet, her advance men, the ones who decide what's worth her attention. They'll be here within days of the thaw, and my father means to give them a finished, settled history before they've unpacked their bags." Merrit pulled off one glove, finger by finger, buying time. "Which brings me to why I've really come."

"And here I'd hoped it was the compliments."

"Hollis kept papers."

Something moved behind Tamsin's eyes — quick, guarded, quickly shuttered. "A man keeps papers."

"A copybook." Merrit said it flatly, watching. "Child's hand. Eleven names."

The silence that followed had weight. Outside, a crow crossed the white sky, and somewhere down the lane a cart creaked, and Tamsin Ashgrove stood very still among her copper plates with the rag hanging forgotten from her fist.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Tamsin said.

"Yes," Merrit said quietly, "you do."

* * *

Here is what Merrit had prepared, walking the frozen lane: the offer, dressed in velvet. Here is what she had rehearsed before the glass while her maid pretended not to listen: *my father will give you the workshop freehold, your name on the title page, patronage for life, a place in the town's history beside the masters* — all of it true, all of it real, her father's offers were always real, that was what made them terrible — and underneath it, unspoken but perfectly legible, the rest: *or you will be ruined, quietly, administratively, the way everything inconvenient in this town is ruined.*

She delivered it nearly as rehearsed. The lease converted to freehold. The atlas published under both their names — Hollis posthumous, Tamsin living, engraved by *Tamsin Ashgrove* in letters her own mother would have wept over. A commission from the county. A future in which no one ever asked her again who owned the orchard cottage before the Ashgroves.

"In exchange?"

"The copybook burns. In your furnace, tonight, and I watch it burn. Any notes Hollis made toward it — letters, drafts, whatever else he hid in that desk. And you finish the atlas as it stands." Merrit nodded at the proofs on the wall. "The town as it is. As it has been for sixteen years."

"And if I refuse?"

"There is no refusing, Tamsin. There is only choosing which version of you survives the spring." She heard the cruelty in it as it left her and hated it, and kept going anyway, because the alternative was to stop and admit what the cruelty was covering. "My father broke a printer's hands in '34 for less. He broke a schoolteacher's livelihood for less. He doesn't rage, he *administrates*. You'll lose the lease. Brida Ferris will lose her license. Every debt your master ever carried will come due at once, called in by men who hold the paper. And when the historians arrive, they will be handed a complete and beautiful record, and you will be a footnote at best, or at worst a name in a margin, scribbled out."

Tamsin set down the rag. She did it slowly, deliberately, the way she did everything, and when she looked up there was no fear in her face at all, which frightened Merrit more than begging would have.

"You said eleven names," Tamsin said. "How would you know the number? If there were no row, no flood, no eleven children — how does your father know the count, Merrit?"

It was a good question. It was the question Merrit had lain awake turning over since her father's mouth had given the number away at dawn, casual as arithmetic, and she had understood then that he had slipped, or worse, that he had never once in her life thought of those children as anything but a number to be managed — including, possibly, her.

"I know what I've been told," Merrit said.

"No." Tamsin came around the table, and up close her eyes were the color of the river before ice, grey-green and moving over something dark. "You know what you've been *allowed*. There's a difference. I used to think paper was neutral — that a map just recorded the world and left it alone. Then a man with a census book asked me one question too many, and I lied for the first time in my life, and I've been pulling threads ever since. Your father wove every one of them, Merrit. So before you stand in this workshop and offer me my own ruin wrapped in ribbon, ask yourself what he's told you about yourself. Ask him why he knows there were eleven."

The words landed harder than Merrit had prepared for. She reached for the counterblow — *you are nothing, a foundling with borrowed tools* — and found, to her horror, that she could not say it, because it had stopped feeling true somewhere in the last minute, or had perhaps never been true at all, and there are only so many walls a person can defend before she starts wondering whose wall she's standing on.

"The offer stands until the ice breaks," Merrit said instead, and her voice came out quieter than she intended. "After that, it won't be mine to give."

"Then I'll answer you before the ice breaks."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have left." Tamsin turned back to her plate, dismissal and invitation at once. "Good day, Miss Corven."

* * *

Merrit walked back down the lane faster than she'd come, gloves clenched in her fist though the cold bit through them, and it was only at the gate that she allowed herself to stop and look back at the cottage — at the smoke rising thin from its chimney, at the dark shape moving behind the frosted glass, bending over copper and truth.

Her father would ask what answer she'd gotten. She would tell him the girl was proud, and stubborn, and would come around — a report calibrated to buy time, because time was suddenly the only currency either side had. The warm wind was already moving in the treetops. Two days, maybe three, and the river would groan awake, and the steamer would come, and everything would be decided.

But it was none of those calculations that stayed with her on the walk home. What stayed was the copybook — eleven names in a child's hand, ten struck through, and the eleventh waiting. What stayed was the way her father had said *eleven* without flinching, as though he counted them yearly, the way other men counted their money.

And what stayed, most of all, small and sharp as a burin point, was the thought she had been refusing since dawn:

*If there were eleven children scattered for their own good — who counted nine?*

She climbed the hill to her father's house with the wind rising at her back, warm and wrong for February, smelling of thaw. By nightfall the whole town would hear the ice beginning to talk.
