## 10. The Orchard Cottage

The key had hung on its nail in the workshop for as long as Tamsin could remember — a black iron thing, heavy as a pistol, tagged with a scrap of leather stamped *ORCHARD* in Master Hollis's cramped hand. She had taken it down a hundred times without once wondering why a man who kept no property at the cottage should keep its key in his workshop, among his plates and acids and the locked drawer where his money lived.

She wondered now.

She had not slept. The copybook lay open on her workbench where she'd left it before dawn, eleven names struck through in a child's careful pencil, and beside them that single surviving initial — *T.A.* — unstruck, patient, waiting for her like a trap she had already stepped in. She had told herself all night it meant nothing. That there were other T.A.s in the world. That initials were cheap as rain.

But her hands had begun to shake somewhere around the third hour, and she knew what shaking hands meant. They meant the body knew something the mind was still refusing to read aloud.

She dressed in the dark, took the lantern, the key, and the crowbar Hollis used for lifting crate lids, and went out into the last cold of February.

* * *

The orchard road ran west out of Marlow Bend along the high bank of the river, past the tannery and the disused lime kilns, and then climbed through bare apple trees gone wild with neglect — forty years untended, Brida would have said, though Tamsin had never thought to ask anyone what the orchard had been before the Ashgroves bought it. That was the trouble with questions. Once you started asking where things came from, you could not stop at the edge of your own life.

Frost furred the grass in silver whorls. The trees stood in rows so regular they could only have been planted by hand, by many hands, and she counted them as she walked because counting steadied her: thirty-one, thirty-two. Someone had planted this orchard deliberately, in straight lines, someone who expected to eat from it for years. The Ashgroves had owned it only nine. Before that—

Before that was a blank, smooth as an unscribed plate. She had never once wondered at the blankness. That frightened her more than anything else: how thoroughly a person could be taught not to look.

The cottage appeared through the trees low and grey, its thatch sagging, windows blind with grime. It looked smaller than memory held. Everything about her childhood was going to look smaller than memory held, she suspected, if memory turned out to be a thing that had been built for her rather than grown in her.

The door gave to the key with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, dust stood ankle-deep in the corners and hung in the air like ground glass when her lantern stirred it. A table. Two chairs. A cold hearth with a kettle still on its crane, rusted to the iron. On the mantel, a small clay pipe and, beside it, a child's wooden horse with one leg snapped off — left where it fell, or left where someone wanted it remembered falling.

Tamsin stood in the doorway a long time, letting the light of the lantern find everything slowly, the way light finds the floor of a river. This was the Ashgrove cottage. She had been told stories about it: how old Ashgrove had kept bees here, how her mother — no. Not her mother. Mrs. Ashgrove, the woman whose name she carried, whom she could not remember at all, though she had been assured the woman had dandled her on her knee. Assured by whom? By Hollis. By letters. By paper.

Paper again. Paper everywhere, and every sheet of it lately turning in her hands like a card marked on one side.

She searched the ground floor first, methodically, because method was the only rope she had. Cupboards empty but for a mouse nest. Under the floorboards behind the hearth: nothing but a mummified cat curled like a question mark. In the bedroom upstairs, under the mattress frame, a tin box — her heart leapt, then broke — containing buttons. Just buttons. Dozens of them, bone and horn and brass, sorted by size into paper twists, the way a mother sorts buttons, the way a poor household hoards what can be sold later. She sat on the dusty bed with the box in her lap and did not know whose fingers had last touched these, and whether they were strangers' or her own, and the not-knowing sat on her chest like a stone.

It was nearly noon when she went down to the cellar.

* * *

The cellar door was beneath the stairs, and it had been painted shut — recently painted, she saw at once, the brushwork too fresh for a house abandoned decades ago. Paint over a door that nobody used. Her pulse ticked up a notch, the engraver in her noting details the way she noted flaws in copper: someone had cared about sealing this room, and caring costs money, and money is never spent on nothing.

The crowbar persuaded the door in three pulls. Cold air breathed out of the darkness, smelling of earth and apples gone to vinegar and something older underneath — smoke. Faint, mineral, baked-in. Smoke that had become part of the stone.

Stone steps. Seven of them. She counted going down because counting still steadied her, though it steadied her less each time.

The cellar was larger than the cottage above it — dug back into the hillside, brick-vaulted, the kind of space built to store a harvest for a village, not a family. Racks along one wall, rotted to lace. Barrels collapsed into their own staves. And against the far wall, where the hillside met the brickwork, a stretch of plastered stone, pale in the lantern light, wide as a barn door.

She swept the lantern across it and stopped breathing.

Initials. Eleven sets of them, carved small and low in the wall, at the height of children's hands. Not scratched in haste — cut with care, with whatever tool a child could beg or steal, each letter finished, each set arranged in its own little space as though the wall were a register and the children its clerk. Some had dates beside them, crude numerals. One had a tiny apple tree drawn beneath the letters, five strokes for branches, two for roots.

*T.R.*

*M.C.*

*A.Q.*

*B.F.*

*J.R.*

She read them downward, and the names from the copybook rose up to meet them one by one, struck-through names, dead-or-scattered names, and the cellar tilted gently around her like the deck of Brida's ferry.

Then she reached the eleventh.

Lowest on the wall, nearest the floor, carved by the smallest hand — and she knew that hand. Not recognized; *knew*, the way you know your own signature on a deed, the way your fingers know the weight of a burin before the eye confirms it. A particular slant to the crossbar. A certain deep-cut confidence in the curve, made by a child who had already been taught her letters properly and took pride in them.

*T.A.*

Her initials. Her hand. Cut into this wall when she was — how old? Six? Seven? — by a child standing exactly where Tamsin now stood, in a cellar Tamsin had never seen, in a house Tamsin had been told held nothing belonging to her.

The lantern shook. She set it down on a barrel head because she did not trust herself to hold it, and the light threw her shadow huge against the wall, across all those little letters, so that her silhouette seemed to stand over them like a giant over a graveyard — or like a woman reading a list of the dead and finding her own name at the bottom.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, and her voice came back off the vaulting flat and strange.

But it was possible, because there it was, and the tool-marks were hers. Twenty years of engraving had taught her to read a cut line the way a priest reads scripture — pressure, angle, hesitation, confidence. She could tell you which of Master Hollis's lines he'd cut tired and which drunk. She could tell a forgery at twenty paces by the cowardice of its curves. And these letters were no forgery. No one had cut *T.A.* into this wall to deceive her, because no one alive could have known she would come here, and no one could have matched a seven-year-old's grip so perfectly anyway. Children cut differently. Shallower on the pull. These were shallow on the pull.

She crouched down in the dust until her face was level with the letters, close enough to feel the stone's cold breath, and pressed her fingertips into the grooves the way the faithful press fingers into the feet of saints.

Nothing came. No memory. Not a flicker. She closed her eyes and reached backward through herself — through the workshop, through the Ashgrove house where she'd supposedly lived till she was orphaned a second time at nine, through the foundling story, the kind master, the apprenticeship — reaching for smoke, for a woman singing, for anything — and found only the smooth blank she'd always carried where other people kept their earliest years. She had thought it grief. Everyone had told her it was grief, the mind mercifully closing over the loss of both parents. Grief, yes, and God knows she had grieved.

But you do not grieve a childhood that never happened. You grieve the one that did. And the child who cut these letters had stood in a cellar full of other children's names, and had known them, and had belonged to them — and that child had been taken out of this place and given a new story the way you rebind a book in new boards, trimming away the old pages to make them fit.

Whose idea had the new binding been?

Hollis. The word arrived without being summoned, quiet and enormous. Hollis, who kept the key to this door in his workshop. Hollis, who had raised the eleventh child and never once let her walk the orchard road. Hollis, who had razored a page from the parish register and sealed a letter addressed to *the eleventh child* and hidden a copybook of ten struck-through names in his desk as if even his guilt had needed somewhere to live.

He had known. All those years at the bench, all those lessons in line and letter, all those evenings he'd watched her work with that look she'd always taken for pride — he had known whose hand he was teaching, and what had been done to her, and what had been done to ten others, and he had said nothing, cut nothing into any plate, written nothing she was allowed to find until he was safely dead.

She should have wept. She felt the tears standing in her eyes, hot and useless, but they would not fall, because beneath the grief was something harder and more useful: fury, clean as aquafortis, biting down through twenty years of varnish.

She got up. Her knees cracked. The cellar was very cold and very still, and the eleven names waited on their wall with the patience of everything that has been waiting a long time and is prepared to wait longer.

"Who are you?" she whispered to the small letters — to *M.C.*, whoever Merrit Corven might be beneath her silk; to *J.R.*, to *A.Q.*, to all the scattered rest. "And what did they do to us?"

The wall answered nothing. Walls never do. That was the whole of her trade: walls and paper hold what people put into them, and give back nothing unless someone comes asking with a light.

Well. She had a light now. She had a lantern and a burin and a workshop full of copper, and she was done believing that records were neutral, done believing that maps simply recorded the world. Maps recorded what the powerful paid to have recorded. She had learned that from a census taker's hovering pen and a razored register page and a child's copybook, and she would learn the rest from this wall.

She worked by instinct after that, moving through the cellar the way she moved through Hollis's papers, looking for what was *not* there. The floor near the east wall had been relaid — newer bricks, mortared clumsily, a patch the size of a grave. She pried at it with the crowbar until two bricks came loose, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, she found nothing but a shallow cavity, empty, swept clean, sized to hold a strongbox or a bundle of papers.

Someone had beaten her here. Or — colder thought — someone had emptied it and left the cavity open to be found. A message in absence. *Look further.*

Or the answer wasn't buried at all. Hollis had never trusted the ground; he'd said so a dozen times, half-joking, half not — *earth gives things up to flood and shovel, girl, but a seal keeps until a hand chooses to break it.* A sealed letter, addressed to the eleventh child, sitting in his desk since before she could remember, waiting for precisely this hour.

Waiting for her to know enough to deserve it. Or to hurt enough.

Tamsin stood alone in the cold vault with the eleven names at her back, and understood that she was standing at the exact center of her own life, the hinge-point on which everything before was fiction and everything after would be fact. On the wall above the smallest initials, her shadow trembled with the lantern flame, huge and wavering, a figure out of someone else's map.

"I'm coming back," she told the names quietly. "All of you. I don't know how yet. But I'm coming back with paper enough for everyone."

She climbed the seven steps, and did not let herself count them.

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had shifted round to the south — soft, wrong, carrying the first smell of thaw off the river miles away. Somewhere downstream, under the ice, something boomed once, deep, like a door being tested.

She pulled the cottage door shut behind her, locked it, and stood a moment on the frost-killed grass with the key burning in her fist.

Then she turned toward town, toward the workshop, toward the desk with its sealed letter — and began, for the first time in her life, to run.
