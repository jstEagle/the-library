## 11. Hollis's Letter

The seal broke with a sound like a small bone snapping.

Tamsin had carried the letter up from the cellar in her apron pocket all afternoon, feeling it against her hip as she went through motions that no longer meant anything: banking the stove, trimming the wicks, wiping burin shavings from the bench where she had worked for eleven years. The paper was warm from her body by the time she sat down at Hollis's own desk — his chair still held the shape of him, or she imagined it did — and turned it over twice before she could bring herself to slide the knife under the wax.

The wax was black. He had used black for nothing else; every commission, every client's proof, every letter to the London houses had gone out under plain red. Black was what he sealed his private accounts with, and there were only three of those in the drawer, and two of them were blank.

The third was this.

*To the eleventh child,* the front read, in his upright engraver's hand, each letter cut as clean as if he had graved it into copper instead of inked it onto rag paper. *If you are reading this, then you have found the copybook, and you have not burned it.*

Her breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door is opened in winter.

*So much for my hope that you would be sensible.*

She almost laughed. It came out of her as something between a laugh and a sob, one syllable, and then she pressed her knuckles to her mouth and made herself be quiet, because the workshop was listening. It always listened. She had grown up inside its silences and knew their grammar: the tick of the cooling press screw, the settle of the roof timbers, the river muttering under the ice beyond the wall. All evening they had sounded like an empty house. Now they sounded like witnesses.

She read.

*You will want to know why I never told you. I have begun this letter eleven times — that number again, eleven, it follows me like a debt — and every version begins with an excuse, so I will strike them all and begin with the truth, which is this: I was a coward, and then I was worse than a coward, because I taught myself to forget.*

*In the winter of '31 I was thirty-one years old and I believed exactly what you believe now, child, or what you believed last month: that paper is neutral. That a map records the world and owes the world nothing else. Corven's father came to me on the fourth of February with a plate under his arm and a purse on my bench, and he asked me to remove a row of houses from the county map. Not to move them. To remove them. As though they had never stood between the mill race and the water.*

*I told myself the houses were gone anyway. The flood had taken them; the fire had taken what the flood left. A map shows what is, I said, and what is, is ash. I took the purse. I cut the row out of the copper with my own hand, and I remember — God forgive me, I remember this precisely — that it was good work. Clean work. Some of the finest scraping I ever did, and I did it to make dead people into no one.*

*That is the first sin and it is the common one. Half this town committed it. But hear the second, which is mine alone.*

*Three nights later a woman came to my door with a child in her arms and frost in her hair, and she said, You are the mapmaker, and I said I was, and she said, Then you know what happened to us, because someone drew us off the earth. She put the child down on my bench — on the very bench where I had scraped them away — and she said, Keep this one honest, and she walked back out into the snow, and I never learned her name, and I have spent nineteen years being glad of that, because a name would have been a debt I could see.*

*The child was you.*

The workshop ticked and settled. Down the hill, faintly, the river groaned — a long shifting note, ice remembering it was water — and Tamsin found she had risen halfway out of the chair without knowing it, one hand flat on the desk, as though the letter had physically pushed her. She sat back down. Her legs felt borrowed.

*There were eleven of you. You know that now, or you suspect it, or you would not be holding this page. Brida Ferris took four across the river before the ice closed. The Quill woman — young then, stubborn even then — hid three in her school through the worst of it. The rest were scattered the way seed is scattered: not planted, thrown. And the eleventh was left on a mapmaker's bench because her mother had seen what I could do with a line, and gambled that a man who could erase a town might also be able to keep one small truth alive inside himself.*

*I failed her mother in every way but the last one.*

*I registered you as my niece. I said your people were Ashgroves from upriver, distant kin, and I said it so many times that it stopped being a lie. That is the trick of guilt, child — it does not gnaw, it sedates. By your fifth year I truly remembered you as kin. By your tenth I had built the whole cottage story, the orchard, the aunt who died of fever — I gave you a past the way Corven's father gave the county a past, and I told myself mine was kinder because I loved you, and love is the coin every liar pays himself in.*

*Do you understand yet? We are the same crime, you and I. They erased a row of houses. I erased a girl. And I did it with better tools, because I had been trained in exactness.*

Tamsin set the letter down.

Her hands were steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. She looked around the workshop — at the racks of plates, at the great press with its iron screw worn bright by decades of his grip, at the window where the orchard showed white and bare against the dusk — and everything in it had changed weight. This room had raised her. She had thought of it as a sanctuary her whole life, the one clean place in Marlow Bend, and now she understood that it had been built over a grave the way the county atlas was built over the laborers' row: deliberately, lovingly, with the best materials available.

She picked the letter up again.

*Now to the atlas, because the atlas is the reason for this letter, and I will come to it plainly since I have earned no right to circle it.*

*I left it unfinished on purpose.*

*You will have guessed some of this already — you are too good a hand not to have noticed the gaps, the provenance notes missing, the survey dates that do not agree. Hear it stated: I could have finished it in eighteen months. I chose not to. Every year I delayed, I told the London house the engravings wanted refinement, and every year the lie grew another ring like a tree, until the delay itself became the truest thing in the file.*

*Because here is what I learned on the night I scraped the row from the copper, and what I have paid nineteen years' interest to learn properly: a finished map is a verdict. Once it is bound and sold and shelved in libraries, it stops being an argument and becomes the world. Whoever holds the pen at the moment of finishing decides what the children of '61 will believe about the winter of '31. Corven knows this. It is why he presses me — gently now, with dinners and honors instead of purses — to complete the work. He does not want a map. He wants a verdict, rendered in my hand, certified by my reputation, sealing his father's crime under gilt and leather forever.*

*And I will not give it to him. But neither will I render the opposite verdict myself, and I will tell you why, and this is the hardest sentence I have ever written: because I am not clean enough to write the truth. My name is on the altered plates. If I engrave the row back into the world, every defender of the erasure will say, Ah, but the master repented — sentiment, conscience, an old man's fancy — and they will bury the truth under the argument about me instead of the facts themselves. A witness with blood on his hands testifies badly. I have known this since '31 and I have hidden behind it for nineteen years, and I am done hiding, and this letter is how.*

*So I leave the atlas unfinished, and I leave it to whoever finds the copybook and does not burn it.*

*Finish it honestly, and it damns me along with Corven. Finish it as he wishes, and it damns the drowned. Or refuse it altogether — burn the plates, walk away, let the river keep its dead — and the lie wins by default, which is the same as winning. There is no innocent choice, child. I have arranged it so there can be none. That is not cruelty. It is the only inheritance I have that is worth anything: the certainty that this decision cannot be made comfortably, by anyone, ever.*

*If you finish it honestly, they will come for you as they came for me — softly at first, with dinners. Merrit Corven will look at you the way her father looks at ledgers. Pike will sign whatever he is told; he has been signing since he was nine years old and learned that survival is a signature. Vane the census man may surprise everyone; men who count things for a living sometimes count the wrong thing once and are ruined by it, in the good way. And Brida Ferris will hold, because Brida has been holding since before you were born, and the ferry is the one road in this town no founder has ever dared close.*

*They will offer you everything I could never give you. Legitimacy. Patronage. A name in the front matter, embossed. Take nothing. Not because the offers are false — some of them will be sincere, that is the horror of it — but because anything they give you, they can take back, and what they take back they will take back publicly, and public taking-back is how towns like this execute people without rope.*

*Keep the copybook. Keep the names. And when you doubt yourself — you will doubt, the doubting is not weakness, it is the work — go down to the orchard cellar and look at the wall.*

*You carved it, child. You were six years old and I brought you there the week after your mother left you, and I told you nothing, and you carved your initial among the others because some part of you knew, the way water knows downhill. I have kept that cellar locked in your memory by never once mentioning it, and you have kept it locked by never once asking, and between us we built a silence so complete I half believed it myself.*

*But walls remember. Walls are the one record no founder has learned to buy.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop tormenting you, though torment is perhaps the honest word for inheritance.*

*Your mother sang. On the walk from the door to the bench, she sang — low, three lines of something I did not know, and I have tried for nineteen years to find the song and failed, and I am sorry for that failure most of all, because a song is the one document I could not preserve for you. Everything else I kept: the copybook, the letter, the plates beneath the floor of my own making. But the song went out the door with her and the snow took it, and if you ever meet a survivor who remembers it, you will know your people by it, and I envy you that moment so fiercely that I have had to forgive myself in advance for envying a child.*

*The atlas is yours. The choice is yours. I have signed nothing below because signatures are what got us here.*

*— H.*

There was no date. Of course there was no date. Dates were how records caught you, and Hollis had spent nineteen years teaching himself to live outside them.

Tamsin read the letter a second time, and a third, and somewhere in the third reading the candle guttered and she did not trim it, and the words swam in and out of the light like fish under ice. Certain sentences surfaced and stayed. *I erased a girl.* *Some part of you knew, the way water knows downhill.* *Walls are the one record no founder has learned to buy.*

She thought of the razored page in the parish register. She thought of the census taker's hovering pen, and of her own voice saying *the Ashgroves have held the orchard land these forty years*, the first lie of her life, delivered so smoothly that Elias Vane had written it down without suspicion. She had lied the way Hollis scraped: cleanly. Well. The family talent.

And underneath all of it, rising slowly like a flood finding its level, the other thing — the thing the letter had named and she had not yet dared feel.

He had chosen her. Not as a niece, not as an apprentice, not even as a daughter of convenience. He had taken the eleventh child onto his bench and built her a false past and taught her his craft and loved her — she did not doubt the love, the love was in every cramped line of this letter, pressing at the margins — and he had done it all inside a lie, so that the love itself was load-bearing masonry in the architecture of the erasure. She had been raised inside the cover-up the way the town was raised inside the atlas: comfortably, warmly, without one honest wall.

Grief, she discovered, could be reframed. It did not lessen. It simply changed what it was grief *for*. All winter she had mourned a master, and the mourning had been simple and clean and had given her purpose: finish the atlas, honor the dead man, keep his standards. Now the dead man stepped out from behind his own monument and handed her the chisel and said: the monument is a test, and I designed it, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry, and both of those are true.

She blew out the candle because her eyes were burning and the dark was kinder.

In the dark, the workshop spoke its nightly grammar — tick, settle, the river's long groan — and Tamsin Ashgrove, née nothing, niece of nobody, eleventh child of a drowned row, sat in her master's chair and let the new shape of herself settle around her like a coat cut for someone else.

Then she stood, took the lamp, and went down the outside stairs into the cold, across the frozen garden, to the orchard cottage.

The cellar door stuck, as it always stuck, and she shouldered it the way she had since childhood, and the smell rose up to meet her — earth and stone and apples long rotted to cider-sweetness — and she stood at the top of the steps with the lamp held high, looking down into the dark at the far wall, at the pale scatter of marks she had never once let herself examine closely in nineteen years of living above them.

Eleven initials. Carved by children's hands. One of them hers.

She went down the stairs slowly, one step at a time, the lamp throwing her shadow huge against the vaulting, and she did not yet know what she would do when she reached the wall — whether she would touch the carving or strike it or simply stand there until dawn — but she knew one thing with the whole of her recovered heart:

Tomorrow she would walk down to the ferry, and she would ask old Brida Ferris to teach her the names of the ten, because a letter was testimony but a wall was evidence, and she intended to need both.

Above her, far off toward the river, the ice groaned again — longer this time, deeper, like something vast turning over in its sleep — and Tamsin, standing in the cellar of her own erased childhood, heard it and understood that the thaw had begun, and that she had perhaps a season, perhaps less, to decide what the world would read.
