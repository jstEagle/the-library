## 12. Gathering the Eleven

Brida Ferris kept her ferry moored in a slough of black ice below the town landing, and she received Tamsin that morning the way she seemed to receive everything — with a flat look, a jerk of the chin, and no wasted words.

"You came back, then." She was mending net, though the river gave up nothing in February but slush and the occasional drowned branch. Her hands moved through the work without her eyes. "Sit on the crate. Don't touch my lines."

"I want to find them," Tamsin said. "The others. The ones who lived."

Brida's hands stopped. For a moment the only sound was the river working under its lid of ice, that low grinding complaint that had become, in the past week, the sound of Tamsin's own heartbeat.

"Find them," Brida repeated. "And then what?"

"Testimony. Names. Anything written, anything remembered. I have Hollis's letter and I have the wall and I have eleven initials, and none of it will stand against Corven's paper unless living people stand behind it."

"And you think they'll stand for you." It wasn't quite a question. Brida threaded her needle through the mesh and pulled it tight with a small vicious snap. "Girl, do you know what happened to the last person who asked around about '31?"

"No one has asked in twenty years."

"That's what happened to him." She set down the net at last and looked at Tamsin properly, and her eyes were pale as shallow water and just as hard to see into. "A tax man from the county seat. Came sniffing after the mill fire — insurance claim, nothing political. Asked three questions too many. Left on the next boat and never filed his report. Or so they say." She spat over the side into the slush. "Corven's father sat where Corven sits now. The family keeps its accounts current."

Tamsin crouched by the crate anyway, close enough that the old woman could not pretend not to hear her. "You told me once to ask about the winter of '31 and decide if I wanted the answer. I've decided. I'm one of them, Brida. The eleventh. Hollis took me in off the row when I was four years old and let me believe I was something else for twenty years, because believing something else was safer than being what I am. Well. I know now. And I would rather be what I am and ruined than be what they made me and safe."

Something shifted in the ferry-woman's face — not softening, exactly. Recognition, perhaps. The look of a woman hearing her own young voice come out of someone else's mouth.

"Huh," she said. And then, grudgingly, as if each word cost her a coin: "There were eleven of you. Ten scattered, one kept local. You're the kept one, though I'd wager coin you didn't know it. The others went where the founders' charity sent them, which is to say wherever they'd cause least trouble."

"Where?"

Brida held up fingers, counting off against the wind. "Ada Quill, the Quill girl — she was six — they put her out as a servant to a schoolteacher over in Wexley parish, and the schoolteacher found she had a head like a slate and taught her instead. Teaches school herself now, down in Millbrook, if she's still there. That's two hours' walk and an easy ask; she'll see your face and know why you came whether you say it or not." A second finger. "Joss Renner. His story's shorter. He stole bread at nine years old, they transported him at fourteen, and he's been in Van Diemen's Land these seven years. If he's alive, he's not coming home, and if he comes home he won't thank you for digging him up."

"Then there are eight."

"There are eight within reach." Brida folded her hands atop the net, done giving. "But hear me, girl, before you go knocking on doors with the truth in your pocket. Not everyone wants it back. Twenty years is long enough to build a whole life on top of a grave. Some of them have built fine houses on it. You start pulling up floorboards, don't be surprised when the people inside fight you harder than Corven ever will."

* * *

Millbrook was two hours east through bare woodland, and Tamsin walked it in a silence broken only by her own rehearsed sentences. *I am Tamsin Ashgrove. I was born on the laborers' row. I am making a record.* By the third mile she had abandoned the rehearsal entirely; she understood that whatever she said first would be wrong, and that the right words would only arrive in the moment, or not at all.

She knew the schoolhouse before she saw the sign — a low stone building with smoke rising straight from its chimney in the still air, and chalk dust ghosting the windowsills. Inside, a dozen children recited their sums in ragged unison while a woman in dove-grey moved between the desks. She was perhaps thirty, upright as a fence post, with a face made for sternness that had been worn, somewhere along the way, into something gentler.

Tamsin waited in the cold until the bell rang and the children spilled out like water finding every gap in a fence. Then she stepped inside, into warmth and beeswax and the particular smell of chalk that hit her like a fist under the ribs. She knew that smell. She did not know how she knew it.

The schoolmistress looked up from stacking slates and went very still.

"Yes?" she said. Carefully. As though the word were a door she might need to bar.

"My name is Tamsin Ashgrove," Tamsin said. "Of Marlow Bend. Master Hollis raised me — the mapmaker."

"I know who Hollis is. Was." Ada Quill set down the slate she was holding, aligned its edges with the stack beneath it, and did not look up. "What do you want?"

"The winter of '31. The laborers' row. The eleven children."

For a long moment the schoolroom was silent enough that Tamsin could hear the fire ticking in its grate. When Ada finally raised her eyes, they were dry and bright and furious in a way that had clearly been furious for a very long time.

"Close the door," she said. "And sit down. You look like you haven't eaten since yesterday, and I can't have testimony collapsing on me."

It was not agreement. But it was not refusal either, and Tamsin had learned in the past fortnight to take what the world offered and call it progress.

Ada poured tea without asking whether it was wanted, and sat across the schoolroom table from her, hands wrapped around her own cup as though the day were colder than it was.

"Brida sent you," she said.

"Brida told me where to walk. That's all."

"Mm. Brida never does more than she must and never less than she chooses." Ada studied her over the rim of the cup. "You have Hollis's jaw. Did you know that? The stubborn part of it. He used to bring you to the ferry landing some Sundays, when you were small. I watched from the boat. I never spoke to you." A pause. "I was afraid of what I'd say."

"What would you have said?"

"*Run,* probably." She laughed — a short, bitter sound with no humor in it whatsoever. "As if any of us ever ran anywhere useful. Sit there, girl. You want testimony? I'll give you testimony. But understand what you're asking me to risk. This post is mine. My name is on the lease, my wages feed me, and this school is the only thing I have ever built. If Corven learns that Ada Quill remembers being Ada Quill—"

"He already knows who you are. They all do. They placed you here themselves."

"They placed a child named Ada Quill here. Whether that child grew into a woman who keeps records is another matter entirely." She set down her cup. Something in her posture changed — the teacher's spine going rigid, the witness assembling itself behind the schoolmistress's face. "Ask your questions, then. I've kept answers longer than you've been alive."

* * *

She talked for two hours, and Tamsin wrote until her hand ached.

Not everything was usable — memory at six years old is a lantern in fog, showing bright circles and darkness between — but fragments came out of Ada with the terrible clarity of things seen by a child at eye level. The row itself: twelve houses in a line, painted the same grey-brown, gardens running down to the water. The names of neighbors. The night the ice dam broke — she remembered the sound, she said, a sound like God clearing his throat, and her mother lifting her through a window into snow. And she remembered the morning after the fire, standing in a line with other children on the riverbank, being looked at.

"Looked at," Tamsin repeated. Her pen had stopped.

"Like stock," Ada said flatly. "Magistrate Corven's father walked the line twice. He had a list. He matched faces to it and assigned us aloud, the way a man assigns horses. *This one to the Ferris woman, she owes us. This one to the parish. This one to Wexley.* He touched Joss Renner under the chin, tipped his face up like checking a horse's teeth. Joss bit him." For the first time something almost warm crossed her face. "They took Joss away in irons three days later. He was nine. Whatever became of him, we may lay at that man's feet alongside the flood."

"And the others? The families who took you in — did they tell you anything?"

"They told me my parents died of fever. All our parents died of fever. It was a very industrious fever, killed eleven households in one night and left the founders' land conveniently clear." Ada's mouth twisted. "I believed it until I was fourteen. Then I found my mother's Bible in a box of linens I was given when the old schoolteacher died. Inside the cover, in my mother's hand: *Sarah Quill, born of the row, wife of Daniel.* Fever doesn't write in Bibles, Miss Ashgrove. After that I started keeping a slate of my own."

"A slate?"

Ada rose, crossed to the schoolroom cupboard, and knelt before it. When she turned back, she was holding a child's writing slate, its wooden frame cracked and mended with wire, its surface crowded edge to edge with names in careful schoolroom script — dozens of names, some struck through and rewritten, dated and re-dated across what must have been years.

"The true names of the drowned," she said quietly. "Gathered piece by piece. From Bibles, from letters, from old women who talked too much before they learned better. Every time I learned a name I wrote it here, and every year I risked my post carrying it from lodging to lodging like a criminal." She set it on the table between them with a click that sounded final. "Twenty years I've waited for someone to ask me for this. I'd begun to think no one would. So yes, Tamsin Ashgrove — you may count me among your witnesses. Write it in your book: Ada Quill, schoolmistress, remembers."

Tamsin looked at the slate and could not speak. It was the first evidence she had held that was not stone or paper or her own uncertain memory — it was a life, carried openly through enemy country for two decades, waiting.

"Two more things," Ada said, as Tamsin carefully wrapped the slate in flannel. "First: Alderman Pike. You'll want him — he's one of ours, he was on the bank that morning, older than the rest of us by two years and sharp as a nail. He remembers everything."

"You say that as though it's a warning."

"It is. Pike remembers everything and has spent twenty years ensuring no one knows it. He signed their affidavit, girl — the one declaring the row never stood. He's alderman now, with warehouses on the wharf and a pew with his family name carved on it. Go to him if you must. But knock gently. Men like Pike keep their memories the way misers keep gold, and they bite the hand that asks to spend it."

* * *

Pike's house stood at the top of Wharf Hill, brick-faced and symmetrical, with a brass knocker polished to a shine that seemed designed to repel rather than welcome. Tamsin knocked anyway.

The servant who admitted her showed her into a drawing room furnished in the style of a man who had studied wealth from the outside and reproduced it faithfully: heavy drapes, a portrait of nobody in particular above the mantel, everything immaculate and nothing loved. Alderman Pike rose from his chair to greet her — a broad, comfortable-looking man in his late twenties, well-fed, balding early, with small quick eyes and the handshake of a fishmonger assessing a catch.

"Miss Ashgrove. Of course — the mapmaker's daughter. Your atlas is awaited eagerly, I'm told. Corven speaks highly of it." He seated her, waved a servant out, and smiled with practiced warmth. "To what do I owe the pleasure?"

"Alderman Pike. Were you born on the laborers' row?"

The smile remained. Everything behind it stopped moving.

"I'm sorry?"

"The laborers' row," Tamsin said. "Twelve houses below the mill. Drowned in the winter of '31, burned the same night, eleven children dispersed. You were the oldest of them. You were seven."

What happened to Pike's face was worth studying, and Tamsin studied it. There was fear in it — she had expected fear. What she had not expected was the contempt that arrived on top of the fear, fast and hard, like a door slamming.

"Young woman," he said, "I was born in Marlow Bend, to respectable parents, both deceased. I attended the parish school. I have built a business and a name from nothing, which is more than can be said for some. And I will not sit in my own house and be quizzed about fairy stories by—" he caught himself, recalculated, resumed the smile — "by an enthusiastic young lady who has clearly been listening to ferry-women's gossip."

"Brida Ferris was there. Ada Quill was there. I have a copybook in Hollis's hand listing eleven names, and yours is second from the top. *Josiah Pike.*"

He stood. It was done smoothly, courteously, a host ending an interview that had run long, but his hands were not smooth at all — they had curled slightly, the way hands curl around a thing they want to crush.

"Miss Ashgrove. You seem intelligent, so I will speak plainly, for your own good. Whatever papers you have found, whatever tales you've collected — drop them. There is no row. There is no affidavit worth the name. There is a town with a future, and a handful of old women with grievances, and there is you, a foundling playing at historian with a dead man's tools. Corven will make short work of you, and he will not be gentle, and no one — *no one* — will stand beside you when he does." He opened the drawing room door. "Good afternoon."

"One question," Tamsin said, not moving. "Your mother. What was her name?"

For half a heartbeat — no longer — something broke open in the small quick eyes. Grief, or rage, or both wearing each other's clothes. Then it shut again, seamless, and the alderman was looking down at her from his full height with nothing in his face at all.

"Good *afternoon,* Miss Ashgrove."

The door closed behind her with a click as final as Ada's slate hitting the table.

* * *

She walked back toward Marlow Bend in the blue hour, the slate heavy in her satchel against her hip, and let herself take inventory. One witness gained, sworn and armed with twenty years of names. One witness refused, wealthy and frightened and lying through polished teeth — and lying, which meant remembering. One transported to the far side of the world, dead or worse than dead. Six more scattered somewhere between Wexley and the horizon, unlocated, unwoken.

Hope and resistance in equal measure, then. She had expected as much. What she had not expected was how much worse Pike's denial had landed than any threat from Corven. Corven was the enemy; she had known how to hold a shield against him since the founders' dinner. But Pike was one of the eleven. Pike was the row refusing to save itself, and that was a wound no shield covered.

At the top of Ferry Lane she saw Brida's lamp burning by the mooring, and beyond it the lights of Marlow Bend spread out along the darkening river, every window of every founding-family house glowing steady and secure, lit by money that had flowed in over drowned ground.

She was halfway down the lane when Brida's voice stopped her.

"Girl." The old woman stood by her boat, and there was something in her stance Tamsin hadn't seen before — weight, urgency, the posture of a woman about to say a thing she'd decided not to say. "While you were walking, a boy came down from the town with word. The Renner lad's back."

Tamsin's breath stopped. "Joss? He's — he's here? In Marlow Bend?"

"Landed Tuesday on a coastal packet, paid his passage with coin nobody asks about. Keeps himself at the derelict lime kilns above the quarry. Won't come to town, won't show his face by daylight." Brida's pale eyes fixed on her. "Seven years in the colonies changes a man, and he went out angry to begin with. If you go up that path, go in daylight, go alone, and go knowing he may not thank you for dragging the row back out of the grave he's spent seven years walking away from."

Above them the ice groaned — long, deep, nearer now, as though the river itself were leaning in to listen.

"And Tamsin." Brida lowered her voice. "He's not the only one asking questions anymore. There's been a man at the landing twice this week. Fine coat, city boots, taking notes. Asking after shipping dates — when the ice breaks, when the first boat from the capital can dock." She paused. "Corven's historians are coming. Whoever wins the spring, they mean to have it written down. The only question left is whose ink dries first."

Tamsin tightened the strap of her satchel over the slate, over the copybook's absence, over everything she carried, and looked up at the black shapes of the lime kilns on the ridge.

"Then I'll go and collect my convict," she said.
