## 13. The Alderman's Denial

There were three clocks in Alderman Josiah Pike's house on Cordwainer Row, and every one of them was right twice a day, which was more than could be said for most men.

He wound them each morning at six, before the fire was lit, before his wife's maid drew the curtains, standing in his dressing gown in the blue half-dark with the key cold in his fingers. It was the only hour of the day he still recognized himself in. By seven he was the alderman — the collar, the ledger, the measured voice that never rose because it never needed to. By nine he was Pike of Pike & Sons Timber, whose barges owned the southern moorings and whose credit was quoted like scripture. But at six, winding the clocks, he was briefly nobody, and nobody was the closest thing to a name he had been given at the start.

He did not think of the winter of '31. That was the discipline of it. Men believed forgetting was a failure of will, a softness, a door left unlatched. They were wrong. Forgetting was a trade, like cooperage or engraving, and it took twenty years of daily practice to do it well. He had served his apprenticeship. He was a master of it now.

So when the girl came to his offices on a Tuesday in February — the cartographer's apprentice, Ashgrove's girl, the one with ink-stained cuffs and her master's stubborn chin — he was ready for her. He had been ready for her for two decades without knowing it.

"Mr. Pike." She stood in the doorway of the counting room with snow melting on her shoulders and a portfolio under her arm, and she looked at him the way no one in Marlow Bend dared look at an alderman: straight on, level, as if they were the same height. "I'm compiling provenance notes for Master Hollis's atlas. A formality. I need your confirmation of landholdings along the mill race for the years 1829 through 1832."

"A formality," he said, and gestured her to the chair across the desk. "Sit, Miss Ashgrove. You'll take tea?"

"I won't take up your morning."

"You'll take tea." He rang the little bell because courtesy was armor and he wore all of his armor in layers. While the maid poured, he studied her over the rim of his own cup. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen. Dark hair pinned carelessly. Hands that had held a burin since childhood — he knew the calluses, having once known hands like them from the inside. She had Hollis's way of sitting too still, of letting silence do her asking for her.

He answered the question she hadn't asked yet.

"The holdings along the mill race in those years were as the deeds record them," he said. "Corven. Harrowgate. My father. The foundry parcel, later consolidated by Corven. Nothing else."

"Nothing else," she repeated. Not a question. A weighing.

"The atlas will say what the records say," he said pleasantly. "Records are the bones of a town, Miss Ashgrove. A body can't stand without them, and it can't stand on two sets."

"No," she said. "I suppose one set is cleaner." And she drank her tea, and thanked him, and left, and her boots rang on the stair like small verdicts.

* * *

That night he did not sleep, which was rare enough that he marked it. He lay beside his sleeping wife in the good feather bed he had earned with thirty years of careful silence and watched the ceiling, and the ceiling became the underside of a roof he had not thought about in twenty years, and he lay very still and let none of it come.

This was the method. Not suppression — suppression was for amateurs and it always failed, because suppressed things press outward like water under ice. No: redirection. When the winter of '31 knocked, you gave it something else to look at. He gave it timber prices. He gave it the tonnage ledgers of 1844. He gave it, if he was desperate, the memory of his daughter's christening, the smell of lilies, his wife's hand tight on his arm — safe memories, purchased memories, memories that belonged to the man he had built out of the boy who drowned.

Because that was the truth of it, though he would have cut out his tongue before saying it aloud: the boy who had lived in the laborers' row had drowned too. He had drowned in '31 along with everything else, and the thing that walked out of the floodwaters and into the Corven household as a ward, and then into a counting house as an apprentice, and then into Cordwainer Row as an alderman, was a new creature entirely. Josiah Pike. Solid name. Solid man. Built on solid ground that had, until the spring thaw of 1831, belonged to other people's grief.

* * *

The affidavit sat in his memory the way a scar sits in flesh: not painful anymore, exactly, but present, weather-sensitive, aching when the season turned.

March of '32. The magistrate's study, the good fire, Corven behind his desk not yet the patriarch he'd become but already the shape of him, and four men seated with pens and paper while a lawyer read aloud the language they had all agreed upon: that no row of laborers' dwellings had ever stood upon the parcels in question; that the flood of February 1831 had damaged only unimproved land; that the eleven children subsequently boarded among respectable households had been foundlings of no settled origin; and that any claim advanced to the contrary was malicious fiction.

Malicious fiction. He remembered the taste of the ink. He remembered his own hand — a boy's hand still, seventeen and shaking — and Corven's voice, gentle as a confessor's: *You sign for your own sake, lad. There's nothing behind you now but water.*

And there wasn't. That was the thing the girl with the ink-stained cuffs would never understand, would never be able to understand, because understanding required standing where he had stood: sixteen hundred miles of nothing in every direction. His parents gone down with the row when the ice dam let go and the river took the low ground in the dark. His sister — he touched the word carefully, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — his sister dispersed to a farm upcountry and never written to, because writing would have been a thread, and threads unravel cloth. The row itself burned the same night, the mill fire eating the last physical argument anyone could have made. And eleven children, orphaned, homeless, property-less, aged three to fourteen, standing in the slush outside the magistrate's house while the founding families divided the drowned men's leases between them with the smooth efficiency of men dividing a pie.

What was he to have done? Refused? A child of nothing, refusing the only roof offered? Insisted on the truth, and been turned out, and starved, and become a cautionary tale instead of an alderman? Truth had not fed him. Truth had not taught him figures. Truth had not put the deed to Cordwainer Row in his hand. Corven had done those things, and Corven had asked one price, once, on one page, in March of '32, and had never spoken of it since — which Pike had learned to recognize as the man's particular mercy, or his particular cunning; after twenty years the two were indistinguishable.

Memory is a luxury of the unruined. He had arrived at that sentence years ago, turning it over like a coin he'd found in the street, testing its weight, finding it true. The rich could afford to remember. Remembering cost nothing to a man with land and standing and a town full of people obliged to agree with his version of himself. But the poor paid for memory in bread. The ruined paid in blood. He had been ruined, and he had traded memory for survival, and survival was not a sin — survival was the first law, older than any commandment, older than any map. Every animal in the winter wood knew it. Only men had invented a morality that punished the living for outliving.

He told himself this the way other men said their prayers: nightly, kneeling, believing hard.

* * *

But there was the matter of the clocks.

He had bought the first one in 1838, a case clock tall as a coffin, from a peddler who swore it had come out of a great house downriver. He had not known why he wanted it. He had told his wife it kept excellent time. Then the second, and the third, and the mantel pieces, and the watch he consulted a dozen times a day, and it was years before he understood what he was doing, and when he understood he did not stop, because stopping would have been an admission and admissions were the one currency he refused to hold.

A man who has lost his history measures time instead. He builds it, winds it, corrects it, makes it obey. Every chime in that house was a small victory over the flood, which had come in the night, without warning, without regard for what hour it was. He controlled the hours now. Let the river try anything.

His wife thought it a harmless eccentricity. His clerks thought their master a precise man. Only Pike knew that the ticking was a wall, and behind the wall was the sound of ice going out, and behind that sound was a woman singing — low, half-remembered, some song about green fields or far water — and he did not open that door. Twenty years of practice. Master of the trade.

* * *

Thursday brought the second visit, and this one he had not prepared for.

Not the girl. An old woman. Brida Ferris, ferrywoman, wrapped in a coat that predated the flood and looking at his counting room the way a gull looks at a fish stall. His clerk tried to turn her away and Pike heard himself say, absurdly, "Show her in," and hated the word *show*, as if she were a curiosity, as if she didn't know exactly what she was.

She sat where the girl had sat and looked at everything he had built — the mahogany, the brass rails on the stairs, the framed charter of the timber guild — with an expression that was not envy. That was the worst of it. It was inventory.

"You've done well, Joey," she said.

No one had called him Joey in twenty years. The name went through the wall like it wasn't there. He kept his face still. He had a face that kept still; it had won him contracts.

"Mistress Ferris. If you've business—"

"I've no business. I've a message." She leaned forward, and he smelled river on her, ice and weed and rope, the whole drowned world rising off her shoulders. "There's a girl asking questions. Hollis's girl. She'll come to you for testimony, sooner or later, and I'm here to tell you what I told the others: you can give it or withhold it, but don't lie to her. She's got her master's papers and my sworn dictation and Ada Quill's slate, and she's got something else you haven't priced yet."

"Which is?"

"She doesn't remember either," Brida said. "And she's going to. And when she does, Joey, she'll understand us all better than we understand ourselves, because she'll have stood on both sides of the forgetting." She rose, joints cracking like river ice, and paused at the door. "You signed once. I never blamed you. You were a babe in a wet coat. But signing again — that's a grown man's sin, and there's less thaw left in me than there used to be, and I mean to see it set right before I go under."

Then she was gone, leaving river-smell in his counting room, and Pike sat for a long time with his hands flat on the desk, feeling the floor of his life tilt a degree, the way ground tilts just before it moves.

* * *

Friday evening, the note came from the magistrate's house. Cream paper, black wax, five lines in the secretary's hand requesting the honor of Alderman Pike's attendance on Sunday, and beneath the secretary's flourish a single line in Corven's own script, added after, the way a man adds a hook after baiting it:

*Bring your pen, Josiah. We have documents to refresh.*

Pike read it twice. He read it a third time standing at the window while the lamps of Marlow Bend came on below him one by one, obedient lights in an ordered town, a town without tragedy, a town built on a page he had signed.

Refresh. As if memory were a document. As if the affidavit were a living thing that needed feeding.

Down in the hall, the tall case clock struck eight, and eight, and eight — no, he corrected himself, listening properly, it struck eight once, cleanly, and the sound went through the house like a nail driven home, and Josiah Pike, master of forgetting, keeper of hours, survivor, liar, orphan of the laborers' row, folded the magistrate's note into his breast pocket, directly over his heart, and began to decide what kind of man he would be when the ice went out.
