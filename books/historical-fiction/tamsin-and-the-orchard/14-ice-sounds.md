## 14. Ice Sounds

The first sound came in the night, and it was nothing like cracking.

Tamsin had expected cracking — the pistol-shot reports of ice splitting that she remembered from childhood winters, if indeed any part of her childhood was truly hers to remember. What she heard instead, lying awake in her narrow bed above the workshop with Hollis's letter folded beneath her pillow like a stone, was a groan. Long, low, and wet, as though something enormous were turning over in its sleep at the bottom of the river. It went on for the better part of a minute, rising in pitch until it became almost a note, almost a voice, and then it stopped so completely that the silence rang after it.

She sat up. The candle had guttered out hours ago. Through the frost-feathered window she could see the river, or rather could see where the river was: a pale grey expanse under a paler sky, running from the bend down past the mill ruins toward the ferry crossing. All winter it had lain there flat and dumb as a sheet of paper waiting for ink.

Now it was speaking.

*The thaw*, she thought, and felt the words land in her chest like a dropped plate.

She dressed in the dark — wool over wool, fingers stupid with cold — and climbed to the workshop, lighting only the small lamp she kept for close work so its glow wouldn't spill into the lane. The supplementary plate lay where she'd left it, wrapped in oilcloth beside the burin case, half-engraved. Eleven names along the flood line, seven cut clean, four roughed out. The row itself — thirty-two little houses in two ranks, drawn from Brida's dictation and Ada Quill's slate and Joss Renner's furious memory — was finished and burnished, the lines holding light the way a scar holds light.

She stood over it without touching it. In the lamplight the copper looked less like metal than like skin, warm-colored, faintly breathing.

*You have time,* she told herself. *The river is talking but it has not yet moved.*

Then the river groaned again, longer this time, and somewhere far downstream something boomed — one deep concussion, then another, spaced like footsteps of something walking up beneath the ice toward Marlow Bend.

* * *

Word traveled through a river town faster than weather.

By midmorning everyone at the market knew the ice had "gone rotten at the shallows," which meant the ferry would run late and treacherous; by noon they knew that a load of salt pork had been ordered from the capital warehouse in quantities that suggested buyers who intended to stay; and by the time the light went long and yellow over the western hills, they knew the thing Tamsin had been dreading since Merrit Corven first spoke it aloud in the orchard lane.

Historians. Engaged by Magistrate Corven himself. Two of them — a professor and his amanuensis — paid to travel by the first boat of the season, which the ice-sounds said would be soon. Days, perhaps. A week at the outside.

Elias Vane brought the news to her himself, arriving at the workshop door just as the dusk was thickening, his census satchel slung across his chest and snowmelt darkening the shoulders of his coat. He didn't come in at first. He stood on the step working off his wet gloves, and Tamsin saw that he had taken trouble to be seen arriving openly — that he wanted the street to watch him knock.

"You've heard, then," he said.

"Half the town's heard. The other half is inventing the rest."

"The part you haven't heard is the names." He stepped inside when she nodded him in, and stood warming his hands over the banked stove, speaking low. "Professor Ilsa Maren, of the Institute. And an amanuensis named Fenn. Maren wrote the monograph on the valley surveys of 1809. She's not Corven's creature, Tamsin — that's what frightens me. If Corven had hired a hack, we could hope for sloth. He's hired rigor."

"Rigor cuts both ways." Tamsin pulled the oilcloth back from the plate. "Rigor will want primary evidence. We have primary evidence. We have initials carved in cellar plaster and a slate with dead men's true names on it and Brida's sworn testimony."

"Corven knows that too. That's why he's spending money." Elias crouched to study the engraving, and she watched his eye move along the flood line the way it moved along a column of figures — hunting the discrepancy, loving the discrepancy. "He doesn't need to destroy your evidence. He needs to arrive at an official account before your evidence can organize itself into one. A history is a race, and he's bought the fast horse. When Maren docks, she'll be met at the landing by the magistrate, the alderman, the parish clerk, and a bound copy of the atlas with the official plates in it. She'll spend a fortnight being hospitably delayed while Pike swears whatever affidavit Corven puts in front of him. By the time anyone mentions the laborers' row, the inquiry will already be concluded, signed, and shipped."

"Then we finish before the boat."

He looked up at her. In the stove-light his face was tired and honest and afraid, and she realized he was afraid *for her*, which was a thing no one had been in a very long time — not since Hollis, and Hollis's fear had always been for himself, guilt wearing love's clothing.

"The ice broke early last night at Hallow Ford," Elias said quietly. "Twelve days ahead of any year in the parish register. Corven's men were seen measuring the channel depth at the landing yesterday. They're dredging it wider, Tamsin. You don't dredge a channel for spring trade. You dredge it so a steamer can come straight up to the town stairs instead of anchoring below the shallows." He straightened. "They're clearing the road for their historians. Every day the river loses is a day they're taking back."

After he left, Tamsin stood a long while at the window, watching the ice go grey and rotten in the failing light, and understood that the clock had stopped being a metaphor. It groaned now. It walked beneath the floor of the world on feet of water.

* * *

She worked until her eyes streamed and then worked past that.

There was a discipline to engraving that she had loved all her life without ever naming it: the way the burin demanded exactly what the hand had, no more, no less; the way error could not be apologized away but only cut deeper into ruin. Tonight the discipline turned against her comfort. Every name she cut had to survive being read by strangers a century hence, and so each letter took twice its usual time, and twice its usual toll.

*Maren Ashgrove. Drowned, the ninth of December, 1831.*

No. Not that one. That name belonged to the woman who sang, the one whose voice lived behind the smoke-memory that Brida was slowly coaxing loose from her. Tamsin set that aside, uncut yet, because some letters deserved a steadier hand than grief could give at midnight.

Instead she cut the ones she knew cold: *Ferris. Quill. Renner.* The parents' names above, the children's below — because the plate showed the row as it had been, and a row was made of families, and a family was made of names that someone had bothered to write down. Ada's slate had given her eleven households' worth. Brida's memory had filled the gaps where the slate had faded. And where neither reached, she had left the space blank and ruled a fine line around it — because Hollis had taught her that an honest map marked the limits of its own knowledge, that a dotted line confessed more truth than a confident solid one.

*A dotted line confesses more than a solid lie,* he used to say, tapping the burin against his teeth. *Remember that, girl, when some magistrate wants your coastlines tidy.*

She wondered, cutting, whether he had thought of her at all in those last months — whether the guilt had sat with him at this same bench, watching her bent over his plates, knowing what she was and what he'd let her believe she wasn't. The letter said he had chosen her deliberately, that leaving the atlas unfinished was itself a message. But a letter was also a document, and documents could flatter their writers. She would never know now whether he had truly meant to arm her or only meant to forgive himself.

The lamp burned low. Her right hand had gone numb past the second knuckle, and when she flexed it, blood welled slow and black-looking from a blister torn open hours ago. She wrapped it in rag without stopping, clamping the burin awkwardly between bandaged fingers, and kept cutting.

Somewhere below the window, the river groaned again. Closer now. Or maybe only louder because everything else had gone quiet.

* * *

Brida Ferris found her at dawn, which meant Brida had crossed the rotten ice to do it, which meant Brida had judged it worth her life.

The old ferry-woman came through the workshop door trailing river-cold air and the smell of tar, and stood looking down at the plate with her fists on her hips, reading it the way illiterate people often read — wholly, with the whole body, head cocked, lips moving over shapes she couldn't parse but trusted anyway.

"It's singing all night," Brida said, jerking her chin at the window and the river beyond. "You hear it?"

"I hear it."

"My mother called that sound the river counting." She lowered herself onto the work stool with the care of old joints, and her eyes, small and bright as a wren's, went from the plate to Tamsin's bandaged hand and back. "Counting the winters till it gets its drowned folk back, she said. Superstition. But I'll tell you what's not superstition, girl: I've run that crossing forty years, and ice that talks like this goes out within the week. Three days if the wind turns southerly. And the first boat up after ice-out is always—" 

"—Corven's steamer," Tamsin said. "With his historians aboard."

"With his *account* aboard, dressed up as historians." Brida leaned forward and tapped the copper with one cracked fingernail, precisely, on the ruled blank space where a name was missing. "And here's what I came to say, so mind me. You're cutting names of the dead, and that's right and proper. But dead folk don't dock at landings. Living ones do. And you've got living ones scattered from here to the coast who don't know yet that the reckoning's coming, or think it's already failed, or are like Pike and praying it fails. Joss Renner's lodging over the tannery and drinking himself blind every night since he came back. Ada Quill's slate is still hidden in her schoolhouse wall and she's still terrified of losing the only post she's ever held. And that alderman—"

"Pike will do whatever keeps him comfortable."

"Aye. But comfortable men can be made uncomfortable." Brida's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "What I'm saying is: the map won't argue for itself. Maps never do. People argue for maps. So while you sit there bleeding into your rags over copper, somebody's got to be gathering the witnesses and standing them up in a row before the boat arrives — and I'm too old to climb schoolhouse walls, and you're too necessary to that plate to leave it."

"I can't do both."

"No," Brida agreed placidly. "But there's more of us than there is of you. There always was. That was the whole trick of them, girl — they made eleven children believe they were alone, one apiece, dispersed like seed. Eleven alone is nothing. Eleven together—" She tapped the plate again, on the row of little houses. "Eleven together is a row."

She rose, joints crackling, and took her leave at the door with a look back that Tamsin would carry a long time: the old woman silhouetted against the grey morning, the rotten ice gleaming behind her like a blade laid flat on the world.

"I'll start with Ada today," Brida said. "You keep your hand moving. And Tamsin—" a pause, the wren-eyes sharp, "—when the river stops talking, that's when you run. Silence means it's about to break."

* * *

It stopped talking at dusk the next day.

Tamsin was at the bench when it happened — the groaning that had gone on for two days simply ceased, between one stroke of the burin and the next, and the silence that followed was so complete and so wrong that she set the tool down with a click that sounded like a shout. She went to the window. The whole river lay still under a sky the color of pewter, and the stillness was worse than any sound, a held breath, a drawn bow.

In the lane below, dogs began to howl, one after another, up the hill and down.

And then she saw Elias Vane coming up the street at a dead run, coat flying, hatless, and knew before he reached the door that the news had outrun the ice.

He burst in without knocking, chest heaving, snowmelt spraying from his boots. For a moment he could only stand and hold up one finger, gasping. Then:

"Landing master's boy just came through. The channel dredging is done — finished overnight, men working by lantern. And there's a telegram at the relay office, addressed to Corven, and the operator's boy is a friend of mine." He got his breath back in a single hard pull. "Steamer *Persephone*, out of the capital. Ice-out convoy, first vessel through. She cleared the lower locks this morning." His eyes went to the plate, to the four unfinished names, to the bandaged hand. "Two days, Tamsin. Maybe three if the wind fouls them at the narrows. Two days."

Tamsin looked down at the copper. Seven names clean. Four roughed out. One blank, ruled and waiting, that might never hold its letters now. Two days of daylight work, and her hand was already ruined meat, and the press needed testing, and the binding of the supplementary plate into the existing copies hadn't even begun—

"Then I need hands," she said, and heard her own voice come out level and strange, the voice of someone standing on ice that has begun to move beneath them. "Not mine alone. Go to Brida. Tell her what you told me. Tell her the river's stopped counting."

Elias hesitated at the door, and in that hesitation she saw the census taker weighing something — the man who verified households against a ledger that had lied for sixteen years, deciding once more which side of the record he stood on.

"And tell her," Tamsin added, turning back to the bench, taking up the burin with her bandaged fist, "to bring the others tonight. All of them. Even Renner. *Especially* Renner."

Outside, the river lay silent as an unwritten page, and the wind had begun, very softly, to turn south.
