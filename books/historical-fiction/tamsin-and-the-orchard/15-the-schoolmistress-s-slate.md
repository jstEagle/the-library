## 15. The Schoolmistress's Slate

The schoolhouse smelled of chalk dust and coal smoke and sixteen children who had run out into the grey morning like water through open fingers, leaving Ada Quill alone with the aftermath — forty slates to wipe, six inkwells to stopper, one boy's abandoned mitten thawing on the stove rail in a slow dark puddle.

She did not wipe the small slate first. She never wiped it first.

It lived at the bottom of her desk drawer, beneath the attendance ledger and the bundle of canes she had never once used, wrapped in an oilcloth that was going soft with age. She took it out now, as she took it out every afternoon when the room emptied, because twenty years of habit had worn a groove in her the way a drip wears stone, and habits cut in grief do not fill in.

The slate was a child's slate, no bigger than her two hands spread together. The frame was beechwood gone silver with handling. On its face, in white letters so fine they might have been mistaken for a teacher's careful demonstration of penmanship, were eleven lines of names.

Not the children's names. She knew the children's names; they were scratched into her heart where no eraser reached. These were the names of the drowned — the mothers and fathers of the laborers' row, the men who had fed the mill fires and the women who had carried water for the whole lower town and been repaid, in the winter of '31, with a river.

*Josiah Renner, wheelwright. Maren Renner. Tobias Pike, sawyer. Liddy Pike. Samuel Oakes. Ruth Oakes. Daniel Ferris. Susannah Ferris. Enoch Vane. Prudence Vane. And Abel Ashgrove, orchardman.*

Eleven households. Eleven hearths drowned out of existence in a single night of ice and fire. Sixteen years old when she wrote them, Ada had stood in this same room with the shutters barred and the stove cold because she could not risk even the smoke looking unusual, and she had written the true names of the dead in a child's hand on a child's slate because there was nowhere else in Marlow Bend they were permitted to exist.

She read them now the way she read them every day — lips moving, finger under each line, the old litany. *Josiah Renner.* Dead in the flood, they said, though Joss Renner his son had been transported for theft two years after, which was what happened to orphaned boys who stole bread from the men who had stolen their houses. *Tobias Pike.* His son had grown up to be Alderman Pike, which was a longer story and a colder one. *Abel Ashgrove* —

The schoolhouse door opened on a gust that set the fire snapping, and Ada's thumb came down over the last line before she had even decided to move it.

Brida Ferris filled the doorway the way weather fills a window. She was seventy if she was a day, wide as a door herself, in a man's oilskin coat crusted with river ice, and she looked around the empty schoolroom with the expression of a woman assessing whether a boat would hold.

"Miss Quill," she said. "Still teaching the town its letters."

"Some of them," Ada said. "Some of them teach me." She slid the drawer shut with her hip, smooth as closing a hymnal. "You're a long way off your ferry."

"Ferry's iced in till Thursday. I walked the bank road." Brida came forward without invitation, the way she did everything, and stood over the desks with their rows of carefully inked copybooks. "I've brought you a visitor. Or I've come ahead of one. Depends how you take it."

Ada felt the cold come off the woman and settle somewhere behind her ribs. Twenty years of keeping a secret teaches a person to recognize the shape of its approach. "Take what?"

"The winter of '31," Brida said. "I've told it. To the Hollis girl. Tamsin. You'll have seen her about — Master Hollis's apprentice, the engraver. Quiet girl, steady hands."

"I know her." Everyone knew Tamsin Ashgrove by reputation if nothing else; the atlas had been the talk of the town since autumn, and Miss Ashgrove's name was printed in the prospectus in letters finer than any child in this room would ever manage. "What has the winter of '31 to do with her?"

Brida looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing early the way February light fails, blue shadows pooling in the corners of the room, and somewhere down the hill a dog was barking at the river.

"It has everything to do with everyone," Brida said finally. "But mostly it has to do with the fact that the girl's found out the row existed. Found out it was erased. And she means to put it back." Her eyes went, deliberate and unhurried, to the desk drawer. "And I thought — being as you're the only other soul in this town I know of who's kept faith with those names — you'd want to decide for yourself whether you're in it or out of it. Before she comes asking. Because she will come asking. She's got her master's stubbornness, and she doesn't know yet whose that is."

Ada's hand had gone flat against the drawer front without her permission. She made herself take it away. Made herself fold both hands at her waist like a schoolgirl reciting.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, "by kept faith."

"No," Brida agreed pleasantly. "Course you don't." She pulled out a pupil's bench — she had to turn sideways to fit into it, and the bench groaned — and sat, and put her great cracked hands on the desk before her like a farmer setting down a sack. "Sit down, Ada. I'm not here to catch you out. I'm here because I'm tired, and I'm old, and I've carried my piece of this for twenty years alone except for the river, and the river don't answer back. If there's two of us, there's two of us. That's all. That's the whole arithmetic."

* * *

Ada did not sit. She stood at the window instead, watching the last light go off the frozen river, and let the silence stretch until Brida understood she would speak when she was ready and not before. It was a trick she'd learned in her first year of teaching, and it worked on magistrates' sons and ferry-women alike.

"You want to know why I did it," she said at last. "Everyone always wants to know why. As if the why were the hard part. The why is easy. The why is that I was sixteen and I buried my own mother that October, and in January the river ate eleven families and the town held a memorial service for a boiler accident, and I sat in this room — this exact room — and listened to Magistrate Corven tell a room full of children that God had called home nine souls in the night, nine, when I had counted eleven myself from the ferry landing with my own eyes, counting the lit windows that never lit again." Her voice stayed level. It had practice. "Nine. He gave them back two fewer than God took. And nobody corrected him. Not the parson. Not the elders. Nobody."

"And so you wrote them down," Brida said.

"And so I wrote them down." Ada turned from the window. "Do you know what a schoolmistress is in a town like this, Mrs. Ferris? She is a woman with no husband, no property, no vote, and no family but the families who pay her wage. I could be dismissed for a crossed word. I could be dismissed for no reason at all. Corven's father put me in this post when I was nineteen because I was clever and poor and grateful, and he reminded me of all three whenever it suited him. And I stood up in front of his grandchildren every morning and taught them reading and ciphering and the history of Marlow Bend, which is a history with a hole in it shaped like eleven families, and I smiled, and I curtsied, and I went home and I read the names off a slate meant for a seven-year-old's alphabet until I had them by heart, and then I read them anyway, because—" Her voice caught, just once, and she mastered it. "Because a thing unwritten can be denied forever. But a thing written is a witness. Even if it never speaks. Even if it only sits in a drawer. It *witnesses*. I wanted there to be a witness."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls a mouse moved its small household along.

"My mother taught me my letters," Ada said, quieter now. "On a slate exactly like this one. She died owing money to three of the men who signed the affidavit saying the row never stood. I used to think about that. How a debt gets forgiven and a family doesn't. How paper works. Which side of it you're on." She looked at Brida. "You said the girl means to put them back. Put what back, precisely?"

"The row. The names. All of it." Brida's voice had lost its bluster; what remained underneath was older and wearier and much more formidable. "She's found the copybook. Hollis kept it — the list of us children, the eleven. And she's cutting a plate. A second map, to bind into the atlas. The row as it stood, the flood line, the names of the drowned. She means to publish the truth inside the lie, stitched into the very book they commissioned to bury it." A grim almost-smile. "It's a beautiful wicked thing. I'd have thought of it if I'd had twenty more years and half her nerve."

Ada stood very still.

An atlas. Not a whispered testimony, not a smuggled letter, not a slate in a drawer that would molder when she died and be burned by whoever cleared out her rooms. An atlas. Printed. Bound. Set on the shelves of gentlemen and libraries and the offices of men who dealt in land, permanent as scripture, carrying the eleven names into every future year whether Marlow Bend liked it or not.

"They'll destroy her," she said. "Corven will destroy her. He'll ruin the workshop, ruin her name, and if she pushes him far enough he'll find a magistrate's reason to ruin her body too. He's done less for more."

"He's trying. Lease revoked already. My license under review. The Quill girl—" Brida stopped. Corrected herself, gently, watching Ada's face. "*You.* Dismissed. The notice goes to the board Monday, unless I miss my guess. He's sweeping the board clean of anyone who remembers."

Ada heard it land, and was surprised to find that it landed softly. Twenty years of waiting for the blow had a way of taking the sting out of its arrival. She thought of the notice she would receive — *the board regrets* — and of the drawer, and of the slate, and of what a dismissed schoolmistress had left to lose.

Nothing, she realized. Nothing at all. It was the freest arithmetic she had ever done.

"Then we haven't time to stand about," she said.

* * *

She knelt at the drawer, and Brida watched her take out the oilcloth bundle with the reverence of a sexton lifting a reliquary, and unwrap it, and lay the small slate on the master's desk in the middle of the empty schoolroom, in the last blue light.

For a moment Ada simply looked at it. Twenty years, and she had never once shown it to a living soul. There had been nights — bad ones, feverish ones, the winter her lungs went wrong and she'd lain listening to the ice boom on the river like cannon — when she had genuinely believed she would die with it unspoken, and she had made her peace with that, more or less, the way you make peace with a stone in your boot.

"There," she said. Her voice came out strange to her. "Read it. Aloud, if you would. I've read it alone ten thousand times. I should like to hear it in company, once, before I die of it."

Brida bent close. Her lips moved. Then she straightened, and cleared her throat, and read, slow and full, each name given its whole weight like a bell struck once:

"Josiah Renner. Maren Renner. Tobias Pike. Liddy Pike. Samuel Oakes. Ruth Oakes. Daniel Ferris. Susannah Ferris." Her voice caught on her own dead, and Ada watched her grip the edge of the desk and push through it. "Enoch Vane. Prudence Vane. And Abel Ashgrove, orchardman."

Silence. The stove settled. Out on the river, faint and far off, the ice made a long groaning sound, like a door opening in the floor of the world.

"Ashgrove," Brida said at last, carefully. "Abel Ashgrove. Orchardman. The girl's name is Tamsin Ashgrove. Hollis's apprentice. You see it, don't you. You see what that means."

"I see that the girl took the name of a drowned man," Ada said slowly, "or was given it, or — " She stopped. Something was pricking at her, some old teacher's instinct, the feeling of a sum that won't balance. She looked down at the slate, at the eleventh line, at *Abel Ashgrove*, and then past it, at the frame, at the wood.

And she saw it. What she had polished past ten thousand times without seeing, because the eye protects itself.

There was a twelfth line on the slate. Faint — scratched shallow, half-erased, done in a hurry by a sixteen-year-old's hand on some long-forgotten night and then scrubbed at with a wet cloth until only the ghost of it remained. She had never been able to read it. She had told herself it didn't matter.

Now, tilted to the window's last light, she read it.

Her mouth went dry.

"Brida," she said. "When the girl comes — and she'll come tomorrow, won't she, knowing you — there's something she'll need to see. Something I couldn't ever make sense of." Her finger hovered above the ghost-line without touching it, the way you hover over a sleeping thing you're afraid to wake. "There's a name on this slate that isn't one of the drowned. It was struck out — I struck it out myself, I remember striking it — but I never remembered *writing* it, and I never could read it again. Until tonight."

Brida leaned in. Read it. Read it again. And then the old ferry-woman sat down heavily on a child's bench, all her weather gone out of her, and said, in a voice Ada had never heard from her and hoped never to hear again:

"Sweet heaven. That's not a drowned name, girl. That's a *founder's* name. That name is carved over the courthouse door."

Outside, down along the black river, the ice boomed again — closer now, and longer, like something vast turning over in its sleep — and in the schoolroom the two women looked at each other across a child's slate, while the spring, early and merciless, began to break the world open.
