## 16. What Tamsin Remembers

Brida Ferris's house sat at the very lip of the river, so close that in spring the water had been known to lick her doorstep, and she had never once moved the doorstep. Tamsin understood now that this was not stubbornness. It was testimony. The woman had planted herself at the scene of the thing and refused to be washed away.

The house smelled of tar and woodsmoke and dried nettle. A kettle hung over the fire, and Brida set it to boil without asking whether Tamsin wanted tea, because Brida did not believe in asking permission for kindnesses — she simply performed them, like a ferry crossing, whether you'd booked passage or not.

"Sit," she said, nodding toward a chair by the hearth. It was a chair built for someone smaller than Tamsin. Someone younger. She sat anyway, and found her knees came up higher than they should have, and there was something about that — being folded into furniture meant for a child — that made her hands begin to shake before she'd said a word.

Brida watched her shake and did not comment on it. She poured tea into two mismatched cups and set one within reach of Tamsin's knee, and then she dragged her own stool around so that she sat facing her directly, knees nearly touching, the way a woman sits when she means to deliver a baby or pull a splinter from an eye. Up close, without the river wind between them, Brida Ferris was older than the town itself seemed. Her face was a map of channels, all of them running down.

"Now," she said. "I'm not going to ask you anything. Not yet."

Tamsin blinked. "You said—"

"I know what I said." Brida sipped her tea. Steam curled up into the deep lines of her face. "I said ask about the winter of '31, and then decide if you want the answer. But deciding comes after, girl, not before. First we find out what you're carrying. Most folk carry more than they know. They just set it down so young it sinks into the floorboards of them, and they walk around their whole lives on top of it wondering why the ground feels strange."

"I don't remember anything," Tamsin said. "That's what I came to tell you. I've been through Hollis's papers, I've read the copybook until the letters swim, I stood in that cellar yesterday with my fingers on my own initials carved into a wall I have no memory of carving — and I remember *nothing*. There is nothing under me. I am a woman standing on air."

"Are you." Brida said it mildly, the way another woman might say *is that so* about the weather. "And how do you know?"

"How do I know what?"

"That there's nothing under you. You felt the nothing just now, didn't you. Felt it plain as a missing tooth. That's not nothing, girl. That's a shape. Nothing doesn't ache. Only a hole does."

Tamsin opened her mouth to argue — and closed it. Because the hole did ache. It had been aching for six days, since the census taker's pen had hovered over his page and asked its quiet question, and every day since the ache had grown a new edge, sharper, better defined, until it was less like emptiness and more like a wound shaped exactly like a four-year-old child.

She drank her tea because her hands needed occupation. The cup was chipped at the rim and the chip caught her lip in the same place each time she drank, and this too seemed meaningful, everything seemed meaningful now, the whole world had turned into evidence.

"Brida," she said. "If I tell you what I do have — not memories, but — pieces. Rubbish. Things I've carried my whole life without knowing why. Would you know what to do with them?"

The old woman set down her cup. Her eyes, pale as river ice, went very still.

"Child," she said softly, "that is the only thing in this world I would know what to do with."

* * *

It began with the smoke.

Tamsin had always hated smoke — not feared it, which would have been sensible, but *hated* it, with a personal loathing that made no sense. Pipe smoke, chimney smoke, the sweet haze off a harvest bonfire: each one landed on her like an insult. Hollis had been a pipe smoker, and she remembered, with a stab of guilt sharp enough to draw blood, the elaborate strategies she had devised as a girl to make him smoke outside. The sulks. The headaches that arrived precisely when he lit up by the fire. He had always given in, always, grumbling and fond, taking his pipe out to the cold workshop steps, and she had believed all her life that she had simply won those battles through childish tyranny.

"He gave in because he knew," Brida said quietly, when Tamsin told her this. "Or half-knew. Go on."

"There's a song." Tamsin's voice had gone strange in her own ears, thinner, younger. "I don't know where it's from. Four notes, rising, then falling back. I hum it when I'm working — I didn't even know I hummed it until Merrit Corven heard me at the engraving bench last year and asked what it was called, and I said I didn't know, and she went white as milk and left the room. I thought she was ill."

"What are the words? If it has words."

"It has words." And here Tamsin stopped, because saying them aloud in Brida's kitchen was different from humming them alone at a bench over a copper plate. Saying them aloud made them *real*, and real things could not be taken back. She looked at the fire. She looked at the river through the small dark window, at the ice that groaned and boomed out there in the dusk like a great animal in pain.

"Sing it," Brida said. "Don't tell me. Sing. Songs keep what heads can't."

So Tamsin sang. Her voice cracked on the first note and steadied on the second, four tones rising and falling back, and the words came up out of her like water from a well no one had suspected was there:

*Row upon row, the water's low,*
*Lay your head where the poppies grow,*
*Mother's gone to fetch the bread,*
*Sleep, my lamb, the ice won't break—*

She stopped. The last line hung in the kitchen. *The ice won't break.*

Outside, right on cue, the ice broke — a long report, rolling upriver, followed by the groan of shifting weight, and both women sat motionless while it passed over them and away.

"Lullaby," Brida said at last, and her voice was wrecked and level at once, a voice holding itself together with both hands. "Laborers' row. Every mother on that row sang some version of it. That verse is the Marlow Bend one — the poppies grew along the millrace, see, that's why the founders' ballad puts poppies in the *mill owner's* garden instead, they stole even the flowers when they stole the row." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, as if drying dishes. "A woman taught it to Ada Quill once. A woman who didn't live to see the thaw."

"A woman singing." Tamsin whispered it. The fragments were moving now, loosening like ice in a current, and it terrified her more than any document had. "I have — I have a woman singing. Not the song, just — the *fact* of it. Dark hair coming loose from its pins. Smoke above her, and she's singing anyway, and I'm — I'm somewhere low. Under something. Under a table? There's a table leg. I can see a table leg and a floor and the light is orange and wrong, and she's singing so I'll know where she is. So I can crawl toward the singing."

"The mill burned the same night the dam went," Brida said. "Eleven children pulled out of that smoke and flood-water between midnight and dawn. I ferried three of them across myself, wrapped in sailcloth, while the founders stood on the bank deciding their futures like a parcel of land." Her jaw worked. "Go on. Don't stop now, whatever it costs. Stopping lets it sink again."

"I'm being carried." Tamsin's hands had come up without her volition, curled against her own chest the way a carried child holds its own shoulders. She stared at them as though they belonged to a stranger. "Across — it's cold, it's so cold, and the whole world is sliding, and there's a light swinging. A lantern. Someone is walking on the ice with me and singing under his breath because his breath is short, the ice cracks under every step and he sings *louder* when it cracks—"

"*Louder,*" Brida breathed.

"—and I'm not afraid. That's the worst part. I'm not afraid at all, because he keeps singing, and a voice that sings louder when the ice cracks cannot drop you. I know that. I knew it when I was four. I know it now." She looked up, and her eyes were streaming without her having noticed. "Who walks on ice singing louder, Brida? Who does that?"

The old woman did not answer at once. She rose, went to the window, and stood looking out at the black grinding river for a long time. When she turned around, her face had aged another decade in the space of a minute.

"Young Hollis," she said. "He was thirty-one that winter. Big man, loud man, laughed like a landslide — you'd never know it, seeing the bent quiet stick he got to be. He carried two of you across that night. I took the other nine by boat along the open channel below the dam, but the channel iced up mid-crossing and he wouldn't wait, so he walked the ice with a child under each arm and a lantern in his teeth, God preserve us, and everyone on the bank swore afterward they'd watched a dead man walking. And when he handed the second child over he looked at her a long time, and then he looked at the founders, and he said—" Brida's voice failed, steadied. "He said, *this one's mine, then. This one nobody counts.* And he carried her off to his workshop and that was the last anyone official ever saw of either of them."

The fire ticked. The river boomed. Tamsin sat in the child-sized chair with her tea going cold in her hands and felt the ground — the true ground, the ground she had stood on for twenty years — give way beneath her with a silence worse than the ice's roaring.

Hollis. Master Hollis, whom she had mourned as a master, grieved as a benefactor, owed as an orphan owes. Who had smoked outside because a burnt child flinched. Who had taught her letters on his knee, taught her the graver, the acid, the plate, taught her that maps record the world and never change it. Who had died leaving her an atlas and a lie, and a sealed letter she had not yet had the courage to open.

"He never told me," she said. "Twenty years under his roof and he never told me."

"No," Brida agreed. "And you never asked. That's how a lie that size survives, girl. Two people agreeing, without ever saying so, not to look down."

* * *

They talked until the candle gutted. Piece by piece Tamsin laid out her rubbish — the hatred of smoke, the song, the table leg, the swinging lantern — and Brida sorted it like a fishwife sorting catch, keeping this, throwing back that, connecting a knot here, cutting a line there. The smell of tar that made Tamsin inexplicably calm: sailcloth, the wrapping on the boat. The way she counted things in sevens without knowing why: seven steps to the cellar, seven strokes to sharpen a graver. The number of children on the row had been forty-three; seven families; Brida wrote it on the hearthstone in chalk and Tamsin stared at the figure as though it were a mirror.

And underneath all of it, threaded through every fragment like wire through wax, ran a single unbearable fact:

She had been loved. Before Hollis, before the atlas, before Marlow Bend decided what she was — she had been somebody's child, sung to, carried, wrapped in sailcloth, given a name that someone had carved into a cellar wall beside ten others. And then the town had reached down and lifted that life out of the record the way you lift a stone out of a wall, and had handed her a different story — orphan, ward, apprentice, Ashgrove — a story with no mother's voice in it at all, and she had lived inside it faithfully for twenty years, grateful, dutiful, *good.*

"I mourned him for a master," she said at last, hollow. "I should have been mourning him for a father. And I can't even do that properly, because the girl who was carried across the ice — I don't know her name. I know her initials. T.A. That's all the world left her. Two letters."

"Tamsin Ashgrove," Brida said slowly, "was invented by men who needed the wall clean."

"Then who am I?"

Brida crossed the room and took Tamsin's face in both her rough hands, and held it, firm as a mooring post.

"You're the eleventh child," she said. "You're the one he chose. And whoever you were before — your mother's daughter, that girl — her name isn't lost, child. It's written down. It's written in the one place no founder thought to burn, because no founder ever learned to read it." She nodded toward Tamsin's hands. "You're a cartographer. Where do names live longest?"

"On the plates," Tamsin whispered. "On the copper. Engraved."

"Hollis kept every plate he ever cut, and cut a few he never showed anyone. I've seen his cellar shelves. Whatever your name was, girl — it's down there in the metal, waiting for you to come read yourself home."

Tamsin stood on legs that did not feel like her own. Through the window the river was a chaos of black grinding plates, the ice going out at last, early and furious, and somewhere downstream — two days, perhaps three — a steamer full of historians was making ready to write Marlow Bend's story without her in it.

She picked up her coat. At the door she paused, and did not turn around, because if she turned around she would sit back down in that small chair and never leave it.

"Brida. When the letter says *the eleventh child* — Hollis addressed it that way. Not to Tamsin. To the child."

"Aye."

"Then he knew I'd have to choose to become her. He left me the choice." Her hand found the latch. "Tomorrow I open the letter. And tomorrow night I start cutting the second plate."

Behind her, the old ferry-woman said nothing at all — only, softly, began to hum four rising notes, falling back — and Tamsin walked out into the noise of the breaking river, carrying her recovered name like a coal cupped in bleeding hands.
