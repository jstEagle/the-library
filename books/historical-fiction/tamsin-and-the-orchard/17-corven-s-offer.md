## 17. Corven's Offer

Magistrate Ambrose Corven kept his study at the top of Corven House because he liked to see the river from above — the whole grey length of it, the ice holding its breath, the town arranged beneath him like a diagram of everything he had built and everything he had buried. From that height, Marlow Bend looked honest. Rooftops squared away like ledger entries. Smoke rising straight. A place where nothing had ever gone wrong.

He knew better. He had made certain the record said nothing else.

The summons went down the hill by hand, carried by his clerk, addressed in his own writing because a printed note could be refused and a handwritten one was an honor. *Miss Tamsin Ashgrove is requested to attend Magistrate Corven at four o'clock, regarding matters pertaining to the county atlas.* He read it over twice before sealing it, weighing each word as he would weigh coin. Not summoned. Requested. Not ordered to explain herself — invited regarding her own great work. The distinction mattered. Fear closed mouths; flattery opened them wider than fear ever could, and what Corven needed from Miss Ashgrove could not be taken. It had to be given.

He spent the hours until four preparing the room the way another man might prepare an argument. The fire was laid but not yet lit — she should watch him light it himself, an act of hospitality, hands unafraid of work. The decanter held the good Madeira, the bottle he saved for the surveyors' dinners. And on the table between the two chairs, angled so that anyone entering would see it first: a copy of Hollis's prospectus, twenty years old, the paper gone the color of weak tea. *A Complete Topographical Atlas of Marlow Bend and Environs, to be executed with the newest methods of steel-plate engraving.* Hollis's own ambitions, printed in his own confident italics. Corven had bought three copies of that prospectus over the years, from three different men who thought they were selling him paper. One of them he still kept in a drawer with his pistols.

At half past three he stood at the window and watched her come up Fore Street.

She walked like her master had taught her, or like grief had taught her — head down against the wind, shoulders set, a woman carrying something heavy even when her hands were empty. Young. That was the thing people forgot about her when they spoke of her craft, that she was barely past twenty and already the best engraver the valley had produced since old Manfred Ashgrove, whoever he had been, whatever name he'd carried before the orchard took him in. Corven had read the parish entries himself, all eleven, back in the autumn when the census taker's questions began to ripple outward like cracks across ice. Eleven foundlings. Nine scattered beyond finding or beyond caring. One dead of the fever in '38. And two still in Marlow Bend, if you counted generously — counting the girl raised as an Ashgrove, and the girl raised as a Corven.

His daughter. His ward. His Merrit, who had come to him at six years old with no history and a new name, and who had grown into a woman so like himself that some nights looking at her was like looking into a mirror left out in the weather.

That was the arithmetic of it, then. Two of the eleven under his roof or near enough, and one of them engraving maps.

*She doesn't know,* Merrit had told him, weeks ago, standing in this very room with her arms crossed and her jaw tight. *She thinks she's an Ashgrove. She thinks paper is God.* But Corven had lived sixty-one years among documents, and he knew what paper did when you leaned on it. It bent. And he had seen the girl's face at the founders' dinner — not the smooth presentation face she wore for the room, but the flicker underneath, quick as a fish turning, when old Corven himself had praised the atlas and called Marlow Bend a town without tragedy. She had looked at him for half a heartbeat the way a dog looks at a door it isn't sure is locked.

So he would open the door himself. Better that, than have her find it ajar.

* * *

She arrived on the stroke of four — punctual as an instrument, which pleased him — and stood dripping on his threshold with snowmelt on her boots and her eyes going at once, he noted, to the books along the wall, cataloguing, always cataloguing. He took her coat himself, refusing the maid, and saw the surprise she smoothed away too slowly. Good. Surprise was a crack, and he meant to pour warmth into every crack before the cold got there first.

"Miss Ashgrove. You'll forgive an old man for stealing an hour of an engraver's time."

"Magistrate." She gave him the curtsy the town expected and the directness it didn't. "Your note said the atlas."

"The atlas." He lit the fire while she settled, striking the match with unhurried care, watching her watch him. "Sit, sit. Wine?"

"Thank you, no."

"Water, then. Marta — water for Miss Ashgrove." He waited until the girl had come and gone and the door had closed, and then he let the silence do its work. Silence, in a magistrate's study, was a tool like any other. Most people filled it. Most people told you everything they feared while trying not to seem afraid.

Tamsin Ashgrove sat with her hands folded and let the silence stand. Interesting. Hollis had trained her well — or the work had, for the work did train a person, he supposed. Hours alone with a burin taught patience the way drowning taught swimming.

"You'll want to know why I asked you here," he said at last, "so I won't insult you by circling it. I've had a letter from the capital. The Historical Society means to send a commission when the river opens — Professor Maren's people, two of them, perhaps three, to survey the valley's records. Deeds, registers, the lot." He turned his glass slowly on the arm of his chair. "They'll want to see your atlas, naturally. The finished plates. They'll write it up in their reports, and your name will travel further than any map you ever cut. Master Hollis dreamed of exactly that. It's in his prospectus — I keep his papers in trust, you know, as executor of his estate's affairs." A small lie, seamless. "I've read his plans more times than my own correspondence."

"He wanted the atlas to be definitive," Tamsin said. Her voice was level, but he saw her fingers press together, knuckle whitening against knuckle. "Accurate."

"*Definitive.*" He smiled at the word as though tasting it. "Yes. That was Augustus. Accuracy was his religion, and it cost him, as religions do. You knew him better at the end than any of us. Tell me — did he die believing the work was done?"

Something moved behind her eyes. "He died believing it was nearly done."

"And the rest?" Corven spread his hands. "Forgive me. I don't ask to pry. I ask because I mean to help you finish it properly — and because I know what the capital's historians can do to a small town's reputation, and I'd spare us both the experience."

* * *

He rose then, and crossed to the shelf where he kept the valley's founding documents — the ones fit for showing — and drew out a folder, laying three sheets on the table before her with the reverence of a priest arranging relics. The original land grants, 1794 and 1801. The incorporation charter. And the deed to the mill parcel, signed by his grandfather, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood.

"These are what the professors will see," he said. "Clean chain of title, generation to generation. A town founded by men who bought fairly, built honestly, and prospered by their own labor." He tapped the mill deed once. "This is worth money, Miss Ashgrove. Not to me — I'll be in the churchyard before any of it matters to me. To your neighbors. To the widow Hearn whose pension comes off the mill rents. To the ferry rights, the school fund, the whole lattice of obligations this town runs on. A clouded title doesn't just embarrass a magistrate. It starves widows."

"I hadn't proposed clouding anything."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You hadn't proposed anything. Yet." He returned to his chair and sat, leaning toward her, and now he let the warmth come fully into his voice, the warmth he had used on juries for thirty years, on creditors, on his daughter, on himself. "But I'm told questions have been asked lately. By the census man, for one. Vane — a diligent creature, diligent men are useful but tiring. And I'm told Master Hollis left his workshop to you entire, plates and papers and all, and that you've been going through his effects." He paused, precisely long enough. "Which is right and proper. An executor should know what she holds."

"I'm finishing his atlas. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," Corven echoed, softly, and let it hang. Then, before she could arrange her face around the lie: "Miss Ashgrove, I'm going to tell you something that will be unpleasant, and then I'm going to offer you the finest opportunity this valley has produced in your lifetime, and you may judge for yourself whether the two are connected."

He watched her go very still. She was cleverer than Merrit believed. Merrit, who had grown up learning to read ledgers, could not read people; the engraver read everything.

"In the winter of 1831," Corven said, "the river flooded. There was an ice dam above the bend — the winter was brutal, the worst in living memory — and when it broke, it broke hard. The laborers' row below the mill went under. Eleven households, mostly Irish and Cornish men hired for the mill expansion. Nine adults drowned. Eleven children survived, because children float, and because Brida Ferris rowed the shallows all night like a madwoman, God bless her, and hauled them out one by one."

He said it plainly, without ornament, and watched the color leave her face and return in patches. He did not enjoy this part. People assumed men like him enjoyed it. But surgery was surgery, whether performed with a blade or a sentence, and a surgeon who flinched killed his patient.

"The same night — and this the flood has always obscured — the mill burned. Whether the fire started the dam's collapse or the collapse brought down a lamp line, no inquiry ever settled. The founders — my grandfather among them, and I'll speak no ill of the dead including my own blood — faced ruin. Insurance disputed. Debts called. And a valley full of grieving laborers with grievances, and a charter that required, in effect, order." He refilled his own glass, though he didn't drink. "So they did what frightened powerful men do. They simplified. The flood became a 'spring freshet, minor.' The fire became a chimney accident. The laborers' row became vacant land, purchased quietly, consolidated. And the eleven children — that was the one decent thing in the whole wretched business, and I say that knowing how it sounds — were placed out. Foundlings, given names, given trades, given lives. Scattered so thoroughly that grief couldn't gather around them like a crowd around a scaffold."

Tamsin's voice came out thin. "You're confessing this. To me."

"I'm informing you," Corven corrected gently. "There's a difference, and it matters to both of us. No court in England would hear a confession from a private conversation, and no historian will either — I've checked the law, and I've checked the practice. What I'm doing, Miss Ashgrove, is trusting you." He let that settle. "Because I believe you deserve to hear it from a man's mouth rather than find it in a razored register page, alone, at night, with no one to tell you what it meant. Someone owed you that. Hollis should have managed it years ago, but Augustus was a coward in exactly one direction, and it was the direction of kindness."

Her hands had come apart in her lap. He saw her fighting the tremor and winning, mostly. "Why tell me now?"

"Because of what happens next." He set down the glass and named his price. "The historians arrive with the thaw. They will dig through everything, and they will find the seams, because there are always seams — I know where every one of them is, because my family sewed them. If they find them raw, they will write a report. *Marlow Bend: a case study in documentary suppression.* The title writes itself; those people love a title. Land claims challenged. Rents frozen. Your widow Hearn's pension suspended pending review, and every obligation downstream of it. The town spends a decade in litigation and dies of respectability." He shook his head slowly. "Or —"

* * *

"— or we give them something better than a scandal. We give them a monument."

He stood again, restless now, letting his energy fill the room, and this was the true performance, the one he had rehearsed in front of the dark window at midnight: not the threat, never the threat, but the vision. "Think of what you hold, girl. The finest engraved atlas this side of London, and its maker dead, and its completion in the hands of his acknowledged heir — acknowledged by me, publicly, if you accept. I will endow the publication. Five hundred pounds, more if the plate-work demands it. Your name on the title page as sole cartographer — not Hollis's posthumous shadow, yours. The Society will receive it as a gift from Marlow Bend, presented by its young magistracy, and Professor Maren will spend her career footnoting you instead of excavating us. Every map school from here to Edinburgh will teach from your plates." He turned, and gave her the last of it, the part he knew would cut deepest, because he had read her face at the dinner and understood what moved her. "And the town gets its history straightened. Not falsified — *straightened*. A town without tragedy is not a lie, Miss Ashgrove. It is a mercy. Those eleven children grew up to be a schoolmistress and an alderman and a ferry-woman and — " he met her eyes and did not blink — "an engraver. Would any of them thank you for digging up their parents' names and hanging them on a hook labeled *drowned, forgotten, erased*? Memory is a gift only to those who can afford it. For everyone else it's a tax."

The silence after was long. The fire ticked. Somewhere below, a door closed; Merrit, he guessed, listening at the stairs as she had been forbidden to, and he let her listen. Let her hear what compromise sounded like from the outside.

Tamsin Ashgrove stood up.

She did it slowly, and when she spoke, her voice was steady in a way that told him he had misjudged something fundamental, though he could not yet say what. "You've known who I am. All along."

"Since September."

"And you invited me to your dinner. You praised my work. You let me stand in your hall and drink your wine."

"Yes." He did not apologize. Apology was currency and he never spent it early. "Because everything I said tonight stands regardless of your parentage. The offer is real. The patronage is real. Your name in print is real. I am not buying your silence with flattery, Miss Ashgrove — I am offering you the only version of the truth that helps anyone, dressed in the only clothes that will carry it to London." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to the register he saved for verdicts. "Omit the row. Cut no second plates, bind no corrections, answer no census man's questions. Finish the atlas as Hollis drew it — as Hollis, under pressure, redrew it, though I suspect you've found that already. Do that, and you leave this house as the most celebrated cartographer this county has ever produced, with five hundred pounds and a patron. Refuse —" he sighed, genuinely, the sound of a man burdened by necessity — "and I grieve to say the lease on the workshop reverts to the estate's trustees, the ferry license comes under review, and the schoolmistress Ada Quill finds her position examined, since she's held it these twenty years on a certificate that was always irregular. None of that is a threat, child. It's simply weather. It moves whether you stand in it or not."

She looked at him for a long moment. And then she said, quietly, "My mother sang. While the water came up. Didn't she."

It was not a question, and it undid something in the room that none of his preparation had braced for. He had planned for anger, for tears, for bargaining. He had not planned for a girl of twenty asking after a drowned woman's voice with the calm of someone reading coordinates off a chart.

"I wouldn't know," Corven said. "I wasn't there."

"But you know there was singing." She gathered her shawl. At the door she stopped, one hand on the frame, and did not turn all the way around. "Thank you for telling me what happened, Magistrate. Truly. You're the first man in this town who treated me as though I could bear it." A pause. "But you've made one mistake. You think the atlas is finished and I'd only be adding to it. It isn't. My master left it unfinished on purpose — I understand that now — so that someone would have to choose. And I choose the way he should have."

She was gone down the stairs before he could answer, her footsteps fading into the ordinary noise of the house, and Ambrose Corven stood alone in his warm study with the good fire burning and the founding deeds laid out on the table like a losing hand he had shown too soon.

From the landing, soft as snowfall, came his daughter's voice: "Well, Papa?"

He looked at the window, at the river below, white and silent and holding, and felt through the floorboards the faintest tremor of the ice beginning to move.
