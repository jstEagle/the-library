## 18. Refusal

The magistrate's study smelled of beeswax and cold ash, as though the fire had been lit for appearances only and never truly allowed to burn. Tamsin sat in a chair too fine for her coat, her hands folded in her lap to keep them from trembling, and watched Corven pour two glasses of madeira with the unhurried grace of a man who had already won.

He set one glass before her. She did not touch it.

"You'll forgive the theater," he said, settling behind his desk. "I find that difficult conversations go better with something in the hands."

"Then I'll keep mine empty."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Respect, perhaps, or its close cousin, disappointment. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, silver at the temples, with the kind of voice that had spent thirty years being obeyed and could no longer be raised above a murmur without sounding like thunder.

"Do you know," he said, "when Hollis first came to me about the atlas — this would have been nine years ago now — I told him he was the best thing that ever happened to Marlow Bend. A town is a story, Miss Ashgrove. Most towns are badly told. Ours was going to be told properly, for once, by a man who understood that a map is a promise." He turned his glass slowly on the desk. "And then he began asking questions. And the promise became a problem."

Tamsin kept her eyes on him. "Master Hollis is dead."

"Yes. And I grieved him. Genuinely." He said it simply, and she believed him, which was somehow worse. "But grief doesn't finish atlases. You have. And now the atlas exists, and the question is what it will say when the historians from the capital come to read it."

"The atlas says what the county says."

"The county says what I tell it to say." He smiled, and there was no cruelty in it, which frightened her more than cruelty would have. "Come now. You've been to Brida Ferris. You've read the copybook. You've stood in that cellar — don't look so surprised, girl, I was born in this town; I know every stone of it, including the ones other people wish weren't there. So let us not waste each other's time pretending you are merely a cartographer."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock counted with soft authority.

"I am offering you," Corven continued, "the thing you want most. Not money — though there would be money. Patronage. The atlas published under the town's seal, distributed to every school and library in the province, your name engraved on the title page beside Hollis's. Marlow Bend remembered as a generous place, and you remembered as the woman who made it so. That is not nothing, Miss Ashgrove. That is immortality, offered politely."

"And the laborers' row?"

"Was a row of houses on land that now belongs to lawful owners, washed away by an act of God twenty years before you were old enough to hold a graver." He spread his hands. "Eleven children were taken in by families who could afford to take them. That is not erasure. That is charity, poorly recorded. Paperwork fails in floods. Everyone knows this."

"Everyone who says so has been paid to."

His smile did not waver, but the room grew colder by a degree. "You mistake me for a man who threatens. I don't threaten. Threats are for men who fear their opponents. I am simply describing the weather. Rivers flood. Leases expire. Schoolmistresses grow old. Ferry licenses come up for review. These things happen, Tamsin Ashgrove, whether or not anyone wills them, and a wise woman reads the current before she sets out." He lifted his glass toward her. "Finish the atlas as Hollis began it. One map. A clean map. And everything I have described is yours by Friday."

Tamsin looked at the madeira, amber and still, holding the firelight like a small trapped sun.

She thought of Master Hollis in his last winter, coughing into a rag while his hand still moved across the copper, cutting lines so fine they seemed less drawn than whispered. She thought of the razored page in the parish register, the wound in the paper where eleven names should have been. She thought of Brida Ferris's hands on the ferry rope, cracked and steady, and of Ada Quill's slate hidden behind twenty years of arithmetic, and of the initials in the cellar wall cut by a child's hand that was, impossibly, her own.

*Every map is an argument.* She didn't yet know she would write those words someday. But she felt them forming in her now, in the marrow, where beliefs are actually made.

"My master bled for the truth of that county," she said. "You know it. You pressed him until he bled."

"He complied."

"He complied," she agreed, "and it killed him slower than the consumption did. And you think I will sell the same wound to you at a better price because you've wrapped it in patronage." She stood. Her legs held. She was almost surprised. "No, Magistrate."

"No?" He sounded genuinely curious, a scholar encountering an unexpected result.

"There will be two maps. There may be more than two. Every name that was struck from your records will be cut back into the copper, line by line, and bound into every copy of that atlas, and when Professor Maren and her historians step off the steamer, they will read both versions — yours and the truth's — and they will ask you why the truth needed hiding for twenty years." Her voice did not shake. Later she would wonder whose voice it had been; it hadn't felt entirely like hers. "You offered me immortality. Here is mine: I would rather be forgotten for telling it than remembered for burying it."

For a long moment Corven said nothing. Then he set down his glass with a small, precise click, like a lock turning.

"Well," he said. "There it is."

He did not raise his voice. He did not call for anyone. He simply looked at her with something close to sorrow, and Tamsin understood that the refusal had cost her nothing yet — and that everything it would cost was already moving, out in the town, in offices and ledgers and other men's hands, like water finding its level.

"You may see yourself out," he said. "Mind the step. It's been loose all winter. I keep meaning to have it mended."

* * *

The lease notice was nailed to the workshop door when she returned, still damp from the paste, the ink barely dry. She read it twice because the first time the words refused to cohere.

*NOTICE OF TERMINATION OF TENANCY. Whereas the premises known as the Hollis engraving workshop, Mill Lane, are held under lease from the estate of R. Corven, Esq., and whereas said lease contains provision for termination upon ninety days' notice or immediate vacation upon breach of covenant, the tenant is hereby required to surrender possession within seven days...*

Seven days. The ice would not break for another week at least. The historians would arrive to find the workshop emptied, the press dismantled or seized, the plates — *her* plates, the supplementary plate half-engraved with the flood line and eleven names — impounded as assets of a defaulted tenancy.

She pushed open the door. The workshop was cold; she had banked the furnace low that morning, meaning to return by midday. Now the light came gray through the south windows and lay across the workbench where the supplementary plate sat under its cloth, half-finished, the river rendered in lines fine as hair, waiting for the row of houses that would rise along its bank out of pure stubbornness.

She touched the cloth and did not lift it. If she lifted it, she would start working, and if she started working she would not stop, and she had errands that could not wait, because Corven had not waited either.

* * *

Brida Ferris met her at the ferry landing with the news already in her face.

"They came at noon," the old woman said, coiling rope with hands that did not hurry even now. "Two clerks and a constable. License suspended pending review. 'Irregularities in the renewal record.'" She snorted. "Thirty years I've run this crossing. My renewal record is cleaner than the church Bible. They didn't even bring a boat to take my place. Just left the sign and went." She jerked her chin toward the far bank, where the ferry sat idle against the ice-fringed pilings. "So now nobody crosses unless they swim, and nobody swims in March."

"Brida—"

"Don't." The rope stilled in her hands. "Don't wear that face at me, girl. I've been poor before. I was poor the night the dam broke, and I was poor after, and I'm poor now, and none of it has ever stopped me saying what's true. What stops me is dying, and I'm in no hurry." She looked out over the gray ice, and her voice dropped. "But understand what he's done. He hasn't punished you. He's cut the roads. No ferry, no workshop. He means for you to be alone with your copper plates and nowhere to print them and no way to fetch your witnesses. He's not fighting the truth. He's starving it."

Tamsin's throat tightened. "Then we feed it another way."

"That's the spirit." Brida almost smiled. "Now get off my landing before the constable comes back and finds you consorting with an unlicensed ferrywoman. Scandalous company you keep."

* * *

Ada Quill was packing when Tamsin reached the schoolhouse.

It was the smallness of it that undid her — the slate wiped clean for the last time, the chalk box squared, the pupils' copybooks stacked in a basket with string tied round them. Twenty years of mornings reduced to one basket. Ada worked steadily, folding her shawl, straightening the master's desk, and did not look up until Tamsin's shadow fell across the floorboards.

"They were efficient," Ada said mildly. "A letter arrived at four o'clock yesterday. Dismissal effective immediately, 'for cause to be enumerated.' For cause to be enumerated — twenty years, and they haven't even invented my crime yet. They'll think of something. They always do." She tucked a last book into the basket. "The trustees signed it. All three. Mr. Pike's signature was shaking, if ink can shake. It can. I've seen it."

"Ada, I am so sorry. This is because of me. Because of what I'm doing—"

"It is because of what *we* are doing, and do not take the whole weight onto yourself; it makes the rest of us feel decorative." Ada finally looked up, and her eyes behind the spectacles were dry and very clear. "Do you know why I kept the slate? Not for courage. Courage is a word people use afterward. I kept it because I taught those children — the eleven of you — for six weeks before they were scattered, and on the last morning Merrit Corven asked me to write all the names on the board so she could memorize them properly, and I did, and then the magistrate's man came and told me such lists were inflammatory. Inflammatory. Eleven names." She closed the basket. "So I wrote them again, smaller, on a slate, and hid it behind the multiplication tables, and I checked on it every term for twenty years the way other women check on graves. Dismissing me costs them nothing and gains them nothing. The slate isn't here anymore, Tamsin. It's somewhere they'll never think to look, and it will be produced when it's needed."

"Where?"

Ada permitted herself the ghost of a smile. "Somewhere they'll never think to look. Now help me carry this basket. A dismissed schoolmistress shouldn't be seen struggling; it sets a bad example for the children, who are watching from the fence, because of course they are."

They were — three of them, small faces between the pickets, solemn as owls. As Tamsin carried the basket out, one of them, a girl of seven or so, pressed something through the gap in the fence into her free hand: a piece of chalk, worn to a stub.

"For the slate," the child whispered, and ran.

Tamsin stood in the lane holding the chalk, and grief and fury rose in her together like floodwater. This was how Corven fought — not with violence but with subtraction. Take the workshop. Take the ferry. Take the schoolmistress. Subtract and subtract until the truth had no room left to stand in, and call it order, and call it peace.

* * *

She walked the long way back to Mill Lane, past the frozen millrace, past the founders' church with its clean white steeple, and the town looked exactly as it looked on Corven's map — tidy, prosperous, innocent. That was the genius of it, she thought. The lie wasn't written anywhere. The lie was the absence, and absences don't show.

Joss Renner was waiting outside the workshop, leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed, and his face told her the news before she reached him.

"Saw the notice," he said. "Also saw two men measuring the press just now. Measuring it, like it's furniture they've come to repossess. Which it is, come Friday." He spat into the gutter. "So. Your magistrate moves fast."

"Faster than ice breaks."

"Not necessarily." Joss pushed off the doorframe. "I've broken ice before, in places you don't want to hear about. Ice looks solid right up until it isn't. And underneath, the current's been running your way for weeks — Brida's gossip network alone is worth a regiment." He glanced through the window at the darkened workshop. "You going to sit in there tonight?"

"I'm going to work. While I still legally can."

"Then I'm sitting with you. Not helping," he added quickly. "Sitting. There's a difference, and I'd thank you to remember it."

"There is no difference at all," Tamsin said, and unlocked the door.

Inside, she lit the furnace high for the first time in days, and the copper caught the light, and for two hours the world narrowed to the burin's bite and the slow rising of a drowned street out of blank metal. Joss sat by the window as promised, whittling nothing, watching the lane. Once, near midnight, he said quietly, "Someone's been past twice. Same boots. Heavy on the left heel." And once Tamsin paused, flexing her aching hand, and thought of eleven names and one stub of chalk in her pocket, and cut deeper.

It was nearly two in the morning when she climbed the stairs to her rooms above the workshop, bone-tired, half-blind with strain, and found the door standing open.

She had locked it. She always locked it. The lock hung crooked, forced with a knife blade or a strong screwdriver, quick and professional. Her papers were rifled but not taken — the testimony transcriptions lay in a careless fan across the bed. The drawer of the writing desk hung out at the wrong angle.

The copybook was gone.

Eleven names in a dead child's hand, eight struck through, one initial remaining — the single most dangerous document in Marlow Bend, and it had been sitting in a cheap pine drawer while she engraved downstairs, guarded by nothing but a latch.

Tamsin stood in the doorway a long time, the cold from the stairwell moving against her back, and made herself think instead of feel. Who knew the copybook existed? Brida. Ada. Joss. Elias Vane, who had seen it the day he came with his questions and his hovering pen. Merrit Corven, who had watched her across a dinner table with open hostility and knew precisely what such a list would mean.

Heavy boots, Joss had said. Heavy on the left heel.

She looked down at the forced lock, at the neat, unhurried work of someone who had known exactly which drawer mattered — and heard, distantly, out on the black river, the first long groan of the ice beginning to shift.
