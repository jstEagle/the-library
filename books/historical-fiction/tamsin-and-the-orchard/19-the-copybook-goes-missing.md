## 19. The Copybook Goes Missing

Elias Vane had counted forty-one chimneys on Ferry Lane before he noticed the smoke that didn't belong to any of them.

It was a habit of his, counting things — chimneys, doors, the number of steps between one lamp and the next — because counting was what kept him honest. A census taker who did not count was a liar with a ledger, and Elias Vane had made a vow to himself, years ago, in another town with another set of books: *I will not be a man who writes down what is convenient and calls it the truth.* He had watched men do it. He had watched whole parishes do it. He had once been ordered, by a superior with soft hands and a soft voice, to strike three households from a return because their rents were paid by a man who sat on the county bench — and Elias had struck them, God forgive him, because he was twenty-three years old and wanted to keep his post.

He was thirty-eight now, and he carried that erased line inside him the way other men carried a bullet near the spine.

So when the summons came from the district office — *verify returns for Marlow Bend, note discrepancies* — he had come expecting nothing but tedium. What he had found instead, folded into the 1831 parish returns like a hair laid across a drawer to catch a thief, was an absence so neat it could only have been deliberate.

The laborers' row.

He had walked its length on his second day in town — or where its length should have been. The map said orchard. The deed said orchard, freehold of Corven, parcel eleven, surveyed 1832. The ground itself said something else. Elias had stood at the edge of the frost-stiff grass and looked at the way the apple trees grew — not in orchard rows but scattered, careless, as trees grow when no one has planted them — and beneath them, when he crouched and pushed back the dead leaves, he had found the foundations. Stone footings, running straight as a rule for thirty yards. Chimney falls. A well cap with an iron ring rusted to lace.

Nobody builds a well in an orchard.

He had written none of this in his report. Not yet. He had copied the 1831 register entries instead — eleven households on the row, forty-three souls, occupations noted in the surveyor's cramped hand — and then gone looking for those same souls in the 1841 return. Eleven households had become zero. Forty-three souls had become a footnote about a flood, and even the footnote was missing from the copy held in the magistrate's office, though it was present — barely, water-stained, as if someone had tried to scrub it — in the diocesan archive two towns over.

Someone had cut Marlow Bend's history out with a razor, and someone had paid for the cutting, and every record in the town agreed with every other record the way witnesses agree when they've rehearsed.

Elias Vane had spent fifteen years learning how paper lied. Paper lied by omission, by alignment, by the too-perfect agreement of documents that had never met each other honestly. And now, walking up Ferry Lane in the blue hour before dark, counting chimneys out of habit, he saw smoke rising behind the Ashgrove workshop — thin, steady, wrong. The workshop chimney was cold; he'd checked it that morning, as he checked everything. This smoke came from the cottage behind the orchard, the little house that belonged on no ledger he'd yet found, where Miss Tamsin Ashgrove lived alone among her dead master's tools.

Smoke meant fire. Fire meant warmth. Warmth meant someone was home and awake, and Elias had been waiting two days for the courage to knock on her door with a question that had nothing to do with the census.

Then he saw her come out of the cottage at a half-run, coatless, lantern swinging wild, and go up the path toward the workshop — and stop.

Even across the distance he saw her stop. Saw the small figure go rigid in the doorway of the workshop, saw the lantern lift and hold, saw her stand there for the length of ten heartbeats before she went inside and shut the door behind her with a care that was worse than slamming.

Elias Vane stood in the lane with his notebook in his pocket and understood, with the cold clarity of long practice, that something had just been stolen.

* * *

He gave it an hour. An hour was respectful; less than an hour was voyeurism, and more would look like surveillance, which — he admitted, climbing the orchard path under a sky the color of wet slate — it rather was. But he had learned in fifteen years of records that the hour after a discovery belongs to the discoverer, and the thing you learn in that hour, before you can shape it into sense, is usually the truest thing you'll get.

The workshop door opened to his knock on the third try. Tamsin Ashgrove stood in the gap with the lamp behind her, and Elias, who had catalogued faces all his life the way other men catalogued coins, saw hers and knew.

Her eyes were dry, which meant she had already wept or decided against it. Her hands were steady, which meant they were being held steady by force of will. And there was ink on her right index finger, fresh, and a smear of graphite across her left palm, as if she had spent the last hour copying something out — fast, urgent, committing to paper a thing she feared to lose.

"Mr. Vane," she said. "If this is about the household returns, I signed them Thursday."

"It isn't." He took off his hat. Snow had begun again, fine as sifted flour, settling on his shoulders. "Miss Ashgrove, may I come in? It's about a theft."

Something moved behind her eyes — quick, guarded, gone. "What theft?"

"That's rather my question."

She held him in the doorway a moment longer. Then she stepped aside.

The workshop smelled of oil, iron, and the ghost of acid. Elias had been in engravers' shops before — there had been one in his home town, run by a deaf old man who taught him that a plate remembers everything done to it, every slip, every correction, every burnishing-out — and he knew enough to read the room. The press stood clean and oiled. The plates lay racked in order. The furnace banked low. Everything in its place, everything orderly —

— except the desk.

The desk's top drawer stood open, and the lock was broken, the brass face of it scarred by two neat turns of a blade. Beside the drawer lay a strip of paper, torn from a printed form, blank. And on the blotter, squared to the desk edge with a precision that hurt to look at, sat a single sheet covered in Tamsin Ashgrove's hand: names, dates, cross-references, the whole architecture of a proof, copied out in a hurry by someone who understood exactly what she'd lost and was racing to preserve its shadow.

"You've reported it to the constable," Elias said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The constable," Tamsin said, "is Magistrate Corven's brother-in-law."

"Ah." He hung his hat on the peg by the door, uninvited, and let the gesture say what it said. "Then perhaps you'll report it to me instead."

She turned then, fully, and looked at him the way people look at a locked door when they're deciding whether to pick it. "Why would you help me?"

"Because I've been robbed too." He said it plainly, because plainness was the only currency he had ever trusted. "Not of a book. Of a page. Several pages. Out of the 1831 returns, out of the parish register, out of the flood year's burial roll. Somebody has spent sixteen years taking Marlow Bend apart and putting it back together without a piece in it, and I have been standing in the middle of the puzzle for three weeks pretending not to see the hole, because seeing it makes my position — my commission, my neutrality — very complicated." He nodded at the open drawer. "And now whoever did the first robbing has robbed you of whatever you found in Hollis's papers. Which tells me we are disturbing the same grave."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the door. Neither of them moved to close it.

"What exactly is missing?" Elias asked gently. "Describe it to me. Description is evidence."

Tamsin looked at the desk for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way ice is level — sound until you put your weight on it.

"A copybook. Child's hand. Eleven names, each struck through save one." Her finger curled slightly, as if around a pen that wasn't there. "Master Hollis kept it in the bottom drawer, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath his survey notes. There was also—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "There was a letter. Sealed. Addressed to *the eleventh child*. It's still here." She touched the drawer without closing it. "They didn't take the letter."

"No," Elias said. "They wouldn't."

"Why wouldn't they?"

"Because the letter proves nothing to anyone but you. A sealed letter addressed to an unknown child — it's sentiment. It's a keepsake. If it surfaced at an inquiry, Corven's men would call it a forgery and grieve beautifully over your loss. But a copybook—" He drew a breath. "A copybook in a child's hand listing eleven names, with the strikes and the dates and the schoolmaster's marginalia, is a document. Documents have provenance. Provenance can be tested. Ink ages, paper ages, the hand can be compared against the register entries, against the slate exercises of the period. Whoever took it understands records, Miss Ashgrove. They didn't take your treasure. They took your evidence."

The silence that followed had weight. Outside, the wind came off the river carrying that sound he had been hearing for two days and trying not to name — a deep, structural groan, like a ship working loose of its moorings. The ice was talking. By week's end it would be shouting.

"They went through the lock carefully," Tamsin said at last. "Nothing else disturbed. The plates untouched. The letter untouched. Whoever it was knew precisely which object mattered."

"Yes."

"And knew it was here."

"Yes."

"I told no one." Her voice frayed at the edges, first crack in the ice of it. "Brida Ferris knows of it. Ada Quill knows. Joss Renner knows I found *something*, not what. Mr. Vane, I have told four living souls, and I would stake my hands that none of them—"

"I believe you." He said it quickly, and meant it, and saw her blink at the speed of it. "Which leaves the fifth possibility. That someone learned of it who wasn't told. Someone who watches this house." He paused, and made himself finish, because a census taker who flinched from his own counts was no better than the men who'd taught him to flinch. "Or someone who was watching the night you searched the desk. Or—" and here he chose, deliberately, to say the hard word first, "—someone who lives in a house where such knowledge would travel whether or not you ever spoke it. You dined at the Corven table, Miss Ashgrove. You presented the atlas. You stood in a room full of people whose entire fortune rests on the story that copybook unmakes, and one of them watched you all evening and asked herself what you knew."

"Merrit Corven." The name came out flat.

"She sat opposite you at dinner. She watched you the way a card player watches the deck." Elias turned his hat in his hands. "I'm not saying it's her. I'm saying when I make my list — and I make lists, it's the disease of my trade — her name goes at the top, and underneath it her father's steward, and underneath him the constable, and underneath him a clerk in the diocesan office named Wray who requested the '31 registers on a Tuesday last November and returned them on the Wednesday with his cuffs dusty and his story vague. Five names. One of them took your book."

"You've been investigating." Not accusation. Wonder, almost.

"I've been doing my job badly," Elias said, "and my conscience properly, for three weeks. There's a difference. My commission says count the households. My conscience says count the ones somebody erased." He met her eyes. "In 1829, in Halstead, I was ordered to strike three families from a return. Their landlord sat on the bench. I struck them. I was young and I wanted my post. Two of those families ended in the workhouse, and one of them ended worse, and I have spent nine years learning the difference between a record and a weapon so that I never again hand mine to a man like Corven."

The wind pressed at the windows. In the banked furnace, a knot of coal settled with a tick like a clock.

"So," Tamsin said slowly, "you are telling me that the machinery of records — the commissions, the registers, the returns, the whole apparatus they used to bury these people — has sent me a man who wants to dig."

"I'm telling you the apparatus doesn't know what it sent." A grim almost-smile crossed his face. "Machines don't. Men do. I am a small cog, Miss Ashgrove, and I am rusting in exactly the place you need. I cannot testify — a census taker's private findings are worth nothing at law, and Corven would have my commission revoked by Friday if I spoke aloud. But I can count. I can compare. I can put the 1831 register beside the 1832 survey beside the deed beside the burial roll and write down, in my own dull professional hand, every place where the numbers refuse to agree." He tapped his breast pocket, where the notebook rode. "Numbers refusing to agree is the one crime this town cannot call forgery, because it's committed entirely by their own documents."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something he did not expect: she pulled out the chair at the workbench, and sat down heavily, and put her inked fingers to her temples, and for a moment the celebrated engraver, the keeper of Master Hollis's atlas, looked simply like a tired young woman in a cold room holding up a roof by herself.

"The historians arrive when the ice breaks," she said. "Corven engaged them from the capital. Whatever story is standing when their boat docks is the story that gets carved into the county histories, the schoolbooks, the memorial stones. I was going to have a supplementary plate ready. Now the one document that could anchor it—" Her hands dropped. "Gone."

"Not gone." Elias took out his notebook and opened it flat on the blotter beside her hasty transcription. "Copied. Badly, hastily, but you're an engraver, Miss Ashgrove — you reproduced it stroke for stroke, didn't you? Names, spellings, the direction of the strikes, the wear of the nib?" He looked at her page, then at her, and felt the small hot coal of purpose settle in his chest where the old guilt had banked for nine years. "You haven't lost the evidence. You've become it. A witness who memorizes is worth more than a document, because a document can be stolen twice and you can only be buried once — and Corven, whatever else he is, hasn't the stomach for that in front of Professor Maren's historians. So. We work. Tonight, while the thief believes the matter closed. You give me every name on that page and I find where each one surfaces and vanishes in the returns — births, burials, apprenticeships, the poor rolls. Eleven children leave a wake, Miss Ashgrove, even when the boat is burned. Children get apprenticed. Get schooled. Get bound out. Somewhere in some clerk's dutiful hand, each of those eleven wrote their own name after the erasure, because children sign things — lesson books, indentures, Sunday-school awards — and nobody thinks to burn a seven-year-old's signature."

Tamsin stared at him. Something in her face was shifting — the guardedness giving way, grudgingly, to something hungrier. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since I found the well in the orchard." He uncapped his pen. "Now. First name on your page. Read it to me."

* * *

They worked past midnight.

It was, Elias reflected somewhere around the second candle, the strangest collaboration of his career: the engraver pulling books from her dead master's shelves while he cross-indexed her transcriptions against the copies he'd smuggled out of the diocesan archive in his instrument case, the two of them bent over the workbench like conspirators, which was what they were, though the conspiracy consisted entirely of arithmetic. Three of the eleven names surfaced within the first hour — a girl apprenticed to a tallow-boiler in 1833 under a surname not her own; a boy entered into the militia musters at fourteen; a child's death registration from 1834 that listed cause as *fever* and residence as *the almshouse*, though the almshouse had no beds until 1836.

"Somebody re-homed them and renamed them," Tamsin murmured, copying his findings onto her own sheet in her precise engraver's letters, turning evidence into something that could be cut into copper. "Scattered like seed."

"Like seed, yes." Elias rubbed his eyes. "And one of the seeds grew up rich and respectable and sits on the town council, if my reading of the aldermen's roll is right, but that's a thread for daylight." He paused. "Miss Ashgrove. The name that wasn't struck through. The initial — T.A. Did you ever ask yourself why Hollis kept the book at all? Why a grown man, complicit enough to alter his own plates, kept a child's copybook hidden in oilcloth for sixteen years?"

She was quiet a long time. The river groaned again, longer this time, a sound with a cracking end to it.

"Because guilt is a hoarder," she said finally. "It throws nothing away."

"Or because he was building you a key and couldn't bring himself to label it." Elias capped his pen and began packing his case, and as he did, his eye caught the window — the black rectangle of it, the orchard beyond, the pale smear of the frozen river. And standing in the orchard shadow, at the corner of the cottage wall where the lamplight died, he saw — or believed he saw — a figure. Still. Watching. Gone when he blinked, present when he didn't, the way watchers are.

He said nothing. Some things you verify before you voice.

But at the door, hat in hand, he paused and said quietly: "Lock the letter in the press chest tonight, not the desk. And Miss Ashgrove — if anyone asks, our conversation concerned household schedules and nothing else. Let them think I'm a dull man. Dull men are permitted everywhere."

"Mr. Vane." She stopped him on the threshold, snow swirling in past his shoulders. "Why tell me all this? You could walk away. Report your tidy numbers, collect your fee, go home clean."

Elias looked past her, into the workshop, at the rack of copper plates gleaming dully in the lamplight — the official maps, the beautiful lie, every line of it drawn true except the lines that mattered.

"Because in 1829 I wrote down what was convenient," he said, "and called it the truth, and three families paid for my handwriting." He settled the hat on his head. "I've been looking for the entry that undoes that line for nine years. I begin to think it's in this town."

He went down the orchard path into the snow. Behind him the workshop light went out, and the cottage light bloomed, and the river groaned like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

At the lane's end he stopped, once, and looked back — and saw, clearly this time, framed in the cottage window against the lamp, the silhouette of a woman standing motionless, watching the house. Tall. Still. The posture of someone who had learned stillness in a house where being noticed was dangerous.

The figure turned away from the window. For half a heartbeat the lamp caught the line of a jaw, a fall of fair hair pinned severe, the unmistakable carriage of Merrit Corven — walking not toward the lane and town, but away, along the orchard wall, toward the river path, moving fast now, as though the watching had been only the first errand of her night.

Elias Vane stood in the falling snow and took out his notebook, and by feel, in the dark, wrote a single line beneath the day's date:

*The copybook is at Corven House. Follow her.*
