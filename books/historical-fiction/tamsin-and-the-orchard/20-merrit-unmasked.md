## 20. Merrit Unmasked

The house had gone quiet the way houses do when a secret is awake inside them. Merrit Corven heard it in the settling of the stair treads, in the tick of the case clock in the hall, in her father's voice downstairs saying goodnight to Mr. Quill the solicitor with just a half-beat too much warmth, as though warmth could be laid over a thing like mortar over a crack.

She stood at the window of her old bedroom — the one she had not slept in since she was nineteen and had come back to Marlow Bend only because her mother died and someone had to — and watched lamplight move behind the curtains of Tamsin Ashgrove's rented rooms across the lane.

The copybook was under her floorboard. Third board from the window, where as a girl she had hidden a stolen ribbon and later a letter from a boy whose name she no longer permitted herself to remember. She had chosen the same hiding place without thinking about why, and only afterward understood: it was the one place in this town that had ever felt like hers.

Her door opened without a knock.

She knew who it would be before she turned. The census man moved like paper moving — no weight, no announcement, just suddenly there, occupying a corner of her room the way a filed document occupies a drawer. Elias Vane held his hat in his hands, which struck her as either courtesy or camouflage.

"This is my father's house," she said. "You'll want to state your business to him."

"Your father is at supper with three men who will swear tomorrow that he never left the table." Vane set his hat on the chair by the door, deliberately, the way a man sits down when he intends to stay. "I've spent eleven days verifying households in Marlow Bend, Miss Corven. Do you know what I've learned? That a census is a machine for counting people, and every machine can be made to lie if you grease it properly. Your town is very well greased."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But I know what *you* mean. Or rather—" He reached into his coat and produced a small notebook, worn soft at the spine, and did not open it. "I know what you meant when you were seen leaving Mrs. Ashgrove's lodgings at half past ten on Tuesday night with your shawl over your arm instead of around your shoulders. A woman carrying something flat against her chest carries it that way. Like a book."

Merrit kept her face still. She had been trained to keep her face still since before she could talk; her father used to say a magistrate's daughter must be able to hear anything without blinking. "You're accusing me of theft based on the position of a shawl?"

"I'm accusing you of nothing. I'm telling you what I saw, and then I'm going to tell you what else I know, and then I'm going to leave, and you're going to decide whether any of us sleeps tonight." He glanced at the floor between the bed and the window — third board, third board — and back up, and she saw that he had already looked, that he had probably knelt in this room while the household supped below, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards into the cold dark under the house.

"You went through my room."

"I went through the evidence." Something flickered in his plain face — not triumph; she had expected triumph, and its absence unsettled her more than triumph would have. "Miss Corven, four days ago I requested from the parish archive the register pages for 1831. Three are missing. Not lost — missing. The stubs remain. Pages were cut with a razor, cleanly, by someone who knew how to cut a page so an archivist wouldn't notice for twenty years. I have also read the affidavit your father keeps in his strongbox attesting that no dwellings stood on the north bank above the ferry landing prior to 1834. I have counted seven signatures on it, including a Hollis, cartographer, whose hand trembles so badly on that line that he must have signed it standing up, or crying, or both."

"Get out."

"In a moment." He still hadn't raised his voice. That was the worst of it — the terrible patience of a man who dealt all day in records, who knew that records always won eventually because they outlived everyone's anger. "Here is what I cannot make fit, Miss Corven, and why I am in your bedroom instead of your father's study. If you stole that copybook for the magistrate, it would be in his study. It isn't; I've checked, and checking is what I do. If you stole it to destroy it, there'd be ash in your grate. There isn't. So you took it and hid it under a loose board like a child hiding contraband, which means you took it for yourself. And people do not steal their enemies' secrets to keep them close unless those secrets belong to them too."

* * *

She sat down on the edge of the bed because her legs informed her they were finished pretending.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Downstairs the clock struck nine, each note falling through the floor like stones dropped down a well.

"Third board from the window," Merrit said finally. Her voice came out flat, unfamiliar. "How did you know?"

"Because I was a clerk in the capital for nine years before they sent me out here, and clerks learn that everyone hides things in the same places, and because I watched you cross the lane on Tuesday and I saw you glance at this window first. You checked your own escape route before you committed the crime. Habit of a person who has needed one before." He paused. "Or I simply guessed. It doesn't matter now."

"It matters." She laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "It matters enormously, Mr. Vane, because everything in this town is guessing. My whole life is a guess wearing a dress."

He waited. He was good at waiting. Census men must be.

"You should understand something about my father," she said. "Everyone in Marlow Bend thinks they understand him. Cold, correct, ambitious, fond of order. All true. But none of it explains me, does it? A man like that, with a wife who died and no other children, raising a ward? People say charity. People say my mother's wish. My father says nothing, and his silence has stood there for thirty years like a wall, and I grew up pressing my ear against it trying to hear what was behind it."

"And what was behind it?"

"Nothing," she said. "That's what's behind it. Nothing. That's the whole architecture of my life — a wall built around nothing, load-bearing nothing." She looked at her hands. They were her father's hands, long and precise; she had noticed it with horror at fifteen and never un-noticed it. "I was brought to the Corven house in the spring of '32. I know this because I asked the housekeeper once, years ago, when she was old and her discretion had gone soft as her hands. Brought, she said. Not born. Brought. In a fish cart, wrapped in a blanket that wasn't mine, with a fever, and a tag pinned to the blanket."

Vane had gone very still. Even his breathing seemed filed away.

"The tag had a number on it," Merrit said. "Not a name. A number. Nine. Someone had numbered me, Mr. Vane, the way you number a crate. Nine of something. Eleven of something. I wore that blanket until it fell apart and I burned the tag when I was twelve because I couldn't bear looking at it anymore, and I have regretted burning it every day of my life since, because it was the only piece of paper in the world that told the truth about me and I destroyed it myself."

The words were out now and would not stop, a dam breaking in reverse, everything she had held back flooding forward instead.

"When the Ashgrove woman began asking questions — when Brida Ferris started talking on her ferry, when Ada Quill went strange and careful whenever the year '31 came up — I told myself it was nonsense. Old women's ghosts. But then I saw the copybook. I went to return a message from my father about the lease, that's all, and she had it open on her desk, that child's hand listing names, and I looked at it the way you look into a well, and Mr. Vane—" Her voice cracked clean through. She let it. There was no one left in her to perform for. "I saw my own name. Not the name I go by. The other one. The one underneath. The ninth one. And my hand shook so badly I couldn't read the rest, and I heard her coming up the stairs, and I closed the book and put it in my shawl and walked out of that room a thief, because the alternative was to stand there and find out in front of a stranger whether the ground under thirty years of my life was real."

Silence. The clock ticked. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You didn't read the rest," Vane said quietly.

"I couldn't. I haven't. It's under that board and I have not touched it since Tuesday night and I will not, do you understand? Because as long as I don't read past my own name, it can still be a lie. As long as I don't confirm the others, I can still be merely a madwoman who stole a book. My father might be guilty of erasing a row of drowned laborers from history — that I could survive, God help me, I could survive being his daughter. But if I read those names and trace them, if I follow them to the foundling homes and the indentures and whatever happened to child number eight and child number ten—"

"Child number ten is Joss Renner," Vane said. "Transported in '39 for theft. He came back on the coasting packet last week."

Merrit stared at him. "You knew. You've known all along what this was."

"I suspected. Now I know." He picked up his hat from the chair, then didn't put it on, turning it slowly in his hands — a man holding the one document he couldn't file. "I'll tell you why I came to you and not your father, since you'll wonder. Your father, if confronted, will convene an inquiry, produce forty affidavits, and drown me in paper before the historians' boat even docks. That's what he's built for; it's magnificent, really, watching it work. But you—" He looked at her, and there was no pity in it, which she was grateful for; pity would have broken her. "You stole the truth to keep it under your floor. Whatever you are, Miss Corven, you are not your father's instrument. Nobody hides a thing that carefully unless they intend, someday, to take it back out."

* * *

After he left — hat on now, footsteps fading down the stairs and out into the lane — Merrit knelt on the cold floorboards for a long time without moving.

Third board from the window. She pressed her palm flat against the wood as though taking its pulse. Beneath her hand, separated by an inch of pine, lay eleven names in a child's careful copybook hand, one of them hers, whichever hers was. Above her, through the ceiling, her father's study lamp burned late as it always did, the light of a man keeping his accounts in order.

Two truths existed under this roof now, and she had carried both into the house herself.

She thought of Tamsin Ashgrove across the lane, lighting her lamp alone, not yet knowing her book was gone, and felt something that was not quite guilt and not quite kinship but lived in the address between them. Ninth and eleventh. Two numbers from the same list, scattered thirty years ago like seeds on ice, both of them grown into women shaped entirely by what had been done to hide them.

Merrit slid the board up. The copybook lay there in the dark, small and patient, its cover soft as skin.

She did not read it. But she took it out, and held it against her chest the way she had carried it home, and began — slowly, hearing every step — to climb the stairs toward her father's study, where the lamp still burned, to ask him one question she had saved for thirty-one years:

*What was my name before you gave me yours?*

And if he lied — she knew now, holding the book against her heart, feeling its small weight like a second pulse — she would read the eleventh name aloud on the inquiry floor, in front of the historians, in front of the whole assembled town, and let the truth fall where it fell.
