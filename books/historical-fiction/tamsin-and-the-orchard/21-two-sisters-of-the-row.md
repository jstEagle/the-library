## 21. Two Sisters of the Row

Tamsin had not slept. The copybook lay on the workshop bench between them where Merrit had set it down, its string binding frayed, its cover soft as old cloth from a child's handling. Eleven names in pencil, nine struck through, and at the bottom that single initial — *T.A.* — written in a hand she now knew was her own.

"You stole it," Tamsin said. It was not a question. The lamp between them threw their shadows up the wall, two tall shapes leaning toward each other like figures cut from the same sheet of paper.

"I took it back," Merrit said. "That's how I've always thought of it. Taking back."

"From whom? You were raised in Corven House. Silk on your bed, tutors at your elbow. Who did you take anything *back* from?"

Merrit's laugh was short and had no warmth in it, only edges. "You think I don't know what I ate for breakfast every morning of my childhood? I do. I remember all of it. That's the curse of it, Tamsin. You got to forget." She pulled her gloves off finger by finger, deliberately, as if preparing to handle something unclean. "I never got to forget anything. I just got it renamed."

The fire ticked in the stove. Outside, the river made its low grinding sound, the ice talking to itself in the dark, and both women glanced toward the window at the same instant — the same flinch, the same half-second of listening — before catching themselves doing it.

"You were one of us," Tamsin said slowly. Testing the words. They tasted strange, like coin held too long in the mouth.

"Ninth," Merrit said. "Merrit Anne. My mother named me that. Did you know that? Not after any Corven. After nobody. Just a name she liked, from a song she used to sing on the landing stairs while she mended nets." Her voice stayed flat, controlled, the voice of a woman reading out an inventory. "Magistrate Corven came to the row the week after the flood with his ledger and his charity face, and he looked at eleven children standing in the ash like sparrows after a harvest fire, and he picked me because I didn't cry. He said so. Years later, at dinner, telling the story like it was charming. *She didn't cry, so I knew there was steel in her.*"

"And you thanked him."

"Every day of my life." She said it without shame, which was somehow worse. "I learned Latin. I learned which fork. I learned to sit through sermons without moving my eyes. I learned that if I was very good and very useful and never once mentioned smoke or water or the smell of burning wool, I would be permitted to exist." She looked down at the copybook, and her gloved-off hands hovered over the page without touching it. "Do you know what it is to have your whole survival depend on agreeing to a lie about yourself? To say *yes, I am a ward of charity, yes, my parents were no one, yes, the row was always a field*? I said it so many times it stopped being lying. It became simply — the weather of my life."

Tamsin thought of Hollis's letter, still hidden beneath the floorboard upstairs, the weight of it against the joists. *I hid your origins even from myself.* She had spent three days learning that her childhood was a constructed story, and she was still bleeding from the discovery. But Merrit had been living inside her constructed story for sixteen years, wide awake the entire time.

"At least yours was built by someone who loved you," Merrit said, as though reading her. "Hollis. He took you in out of guilt, kept you, taught you his craft. He gave you a trade, a name, a place at the bench. My builder was a magistrate who wanted a decoration." 

"He also erased you," Tamsin said. "Hollis altered his own plates. He cut the row off the map with his own burin."

"Yes." Merrit's jaw tightened. "And I watched my father do worse than alter plates. He burned the tenancy rolls. I sat in his study at fourteen and watched him feed pages into the stove, one at a time, and he let me watch because he wanted me complicit. Because a witness who says nothing becomes a keeper of the silence." She finally touched the copybook, one fingertip at the corner, light as touching a wound. "So when I saw this sitting in your rooms last week — when I understood what you were doing, what you'd found — I couldn't bear it. Either truth existing. Yours or theirs. Do you understand that? I couldn't bear *either one*."

"Why?"

"Because if the lie stands, then I sold my childhood for nothing. All those years of silence bought a silk bed and a place at the founders' table, and the row stays a field, and my mother stays no one, forever." Her breath caught, and she mastered it, the way she must have mastered everything since she was six years old. "But if the *truth* comes out — if you engrave it into plates and bind it into books and read eleven names aloud in front of the whole town — then what am I? I'm the girl who said yes. Sixteen years of yes. I'd be standing in that ash again, except this time everyone would see what I traded to climb out of it."

The lamp guttered and steadied. Tamsin found she could not look away from her. It was like looking into a mirror hung at the wrong angle — everything recognizable, nothing aligned.

* * *

Merrit had imagined this confrontation a hundred times on the walk over, and in none of those imaginings did she feel this: the sick, vertiginous pull of looking at someone who shared her blood's history and none of her choices.

Tamsin Ashgrove stood behind her master's bench with ink on her fingers and grief worn openly, like a shawl she'd forgotten to remove, and Merrit hated her a little for it. Hated the ease of her mourning. Hated that she had been allowed a master who died leaving her a purpose, while Merrit had been given a keeper who lived and called himself a father.

"You could have come to me," Tamsin said. "Instead of stealing."

"To you?" Merrit let the word hang. "A week ago you were still saying the Ashgroves owned the orchard cottage free and clear. A week ago you believed paper told the truth. You were the atlas's true believer, the dutiful apprentice finishing her dead master's holy work. What would you have done if I'd knocked on your door and said *the town is a forgery and so are you*?"

Tamsin was quiet a long moment. "I'd have called you a liar," she admitted. "Then I'd have gone to the registers. And then—" She stopped.

"And then you'd have found it anyway. Yes. That's the difference between us, isn't it. You find things and they become real for you. I have known things my whole life and worked very hard to make them unreal again." Merrit straightened, and the posture was pure Corven House — spine like a drawn line, chin level, the schooling showing through like bones under skin. "My father thinks I stole the copybook for him. To bring it to him. He's expecting it tomorrow."

Tamsin went very still. "You haven't given it to him."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't know yet whose side of me wins." It came out of her raw, faster than she meant it, and she hated that too. She pressed on regardless, because the alternative was stopping, and if she stopped she would never start again. "There's a girl in me who is nine years old and standing in the flood mud watching men carry her mother's body on a door, and she has waited sixteen years for somebody to say the row existed. And there is a woman in me who is Magistrate Corven's daughter, who has a place, a name, a future, and who knows exactly what happens to that woman if the ninth child stands up in a public inquiry and speaks. They can't both live, Tamsin. That's what nobody tells you about surviving something. You don't get to keep all the selves you might have been."

"You could keep both," Tamsin said quietly. "You could be his daughter and her child. Both maps. Isn't that what we're fighting for — that both can exist side by side?"

"Don't you dare use my father's atlas argument against me."

"It's not his atlas anymore."

Merrit laughed despite herself — a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and bitter and almost warm. Then it died. "No. I suppose it isn't. That's rather the problem, isn't it. Nothing is anyone's anymore. Not the atlas, not the past, not our names." She looked at the copybook again, and this time she turned it around and pushed it across the bench, slowly, until it rested under Tamsin's ink-stained fingers. "Here. Before I change my mind. Or before I don't."

Tamsin's hands closed around it the way a drowning woman takes hold of a plank.

"I'm not giving it to you as a gift," Merrit said sharply, because she needed it said, needed the transaction named, needed it to be anything other than mercy. "Understand me. If you publish this — if you put the ninth name in a book — you put *me* in it. Merrit Corven, born Merrit Anne, daughter of the drowned netmender's widow of the laborers' row. You don't get to choose whether my name appears. If it goes in, it goes in whole. The row and the ward. Both of them. I will not be rescued by halves."

"Both maps," Tamsin said. "Whole names."

"And when the historians come—"

"When the historians come, they'll ask who testified." Tamsin met her eyes, and Merrit saw no pity in them, which was the only reason she could stand it. "They'll want primary evidence. Living witnesses. Names attached to testimony."

"I know what witnesses are."

"Do you?" Tamsin set the copybook down between them, open to the list, and turned it so they could both read it. Nine struck names. Two spared. "Because there's a difference between knowing a thing and saying it where it counts. I only learned that last month. I lied to Elias Vane in my own kitchen, Merrit. First lie of my life, and it was to protect a lie I didn't even know I was keeping. So I won't pretend I'm braver than you. I'm just later to the same war."

Merrit stared at the list. At the ninth name — *Merrit Anne* — struck through in a child's careful pencil, the way children strike through finished sums, tidy and final, closing the account. Someone had decided, sixteen years ago, that she was done. Finished. Transferred to another ledger.

"They crossed us out," she said. Her voice had gone somewhere small. "Like errors."

"They crossed us out," Tamsin agreed. "So we go back in. In our own hand this time."

For a moment — one lamp-lit, ice-grinding moment — Merrit felt the two selves in her stop clawing at each other, and she was terrified by how much she wanted that feeling to last. Recognition without reconciliation; she understood suddenly that this was all they would ever have, she and this stranger-sister. Same mud, same smoke, same night on the river ice. Opposite everything since.

"I won't testify," Merrit said. "Not yet. Don't ask me. If my father hears I've seen you, he'll move against you tonight instead of waiting for the historians, and you'll lose the workshop before you've cut the first plate." She pulled her gloves back on, each finger precise, armor going on piece by piece. "Take the copybook. Cut your plate. And when the inquiry comes — and it will come, he's already drafting the summons — I'll be standing next to him. Remember that, when you see me there. Whatever I do or don't say. Watch my hands."

"Your hands?"

"He taught me cards when I was seven. He always said you can read a player's intention in the hands before the face ever agrees to it." At the door she paused, silhouette against the snow-light, and for one second the schooling dropped and Merrit Anne looked out — nine years old, mud to her knees, listening to her mother sing on the landing stairs. "He's engaged men from the capital. Historians. They arrive when the ice breaks. Whatever you're going to do, Tamsin Ashgrove — do it faster."

Then the door shut, and the cold rushed in to fill the space she'd occupied, and Tamsin stood alone with the copybook and the lamp and the sound of the river getting ready to let go.

She opened the book to the last page. Beneath the eleven names, in the margin, in a smaller hand she hadn't noticed before — Brida Ferris's hand, she realized, the ferry-woman's blunt schoolroom letters — someone had written a single line:

*The river keeps what the town throws away.*

Outside, far downstream, the ice groaned — long, deep, like a hinge turning on a door two days from opening.
