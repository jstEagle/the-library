## 22. The Engraver's Gambit

The copper plate lay on my bench like a held breath.

It was the last blank one in Hollis's stock — a rectangle of metal the size of a psalter, its surface still wearing the mill's dull shine, untouched by graver or acid. He had bought it years ago, I realized now, for a map that never existed. Perhaps he had always meant to cut it and never found the courage. Or perhaps he had left it deliberately, the way a man leaves an empty chair at his table, trusting someone would come to sit in it.

I turned it over in my hands and felt its weight settle into me — not the weight of metal but of intention. Eleven names. One flood line. A row of houses that existed everywhere except on paper.

*They will dock when the ice breaks.* That thought had been beating in me since the river began its low, groaning argument with the shore two nights past. The historians Corven had hired were not villains; they were worse than villains. They were careful men who loved documents, and every document in Marlow Bend told them the laborers' row had never stood. They would write their handsome report, cite the parish register, the deeds, the atlas itself — *my* atlas, Hollis's atlas, the book I had bled over all winter — and the erasure would be finished forever, sealed under a scholarly seal, more permanent than any lie told in a tavern.

Paper could be made to forget. But copper remembered everything you asked of it, and it answered to very few hands.

I set the plate down, lit the second lamp, and took up my sharpest graver.

* * *

I did not begin cutting at once. A plate cut in haste is a plate ruined, and there would be no second chance — not with the thaw coming, not with the workshop lease hanging by whatever thread Corven's patience still possessed. First I needed a plan as exact as a survey.

I cleared the bench and pinned down the working drawings: the official county sheet, reduced from Hollis's original at one inch to four hundred yards; my own tracing of the river bend taken last autumn when the water was low enough to show the old stonework; and Brida Ferris's testimony, copied out fair in my hand because I could not bear to let her voice live only in memory. Beside these I laid the copybook — Elias Vane had returned it to me three days ago with a look I am only now learning to read, the look of a man handing back something heavier than paper.

Eleven names in a child's round hand. Nine struck through in pencil long ago. An initial left waiting like an open door.

I measured the plate against the official sheet. The same scale, the same extent — from the ferry landing in the south to the mill race in the north, the whole crooked mile of Marlow Bend as the founders wished it drawn. Then I laid a sheet of tracing paper over the plate-sized area and began, lightly, in soft graphite, to draw what the official sheet refused to contain.

The laborers' row had stood along the east bank, below the orchard, where the ground rose just enough to keep boots out of the spring mud. Fourteen dwellings, Brida said — she had counted them a hundred times in her head, the way a woman counts things she cannot afford to lose. Fourteen dwellings, a shared pump, a drying line strung between two elms. The flood of '31 had taken the row entire; the fire had taken the mill the same night, whether by accident or by design no living soul would say aloud; and the eleven children had been scattered like seed across the county by men who called it charity.

My graver hovered. My hand did not shake. That surprised me. I had expected fury to make my lines wild. Instead there was a stillness in me I had never felt at the bench, as though every plate I had ever cut — every coastline, every contour, every patient hatching of hills — had been practice for this one.

I drew the row first. Fourteen small rectangles, each with its tiny gable, each labeled in letters no taller than a grain of wheat. Then the flood line: a broken rule sweeping across the bend, marked *Waters of February 1831*, rising to the second step of the churchyard wall exactly as Brida described, drowning the row, licking the edge of the orchard, stopping — this was the detail that caught in my throat — just short of the founders' houses on the high street. Water knows nothing of land claims. But maps, I was learning, know everything.

Last, along the bottom margin where a cartographer puts his dedication, I lettered the eleven names. Full names, recovered from Brida, from Ada Quill's slate, from Joss Renner's bitter memory and Merrit Corven's reluctant whisper. I wrote them in my own hand, which is to say I wrote them in the hand I now knew had learned its letters on a slate in that drowned row, taught by some mother whose face lived behind a smoke-colored curtain in my mind. *Renner. Quill. Pike. Corven.* And the eleventh, which I had written a hundred times before without knowing why my pen lingered on it:

*Tamsin Ashgrove.*

No. That was not true, and the false name looked wrong on the page, a coat that fit badly across the shoulders. I crossed it out — gently, with a single stroke, the way Hollis had struck through nine names in a copybook twenty years ago — and above it I wrote what Ada swore the register once said before the razor found it: *Tabitha Anne Rowan.*

I sat back. The lamp guttered. Outside, the ice spoke again, a long cracking sigh that ran from the ferry landing to the mill race, and I understood that the river and I were keeping the same appointment.

* * *

Brida came at dusk, stamping snow off her boots in the doorway, bringing with her the smell of the crossing — cold iron, wet rope, river.

"Well?" she said. "You've the look of a woman about to do something expensive."

I turned the traced drawing toward her. She bent over it without touching it, the way one bends over a sleeping child. Her finger came up at last and moved along the fourteen rectangles, dwelling on each.

"That one," she said, tapping the third from the pump, "was where your mother kept her loom. You'd sit under it, mind, pulling the waste threads. Two years old and already a magpie for anything shiny." Her voice stayed level, but her eyes did not. "You've got the gables right. Who told you the gables?"

"You did."

"Did I." She straightened, wiped her hands on her apron as if the drawing had been something wet. "Then it's true enough to cut. Listen to me, girl." She gripped the edge of the bench, and the old scarred wood creaked under fingers that had worked ropes for forty winters. "A plate is a promise. Once that image goes into a bound book and a book goes into a bookseller's window, you can't call it back. Not with money, not with magistrates, not with sorry. Are you ready to promise?"

"The historians will dock within days of the break," I said. "Corven's report will be in their hands before their trunks are off the steamer. If there are two maps in the atlas instead of one, they must ask *why*. They are paid not to ask why — I know that — but Professor Maren's reputation rests on asking, and Corven has hired the wrong kind of scholar if he thinks a sealed mouth comes cheap." I heard my own voice go hard and was glad of it. "One map is a story. Two maps are an argument. I mean for them to argue in front of everyone who opens the book."

Brida studied me for a long moment, the way she might study weather coming up the valley.

"Your master left you the tools," she said finally. "I think he left you the plate too, didn't he. Last blank one."

"Yes."

"Then he knew." She picked up her lantern. "Cut clean, Tamsin. And don't work alone past midnight — a lamp burning late tells the whole town what you're about, and half of them will want to help and half will want to stop you, and you won't know which is which until it's too late."

She paused at the door.

"I'll send Ada tomorrow. And I'll send the boy Joss doesn't know I watch — the one who runs errands for the alderman. Pike's clerk has been asking after your lease. Ask after him back."

Then she was gone into the blue snow-light, and the door closed, and I was alone with the plate again.

* * *

Ada Quill arrived the next morning carrying a slate wrapped in flannel like an infant, and Joss Renner arrived ten minutes behind her because he refused to arrive with anyone.

The schoolmistress set her burden on the bench and unwrapped it with ceremony. Twenty years of dust rose off the frame. On the surface, in chalk gone ghost-grey but legible to a woman who had spent her life reading faint things: the true names of the drowned, written in the neat upright hand of a young schoolmistress who had risked her post every single day simply by not erasing.

"These are the spellings as the parents gave them," Ada said. "Not as the parish clerk corrected them. The clerk spelled by ear and pride. The mothers spelled by heart. If you're putting names in copper, Miss Ashgrove, you'll put the mothers' spelling."

I compared her slate against my margin draft and found six discrepancies. Six families whose names the record had quietly reshaped into respectability — the way a tailor lets out a coat until the original owner couldn't wear it. I corrected every one.

Joss stood by the window with his arms folded, watching the street through the gap in the shutter. He had come, I understood, less from conviction than from a hunger he hadn't named yet; the penal colony had burned most words out of him, and the ones remaining were mostly for striking. But when Ada read the list aloud, checking each against the slate, I saw him move his lips with the ninth name, and I saw his jaw set like mortar.

"The fire," he said abruptly, to the window. "Put the fire on the plate too. The mill fire. Same night as the flood. Put it on."

"It's not proven how it started—"

"Put it *on*," he said, turning. His eyes were the flat grey of river ice. "You don't have to say who lit it. Just put the burn scar where the burn scar was. Let the gentlemen from the capital look at the shape of it and do their own arithmetic." He tapped the window frame once, hard, as if knocking on a door twenty years locked. "Everything else on your plate is water and names. The fire is the part they'll try hardest to drown. So mark it."

He was right, and I told him so, and something in his shoulders loosened a degree — the first degree, the way ice gives before it breaks. I added the fire to the drawing: a ruled hatch of scorch reaching north from the mill foundations, dated, unexplained, undeniable.

* * *

That night I began to cut.

I worked the way Hollis had taught me: coarse graver first, laying the skeleton of the coastlines and roads so the eye would accept the map as kin to its official twin — that was essential, the deception of familiarity, for a reader startled by strangeness closes the book, while a reader comforted by the known looks closer. Every road, every bridge, every field boundary identical to Corven's sanctioned sheet. Only then the differences, cut fine and deep: the row, the flood line, the fire, the names.

Copper cuts like a confession. The burr rises along the stroke, a bright lip of metal that catches lamplight, and you learn quickly whether your hand is steady or lying. Mine was steady. Hour after hour the rectangles of the row emerged, then the pump, then the elms — Brida's elms, drawn from her description, their crowns wider than any sapling should be because grief measures generously. The letters went slowest; names in copper must be flawless, for a misspelled name is a second erasure, and I would not be party to that.

The furnace wanted feeding twice in the night. I fed it myself, sleeves rolled, face scorched, thinking of Hollis doing the same thing alone in this room for thirty years, and of the nights he must have stood exactly where I stood, holding a graver over a truth he could not afford to cut and could not afford to lose. He had chosen silence and died of it. I was choosing noise, and we would see what it cost.

Toward dawn I etched the marginal inscription, the sentence I had composed and recomposed forty times until it was plain as a fencepost:

*This plate records the labourers' row, East Bank, destroyed by flood and fire in February 1831, and the eleven children thereof, dispersed by order of the town. Engraved from sworn testimony and surviving witness. Here placed beside the official map, which omits them.*

No accusation. No adjective. Facts, dates, witnesses — the grammar of documents, the language Corven's own historians respected. Let the plate speak record-speak; the record itself would do the accusing.

I printed a proof on the hand press at first light, pulled the damp sheet carefully, and held it to the window.

The row looked back at me.

Fourteen houses. Eleven names. One flood line drawn honest across a bend of river that had spent sixteen years pretending otherwise. It was not beautiful — grief rarely engraves beautifully — but it was *true*, and truth has a finish all its own, like frost on glass.

* * *

There remained the problem of binding.

Thirty-one copies of the atlas sat crated in the workshop's back room, sewn, cased, and awaiting delivery — awaiting, in fact, collection by Corven's agents, who meant to present them to the visiting scholars as finished and final. Thirty-one copies, and one loose supplementary plate, and perhaps five days before the ice broke. Sewing a tipped-in leaf into a bound volume is delicate work even without a magistrate's men at the door.

I spread the copies across the floor and opened each to the plate section, measuring, planning. The supplementary sheet matched the others in size and stock; tipped in at the rear with paste and a guard strip, it would look almost native — almost, but not quite, and that *not quite* was the point. A map inserted secretly might be dismissed as forgery. A map inserted openly, with a printer's note declaring its provenance, would force every reader to choose which map they believed. I did not want to smuggle the truth into the atlas. I wanted to bind it there, in daylight, stitch by stitch, the way Ada had kept her slate and Brida had kept her count: openly, dangerously, in full view of anyone with the courage to look.

But there were thirty-one copies and one of me, and five days, and a town full of eyes.

I heard footsteps on the stair — the uneven tread I now knew better than my own heartbeat — and Joss came in without knocking, as was becoming his custom, carrying a second lamp and a coil of binding cord and an expression that dared me to comment.

"Brida says you'll ruin your eyes and drop dead before the third copy," he said. "She's sending the ferry girl to feed your furnace tomorrow. Ada can fold and guard. I can't sew worth a damn, but I can hold a spine steady, and I can stand at that window with a poker." He dropped the cord on the bench and looked around the room, at the plates, the crates, the proof-sheet still drying on its line — at the whole fragile machinery of the thing we had decided to do. "Thirty-one books. How long per copy?"

"An hour, if nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing will go right," Joss said, with the certainty of a man who had survived a penal colony by expecting the worst and being correct. "So start tonight. And Tamsin—" He hesitated. It was the first hesitation I had ever seen in him. "When the ice goes, it goes fast. Louder than you think. Whole river moves at once, like something waking up angry. You'll hear it from here." He jerked his chin at the window, at the pale seam of dawn over the water. "Best be further along than you planned when it does."

He took up his post at the window, poker in hand, and I heated the paste.

The first atlas opened under my hands with a smell of glue and cold linen. I laid the guard strip, brushed the paste thin, set the plate against the gutter — my own engraved truth sliding into the body of the official lie, home at last — and pressed it flat.

Outside, far down the valley, the river made a sound I had never heard it make: not a groan, not a crack, but a deep, shifting boom, like a door thrown open in the belly of the world.

Joss turned from the window. "That's early," he said. "That's two days early."
