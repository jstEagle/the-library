## 23. Night Work

*Ada Quill's hands had not shaken over a slate in twenty years, but they shook now.*

She stood in the doorway of the workshop at a quarter past eleven, her shawl pulled tight, the cold coming off the river in long gray breaths. Inside, the furnace roared like something alive and hungry, and Tamsin Ashgrove bent over the copper plate with her graver, her spine curved into the work as though she were praying at it.

"You shouldn't be here," Tamsin said without looking up. She'd heard the door. Of course she had; the girl had ears like a fox.

"Miss Ashgrove." Ada closed the door behind her and let the warmth hit her face. It smelled of acid and coal smoke and hot metal — the smell of a schoolroom that had caught fire and decided to keep burning. "I dismissed my last pupil an hour ago. I am free until dawn, and I intend to spend my freedom badly."

Tamsin looked up then, and Ada saw what the lamplight did to her face: the exhaustion underneath the resolve, the bruised hollows under her eyes. The girl had been engraving for three days nearly without sleep. The plate before her was half-finished — Ada could see the laborers' row taking shape, house by house, chimney by chimney, the little street resurrected line by line under Tamsin's hand.

"I brought bread," Ada said, setting the wrapped loaf on the bench. "And I brought myself. You said you needed witnesses."

"Witnesses to what?"

"To the truth of it. That's what testimony is, isn't it? Someone who was there, saying *this happened*." Ada unwound her scarf finger by finger. "I have been there for twenty years, Miss Ashgrove. I have been there every single day, standing in front of children, teaching them the names of rivers and counties from maps that lied about our own street. I would like to be here for one night while someone puts it right."

Tamsin set down her graver. For a moment she looked very young, and very tired, and Ada understood suddenly that this girl — this composed, capable, grief-hardened girl — had been carrying the whole weight of it alone because she believed no one else would take any of it up.

"The furnace needs feeding every two hours," Tamsin said quietly. "The acid bath can't cool or the bite goes wrong. And if Corven's men come—"

"If Corven's men come," Ada said, "they will find a schoolmistress of forty-six years standing between them and a copper plate, and they will be so astonished they will forget what they came for."

Something moved across Tamsin's face that might have been a smile.

"I'll take the first watch," Ada said, pulling a stool to the window. "Go and sleep on Brida's pallet for two hours. That is not a request. A teacher knows when a pupil is about to fall asleep in the inkwell."

* * *

*Brida Ferris had kept the ferry for thirty-one years, and in all that time nobody had told her what to do with her nights.*

She came up from the river path at midnight with a lantern in one fist and a sack of coal on her shoulder, and she did not knock. The workshop door opened before she reached it — the Quill woman, sharp-eyed behind the glass — and Brida shouldered past her into the heat with a grunt that meant *good evening* in the only language she'd ever trusted.

"Well?" Ada said.

"She's sleeping." Brida dumped the coal by the furnace and stood warming her hands, flexing fingers stiffened by thirty years of rope and oar. "Slept the moment her head touched the boards. Like a child after a drowning." She hadn't meant to say that part. It came out anyway, the way true things did when you were old and tired.

Ada looked at her. "You've seen children sleep like that before."

Brida fed the furnace rather than answer. The coals went up orange, and the light moved across the walls, across the racks of plates, across the half-engraved map that was going to cost them all something before the ice broke. She didn't look at it too long. Looking at it too long made her chest do things.

"There were eleven of us watching out for them, back then," she said finally. "The winter of '31. Eleven children, and the whole town pretending they'd sprung up out of the snow like mushrooms." She poked the fire harder than it needed. "Nobody watched then. Everybody looked away. So now we watch. That's all this is. That's all it ever was going to be."

Ada was quiet a moment. "You could have said no, you know. When Tamsin came to your ferry."

"Aye, I could have." Brida snorted. "Thirty-one years on that water, girl. You think I ferried people across because I liked their conversation?"

Outside, Marlow Bend lay dark under a sky scoured clean of cloud, stars sharp as nailheads. From the workshop windows the furnace-light spilled yellow onto the snow, and anyone walking the lane below — the baker's boy up early, the constable making his rounds, some insomniac leaning at a window — would see it plain as a sermon: lights burning at Hollis's old workshop through the small hours, figures passing behind the glass, smoke rising steady from a chimney that ought to be cold.

Let them see, Brida thought. Let them wonder. Wondering was the first crack in any wall, and she'd spent three decades learning how walls came down.

"Tamsin says the plate will need four more nights," Ada said.

"Then we've four more nights of coal and coffee and sitting up like gulls on a mooring post." Brida settled onto the stool by the window, lantern at her knee. "Get you home, schoolmistress. You're no use to anyone yawning."

"I'll relieve you at four."

"Aye. If the river doesn't eat us first."

It was a joke. Mostly.

* * *

*Night the second, and Ada brought more than bread.*

She brought a slate — her own, the old one, the one she'd scrubbed clean every Friday evening for twenty years and never once let out of her sight. She carried it inside her coat like contraband, which, she supposed, it always had been.

Tamsin stared at it when Ada set it on the bench between them. On its surface, in chalk gone ghostly with age and re-tracing, were names. Rowan Ashgrove. Marta Renner. Silas and Edith Pike. Eleven households, written in a young woman's careful hand, preserved through two decades of fear by a schoolmistress who taught the founders' children arithmetic by day and kept the drowned alive by night.

"This is—" Tamsin stopped. Her hand hovered above the slate without touching it, the way you hover over something holy or hot.

"It's why I'm still in Marlow Bend," Ada said. "I could have taken a post in the county seat years ago. Better pay. But a slate leaves a classroom, and a classroom leaves a town, and who would remember?" She smoothed her skirt. "My mother used to say that remembering is a chore like any other. Somebody has to do the washing, somebody has to mend the nets, somebody has to remember."

"Your mother was one of the row." It wasn't a question. Tamsin had read the slate.

"My mother sang while she worked. That's how Joss Renner knew her — he told me once he could hear her over the mill noise, all the way from his father's forge." Ada's voice held steady, which cost her. "She's on your plate, Miss Ashgrove. Third house from the ferry landing. Will you carve her singing into it somehow?"

"No," Tamsin said softly. "But I'll carve her name where the historians can't help reading it."

They worked through the hours — Tamsin at the plate, Ada keeping the furnace and the ledger of hours, marking each watch in pencil like attendance in a register. *Quill, eleven to one. Ferris, one to four. Renner promised for tomorrow.* It looked, Ada thought, absurdly like a school roster. Perhaps that was what it was. The found family assembling itself around a shared act of witness, one name at a time.

At half past two, footsteps crunched the snow outside, and both women went still. A shadow crossed the frosted glass — paused — moved on. When Brida cracked the door, the lane was empty except for bootprints leading toward the Corven house on the hill.

"They're counting our watches," Brida said, coming back in.

"Good," said Tamsin, not lifting her eyes from the plate. "Let them count. Every hour they spend counting is an hour the historians aren't being told we're finished."

* * *

*Brida took the dead watch, three to five, because she slept least anyway.*

Old women and rivers kept the same hours, she'd always said. Up before light, down with the dark, restless in between. So she sat the coldest hours alone by the window with her tea going lukewarm and watched the town breathe.

On the third night, the whispers started in earnest.

She heard the first of them at dawn, down at the ferry, from the fishmonger's boy waiting for the first crossing. *Lights at the Ashgrove place again. Every night. They say the dead master walks.* By noon the fishwife had improved it: *They say the girl's engraving the devil's own map, selling souls by the inch.* By dusk the minister's wife had gotten hold of it, and the version reaching Brida's ear at vespers was that Tamsin Ashgrove was printing money in the cellar, or forging deeds, or — Brida's favorite — communing with the ghost of Elias Vane's grandmother, which was remarkable since Vane's grandmother had died in Kent before either of them was born.

Brida let them talk. Talk was weather; it passed over and left the ground unchanged. What mattered was the ground.

What mattered was that on the fourth night, when she climbed the hill to the workshop at her usual hour, there was already a light in the window that wasn't Tamsin's lamp — and a figure on the step, broad-shouldered, wrapped in a coat that had crossed an ocean in the hold of a prison ship.

"Joss," she said.

He turned. Twenty years had carved him hard — the boy who'd run messages along the row was gone, replaced by a man with a convict's pallor and a sailor's stillness, eyes that measured everything and gave back nothing. He looked at her a long moment.

"Ferris," he said. "Still rowing people around."

"Still breathing. That's more than some of us managed." She studied him. "Word travels."

"Word travels. Word said there was work here worth doing." His jaw worked. "I haven't decided if I believe it."

From inside came the small, relentless sound of the graver on copper — *tick, tick, tick* — patient as a heartbeat. Brida pushed the door open.

"Then don't believe it yet," she said. "Come and look first. Looking's free."

Joss Renner stood on the threshold a moment longer, snow settling on his shoulders, the furnace-light gold against his scarred hands. Then he stepped inside, out of the cold, and the door swung shut behind him — and down in the dark lane below, unseen by any of them, a man in a magistrate's livery turned away from the wall he'd been leaning against and started, quickly, toward the Corven house on the hill.
