## 24. The Convict Returns

The boat came up from Van Diemen's Land the way bad weather comes — announced for weeks, dreaded all the same, and worse than anyone predicted when it finally arrived.

Joss Renner stood at the rail of the *Coriander* with a canvas bag over one shoulder and fourteen years of someone else's country in his bones, and looked at Marlow Bend the way a man looks at a debt. The town had not grown so much as thickened. New slate roofs where shingles had been. A second story on the toll house. The mill rebuilt on the same scar of ground, bigger and stupider than before, its wheel turning like it had never once stopped to consider what it had cost.

He had thought about this moment every day of the voyage home and most days of the seven years before that, and he had imagined it would feel like something. Vengeance, maybe. Or grief. At minimum, anger with a clean edge to it.

What he felt was nothing much at all. Just cold. The river was still locked in March ice, groaning out at midstream like an old man turning over in bed, and the town sat behind it, warm-lit and indifferent, and Joss Renner stood at the rail and understood that he had served his sentence for a crime committed by other people, against him, and no boat was going to fix that arithmetic.

They let him off at the ferry stairs because the proper dock was iced in. Brida Ferris took his coin without looking at his face, then looked at it anyway, and her hands went still on the rope.

"Lord," she said. "Joss Renner."

"Don't." He said it flat, before she could build anything on it. "Don't do the voice. Don't do the face. I'm passing through, Brida. That's all."

She held his eye a moment longer than he liked. She had always been able to do that — hold an eye like a door held open against wind — and he hated it now the way he'd hated it at nine years old, standing in her kitchen while adults decided things above his head.

"There's work," she said. "If you want it."

"I don't."

"There's people."

"That's worse."

He shouldered his bag and walked up the frozen stair into the town that had made him and unmade him and did not remember doing either, and behind him he heard Brida say, quietly, to nobody, "Eleven of us. Still counting."

---

He took a room at the Drowned Rat because it was the cheapest bed and the farthest from Corven's green door, and because he remembered — distantly, the way you remember a smell from childhood — that the landlord's father had once owed money to Joss's mother, and shame keeps ledgers longer than banks do.

The room smelled of tallow and wet wool. He put his bag under the cot, checked that the knife sewn into his coat seam was where it always was, and sat down to count what he owned: a ticket stub, a letter of discharge stamped by a government that had shipped him across the world for stealing bread he hadn't stolen, three pounds eleven, and a burn scar shaped like a hand on his left forearm that he woke up touching some mornings, though he could not have said whose hand.

That last one was new. Or rather, it was old and newly strange. On the voyage north he had begun dreaming of fire — not the prison fire, not the quarry kilns, but a different fire, low and orange and full of singing, a woman's voice going up through smoke — and he would wake with his fingers pressed to the scar as if checking a wound for fever. He did not believe in dreams that meant things. He had spent fourteen years teaching himself that nothing meant anything except what a man could hold in his two hands.

But the dreams kept coming, and Marlow Bend kept sitting across the ice from him, and somewhere in the middle of that first night back he gave up on sleep, went down to the taproom, and got drunk enough to ask questions instead of answering them.

The landlord talked. Landlords always talk; it's why they keep taps. And what the town was talking about that week, in the hushed, delighted way towns talk about scandal, was a woman engraver. Hollis's girl. The map-maker. Who had gone strange since the master died, locking herself in the workshop, burning lamp-oil at hours no Christian kept, asking after records nobody wanted found. There was a magistrate involved. There was talk of an inquiry come spring. There was a rumor — the landlord lowered his voice here, savoring it — that the Ashgrove woman claimed there had once been houses on the river flat where now there was nothing but willow scrub and flood-mud. Houses, and people in them.

"Rubbish, of course," the landlord said comfortably. "Never was any row down there. My grandfather fished that flat all his life."

"Did he," Joss said.

His voice came out wrong. Flat and strange, like a plank dropped on stone. The landlord glanced at him, reassessed, and found urgent business with the tap handles, and Joss sat alone with his ale going warm and his heart going hard, staring at the wall where a faded print of the county hung, and on the print the river flat below the mill was empty, blank, clean paper, and he knew — he knew the way you know your own name — that there had been a window there. Third house from the willow. His mother's window. With a candle in it, some nights, waiting up.

He walked out into the cold without finishing the ale.

* * *

He told himself he wasn't going to the workshop. He told himself it all the way down Tanner's Lane and past the tannery reek and along the frozen ditch, telling himself with each step the way you tell yourself you won't look over a edge even as your feet carry you to it. What he intended — what he swore to himself he intended — was to stand outside and look, the way you look at a grave, and walk away.

What happened was that the door opened before he reached it.

A woman stood in the gap, ink-stained to the wrist, hair escaping its pins in gray-shot ropes, and she looked at him with eyes too old for her face and said, "You're the tenth."

Not *who are you*. Not *can I help you*. *You're the tenth.* As if she'd been counting. As if she'd been standing at that door for days, waiting for a number.

"I'm nobody's," Joss said. "I'm nothing. I'm a man who did seven years' hard labor in a place your maps probably don't bother with either, and I've come home to collect what's owed me, and it isn't from you, engraver, so shut your door."

She didn't shut the door. She stood there in the lamplight with her ruined hands and her stubborn face, and she said, "My name is Tamsin Ashgrove. Will you come in out of the cold? It costs you nothing but an hour, and I think you'll want the hour back afterward."

"I don't want anything back."

"No," she said quietly. "I think you want it forward. Come in, Joss."

Hearing his name in her mouth did what the cold hadn't managed. It went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor. Because she said it like she knew it — not *Renner*, not *prisoner*, not *boy*, but *Joss*, the sound his mother had made over a wooden spoon, the sound the row had known, and there was no earthly way this woman, this stranger, this engraver born to some respectable family or other, had any right to it.

He went in. He told himself it was because of the cold.

* * *

The workshop was a furnace-gutted cathedral of a room, presses and plates and the sweet sharp ghost of acid, and she sat him down by the stove and poured tea he didn't touch and laid it all out for him the way he supposed she laid out engravings — everything squared, everything precise. The copybook. Eleven names in a child's careful hand. The razored parish page. Brida's testimony, written out fair. The laborers' row on the oldest survey maps and gone from every later one. The winter of '31. The flood, the fire, the dispersal, the founders' tidy signatures.

He listened with his arms crossed and his jaw set and his whole body a closed door, and when she finished he laughed. It was not a kind laugh. It was not even entirely sane.

"You want me to weep?" he said. "Is that it? You want the convict to fall on his knees and thank God somebody remembers? I'll tell you what I remember, woman. I remember being nine years old and hungry in a house where the roof leaked and the magistrate's man came every quarter-day to collect rent my mother couldn't pay, and I remember being taken away in a cart with my wrists tied because I stole a ham off Corven's own table — and I didn't steal it, I carried it, there's a difference, and the boy who planned it wore a good coat and grew up to sign papers saying none of us ever existed — and I remember the colony, and I remember the lash, and I remember learning that memory is just the thing that hurts longest. So no. I don't weep. And I don't help. And I especially don't help *her*—" his finger stabbed toward the copybook, toward the neat child's letters, "—because whoever wrote that list was a child who trusted paper, and paper is what buried us. Paper is their weapon. It has been their weapon since the first deed was cut. And you sit there with your maps and your plates wanting to fight paper with paper, and I'm telling you it cannot be done, because they own the paper, they own the ink, they own the men who judge what the ink means. You'll lose. They always win. The only thing a man like me can do is leave."

He was on his feet without remembering rising. His tea untouched. His breath ragged.

Tamsin Ashgrove looked up at him across the littered table, and she did not flinch, and she did not argue, and after a long moment she said only:

"All right."

"All right?"

"You don't owe me belief. You don't owe me anything — least of all the ones who hurt you. Go, if you're going." She rose too, unhurried, and gathered the copybook, and paused. "There's one thing. Before you go. It isn't paper. It's stone. Paper can be razored out, burned, rewritten — I know better than anyone, I've done the rewriting myself. But stone is stupid. Stone just holds what a child's hand cut into it, and waits."

"What are you talking about?"

"The orchard cottage cellar," she said. "Come and see. Then go, if you're still going. I'll not stop you. But I think you should see it with your own eyes rather than mine, because yours are the ones that were there."

* * *

They walked out through the dark together, past the sleeping town, past the mill with its idiot turning wheel, down onto the river flat where the willows stood like a congregation of the drowned. The ice boomed and shifted out in the channel. Somewhere under that ice, under that mud, under forty years of silt, lay whatever remained of the third house from the willow, and Joss Renner walked over it without letting himself know it, because knowing it would break him open and he had spent fourteen years learning how to stay shut.

The orchard cottage sat back from the flat on higher ground, old trees around it bent and black against the stars. Tamsin unlocked the cellar door with a key on a ring she carried like a rosary, lit a lantern, and led him down steps worn hollow by feet.

The cellar was low-ceilinged and dry-smelling, shelved along one wall, and along the far wall — the foundation wall, the oldest stone — the lantern light caught scratches.

Initials. Cut small and high, at the height of children reaching, a scatter of them like birds startled up a wall. Some crude, some careful. Dates beside a few.

And there, third from the left, cut deep with the furious precision of a boy who wanted the world to remember him whether it wished to or not:

**J.R.**

Beside it, smaller, in a different, wobbling hand, a word he had not thought of in thirty years, a word that came up out of the dark of him like a body coming up out of flooded water:

**HOME**

He did not hear himself make the sound he made. He learned of it afterward, from Tamsin, who told him gently that he had gone down on his knees on the cellar floor with both hands flat against the stone, over his own initials, the way a man presses a wound, and that the sound had been low and continuous, like something large tearing slowly.

He touched the letters. The cuts were shallow — a nail, a knife-point, a child's tool — but they were deep enough. Fourteen years of penal stone and quarry dust and other men's names shouted at him in the dark, and here, in a root cellar in the town that had erased him, a nine-year-old boy had scratched *home* into rock with a nail, believing — knowing — that stone would keep it.

Stone had kept it. Stone had kept it the way nothing else in his life had kept anything.

"He carved it," Tamsin said softly. She was crouched nearby, not touching him, giving him the space he'd have killed for and needed equally. "All of them did. Eleven sets. I found mine three weeks ago and it took the ground out from under me, so I know what this is costing you, and I'm sorry for it. But you asked the world to remember you, Joss. Look. It did. We did. I've spent a month proving that eleven children existed and were scattered and struck from the record, and I can prove it on paper well enough — but paper is exactly what you say it is, theirs. What I can't manufacture is witnesses. Living ones. Ones with faces and scars and mothers' names. The historians come when the ice breaks — days now, maybe less — and when they dock, the founders will show them a town with no tragedy in it, signed and sworn and spotless. Unless someone stands up and says otherwise. Unless ten someones do."

He knelt there a long time. The lantern hissed. Out beyond the walls, faintly, the great ice sheet boomed again, a sound like a door being tried.

"My mother sang," he said at last. His voice came out wrecked and he let it. "Low songs, work songs. I dream about it. Fire and singing. I never knew if I dreamed it or lived it."

"You lived it," Tamsin said. "Brida remembers the song. She says it was your mother's, and that the whole row could hear it on washing days."

He closed his eyes. Under his palms, the stone was cold and certain and true.

When he finally stood, his knees cracked like the river ice, and he wiped his face with the back of his wrist — the scarred wrist, the burned wrist — and looked at Tamsin Ashgrove with red-rimmed eyes that had gone, somehow, from dead to lit, the way coals go when the ash is raked off them.

"I'm not promising you gentle," he said. "Fourteen years in that place taught me ways of fighting you don't want printed in your nice atlas. If Corven's men come at that workshop, I'll meet them with more than words. Understand me?"

"Understood."

"And I want the plate. The new one. The one with the row on it." His jaw worked. "I want my mother's house on a map. Third from the willow. Where it belongs. I want it drawn exact, and I want it never erased again as long as paper lasts."

"It will be," Tamsin said. "I swear it on the stone."

He nodded once, curt, done with tenderness forever. Then he turned back to the wall, to the scatter of small letters, and counted them aloud under his breath like a man taking inventory of what the fire had left him. Eleven. All accounted for.

"Tamsin." He said her name differently now — not a stranger's name but a fellow survivor's, a comrade's. "Who else have you found?"

"Ada Quill keeps a schoolmistress's slate with our parents' true names on it. Brida's testified. Merrit Corven—" she hesitated, "—Merrit is complicated. And Pike signed their affidavit. He's theirs now, or says he is."

"Pike." Joss spat, precisely, onto the cellar floor. "Then we'd best move fast. Because I knew Alderman Pike when he was a boy in a good coat, and I know what he's afraid of, and I know what Corven's holding over him. And I heard something on the docks tonight, before I ever came to you — sailors talking in the Rat. The ice is breaking upriver already. Loose floes at Harker's Bend. The steamer's coming early, engraver. Days early."

The lantern flame leaned, as if the earth itself had drawn a sudden breath.

"So whatever you mean to cut into that plate," Joss Renner said, turning for the stairs with the fire fully lit in him now, "you'd best start cutting tonight."
