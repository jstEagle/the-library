## 25. Sabotage

The press screw gave way at half past two in the morning, with a sound like a bone breaking in a closed fist.

Tamsin heard it from the bench where she had been working the supplementary plate by candlelight — a sharp crack beneath the low groan of the furnace, followed by a silence that felt worse than the noise itself. She set down her burin. The workshop was dim, lit only by her candle and the orange seam of the forge door, and for a long moment nothing moved but the shadows.

Then Joss Renner was out of his chair by the wall, where he slept in fits between watches, and across the room faster than she would have believed a man could move after three days of night labor.

"Nobody touch it," he said.

"I didn't—"

"Nobody touch anything." He crouched beside the great iron press, its frame looming over him like a gallows, and ran his hand along the screw housing without touching the screw itself. His fingers stopped. He looked up at her, and in the candlelight his face had gone very still, the way it went still before he swore.

"Tamsin."

"What?"

"This wasn't wear." He took the candle from her side of the room and held it close to the mechanism. "Wear breaks clean. Wear splits along the grain of the casting. Look here." He turned the flame so she could see into the shadowed joint where the screw entered its collar. "There's a notch cut into this thread. Filed in. Then they leaned on the wheel — or put the boys on it, some night when Ada and Brida were walking their watch — and let the pressure do the rest. It didn't snap from use." His voice dropped. "It was broken on purpose."

The words seemed to fall through the floor of the workshop and keep falling.

Tamsin stood very still, her hands hanging at her sides, and felt the shape of the last week reorganize itself around this new fact. The watch rotation. Brida on the ferry steps until midnight, then Ada taking the long walk from the schoolhouse, then Joss sleeping against the wall with one boot on. They had guarded the doors and windows like soldiers guarding a gate — and someone had simply been inside all along, or come and gone as freely as weather, because Marlow Bend did not need to break into a building that belonged to it.

"Who?" she said. And then, hearing how childish the question sounded, she answered it herself: "Anyone. Anyone at all. The cooper's boy who delivers our charcoal. The woman who sells us eggs. Corven has a whole town full of hands, and we've been feeding them."

Joss straightened up slowly. He had a habit, she had noticed, of touching things when he was thinking — the press frame now, his palm flat against the cold iron as though he could read it the way she read plates. "It'll take me a day to cut a new collar," he said. "Two, if I have to recast. I can machine a replacement screw off the lathe in the mill yard, but I'd need—" He stopped. "I'd need to be seen going to the mill yard. Which I will be."

"Then don't go yet. Work it out first. We'll find another way."

"There isn't another way." He said it without heat, which somehow made it heavier. "You want every atlas in that pile sewn with your second plate before the steamer docks. Without the press, binding takes four times as long. We're not choosing between fast and slow anymore. We're choosing between done and not done."

* * *

They found what had been done to the acid bath an hour later, and Tamsin would remember afterward that it was Ada Quill who found it — Ada, who had come off her watch at dawn smelling of river fog, hung her shawl on the peg by the door, and stopped mid-motion with her nose lifted like a hound's.

"Do you smell that?"

The etching room lay behind the second door, the small stone-floored chamber where the copper plates went into their baths of nitric acid and came out carrying rivers and roads and, soon now, if God and eleven stubborn people permitted, the truth. Tamsin opened the door and the smell hit her properly: acid, yes, the familiar sharp bite of it — but under it something else. Something mineral and wrong, like the air over a slag heap.

The bath itself told the story plainly enough once she looked. A crust had formed on the surface overnight, a pale scum that should never have gathered so quickly, and when she drew a glass rod through it the liquid beneath came away clouded, milky, ruined.

"Salt," Joss said behind her. He had come to the doorway and would not step inside; he stood with his arms folded, looking at the bath the way other men looked at graves. "Common salt, thrown in by the handful. It doesn't stop the acid — that's the cleverness of it. It just poisons the bite. You'd dip your plate, think everything's fine, pull it out, and find the lines eaten shallow and pitted. Ruined plate, ruined week, and no one would know why. You'd blame yourself." He spat, carefully, toward the drain. "Somebody in this town knows enough about etching to hurt us proper. That narrows the field more than you'd think."

"Or somebody told them how," Ada said quietly. She had come up beside Tamsin and was peering at the crust with her schoolmistress's squint, the look she wore over a pupil's slate when she already suspected the answer. "Hollis kept apprentices years back. Half the trade in the valley passed through this room."

"And the other half drinks with the magistrate's clerks," said Joss.

Tamsin knelt by the bath and did not say anything for a while. Her reflection floated up at her through the clouded acid, broken and doubled, a face split down the middle. *This is what they think of us,* she thought. *Not enemies at a gate. Vermin in a cellar.* The thought should have frightened her. What it did instead, she noticed with detached interest, was make her angry in a very quiet, very durable way — the anger of a woman who has already lost everything once and knows exactly how much there is left to lose.

"We dump it," she said, standing. "All of it. Scrub the trough with lye water, rinse twice, mix fresh from Hollis's stores. Joss, can you test the new batch on scrap before we trust it to the plate?"

"I'll test it on my own hide if I have to."

"No," Tamsin said. "On mine. If someone salts it again, better my hands than yours. Yours are the only ones in this town that can mend a press screw."

* * *

By noon the workshop had transformed into something between a fortress and an operating theater. Brida arrived from the ferry with her skirts frozen stiff to the knee, having crossed the half-thawed river at first light to spread word among her network of gossips and fishwives — and having brought back, as payment for her trouble, a sack of charcoal, a jug of vinegar, and a report.

"The cooper's boy swears he saw a light in your yard Thursday past," she said, unwinding her scarf in the doorway, her old face ruddy with cold and satisfaction both. "Thought it was you, working late. Said it moved along the north wall and then was gone. And Mrs. Halloway at the chandlery says she sold two pounds of common salt to a man she didn't recognize, day before yesterday, paid silver and wouldn't give a name." Brida's eyes glittered. "Salt. To a stranger. In a river town, in February, where everyone's salting their own pork since November and nobody needs more."

"A stranger," Tamsin repeated. "Or a face nobody looks at anymore."

"That too." Brida settled onto the stool by the stove with the groan of a woman sixty years at the oars. "Girl, listen to me. I've been alive a long time and most of it in this bend of the river, and I'll tell you what I know about people doing wicked work. They don't come in masks. They come in the faces you've stopped seeing. The clerk who's always at the counter. The nephew who visits his aunt. The man who delivers the coal." She held Tamsin's gaze with eyes that had watched the ice dam break thirty years ago and had never entirely blinked since. "Corven doesn't need to send hired men from the capital. Why would he? He owns every ordinary hour in this town. He just has to ask, casual-like, at the right dinner table, and some respectable soul does the wicked thing between supper and bed, and sleeps fine after."

Ada, at the workbench, had stopped sanding her slate-copies to listen. "Brida's right. And it means the guard rotation is useless — forgive me, Joss, but it is. We're watching the doors of a house whose walls are made of neighbors."

"Then we change what we guard," Joss said. He had spent the morning at the lathe he'd rigged from scavenged parts, cutting threads into a new collar blank, and his forearms were black with iron filings. He came over wiping his hands. "We can't watch people. There are too many and we don't know which. So we watch the work instead. From today, nothing sensitive happens in this building that one person knows alone. The fresh acid gets mixed by two of us, always. The finished plates don't sleep here — they go to the cellar under the orchard cottage, same as the others, carried one at a time, different hours, different carriers. And the press—" He laid his hand on the broken mechanism almost tenderly. "The press I fix myself, tonight, and tomorrow I start teaching Tamsin to run it solo, so that even if they take me, the work goes on."

"You assume they'll take you," Tamsin said.

"I assume they'll try something again," Joss said, "and next time they won't bother being clever about it." He said it flatly, without drama, the way men spoke who had been transported across an ocean at fourteen and had stopped expecting the world to be kind. "Fire next time. Or the lease revoked outright — though Corven's too smart for that while the historians are watching. No. Next time it'll be fire, and we ought to decide tonight who sleeps here and who sleeps elsewhere, so nobody's inside when it comes."

The word *fire* sat in the middle of the room. None of them touched it. All of them felt it. Eleven children had survived one fire in the winter of '31; none of them had forgotten what fire was for, or whose hands had held the torch.

"We rotate," Tamsin said at last. "Nobody sleeps alone in the building. Two at all times, one awake always. And we move the master copies of the plate out today — I'll carry the first myself, at noon, in daylight, where anyone who wants to watch me can watch me. Let them know we expect their eyes. Maybe shame slows them down. Maybe not. But hiding has gotten us exactly one broken screw and a poisoned bath."

* * *

She carried the wrapped plate to the orchard cottage at noon, through streets that felt suddenly populated with watchers. Every figure at a window became a spy; every boy with a handcart became a courier of salt and files. She hated herself a little for it — hated the way suspicion colonized the mind, filling familiar lanes with strangers. This was what Corven had built, she realized, whether he designed it or merely profited by it: a town where the truth could not be whispered above a certain volume, because the walls themselves had ears and the ears had wages and families and fears. The founders' erasure had never ended in 1831. It was renewed daily, by hundreds of small complicit silences, and she had lived twenty years inside one of those silences without ever hearing it.

In the cellar of the orchard cottage she knelt by the wall where the eleven initials were carved — her own hand among them, carved by a child she could not remember being — and slid the wrapped plate into the cavity beneath the loose flagstone, beside its sisters. Her fingers brushed the carved letters as she worked. *T.A.* She had begun, in these last weeks, to touch them deliberately, the way one touches a scar to confirm it still hurts. Evidence, Ada called it. Proof for Professor Maren when the historians came. But privately, in the cold dark, Tamsin knew it was also a message across time: *you were here, you were real, hold on.*

She was climbing the cellar stairs when she heard the bell at the workshop — three quick strikes, the signal they had agreed on, meaning *come now, bring no one.*

She ran the whole way, skirts gathered, breath smoking, and burst through the door to find Joss holding Brida's arm. Brida, who had walked her ferry route twice a day for forty years, was grey-faced and leaning hard on him, and there was blood on her temple, a thin line of it, already darkening.

"Ferry license," she said before Tamsin could speak, with terrible calm. "'Under review,' the notice said, nailed to the landing not two hours past. Effective immediately. I'm not to carry passengers till the review concludes." She laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it anywhere. "A review, girl. Conducted by whom? By Corven's office. Concluding when? When I'm dead, or when you are."

"They struck at you because you're the testimony," Tamsin said. "Because you're the voice."

"They struck at me because I'm the easiest," Brida corrected. "An old woman and a boat. Cheap to break. It's how they start, child — they take the cheapest thing first, to see what you'll do." Her grip tightened on Joss's arm, and her eyes, fierce under the bloodied brow, fixed on Tamsin's. "So the question isn't what they'll do next. The question is what you'll do. Because I'll tell you plain: they will come for this workshop, and they will come for Ada's post, and they will come for every one of us, one cheap thing at a time, until either we stop or they do."

Outside, the light was failing early behind the river fog, and somewhere down at the landing the ice boomed — a long, deep note, like a struck drum, the thaw talking in its sleep. Tamsin looked at her small band of witnesses — the ferrywoman bleeding, the convict with iron dust to his elbows, the schoolmistress white-lipped at the window — and understood that the siege had already begun, and that the enemy had never once needed to show a face.

That night, as she mixed the fresh acid with Ada standing witness beside her, measuring each grain twice, a boy's knock came at the workshop door. On the step, shivering, stood the cooper's son — the same boy whose charcoal deliveries fed their furnace — holding out a folded paper.

"Beg pardon, miss. A gentleman paid me a penny to give you this. Didn't say no name." He was gone before she could unfold it.

Inside, in a careful clerk's hand she did not recognize, was a single line:

*The eleventh plate is already spoken for. Stop engraving. Last kindness you'll get.*

Tamsin read it twice. Then she fed it to the furnace, watched it curl and blaze, and went back to her bench — and picked up her burin, and began, line by patient line, to cut the flood of 1831 into copper.
