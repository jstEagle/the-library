## 26. Pike's Price

The affidavit was already written when Alderman Nathaniel Pike arrived at Corven House. That was the first thing he noticed — the paper lying square on the magistrate's desk, ink dry, waiting only for a name. Not a draft to be discussed. A finished thing. He had been summoned not to compose but to sign.

"You'll take tea," Corven said. It was not a question.

The study smelled of beeswax and pipe smoke and, beneath it, something older — the cold stone smell of a house that had stood through the flood of '31 without its foundations so much as shifting. Pike had always envied that about Corven House. Nothing in it had ever been washed away.

He sat. The tea came in the good china, the blue-rimmed Wedgwood with the hairline crack in the saucer that Corven refused to replace because, he liked to say, a flaw honestly acknowledged is worth ten perfections pretended. Pike had heard him say it at a dozen dinners. He had laughed at it a dozen times. Tonight the phrase sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"Read it through," Corven said, sliding the paper across with two fingers, the way a man passes a bill he has already counted twice.

Pike read it. It was short. That was the craft of it — no protestations, no elaborate denials. Denials invite scrutiny; brevity invites boredom. *I, Nathaniel Pike, alderman of Marlow Bend, being of sound mind and full knowledge, do swear that no row of laborers' dwellings stood upon the river lots south of the mill prior to the year 1832, and that no persons were thereby displaced or lost.* Full knowledge. Sound mind. Every word chosen like a stone for a wall.

"It's accurate as far as it goes," Pike said carefully.

"It's accurate entirely." Corven leaned back. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, silver-haired, with the kind of face that juries trusted and children instinctively obeyed. "That is the whole art of it, Nathaniel. We are not asking you to lie. We are asking you to decline to remember."

*Decline to remember.* As though memory were a dinner invitation one might politely put off until spring.

"The historians arrive with the thaw," Corven went on, pouring his own tea because his housekeeper had learned to leave them alone on business nights. "A Professor Maren out of the capital — dry as August grass, thorough as a maggot in a corpse. She will want documents. She will want testimony. And if she gets testimony from Brida Ferris, from that Quill woman, from God knows which of the scattered remnant, then everything this town built on the river lots becomes clouded title. Every deed. Every inheritance. Yours, Nathaniel. Mine. The churchyard ground itself."

"I understand the stakes."

"I know you do. You've always understood stakes better than any man on the council. It's why I asked for you specifically." Corven smiled, and it was almost warm. "Not Hargrave, who'd sign anything and be worth nothing. Not young Tulliver, who'd sign nothing and be worth less. You. Because your signature means something. Because you were *there*."

The word landed harder than Corven could have known. Or perhaps he knew exactly. Perhaps that was the point of choosing Pike — not his respectability but his proximity. He had been there. He had been nine years old, standing on the high bank with his mother's hand crushing his shoulder, watching the ice dam let go with a sound like the sky tearing, watching the brown water take the laborers' row house by house, roof by roof, while the mill fire lit the whole scene orange from behind like a stage effect in some terrible play.

*Don't look,* his mother had said. And then, quieter, in the voice she used for things that were true: *But if you must look, Nathaniel, then look properly, and never say you didn't see.*

He had spent forty years saying he didn't see.

* * *

Corven talked for another quarter hour — about the town's future, about the railway survey that would come up the valley within five years if the title question stayed clean, about legacy, about how history is not what happened but what is agreed upon by men of substance. Pike heard all of it the way one hears rain on a roof. His eyes kept going back to the blank line at the bottom of the affidavit. It was a good line, ruled straight and true, wide enough for a confident hand. It looked like a road. It looked like a grave.

"Nathaniel." Corven set down his cup. "I'll ask plainly, because you're owed plainness. Is there a difficulty?"

"There's a woman," Pike said. "Ashgrove's engraver. She's been asking questions."

"I know what she's been doing. I've had my eye on her since autumn." Corven waved this off as one waves off weather. "She's a foundling with a burin and a grievance she hasn't fully located yet. Grief makes people dig. But grief tires, Nathaniel. Institutions don't. When the historians dock, they will weigh her word against yours — against the sworn statement of an alderman, a man of property, a founder's grandson. Do you imagine for a moment they'll weigh it any other way?"

No. That was the humiliating truth of it. They would not. Paper recognized paper. A man who owned three warehouses and a pew box at St. Brannoc's outweighed a ferry-woman and a schoolmistress in any ledger the capital kept. Pike had built his life on exactly that arithmetic, and now it rose up before him not as comfort but as accusation.

"And if I decline?" he asked.

Corven's expression did not change, which was itself an answer. "Then I shall regretfully conclude that your health has failed you, and the council will need to relieve you of your committees. The warehouse leases come up for renewal in June. There are parties who would view a vacancy favorably. Your son is at Oxford, I believe. Michaelmas term. Fees are paid quarterly." He said it all gently, the way a surgeon describes an amputation. "I am not threatening you, old friend. I am describing a river. One may swim in it or be carried by it, but one does not argue with the current."

*One does not argue with the current.* His mother had argued with a current once. Not the flood — after the flood there had been no arguing left in her, only a silence that lasted the remaining nineteen years of her life. No. Earlier. Before. Pike remembered it suddenly, the way a smell will unlock a room long sealed: his mother at the parish register, years before the flood, arguing with the curate over the recording of a stillbirth among the mill families. *Every soul gets a line,* she had insisted, finger stabbing the page. *If the book won't hold them, the book is broken, not the child.* The curate had given in. One line, one small name, ink faded now past reading, but present. His mother had believed the book could be made to hold.

And Hollis — God rot him, God rest him — Hollis had believed it too, in the end. Enough to razor a page and seal a letter and leave an unfinished atlas like a loaded weapon on a table, trusting someone would pick it up.

* * *

Pike walked home the long way, along the river path, though the wind came off the ice sharp enough to peel skin. He told himself it was to clear his head. He knew it was because the river path passed the south lots.

In the dark the flood meadow was only a pale suggestion under starlight, flat and innocent, fenced now into neat paddocks. The ice groaned somewhere out mid-channel, a long descending note like a ship's cable taking strain. The thaw was close. Everyone in Marlow Bend could feel it in their teeth.

His grandfather had helped lay out those paddocks. Had drawn the first fair lines across ground that, eighteen months earlier, had held fourteen houses and a well and a washing green and a row of gardens where the mill families grew beans and kept bees. Fourteen houses. Eleven surviving children. Those numbers lived in him side by side like two animals in a cage that had long ago stopped fighting, having gnawed each other down to something that fit.

He had signed the original affidavit in 1833, at nineteen, his father's hand guiding his elbow. *For their own good,* his father had said of the dispersal, and Pike had believed it because believing was easier than the alternative, and because the eleven children had been scattered beyond his sight within a fortnight, and what is out of sight is very nearly out of existence. He had built a life on that near. Warehouses. A seat on the council. A wife who believed him solid, sons who believed him good, a granddaughter who thought his word was the same thing as truth.

Now the eleventh child had come back wearing an engraver's apron and carrying a dead man's atlas, and the arithmetic was failing.

At his own gate he stopped. The house windows glowed yellow against the dark — his wife reading, his granddaughter's doll collection arranged on the parlor sill where she left it every night, a small porcelain congregation. Everything he had was downstream of that signature. Everything he loved had been purchased with the coin of a sentence he had never once said aloud: *it wasn't like that.*

Except that it had been exactly like that, and he had seen it, and his mother had told him to look properly.

*Never say you didn't see.*

He went inside. He did not sleep. At two in the morning he sat at his desk with the affidavit copied out fair in his own hand — Corven had insisted he take a copy to review overnight, a courtesy, a kindness, a leash — and he read it again by candlelight. *Being of sound mind and full knowledge.* His pen hovered over the signature space on his copy, practicing, he told himself. Testing the weight of the letters. N-a-t-h-a-n-i-e-l. The nib touched the paper and produced a single blot, round and black, like a period at the end of a sentence he hadn't written yet.

His mother's voice, faint as the ice-groan on the river: *If the book won't hold them, the book is broken.*

There was a knock at the door of the study. His wife, in shawl and nightcap, gray braid over one shoulder, looking at him with forty years of knowing him in her face.

"You've been sitting here since midnight," she said.

"I have council business."

"On a Sunday night, with a candle burning down and your coat still on?" She came closer. She did not touch the paper; she had never once in their marriage read his papers uninvited, and he had always taken her discretion for ignorance. Now he saw it had been patience. "Nathaniel. Whatever it is — and I can see that it is something, I've watched it walking around inside you since the Ashgrove girl started asking her questions — whatever it is, you cannot sign your way out of it. Men like you never can. You only ever sign your way deeper in."

When she had gone he sat until the candle drowned in its own wax. Then he took his hat and coat again, quietly, and let himself out the back, and stood a moment in the frozen garden breathing white breath, looking toward the orchard lane where the old cottage stood dark under its bare apple trees.

He did not yet know what he meant to do there. He knew only that the blank line was waiting for him in two places — on Corven's desk, and at the bottom of himself — and that he could not fill either of them tomorrow. Perhaps there was a third place the line could go. Perhaps someone who dealt in maps and plates and the exact engraving of what stood where would know what a man ought to write in a space shaped like that.

The ice boomed again, nearer now, splitting somewhere upstream. The thaw was coming whether the town willed it or no. Nathaniel Pike pulled his collar up and walked, alone and unhurried, toward the orchard cottage — carrying the unsigned paper folded in his breast pocket like a coal he could neither hold nor drop.
