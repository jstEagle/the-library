## 28. First Boat Sighted

The sound woke the whole town before it woke the sun.

Elias Vane had been awake anyway — he was always awake before dawn now, the way a man is awake who keeps a ledger in his head that never quite balances — and so he heard the river crack open at half past four on the morning of the twelfth of March, a sound like the sky splitting along a seam no one had known was there. He stood at his window over the census office with his shirt half-buttoned and watched the ice on the Marlow go white to gray to nothing, the whole sheet groaning, heaving, shrugging off winter two full weeks early.

*Early,* he thought, and the word tasted like iron.

By six the news had run through Marlow Bend faster than the meltwater: the ice was out, or nearly out; the channel would be clear within a day; and riding down that channel, from the capital, would come a steamer carrying Professor Ilsa Maren and whatever company of historians Magistrate Corven had hired to make the past lie down and stay where he'd put it.

Elias dressed quickly and went out into a morning that smelled of mud and thawing wood and something older underneath — the river's own smell, freed after months under its lid. Ice floes the size of barn doors rode the current past the ferry landing, grinding against the pilings with a low continuous complaint. Brida Ferris's ferry sat tied up and idle; nobody was crossing anything today, and the old woman herself stood at the rail watching the wreckage of winter go by, her breath steaming.

"You heard," she said, without turning. It wasn't a question. Nothing Brida said ever was.

"I heard."

"Two days," she said. "Maybe three if the wind turns. The *Corvina* makes good time downstream." She spat over the rail, an old habit aimed at nothing. "He named his boat after himself, you know. That tells you most of what a man is."

Elias came to stand beside her. Below them the water ran brown and muscular, carrying branches, fence rails, one entire henhouse roof. Somewhere upstream, whole fields were being unmade and delivered to the sea.

"She's not finished," he said. "The plate. I saw it last night — she'd cut maybe half the names. Half the row. She's engraving like a woman writing a will."

Brida turned then and looked at him, and her eyes were pale as the old ice going out. "Then we'd best see she doesn't stop for sleep, or food, or bleeding. Can you keep time, census man? You're good at keeping things. Keep this: every hour between now and when that boat docks belongs to Tamsin Ashgrove, and woe to any soul who spends one minute of it on anything else."

---

The workshop on Tanner's Lane had become, in the space of ten days, the strangest establishment in Marlow Bend. Elias had catalogued stranger things in his career — a household of nine living in a root cellar, a widow listed as her own deceased husband, a parish register with more ink-blots than baptisms — but he had never seen anything like what happened inside those walls after dark.

Ada Quill kept the first watch, sitting by the door with her schoolmistress's shawl around her shoulders and a poker across her knees, reading by candlelight and looking, if anyone peered in, exactly like what she appeared to be: a spinster finishing her mending. At midnight Joss Renner took over, moving through the yard with the silence of a man who had learned in a penal colony which footsteps carried. Brida relieved him at four. And all night long the furnace roared, fed by anyone with arms, because the annealing plate could not be allowed to cool — not now, not with half a map still waiting inside the copper.

Elias arrived at seven with bread, cheese, a jar of pickled onions Ada had demanded, and the particular authority of a man carrying breakfast. He let himself in past the smell of acid and hot metal and found Tamsin at the workbench, bent over the supplementary plate with her burin held the way a surgeon holds a lancet.

She did not look up.

"Tamsin."

"I saw you come in." Her voice was flat, scraped thin, the voice of someone who has been talking to metal for too many hours. "Put it by the furnace. I'll eat when the flood line's done."

"The flood line can wait five minutes."

"It cannot." Now she looked up, and Elias felt something turn over in his chest. Her eyes were rimmed red and sunk deep, and there was a smear of blood along her jaw where she'd pushed back her hair with a hand that had been holding something sharp. Her right thumb was wrapped in a rag that had been white once. "Everything about this plate can't wait. That's rather the point of it."

He set the food down anyway and pulled the second stool to the bench, close enough that their elbows almost touched. On the bench lay the plate itself, and he made himself look at it properly — the laborers' row rising in fine parallel strokes along the riverbank where the official atlas showed empty pasture; the flood line drawn as a broken thread reaching halfway up the hill toward the church; and beneath it, in letters small enough to require a lens but cut deep enough to survive two hundred years, eleven names.

*Harrow. Ferris. Renner. Quill.* Names he knew now, faces he'd begun to match them to. *Pike.* A name that had signed the other side. And further along, half-emerged from the copper like figures rising out of a fog —

*Tamsin Ashgrove.*

No. Not Ashgrove. The name on the plate was older than that, a name she hadn't spoken aloud yet, a name she was cutting into metal letter by letter as though carving it into her own history with a graver's blade.

"How is the hand?" he asked.

"Bleeding." She flexed the wrapped thumb without looking at it. "It does that. Hollis used to say an engraver's blood belongs in the plate — it seasons the lines." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "I thought he was being poetic. He was being practical. You can't hold a burin eight hours a day and keep your skin."

"You've been at it since when?"

"Since Brida went home." Which meant since four o'clock. Three hours. His eye went to the corner of the bench where three discarded rags lay darkened stiff.

"Tamsin—"

"Elias." She set down the burin and finally turned to face him fully, and he saw that beneath the exhaustion there was something burning, steady and terrible, a fire banked so tight it gave no light but all the heat in the world. "Two days. Perhaps less. In two days a boat comes carrying people whose entire profession is deciding what the past was. Corven has had thirty years, his father before him, a town's worth of paper, and eleven frightened children scattered to the winds — and it *held*. The erasure held. Do you understand what that means? It means erasure works. It works unless someone stands in front of it with the truth in their hands and refuses to move." She touched the plate, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping child. "This plate is me refusing to move. So the hand bleeds. Let it bleed."

There was nothing to say to that, so Elias didn't try. He unwrapped the cheese, cut a slice, and put it in her free hand.

"Eat while you argue with me," he said. "That's a compromise even the flood line can live with."

---

* * *

Midmorning brought word that changed the arithmetic.

It came by boy — one of the dockside children who ran errands for pennies and missed nothing — pounding up Tanner's Lane with his cap in his fist. "Steamer sighted off Halloway Point! Coming down fast, they say, riding the freshet!" He gulped air. "Mr. Vane — they're saying at the landing she'll make Marlow by tomorrow evening. Tomorrow!"

The workshop went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when a floorboard cracks.

"Tomorrow evening," Brida repeated, from her post by the door. "Not two days. One."

"That's impossible," Ada said. "The ice only went out this morning—"

"The freshet carries her," Joss said. He'd come in from the yard, snow-melt dripping off his coat, and there was a hard calm in his voice that Elias was learning to read as the convict's substitute for alarm. "High water runs quick. If they've a pilot who knows the channel, and Corven's paying, he knows it." He looked at Tamsin. "Half a plate in one day and a night. Can it be done?"

Tamsin was already standing, already pulling the magnifier down from its hook, already turning back to the bench. "It will be done."

"Tamsin—" Ada began.

"It *will be done*, Ada." Not shouted. Worse than shouted — spoken with the level clarity of a woman stating the weather. "Corven altered two plates of Master Hollis's atlas while the man was dying, and the world called it history. Tomorrow a professor steps onto that dock, and either she sees the row on copper or she sees nothing but affidavits. There is no version of tomorrow where I have stopped to rest." She seated the burin against the plate. "Brida, keep the door. Joss, the furnace wants coal — there's a delivery coming from the chandler at noon, pay him from the tin. Ada—"

"I know," Ada said. "Letters. If Maren won't take testimony on the dock, she'll want corroboration in writing. I'm copying the slate fair while I watch."

"Then everyone knows their work better than I know mine," Tamsin said, and bent to the copper, and did not look up again.

Elias lingered a moment longer than the others. Watching her work was like watching weather — something vast operating according to laws beyond argument. The burin moved in strokes no wider than a hair, and where it passed, truth accumulated in copper the way silt accumulates in a flood plain: grain by grain, irresistible, patient even in panic.

He left the rest of the bread within reach of her elbow and went out into the thaw.

---

* * *

His own work, that afternoon, was the work Elias Vane understood best: paper.

If Maren wanted primary evidence — and everything Brida's network whispered about the woman suggested she would accept nothing else — then the evidence needed organizing, indexing, protecting. He spent three hours at the census office compiling, from memory and from files, the discrepancies he'd been chasing quietly for two years: the 1831 census that counted forty-one souls in the laborers' row and the 1841 census that counted none; the tax roll of '32 with its careful blank where the row's assessments should have been; the militia list, the school rolls, the burial register with its razored page.

Page by page he laid them side by side and watched, again, the machinery of the erasure assemble itself in his hands — not one great lie but a hundred small ones, each plausible alone, each requiring only that no one ever ask the next question.

*I asked the next question,* he thought. Standing in the orchard doorway with his pen hovering over a household that officially did not exist, asking Tamsin Ashgrove who owned the cottage before the Ashgroves — that question had cost him his comfortable ignorance and given him this: a table covered in contradictory paper, a stolen copybook missing from her rooms, and a conviction he could no more put down than she could put down the burin.

The copybook. He turned that over again as he worked, the way he turned over every unsolved entry. Stolen from her rooms four days ago, while she worked here. No sign of forced entry — taken by someone she'd have admitted, or someone with skill enough not to need admitting. Merrit Corven had been seen twice near Tanner's Lane that week, once buying candles, once doing nothing at all beside the bridge with the patience of a woman waiting for something.

And yet. Elias had spent twenty years reading households, and he had learned that the obvious suspect and the true one are related the way a map is related to a territory — sometimes accurate, often convenient, never to be trusted without walking the ground.

He sealed his compilation in oilcloth, addressed it to himself, and tucked it inside his coat. Whatever happened tomorrow, the paper would survive. Paper was stubborn that way. It was the one lesson of his profession he still believed in.

---

* * *

Dusk came down blue and cold over the melting river, and Elias walked back to Tanner's Lane through a town holding its breath. He passed the Corven house on the square, windows blazing, carriages at the door — the founding families gathering, preparing their welcome for the historians, rehearsing their town-without-tragedy. Through the dining room window he glimpsed Magistrate Corven standing at the head of his table with a glass raised, and the sight of it made Elias's teeth ache: a man toasting the successful murder of thirty years' worth of neighbors, and calling the toast hospitality.

At the workshop, the lights burned as they'd burned all day.

Inside, the scene had shifted and tightened. Ada had fallen asleep in her chair over her fair copies, pencil still in her hand, and Brida had draped a blanket over her without waking her. Joss was at the furnace, feeding it, his scarred forearms orange in the firelight. And Tamsin —

Tamsin was swaying at the bench.

She was still working — the burin still moved, stroke and pause, stroke and pause, metronomic, mechanical — but her body rocked faintly with each stroke, the sway of a rider asleep in the saddle, and the rag on her thumb had gone from darkened to dripping. A thread of blood ran from beneath the wrapping down her wrist and dripped, softly, regularly, onto the floorboards. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* She didn't appear to hear it.

Elias crossed the room and caught her wrist.

The burin stopped. For a moment she simply breathed, staring at the plate, and then her eyes focused on his hand around her wrist as though trying to identify the object.

"Let go," she said.

"No."

"Tamsin." Brida was there suddenly, old and immovable. "Look at your hand, girl."

Tamsin looked. Something in her face flickered — surprise, almost, as though the blood belonged to a stranger. Then she laughed, a short cracked sound with nothing in it of humor. "Well," she said. "That's inconvenient."

"You're done for tonight," Brida said.

"I'm done when the plate is done."

"You're done when you drop, and then you're done for a week, and the plate dies with you." Brida took her other arm, and between the two of them they walked her, unprotesting now, resistance draining out of her as fast as it had flared, to the cot in the corner that had appeared sometime in the last week — Elias didn't ask whose idea it had been; the workshop had become the kind of place where cots simply materialized. She sat. She lay back. She was asleep between one breath and the next, mid-sentence almost, her bandaged hand curled against her chest like a bird with a broken wing.

They stood over her a moment. The furnace ticked. Outside, the river ran loud and free, hauling winter away to the sea.

"One day," Joss said quietly. "Maybe a day and a morning. Half the names left, the flood line, the border lettering. Can she cut that much in that little?"

"She'll have to," Brida said. Then she looked at Elias, and her pale eyes had a glint in them that he had learned to fear slightly, the glint of a woman about to assign work. "Unless," she said, "she isn't the only pair of hands in this room."

"I can't engrave."

"You can't engrave," Brida agreed. "But you can grind ink. You can wipe plates. You can mix wax, fetch copper, hold a straightedge steady while a shaking hand cuts against it. My father was a shipwright — I've watched men build a hull in a week when the storm was coming, and not one of them built it alone." She nodded at the sleeping woman. "Tomorrow she works until her hands give out, and when they give out, someone else's take over for the parts that don't need an artist. And the parts that do—" her jaw set, "—the parts that do, she finishes if she has to bite the burin."

Elias looked at the plate on the bench. Eleven names, five and a half of them cut. The flood line waiting. The border waiting. Two days of work compressed into eighteen hours, and behind it all the sound of a steamer he couldn't hear yet but could feel coming up the river like a change in the weather.

"All right," he said, and began rolling up his sleeves.

---

* * *

He worked through the evening by lamplight while Tamsin slept and the others rotated watches, and it was near midnight, wiping wax across the cold half of the plate, that he heard her stir.

She stood at his shoulder before he knew she was awake, hair loose, eyes clearer than they'd been — the dead sleep had bought her something back. She looked at the plate for a long time. At the half-finished names. At her own name among them, still waiting for its final letters.

"If I don't finish," she said quietly, "will you finish it? After. However it goes tomorrow — if they seize the shop, if they take the plates — will someone finish it?"

Elias set down the cloth. "Yes," he said.

"Not *someone*." She was looking at him directly now, and there was no fever left in her gaze at all, only that banked fire and, beneath it, something rawer — trust, offered the way she offered very few things, whole and without retreat. "*You.* I've watched your hands all evening. They're careful. Careful is the whole art, everything else is practice." A breath. "Will you learn the burin by morning?"

He understood what she was asking, and what it cost her — the craftsman's oldest terror, handing the blade to another. He understood also that it wasn't really about him.

"Yes," he said again. "Teach me now, while your hands can still show me."

So at midnight, in a workshop that smelled of acid and blood and coal smoke, with a steamer crawling down a flooded river toward a town built on a lie, the census taker of Marlow Bend took up an engraver's burin for the first time in his life, and Tamsin Ashgrove closed her bleeding hand over his, and together they began to cut the eleventh name into copper.

Outside, far off, so faint it might have been the ice settling, came the first low cry of a steam whistle on the water.
