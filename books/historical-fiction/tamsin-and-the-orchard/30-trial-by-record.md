## 30. Trial by Record

### Brida

The courthouse had never in Brida Ferris's sixty years smelled of anything but lamp oil and cold stone and the particular dust of men who believed themselves permanent. Today it smelled of too many bodies packed into a room built for half as many — wool and wet boots and the sour breath of people who had come to watch a fight they were afraid to join.

She sat where the bailiff pointed her, hands folded over her stick, and let the room look at her. Let them. Forty years she'd poled them across the river — brides and corpses, magistrates and drunks, every soul in Marlow Bend sooner or later — and not one of them had ever truly seen her. She had been furniture with opinions about the weather. Now they stared as if she'd grown a second head, because a notice had gone up on the church door naming her a *person of testimony*, and persons of testimony did not pole ferries. Persons of testimony wore their good shawls and sat in courthouses and said things that could not be taken back.

Magistrate Corven presided from the high bench like a man warming himself at his own fire. He was handsome still, damn him. Silver at the temples now instead of gold, but the same easy jaw, the same voice like warmed butter that could coat any lie going down. He had been twenty-six the winter of '31. He had stood on the levee with his coat buttoned wrong and told the crowd there was nothing more to be done for the row.

Brida remembered his buttons. That was the thing about memory nobody warned you of. It kept the useless details sharp as razors while the faces of your dead went soft.

"Mrs. Ferris." Corven's clerk read the charge of the inquiry from a paper that had been drafted before anyone was called to speak it. "You have sworn an affidavit in the year 1834 stating, quote, *that no dwellings of laborers stood upon the river lots east of the mill race, nor within living memory.* Do you stand by this affidavit?"

"I do," Brida said.

A rustle went through the benches. Corven permitted himself the smallest nod, the nod of a man whose machinery is running true.

"And will you now affirm," the clerk continued, "that no such dwellings existed?"

"No," said Brida.

The rustle became a roar and then a silence, which was worse.

"You'll explain yourself, madam," Corven said, mild as milk.

"Gladly." She turned on the bench so she faced the whole room, not just the bench, because this part wasn't for him. "I signed what Mr. Corven put in front of me in 1834 because my ferry license was in his fist and my sister's widow pension was in his fist, and I was thirty-eight years old with two children to feed and he had already shown us all what happened to people who said otherwise. You want to know if I stand by it? I stand by it the way you stand by a bruise. It happened to me. I didn't choose it."

"Mrs. Ferris—"

"Eleven children came off that row alive." Her voice cracked down the middle like river ice and she let it, let them hear it. "I carried three of them across the water myself the night the dam broke, and the mill burned behind us, and I can tell you the color of every coat they wore because I wrapped them in my own blankets. Ada Quill. Joss Renner. A boy called Tam who didn't live out the spring. And seven more. Eleven names exist whether you've got paper for them or not, and paper—" she looked straight up at Corven then, and saw for the first time how tired his eyes were behind the authority, and hated him anyway— "paper is only skin, magistrate. What's underneath doesn't stop breathing just because you've written it away."

Corven let the silence sit exactly long enough to seem patient. "The affidavit of 1834," he said gently, "was signed freely."

"It was signed hungry."

"The inquiry thanks you." He turned to his clerk. "Enter Mrs. Ferris's testimony as *confused*."

Confused. Brida sat back. Around her she heard the town murmur — some in shame, some in satisfaction, most in the terrible neutral hum of people deciding which side of history to be counted on. She found Tamsin Ashgrove in the third row, pale as paper herself, watching everything with those engraver's eyes that missed nothing, and gave her one small nod.

*It's begun,* the nod said. *Now we see who they are.*

### Ada

Ada Quill had taught school for nineteen years and had never once lost control of a room. She had faced down farm boys bigger than she was, fathers drunk on corn liquor come to argue a thrashing, one memorable flood that put three feet of water through her schoolhouse and left her teaching arithmetic on rooftops. But her hands shook now, and she despised them for it.

They had seated the witnesses apart from each other — she noticed that, noted it the way she'd note a child cheating at sums — Brida by the wall, the convict Renner manacled at the end of a bench under two constables' eyes, herself in the middle of the room where everyone could look at her. Isolate the variables. Corven had been a clever boy; she'd taught him long division. He hadn't changed his methods, only enlarged the classroom.

Miss Quill, the clerk called. State your occupation.

"Schoolmistress. Nineteen years."

"And you keep the parish records of attendance for the eastern district."

"I keep my own records," Ada said. "The parish keeps theirs."

A small smile touched Corven's mouth, and Ada understood suddenly that he wanted this. He had summoned the inquiry not despite the risk of witnesses but because of it. Every witness dragged in here to be discredited made the erasure stronger. Confusion entered into record. Contradiction entered into record. By the time Professor Maren and her historians stepped off the steamer, there would be a tidy official transcript proving the whole matter was the invention of a superstitious ferrywoman, a disgraced schoolmistress, and a felon. He was building his affidavit out of their very mouths.

So don't give him contradictions, Ada thought. Give him arithmetic.

"You are alleged," the clerk read, "to have maintained a slate bearing names of persons drowned in the flood of 1831. Persons who, per the record, cannot be identified."

"They can be identified," Ada said. "I identified them. I wrote their names down the week after the flood, when the grief was fresh enough that every neighbor in the district could spell them for me. Marta Renner. Josiah Pike the elder. Widow Crane. Silas and Fenna Oduya. Six more. Fourteen adults died in the laborers' row the night of February the ninth, 1831, and eleven children survived them, and I know this because I taught six of those eleven children to read, and a teacher does not forget her roll."

"On what document do these names appear?"

Ada reached into her bag and brought out the slate.

She had expected it to be confiscated the moment she touched it. The constables stirred; Corven lifted two fingers and they stilled. He wanted it entered. Of course he did. A slate was not a record. A slate was chalk and gossip.

"May I?" she asked the room at large, and without waiting rose, crossed to the great ledger stand beneath the bench where the county's founding deed lay open under glass, and set the little schoolroom slate beside it. Thirty years of chalk-ghosts under the wax of her careful preserving. Fourteen names in a child's copperplate hand.

"For the record," Corven said pleasantly, "the inquiry observes that a slate bears no date, no signature, no seal, and may have been prepared at any time by any party."

"The deed under your glass, Magistrate, bears a seal," Ada said, turning back to face the benches, "and it says the east lots are pasture, and it is a lie, and both documents are sitting side by side right now so that every soul in this room can decide for themselves which one was made by a person who loved somebody and which was made by a person who wanted something. That's the whole of historiography. I teach it to ten-year-olds."

Someone laughed — one short bark from the back, quickly smothered, but a laugh. Ada saw Corven register it. Saw something flicker behind his eyes that was not quite anger. Doubt, perhaps, or its expensive cousin, calculation.

"Enter the slate as *an unverified object*," he said.

"Unverified," Ada repeated, returning to her seat. "Like the moon, before instruments. Like America, before Columbus got the credit. Everything true starts unverified, Magistrate. Verification is merely the truth being patient."

This time the laugh was longer, and it was not entirely smothered, and it came from more than one throat.

### Joss

Joss Renner had decided, somewhere on the transport ship out of Van Diemen's Land somewhere past the Cape, that if he ever got back to Marlow Bend he would burn it down. Not the buildings. The idea of it. He would burn the idea of the town out of his chest or die trying, and either outcome suited him.

Instead here he was, shackled in a courtroom, being asked to help.

"—convicted in 1838 of theft and assault," the clerk was reading, "transported seven years, returned last autumn. Mr. Renner, you were a child resident of the east lots in 1831, according to certain testimony given today. Do you confirm this?"

Joss looked at the clerk. He looked at Corven, who watched him with the frank appraisal of a horse trader. He looked at the constables flanking him, at the townsfolk packed in like herring, at old Brida with her chin up, at the schoolmistress with her slate, at Tamsin Ashgrove sitting rigid in the third row with ink stains still on her fingers from whatever she'd been doing all night.

"Nah," Joss said.

Corven raised an eyebrow, inviting the room to share his amusement. "You deny it?"

"I deny answering questions in a room where you're the one holding the pen." Joss shifted so his chains clanked, loud, deliberate. "Here's what I know, Magistrate. In '31 I was six. My mum sang me to sleep in a house with a green door on the east lots, and I woke up on the ice with a stranger's blanket round me, and the house was gone and the street was gone and everyone who could say my name was dead or scattered, and then a bunch of gentlemen in good coats told the world none of it ever happened. That's not a question. That's just what happened. You want confirmation, ask your own father. Oh—" he smiled, showing teeth gone grey from lime-water— "I forget. Your family's the ones holding the pen."

"Mr. Renner has a documented history of hostility—"

"I got a documented history of *existing*, mate, which is apparently the same crime in this town." He turned, slow, letting the chain drag, and addressed the benches directly. "Every one of you knew. Don't sit there looking holy. You bought your eggs from the row. Your kids swam off the east lots. You watched them carry fourteen boxes out of the mud and you watched the lots get fenced and you said nothing for sixteen years, and now a girl wants to write down what happened and you're all in here to watch whether it's safe to admit you remember. Well." He leaned forward as far as the irons allowed. "Is it? Safe? That's the actual question of this whole circus, and no affidavit answers it. Only you do. Each of you. Tonight."

The silence that followed had weight. Joss sat back, satisfied and sick in equal measure, because he'd spent seven years learning that truth-telling felt magnificent for exactly as long as it took the guards to come for you.

Corven recovered beautifully. He always would. "The inquiry thanks Mr. Renner," he said, "and notes his testimony as *inadmissible by reason of character*."

"There it is," Joss told the room. "There's the whole trick. Write down what I am, and you never have to hear what I say."

### Merrit

Merrit Corven sat in the front row, where the magistrate's family always sat, and kept her spine straight and her gloved hands folded, and inside her chest something was coming apart with a sound only she could hear.

Her father was winning. That was the horror of it. She had watched him work a room since she was tall enough to see over the bench, and he was working it now — the gentle corrections, the patient classifications, *confused*, *unverified*, *inadmissible*, each witness handed back to the town pre-broken, labeled like jars on a shelf. By supper the talk in every taproom would be not *did it happen* but *what's wrong with those poor people*. He was not denying the row. He was performing its impossibility, and the performance was flawless.

And she had helped build the stage for it. She had stolen the copybook. She had meant — God, what had she meant? To protect him. To protect herself. To make sure that the ninth child stayed a story with no ending, because an ending meant choosing, and choosing meant losing either the father who raised her or the mother whose face she could not remember.

"Tamsin Ashgrove," the clerk called.

Merrit watched her rise. The engraver walked to the stand like a woman walking a plank and knowing the water below, and Merrit thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *she's going to tell them. She's going to stand in front of my father and say it.*

And Merrit discovered that she could not bear it — not the exposure, not the ruin, not even the truth itself, but the thought of hearing the eleven names read aloud by someone else, in someone else's voice, in a room where her father controlled the meaning of every word. They were hers. The names. She had carried them in her pocket for a month like stones, like sins, like the only inheritance she had. If they were to be spoken in this room, torn out of secrecy and made into evidence—

They would be spoken by her. Or not at all.

"—state your name and occupation," the clerk was saying.

Tamsin opened her mouth.

And Merrit Corven, daughter of the magistrate, ward-raised ninth child of the drowned laborers' row, stood up in the front row of the Marlow Bend courthouse and said, in a voice that cracked the room open like river ice:

"Papa. Stop."

* * *

Every head turned. Corven's gavel was halfway raised and hung there, suspended, as his daughter stepped out of the family pew and into the aisle with her chin high and her face white as fresh paper — and her hand, Tamsin saw, pressed flat against her coat, against the hard rectangular shape hidden inside it, the shape of a child's copybook.

"Merrit," Corven said quietly. A warning, a plea, a door held open. "Sit down."

The whole town leaned in. Outside, far off, faint under everything, the river groaned against the ice — a long shifting sound, like a giant rolling over in its sleep, like a countdown beginning.

"No," Merrit said.
