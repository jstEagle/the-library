## 31. Merrit Breaks

The inquiry room smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and Merrit Corven had never hated a building more.

They had dressed it up for the occasion — the magistrate's court on Founders' Row did not often host hearings in February — with the town's good chairs borrowed from the assembly hall and a long table draped in green baize so the scarred oak beneath wouldn't shame anyone. Bunting over the windows. A pitcher of water and six glasses arranged with geometric precision. Her father had overseen every detail himself, walking the room twice with his hands clasped behind his back like a general inspecting ground he intended to hold.

*We are not hiding anything, Merrit,* he'd said that morning, knotting his cravat before the glass while she held his coat. *That is precisely the point. We have nothing to hide, and we will prove it in open session, before witnesses, before the historians even set foot on our soil. Let them arrive to find the matter already settled by due process.* He had smiled at her in the mirror, that warm, certain smile that had governed her whole life the way a river governs a mill. *Truth loves an open room.*

She had believed him once. She had believed him for twenty years, because believing was easier than the alternative, and because a girl raised as a Corven ward learns early that gratitude is a full-time occupation. But she had spent last night with a child's copybook open under her blanket and a candle burned to a stub, reading eleven names in a laborer's careful hand until the letters swam, and this morning when her father asked if she was well she had said yes, and it was the second great lie of her life, and it had come out smooth as river ice.

The room filled. Magistrate Corven took the head of the table flanked by Alderman Pike — gray-faced, hands folded, looking like a man attending his own autopsy — and two of the founders' sons who would sign whatever was put in front of them. Elias Vane sat apart with his ledger and his terrible patient pen. The townspeople crowded the back wall: millworkers, ferry folk, the fishwife, the cooper, faces Merrit had passed all her life without seeing, now watching her family's table the way watchers watch a scaffold.

And at the far end, alone, Tamsin Ashgrove.

Merrit made herself look at her. That was the discipline she had set for herself since the night in the workshop stairwell, since *I know what you are because I am it too* — she looked, and she did not look away. Tamsin sat very straight, her engraver's hands flat on the table before her, ink stains in the creases like old bruises. There was a burn bandaged on her left thumb. She did not look frightened. She looked like someone waiting out weather.

Her father rose to open the proceedings, and his voice filled the room the way it always did, warm and resonant, a voice built for courtrooms and christenings.

"Neighbors. Friends. It grieves me that we are gathered here at all." A pause, measured to the half-second. "It has been alleged — whispered, rather, for no one has dared say it plainly to my face — that this town conceals some ancient wrong. That our records lie. That our founding families, my own family among them, conspired to erase a tragedy from the history of Marlow Bend." He let his gaze travel the room, sorrowful, reasonable, wounded. "I will not insult you by pretending such rumors do no harm. Rumors are rust; they eat quietly. So today we shall scrape the metal clean. I have called this inquiry myself. I invite testimony. I welcome scrutiny." He spread his hands. "Because we have nothing to hide."

Merrit watched the word go through the room like a coin passed hand to hand. *Nothing to hide.* The fishwife's mouth tightened. Old Brida Ferris, seated near the stove with her stick across her knees, did not react at all, which was its own kind of eloquence.

The testimonies began. They were rehearsed; Merrit could hear the seams. A founder's son attested that the land north of the mill race had been pasture "as long as any man living could recall." A deed was read aloud, dated 1833, clean as a hymn. Pike was called, and Merrit's stomach clenched as he rose — but Pike recited the affidavit in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant, and signed his name again on a fresh copy produced for the record, and the pen scratched, and the scratch was the loudest sound in the room.

Then her father turned, gracious as a host, toward the end of the table.

"And of course, Miss Ashgrove. Master Hollis's own apprentice. No one knows the atlas better." His smile was kind. Kindness was the sharpest tool he owned. "Tell us, Miss Ashgrove — you have studied the plates, the registers, the deeds these many months, we're told. Have you found in them any evidence of this — phantom row? These eleven children the rumor-mongers speak of?"

The room went still. This was the hinge. Merrit saw it and knew her father saw it too: everything had been arranged to produce this moment, the moment where the cartographer herself, the master's own hand, would stand before the town and deny it. And then whatever Tamsin said afterward, however loud, would be the ravings of a disgraced woman contradicting her own testimony.

Tamsin stood. She did not speak at once. She looked down at her ink-stained hands, and when she raised her head there was something in her face that Merrit recognized from the mirror on bad nights — the expression of a person standing at the edge of a frozen river, weighing the ice.

"I came here prepared to answer your question, Magistrate," Tamsin said. "I've asked it of myself enough times. Whether paper can be trusted. Whether a map is a record or a verdict." Her voice was steady, workshop-steady, the voice of someone who had spent years making marks that had to last centuries. "But you've asked me to corroborate your documents, and I find I can't. Because I've spent this winter learning what your documents cost."

"Merrit," her father said quietly, without turning his head, "the copybook, if you please. From the evidence box."

Merrit's blood went cold. The evidence box. Of course. He had planned this too — he had known about the copybook, or guessed at it, had perhaps known for weeks that it existed and where it must be kept, and he meant to produce it himself, on his own terms, as a fabrication, a forgery planted by Tamsin and her confederates, discredited before it was ever read. *Let them bring their little book,* he'd say. *We'll show it for what it is.*

He nodded to her. The room waited. The bailiff stood ready by the evidence box with its wax-sealed lid.

Merrit stood up.

Every eye came to her — her father's first, confident, expecting obedience the way the river expects to run downhill — and she felt the strangest sensation, as though she were standing slightly outside her own body, watching a woman rise from a chair in a green-baize room, and trying to warn her.

"The copybook isn't in the box," Merrit said.

A flicker of irritation crossed her father's face, smoothed instantly into public patience. "Then fetch it, daughter. From your rooms—"

"No." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. She cleared her throat. "Father. I have it."

The silence that followed had texture. She heard the stove tick. She heard the river, far off under the ice, doing whatever rivers do in February — groaning, shifting, remembering it was water.

She walked to the table. She took from her satchel the copybook — the thing she had stolen three weeks ago from Tamsin's rooms, the thing she had told herself she stole to protect her father, and then, after the stairwell, after *I am it too*, had simply been unable to burn. Its cover was soft with handling, gray cardboard gone furry at the corners, a schoolchild's book bought for a penny and kept for twenty years by a man who bled for maps.

"It isn't a forgery," Merrit said, and was surprised at how level her voice had become. "I know handwriting. My father taught me to verify signatures when I was twelve. This is Josiah Renner's writing — the laborer — the same hand appears on the mill pay-rolls of 1829, which I checked. Last night. At midnight." She laid the book on the baize, in front of her father, and pushed it an inch toward him, and watched him not touch it. "There are eleven names in it. Written by a father for his children, so they wouldn't forget each other. Eight are struck through. Do you understand what the strikes mean, Father? Each one means the person who wrote it died, and someone loved him enough to mark it."

"Merrit." Her father's voice dropped into the register he used for calming juries. "Whatever game Miss Ashgrove has drawn you into—"

"She drew me into nothing." And there it was, the truth, arriving late and sideways as truth always did. "You did. Twenty-six years ago. You stood in the ashes of the laborers' row and you chose which children went where, and I was the ninth, and you gave me your name, and you never once — not once in twenty-six years — told me why."

The room made a sound. Not words — just air moving through forty pairs of lungs at once.

Her father had gone very pale. "Merrit. Sit down."

"No." She picked up the copybook again, opened it to the page with the water stain shaped like a bird, and turned to face the room. "No, I don't think I will. You said it yourself this morning. Truth loves an open room." She found Tamsin's eyes down the length of the table. Tamsin had gone white, one hand pressed flat against the table edge as if the floor were tilting. Good, Merrit thought, fiercely. Be dizzy. We'll be dizzy together.

"My name was not always Corven," Merrit said, and the words came easier now, sliding out of her like a splinter that had been working toward the surface for years. "My name — the name written in this book, in my father's hand, beside eight struck names and two living ones — was Merrit Renner. Joss Renner's sister. Josiah and Liddy Renner's daughter. I drowned in the flood of '31 the same as they did, on paper. I was reborn the same night as a magistrate's ward, and I was taught to be grateful, and I was grateful, God help me, right up until I understood that my gratitude was the mortar in the wall."

She began to read.

"*Josiah Renner. Liddy Renner, born Liddy Marsh.*" Her voice cracked on the second name and she let it crack; let them hear it. "*Tom Renner. Sam Renner. Little Josie.*" — the strikes came one after another, and she touched each line as she spoke it, the way you'd touch gravestones — "*Nell Renner. Ada Quill. Brida Ferris. Merrit Renner.*"

She stopped. Looked up.

"That's me. Ninth of eleven. Nine years old, standing on the riverbank in my burned stockings, watching men from Founders' Row divide us up like seed potatoes. *For their own good,* the affidavit says. I've read it. Father, I've read it a hundred times. For our own good. I got the best good of anybody — I got your house, your name, your schools. Do you want to know what my own good consisted of? It consisted of being told, whenever I asked about before, that there was no before. That I had dreamed it. That grief was a bad habit I ought to grow out of." She laughed, and the laugh was awful even to her own ears. "Twenty years I thought I was mad. Mad! That was the price of your mercy, Father. You didn't save nine children. You saved nine convenient ones and buried the rest."

"Pike," her father said sharply, "witnesses are—"

"Sit down, Alderman." It was Brida Ferris, rising slowly by the stove, one hand on her stick, her voice like a rope thrown across water. "You've sworn enough oaths in this room for one winter."

Merrit read the last three names. "*Ada Quill.*" — a stir at the back of the room, a thin woman in a gray dress lifting her chin — "*Brida Ferris.*" — the stick tapped the floor twice, deliberate as a clock — "*Joss Renner.*"

"And the eleventh," Merrit said, and here her voice finally failed her, and she looked down the table at Tamsin, and Tamsin was crying without any noise at all, tears running straight off her jaw onto the green baize, and neither of them looked away.

"The eleventh name has no surname in this book. Only initials, cut small, in a different ink — added later, by a different hand, the way you write something when you've only just allowed yourself to know it." Merrit closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "*T.A.* Tamsin Ashgrove. Who was carried off that ice at four years old by a mapmaker who couldn't save the other ten, and who was given a story instead of a history, and who has spent this winter digging both of us out of the rubble you poured over us."

She lifted the copybook, stepped around the table — her father did not stop her, could not stop her, not with the historians' steamer two days out and forty witnesses watching — and held it out to Tamsin.

"This is yours," Merrit said. "It was never mine to keep. None of it was. Not the name, not the silence, none of it."

Tamsin rose. For a moment the two of them stood with the book between them like a bridge too narrow for either to cross alone, and Merrit saw in Tamsin's face everything she had seen in her own mirror since the stairwell — the orphan and the ward, the erased and the erasure, two girls from the same burning row wearing different lives like borrowed coats. Tamsin took the copybook. Their hands touched on the cover, briefly, and neither pretended it was nothing.

"Read them again," someone called from the back — the fishwife, her voice rough. "Read the parents' names. Slow."

So Tamsin Ashgrove stood in the magistrate's own courtroom, in front of the magistrate's own green baize, and read aloud the names of Josiah Renner and Liddy Marsh and the eight others whose names had not been spoken in Marlow Bend for twenty years, and the room learned them the way a room learns anything true: reluctantly, and then completely.

Her father sat motionless at the head of the table. Merrit had expected rage. What frightened her was the absence of it — he was calculating, she could see the machinery behind his eyes, weighing the copybook against the altered plates, the testimony against the affidavits, and finding, somewhere in that cold arithmetic, that he still had moves left. He rose at last, adjusted his cuffs, and addressed the room in a voice of perfect reasonableness.

"These are grave allegations, and this town does not convict on sentiment. There are documents in this matter — authoritative documents, engraved by a master craftsman — and we shall examine them fully when this session resumes." His eyes found Tamsin, then Merrit, and there was no hatred in them at all, which was worse. "Tomorrow, then. Bring your book, daughters. Both of you."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, and under the street's packed snow everyone could hear it — the river, shifting in its long sleep, groaning like a thing dreaming of breaking.

* * *

They stood afterward in the courthouse yard, Merrit and Tamsin, while the crowd dispersed in knots of urgent whispering. Snow was starting again, fine as sifted flour. Brida Ferris paused beside them long enough to grip each of their arms with her iron old hand — no words, just the grip, a notarization — and moved on.

"You shouldn't have done it," Tamsin said at last. Her voice was hoarse from the reading. "Not for me. He'll take it all out on you."

"He was always going to take it out on somebody." Merrit watched her breath hang in the cold. "It might as well purchase something." She turned. Down the hill, the river lay white and silent between its banks, and far off, past the last bend, the channel stood open water where the current ran fast — a dark seam in the ice, widening. "Two days," she said. "Maybe less, the way she's talking tonight. He'll have the altered plates on that table tomorrow, Tamsin. Hollis's own engravings. He thinks the master's name will bury us."

Tamsin's hand went, unconsciously, to her bandaged thumb, and something moved behind her eyes — not fear. Calculation. The look, Merrit realized, of a woman who had already decided what she would do and was merely measuring the hours remaining to do it.

"Then tomorrow," Tamsin said quietly, "he should hope the ice holds longer than he thinks."

Down at the bend, with a sound like a cannon fired underwater, the first pan of the season shifted, and the seam of open water grew an inch wider, and began, patiently, to run.
