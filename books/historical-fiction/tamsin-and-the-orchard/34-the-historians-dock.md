## 34. The Historians Dock

Elias Vane had counted the days the way he counted everything: in marks, in columns, in the small disciplined strokes of a man who trusted arithmetic more than hope. Forty-one marks since Corven's men had carried off the atlases. Thirty-three since Brida Ferris's ferry license had been suspended and she'd taken to rowing passengers across anyway, daring anyone to stop her. Nine since Tamsin Ashgrove had hidden the supplementary plates beneath eleven carved initials in a cellar wall and walked out of her own workshop with her hands empty and her chin level.

Two marks remained.

He stood on the Marlow Bend landing at dawn with his ledger under his arm and his breath smoking, watching the river do what it had been threatening to do for a week. The ice was going out in slow, grinding convulsions — great plates of it shouldering downstream, groaning like something wounded, folding over one another with a sound Elias could feel in his teeth. The pilings of the ferry dock shuddered each time the floes struck them. The whole town would hear this river for two more days and then never again until next winter, and everyone knew what came downriver on the first open water.

The steamer showed as a smudge against the pale east, then as a shape, then as a fact: black hull, white superstructure, a stack trailing smoke that lay flat on the cold air. *The Perseverance*, out of the capital. Corven had paid for her passage; Elias had read the requisition himself three weeks ago, filed under "Historical Commission — travel and incidentals," as though the truth were an expense to be managed.

Around him the landing began to fill. Word traveled fast in a town that had nothing else to talk about. He saw Alderman Pike arrive early, standing apart from the others in a good coat, his hands clasped behind him like a man waiting to be sentenced. He saw Merrit Corven come down alone, without her father, and take up a position at the end of the dock where she could watch the water without speaking to anyone. He saw Ada Quill in her plainest dress, deliberately plain, the slate-shaped bundle under her shawl making her walk with a careful, protective gait. Joss Renner stood at the far rail, arms crossed, watching the steamer with the flat expression of a man who had seen institutions before and did not expect to be impressed by this one.

And Tamsin Ashgrove came last, walking from the orchard road with ink still dark on her right middle finger, because of course she had been working through the night. She stopped beside Elias and said nothing for a long moment. The two of them had developed that habit over the winter: standing together, saying nothing, letting the records speak.

"She'll want documents," Elias said finally. "Not speeches."

"I have documents," Tamsin said. Her voice was hoarse from furnace fumes and little sleep. "I have too many documents. I don't know which ones she'll believe."

"That's not your problem," Elias said. "That's hers. Your job is only to put them all in front of her and let her choose wrong if she chooses wrong."

Tamsin almost smiled. "You make it sound simple."

"I'm a census taker," Elias said. "Everything is simple. It's only people who complicate the counting."

* * *

The steamer took the channel with the arrogance of deep-draught tonnage, shouldering the last floes aside, and warped in against the pilings while half of Marlow Bend watched from the bank. A gangway went down. Sailors stood at either side of it like fence posts.

She came down the gangway second, after a young man burdened with two leather cases, and Elias understood immediately that everything he had imagined about the capital's historians was wrong.

He had pictured Corven's hirelings: polished men with polished opinions, gentlemen who could be bought by the plate and would dine at the magistrate's table and produce whatever conclusion the dinner conversation required. What came down the gangway instead was a woman of perhaps fifty, dressed in grey wool that had been mended at both cuffs, carrying nothing but a canvas satchel and an expression like a closed ledger. She was not tall. She was not imposing. She looked, Elias thought, like someone who had spent thirty years in archives breathing paper dust, and it had dried her out from the inside, leaving only the parts that worked.

Professor Ilsa Maren paused at the foot of the gangway, looked at the crowd on the landing the way a surveyor looks at unreliable terrain, and said, in a voice dry as August:

"Which of you is Magistrate Corven?"

A ripple went through the crowd. Corven stepped forward from among the townsmen, hat in hand, warmth deployed like a tool. "Professor Maren. Welcome to Marlow Bend. The commission's arrival honors us —"

"It does not honor you," Maren said. "It investigates you. There is a difference, and I will thank you to keep it in mind." She turned a slow quarter-circle, taking in the dock, the town, the faces. "Where is the mapmaker?"

Every eye went to Tamsin.

Maren looked at her for a long moment — at the ink-stained finger, the burn-scarred knuckles, the face that had gone thin over a winter of night work. "You engraved the county atlas?"

"Under Master Hollis. And alone, since his death."

"Hollis." Maren said the name like she was tasting it. "Augustus Hollis. His river surveys are cited in three standard works. His 1826 bridge plans were accurate to within an inch." She shifted the satchel on her shoulder. "You will understand that I find it strange such a man died owing an unfinished atlas in a town that claims to have no history worth recording. Where is his workshop?"

"Seized," Tamsin said. "By order of the magistrate. Three weeks ago."

Maren nodded once, without surprise, and Elias realized she had known. Of course she had known. She had read the correspondence, the complaints, the counter-complaints; somewhere in the capital there was already a file thick with Marlow Bend's accusations, and she had boarded the steamer knowing exactly what kind of town she was sailing into.

"Then we begin with the seizure," she said. "Mr. Corven. Produce the inventory of confiscated materials. Now, please. Before breakfast, before courtesy, before anything."

Corven's warmth flickered. "Professor, the men are weary from their journey —"

"The men," Maren said, "are not the subject. The inventory is." She held out one hand, palm up, and waited. She could apparently wait a very long time. The river groaned behind her, humping a floe the size of a chapel roof, and still her hand stayed out, and still her eyes stayed on Corven, and eventually the magistrate signaled, tight-jawed, to one of his men, and a ledger was fetched from the customs house.

Maren opened it on a bollard and read it standing, page by page, running her finger down the entries. "One press screw, cracked." She looked up. "Cracked how?"

"Fault of age," said the man who'd brought it.

"Were you present when it cracked?"

"...No, ma'am."

"Then we will say 'reported cracked' and move on." Her finger descended. "Eleven bound atlases. Two loose plates, sealed. One acid bath, salted. One copybook, child's hand —" Here her finger stopped. She raised her eyes slowly. "This inventory lists a copybook."

"It was recovered," Corven said smoothly. "Stolen property, returned to its rightful custody."

"And whose custody is that?"

"The magistrate's office. As custodian of town records pending resolution."

Maren closed the inventory with a small, decisive snap. "Then the copybook is now in my custody," she said. "As arbiter of record for the Historical Commission. Fetch it. Along with every register, deed, affidavit, and map this town possesses. I want the parish books, the land office files, the ferry licenses — yes, Mr. Vane, I know about the ferry license, it was in the file — and I want them laid out on tables in whatever hall you possess, catalogued, unsorted, untouched. No one curates my evidence for me. Is that understood?"

The word went through the crowd like a current. *Evidence.* Not stories. Not testimony, not yet. Things. Paper and wood and metal and stone.

Corven's jaw worked. "Professor, certain documents contain private matters —"

"Then they will interest me more," Maren said. "Tables. Hall. Today. And, Mr. Corven —" her voice dropped, not louder but somehow heavier, the way a scale drops when weight is added — "if any document in this town has been altered, razored, re-inked, or conveniently burned in the last sixteen years, I will find it. I have spent my career finding exactly that. Do not mistake me for an audience."

* * *

They commandeered the meeting hall above the corn exchange, and by mid-afternoon it had become something Elias had dreamed about but never expected to see: the whole machinery of Marlow Bend's memory laid open on trestle tables under the tall windows, sorted into nothing, curated by no one. Parish registers. Deed books. The assessor's rolls. Ferry licenses going back forty years. Hollis's seized atlases in their neat official bindings. And beside them, carried in by a procession that included a schoolmistress, a convict, and a ferry-woman operating on a suspended license: Ada Quill's teaching slate with its twenty years of true names chalked and preserved under varnish; Brida Ferris's sworn dictation, witnessed and signed; and the cellar plates themselves, wrapped in sacking, smelling faintly of acid and orchard earth.

Elias watched Maren work and understood that he was watching a different species of the same trade he practiced. He verified households; she verified worlds. She moved down the tables without hurry, handling each document with cotton-gloved fingers, holding pages to the light to read watermarks, tilting registers to catch the shadow of razored stubs at their spines. When she found the gap in the parish register — the page torn out that Tamsin had found months ago — she did not gasp or exclaim. She simply took out a pencil and made a mark in her own notebook, and the mark seemed to Elias more damning than any speech.

She interviewed no one. That was the thing that unnerved the town most, he thought. They had prepared their testimonies, rehearsed their griefs and denials, and Maren declined all of it. "Testimony comes after documents," she told Brida, when Brida presented herself at the tables, upright and fierce. "Documents first. Then I will ask you questions you cannot rehearse for."

Brida had looked at Elias afterward, affronted. "She didn't want to hear me."

"She wants to hear you *last*," Elias said. "When you can't be led."

By evening, Corven made his play. Elias saw it coming the way you see weather coming — the magistrate arrived at the hall with two of his men carrying the official atlas, opened to the county plate, and delivered his argument with the smoothness of long rehearsal: here was the authoritative map, engraved by the celebrated Hollis himself, showing no laborers' row, no flood line, no eleven children; here was the master's own hand endorsing the town's settled history; surely the Commission would weigh the considered work of a great cartographer above the claims of a disgruntled apprentice and a handful of aggrieved persons.

Maren listened to all of it without interruption. Then she said: "Show me the plate."

"The plate?"

"The engraving plate. For this map. Originals survive; apprentices' copies get corrected. I want the copper."

Corven hesitated one beat too long. "Plates are sometimes melted down between editions —"

"Then show me the scrap ledger, the metal weights, the furnace records, or the receipt of sale," Maren said. "Or admit the plates exist and produce them." She let the silence stretch until it hurt. "Mr. Corven, I will tell you how I work, so that you may economize your efforts. I do not decide between narratives. I am not interested in whose story is more moving. I follow material objects, and objects are terrible liars. Copper remembers every re-engraving. Paper remembers every erasure. Wood remembers every fire. If your town's history is true, its objects will confirm it, and I will say so publicly, and you will have won. If your town's history is a fabrication, its objects will betray it, and I will also say so publicly, and no affidavit will save you. Either way, sir, the objects decide. Not you. Not the girl. Me." She gathered her gloves. "Now. Tomorrow morning I inspect the site of this laborers' row. All sites. The mill ruin, the drowned lane, and —" she consulted her notebook, though Elias was quite sure she needed no consultation — "the orchard cottage cellar."

The color left Corven's face so completely that Elias, watching from the doorway, thought for one absurd moment the man might faint.

* * *

After dark, Elias found Tamsin on the landing, wrapped in a coat too big for her — Brida's, he guessed — watching the ice run. The floes were thinner now, the river winning, the grinding grown intermittent. By tomorrow the channel would be clear. Any vessel could come. Including, Elias knew from the harbor master's list, a second steamer, chartered privately, bearing Corven's counsel from the capital.

"They're saying she's bought," Tamsin said, without turning. "Half the town says Corven owns her. The other half says we do."

"Both halves are wrong."

"I know." She was quiet a moment. "That's what frightens me, Elias. I wanted her to be ours. I've spent all winter wanting a judge, and now I have one, and she refuses to be anybody's. She looked at my plates today the same way she looked at Corven's atlas. Like they were all just claims."

"They are all just claims," Elias said. "Until she rules. That's the whole terror and mercy of it."

Tamsin pulled the coat tighter. Below them, the river carried the last great floe past the pilings, turning slowly, catching the moonlight along its cracked face like a page being lifted.

"Do you think the cellar will convince her?" Tamsin asked. "Eleven initials cut into a wall by children's hands. A slate. Some plates buried in the dirt. It isn't much. It's everything we have, and it isn't much."

Elias thought about his own trade — the household marked *vacant* that had started all of this, one honest entry in a dishonest world. He thought about what he had learned this winter: that records were not neutral, but neither were they nothing; that a thing written down could be murdered, and a thing carved into a wall could refuse to die.

"It's more than Corven has," he said. "His whole case rests on documents that don't exist anymore. Yours rests on things that couldn't be destroyed without destroying the town itself. She'll see that. Objects are terrible liars — she said so. Well. Walls are worse. Walls can't even try."

Tamsin laughed — a short, exhausted sound, half sob. Then she straightened, and Elias saw the fear settle into resolve the way sediment settles into stone.

"Tomorrow, then," she said. "The cellar."

"Tomorrow, the cellar," Elias agreed.

Behind them, up the dark street, a lantern bobbed: Professor Maren herself, walking alone past the shuttered shops toward the inn, pausing at the corner to look up at the hills — at the orchard, invisible in the dark but unmistakably there. She stood a long moment, notebook in hand, a dry small figure against the vast indifferent night.

Then she wrote something down, snapped the notebook shut, and walked on.
