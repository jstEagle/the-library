## 36. Both Maps

The hearing room smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and by the third hour of Professor Maren's questioning it had acquired a fourth smell: fear, faint and sour, rising off the benches like steam off a thawing pond.

Maren did not sit like the other arbiters I had imagined in my long nights at the engraving bench. In my imagination she had been a man — they were always men in my imaginings — gray and vast as a courthouse, with a voice that rolled. Instead she was a small dry woman with spectacles that caught the lamplight so her eyes were always hidden behind two coins of gold, and when she spoke it was in the flat, unhurried tone of someone reading an inventory. She had been asking questions since nine o'clock. It was past four now. The stove had gone cold because no one dared feed it while she spoke.

She held Brida's sworn dictation in both hands, the way one holds a bird one has not yet decided to keep.

"You state," she said to Brida, "that on the night of the seventeenth of January, 1831, you ferried eleven children across the Marlow, two at a time, from the north landing."

"Two at a time and once three," said Brida. Her voice was gravel over ice. "The little Corven girl wouldn't let go of my neck."

A murmur went through the benches. Magistrate Corven did not look at his daughter. That was the first thing I understood about that hour: he was not looking at Merrit, and Merrit knew he was not looking, and neither of them would give the other the mercy of a glance.

"And you state further," Maren continued, turning the page without ceremony, "that the laborers' row stood upon land now claimed under the Ashgrove–Corven deed of 1832."

"I state what my eyes saw and my arms carried," said Brida. "You can dress it however suits your coat."

Maren set the paper down. She removed her spectacles, breathed on them, polished them with a handkerchief that had been white sometime before the war of anyone's grandparents, and put them back on. All of this took perhaps forty seconds, and in those forty seconds the whole room leaned toward her like grass toward sun.

Then she looked at me.

"Tamsin Ashgrove. You are the executor of the Hollis atlas."

"I am."

"You have presented to me a sealed letter, a child's copybook, a slate bearing twenty years of teaching notes, a supplementary copper plate engraved within the last month, and a cellar wall bearing eleven sets of initials." She recited it as if reading off a manifest, and indeed she had the manifest; Elias Vane had copied it out for her in his small clerk's hand the night the steamer docked. "Which of these do you consider the strongest evidence?"

I had rehearsed answers to every question but that one. I heard my own silence stretch, and hated it.

"The wall," I said at last. "Paper can be forged. A letter can be dictated under pressure. But eleven children carved their names into a cellar wall with a table knife over the course of a winter, and no one forges seventeen-year-old knife-marks in plaster."

"No one," said Maren, "who is not very patient."

"Professor—" began Corven's counsel, a soft-spoken man named Hallett whose voice I had learned to dread precisely because it was kind.

Maren raised one finger, not looking at him, and he sat down as though a weight had been placed on his shoulders.

* * *

They gave us until morning. That was the cruelty of it, or the justice — I still cannot decide which. Maren announced that she would rule at ten o'clock the following day, and then she closed her ledger, thanked the room in a voice like a closing door, and left through the side entrance with her single leather case, refusing the magistrate's invitation to dinner with a courtesy so complete it drew blood.

I found her that evening, because I could not help myself. She was walking the river road alone, hands clasped behind her back, watching the last pans of ice grind downstream. The air had turned soft. You could smell the mud coming.

"Professor Maren."

She did not stop walking, so I walked beside her.

"You want to know how I'll rule," she said.

"I want to know what you see."

"Ah." She considered that. The ice groaned below the bank, a long whale-song note that seemed to come up through our boots. "I see a town that kept its records too well. Do you understand what I mean by that? A fire happens, Miss Ashgrove. Floods happen. Children die, God knows, more often than any of us can bear to count. What does not happen — what *cannot* happen, in thirty-one separate documents spanning sixteen years — is perfect absence. No register entry, no tax line, no burial record, no school roll, no ferry log, no militia roster. Not one stray mark. Your founders did not lose their history. They curated it. And curation, Miss Ashgrove, leaves a shape. Absence has edges."

"That's what Hollis taught me," I said. "That a map is defined by what it refuses to draw."

"Your master," said Maren, "was either a coward or a conspirator or the most careful man in this county, and I suspect he managed to be all three at once, which takes talent." She stopped at the rail above the ferry landing. Below us, Brida's boat knocked against its mooring in the dark water. "I will ask you something now, and you may refuse to answer, and I will think no less of you. When I rule tomorrow — whichever way I rule — will you be able to live in this town afterward?"

The question went through me like a needle finding the depth of a wound. I thought of Pike standing in the workshop doorway the night before, hat in his hands, asking what name should go where his signature would have been. I thought of Joss Renner sleeping on the workshop floor with his back against the furnace, because he could not yet sleep anywhere with a roof that didn't remind him of something worse.

"I don't know," I said. "But I'll be able to live with myself, which I'm told is rarer."

Something moved behind the gold coins of her spectacles — not a smile exactly, but the place where a smile keeps its tools.

"Good answer," she said. "It's the only kind I trust. Certainty is what your magistrate sells."

* * *

At ten o'clock the next morning the hearing room was full to the doors, and people stood in the snow-melt outside the windows three deep. Corven sat at the front with his counsel, his hands folded on the table before him like a man at prayer who has long since stopped believing anyone is listening. Merrit sat behind him. She wore gray. She had not spoken to me since the inquiry floor, since she read the eleven names aloud in a voice that cracked on the ninth and held steady on the eleventh — mine — and put the copybook into my hands as though returning something far heavier than a book.

Maren stood. She did not use notes.

"I have examined the evidence presented by both parties," she said, "and I will summarize my findings briefly, because I dislike speeches, and because what I have to say requires no decoration.

"First: there existed, until the winter of 1831, a row of dwellings on the north bank known variously as the Laborers' Row, the Mill Row, and in at least one ferry log as Ferris Landing East. Eleven children orphaned in the flood and fire of that winter were removed from the town and dispersed among households of the founding families. This removal was recorded nowhere. That absence, given its completeness, constitutes evidence of design rather than accident.

"Second: the official map of Marlow Bend County, engraved by Master Josiah Hollis and completed posthumously by Tamsin Ashgrove, omits this row. Two plates bear evidence of re-engraving under duress. I do not hold the late Mr. Hollis's compliance against the truth; I merely note that the truth required him to comply, which tells us who held the pen.

"Third:" — and here she paused, and the pause had a blade in it — "the supplementary plate produced by Miss Ashgrove is accurate. I have walked the ground myself, yesterday evening, in the company of a convict, a ferrywoman, and a schoolmistress, none of whom conspired to mislead me, and all of whom, I judge, would have been incapable of doing so even had they wished it. The flood line matches the silt. The foundations match the footings. The initials match the children."

Her gaze crossed the room and settled, for one long moment, on Magistrate Corven.

"Therefore. An atlas that contains a false map is not history; it is testimony — testimony on behalf of whoever paid for it. I will not certify it as such. The atlas of Marlow Bend will be published with both plates bound together — the official map and the engraved truth — or it will not be published at all, and I will report to the Royal Cartographic Society that this county's records are unreliable in their entirety, every deed, every grant, every claim, from 1831 forward. The choice of which outcome occurs belongs entirely," she said, and her voice did not rise at all, "to the magistrate."

The silence afterward had a physical weight. I felt it press on my chest.

Corven rose.

He did not do it quickly. He gathered his papers, squared them, set down his pen parallel to the table's edge — the small tidy motions of a man who has spent a life arranging things — and then he straightened, and looked around the room at the faces of the town he had built twice, once in timber and once in paper.

"The county," he said, "will publish both maps."

Hallett started up beside him — "Magistrate, if we adjourn, if we petition—" — and Corven put one hand flat on the table, and Hallett stopped as if the hand had landed on him.

"The county will publish both maps," Corven said again, quieter, "because the alternative is that every acre I have ever transferred becomes a question. I built this town on the river, gentlemen, and the river has a memory longer than mine. Let it be written down. Let it be *bound*, since that seems to be the fashion." His eyes found mine across the room, and what was in them was not hatred. That was the worst of it. It was exhaustion, ancient and personal, the look of a man setting down a load he has carried so long he had forgotten it was heavy. "Miss Ashgrove. You will find that winning is not what you imagine."

And he walked out. Not hurriedly. He paused at the door where Merrit sat, and for a moment his hand hovered near her shoulder — hovered, trembled almost imperceptibly in the lamplight — and then withdrew, and he went through the door alone, and the sound of his boots faded down the corridor like water going out of a channel.

Merrit did not watch him go. She watched me. And I could not read her face at all, except that it was the face of someone standing in the wreckage of the only house she'd ever been allowed to know was hers.

* * *

Afterward the room emptied slowly, the way a wound bleeds. People came by me in twos and threes — some to nod, some to say nothing meaning a great deal, a few to turn away quickly, and one woman, a millwife whose name I never learned, to grip my wrist hard enough to hurt and whisper, "My grandmother was of the Row. Nobody ever said it out loud before today." Then she was gone into the crowd, and I stood holding my own wrist like something borrowed.

Ada found me on the steps. She had her slate under her arm — she carried it everywhere now, like a shield she'd finally been permitted to raise.

"It's done," she said.

"It's done."

"We won."

I looked out over the square, at the melting snow going gray along the gutters, at the boys already chalking something on the courthouse wall — words, I couldn't read them from here, but the shape of them was triumphant — at Joss leaning against the rail with his arms folded, watching the crowd with the expression of a man who has been told the war is over and doesn't believe wars end.

"Did we?" I said.

Ada followed my gaze. "Pike's not here," she observed.

No, he wasn't. Alderman Pike had sat through the entire ruling with his head bowed, and when Maren finished he had risen before anyone else and gone out the back, and no one had seen which way. I thought of him standing in the workshop dark, asking what name should fill the blank line. I thought of his mother's voice, which he had mentioned once and never again, and which I suspected he heard most clearly whenever he was about to do the wrong thing.

"He'll come around or he won't," Ada said, in her schoolroom voice, the one that forgave and graded in the same breath.

"And Corven?"

"Corven," said Ada, "has discovered that he cannot burn a book once it's been bound. It's the cruelest thing we could have done to him — better than jail. Now he has to live inside the true version." She touched my arm. "Go home, Tamsin. Sleep. Tomorrow we sew."

* * *

I did not go home. I went to the orchard cottage.

The cellar was cold and smelled of earth and old apples, and the light came down the stairs in a thin gray wedge that reached just far enough to touch the wall. I knelt where I had knelt before, and put my fingers again into the carved letters, each groove worn smooth by my own touching these past weeks.

*T.A.* Third from the left. Small, uneven, cut by a hand that had not yet learned what it would become.

We had won. The word kept presenting itself to me, wanting acknowledgment, and I kept declining to receive it, because victory, it turns out, is not a door opening but a bill arriving. Corven had withdrawn rather than be exposed — Maren had said as much, in her dry inventory voice — and withdrawal meant he remained in his house with his law books and his unspent power, nursing the particular fury of a man who has lost by being made honest. Pike was somewhere in the town carrying a blank line in his conscience. Merrit had given me back my name in front of three hundred people and had not once, before or since, let herself be alone in a room with me. And eleven parents lay under the spring mud with their names newly restored to the public record and not one of them alive to hear it.

I traced the *T*. My master had taken me in off the ice and told me a story about who I was, and I had believed it the way you believe a map — completely, gratefully, without ever thinking to walk the ground. Now I had walked the ground. The story was gone. What remained was a girl with two names, one given and one carved, and a workshop full of copper that needed sewing into a hundred books by morning after next.

From above me, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the front door, and footsteps crossing the kitchen overhead — light, deliberate, pausing at the head of the cellar stair.

"Tamsin." Merrit's voice. "Brida says the furnace should be lit by six. And—" a hesitation, the first I had ever heard from her— "Joss wants to know if you'll let him run the press screw himself. He doesn't trust anyone else's hands with it."

I looked one moment longer at the initials, all eleven of them, the archive of children the town had tried to file away under nothing.

"Tell him yes," I called up. "Tell everyone. We start tonight."

There was a silence above me, and then her footsteps moved away toward the door, and beyond them, faint and constant, the river ran loud with the breaking of the ice.
