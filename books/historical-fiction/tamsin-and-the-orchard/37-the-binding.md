## 37. The Binding

The workshop smelled of hide glue and candle smoke, and it was the sweetest thing I had ever known.

We had scrubbed the acid from the floor with lye and river water, replaced the cracked press screw with one Joss machined himself from a length of salvaged axle iron, and rehung the door on hinges so new they squeaked like fledglings. Corven's men had left in defeat two days past, but they had left their mark too — a boot heel through the drying rack, ink ground into the floorboards where they'd emptied a pot out of spite, and a smell of tobacco that would live in the rafters until spring. None of it mattered. The workshop stood. We stood. And stacked along the north wall in careful columns sat three hundred copies of *Hollis & Ashgrove's Atlas of Marlow Bend and Environs* — every one of them incomplete, every one of them waiting.

Waiting for the second plate.

I had cut forty-one of them before the raid. Forty-one plates engraved with the laborers' row as it truly stood: thirty-two houses along the mill race, the flood line of the winter of '31 drawn in fine parallel hatching, the burn scar of the mill marked with Hollis's own cartographic convention for destroyed structures — a symbol he had invented and never once been permitted to use. Eleven names ran down the margin in letters small enough to fit but deep enough to last. Deep enough to be printed ten thousand times and never wear away. I had cut them with bleeding hands by the light of a lamp I dared not let gutter, and when the soldiers came through the front door I had gone out the back into the snow with the plates wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, and I had buried them in the orchard cellar beneath eleven sets of initials carved by children who did not know they were writing their own names for the last time.

Now the plates were dug up, cleaned, and locked in the press room, and there was nothing left between us and the historians' steamer except glue, thread, and the willingness of seven people to work until dawn.

Brida arrived first, as she always did, before full dark, stamping snow off her boots and declaring the river ice "rotten as a magistrate's promise." She brought a ham bone for the soup and her own awl, wrapped in flannel, older than any of us. Ada came next with slate dust still on her cuffs and a schoolmarm's genius for organization; within an hour she had arranged the workroom into stations — folding, sewing, casing — with chalk labels on every bench. Elias Vane appeared at nine o'clock sharp, removed his coat, rolled his sleeves to reveal forearms nobody expects on a census clerk, and said only, "Where do you need me?" Joss took the furnace and the presses, because Joss trusted no one else near machinery, least of all machinery that had already been sabotaged once. And Merrit—

Merrit came at half past nine, hood up, letting herself in without knocking, and stood dripping in the doorway as though she could not decide whether she was entering or fleeing.

"There are two hundred and sixty copies," she said. "I counted them at my father's warehouse. He means to ship them to the capital booksellers the moment the inquiry closes."

"Then we have until the inquiry closes," I said.

"Tamsin." Her voice dropped. "He'll close it tomorrow. He has to, before Maren can—"

"Then we work faster."

She looked at me for a long moment across the candlelit room, this woman who shared my origin and not one inch of my history, and then she pulled back her hood, hung her wet cloak on the hook that had held Master Hollis's coat for twenty years, and picked up a bone folder.

None of us remarked upon it. That was its own kind of covenant.

* * *

You cannot simply sew a plate into a bound book. Any apprentice knows this. A finished atlas is a sewn object; its sections are gathered, stitched through the fold, and cased into boards before the maps go in. To add a plate afterward you must open the spine, which means softening the original adhesive, lifting the sewing threads, inserting the new section — our plate, printed on the good rag stock, folded once to match the others — and resewing the whole gathering before the glue sets wrong or the thread cuts the paper. Done carelessly, you ruin the book. Done well, done patiently, the atlas opens to a page that was always meant to be there, and no one can quite believe it wasn't.

Two hundred and sixty times.

Ada organized us into a line. Brida softened spines over the glue kettle, working in batches of six, her old hands reading the temperature of the paste the way she read the river. Elias lifted the sewing — delicate work, threading a thin blade under stitches fifty years of binders had set tight — and passed each opened volume down the bench to Merrit, who inserted the folded plate and squared it against the shoulder of the book with a precision that surprised everyone, herself included. I sewed. That was my station and no other's. My needle went in at the kettle stitch, along the band, out again, catching the new section to the old ones with linen thread waxed across my palm, and each completed book went onto the rack to wait for its fresh paste and its boards pressed flat.

It takes perhaps twenty minutes to rebind a single atlas properly, if your hands know the work and do not shake.

My hands shook for the first hour. Not from cold.

Because every time the needle went through the fold, it passed through the very center of the book — the gutter — and lying in that gutter, printed in ink I had mixed myself, were eleven names. Ruth Ferris. Ansel Pike. Edith Corven. On down the column, and at the bottom, in the same deep-cut lettering: TAMSIN ASHGROVE, born TAMSIN RENNER'S SISTER'S CHILD? No — that was not what I had cut. I had cut the truth: *Tamsin Ashgrove, called also Tamsin of the Row.* Both names. The given story and the recovered one, sharing a single line of type, the way they now shared a single body. Every book I sewed shut around that name, and every time, some small part of me braced as though the needle might find the nerve instead of the thread.

"You're sewing them too tight," Brida said, somewhere past midnight. "Loosen your grip, girl. The book needs to breathe."

"I don't want it to come apart."

"It won't come apart. It's not supposed to be rigid, love. It's supposed to flex. Stiff binding cracks. Flexible binding survives." She held up her own awl, the flannel-wrapped relic. "My mother made sails. Same principle. You don't fight the wind, you give the canvas room to bell."

I loosened my grip. The books improved.

* * *

Sometime after one in the morning, Joss spoke for the first time in four hours.

"They'll tear them out."

We all looked up. He stood at the furnace with a poker in his hand, firelight turning his prison-honed face into something carved and bitter. "The buyers. Half of Marlow Bend bought the lie with their deeds and their inheritances. They'll buy the atlas, find the second map, and tear it out before supper. You're sewing truth into books that'll be gutted by Sunday."

"Perhaps," Ada said mildly, chalking the next batch number.

"Perhaps?" He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "Pike signed the affidavit. Corven's whole generation will—"

"Joss." Brida didn't raise her voice. She never needed to. "How many books you reckon get printed in the capital, once Professor Maren files her report?"

He scowled. "Hundreds. Thousands, maybe."

"And how many of those buyers ever heard of Marlow Bend? How many of them will look at the second map and see anything but a curiosity?" She turned the ham bone in the soup pot, unhurried. "You're right that folk here will tear pages out. Let them. A torn page leaves a stub, boy. Anyone who opens that book afterward sees the stub and asks: what was here? What was removed, and who removed it, and why does it frighten them still?" She tapped her temple. "I've kept testimony in my head for twenty years because there was nowhere safe to write it. Now it's sewn into three hundred books headed for parlors and libraries and university shelves in cities we'll never see. You think tearing one out stops it?"

Joss looked at her. Then at the rack of finished volumes, pale boards and fresh thread, two hundred and sixty arguments standing upright against the wall.

"The tenth name on that plate," he said finally. "That's mine."

"Yes," I said.

"My father hauled timber at the mill. My mother washed for the Corvens. There's no record of either of them anywhere in this county except that margin." His jaw worked. "I went to the penal colony for stealing bread off a Corven cart when I was fourteen, because there was no other food and no one whose business it was to feed me. Fourteen years old, transported for hunger, and the ledger called me 'boy, unattached.'" He drove the poker into the coals. "Unattached."

"Not anymore," Elias said quietly.

Joss stared into the furnace a long while. When he turned back to the press, he said nothing more, but he worked until dawn beside us, and when the first batch of rebound atlases came off the pressing boards he handled each one the way you handle something that might break — or something that might, if you gripped it wrong, escape.

* * *

Near three o'clock, Merrit stopped mid-fold.

I noticed because her hands never stopped. All night they had moved with that Corven precision, squaring plates, creasing folds, and now they hung in the air above an opened atlas, and she was looking down into the gutter at the flood line, the row, the names.

"Edith Corven," she read aloud. "That's—" Her breath caught. "That's my grandmother."

"Your grandfather's housekeeper," Ada said gently, from the next station. "And Ansel Pike's mother. And mother to three of the drowned men's children besides, though the register calls her 'servant, female.'" Ada wiped chalk from her fingers. "History keeps women in the margins, Merrit. Your grandmother spent her life there. This plate is the only document in the county that gives her a line of her own."

Merrit stood motionless. Then, wordlessly, she picked up the atlas, carried it to the press herself, and cranked the screw down with both hands — harder than necessary, as though she could compress the whole argument of the night into a single square inch of board and paper. When she came back to the bench her eyes were red, and no one was impolite enough to notice.

"She sang," Merrit said, after a time. "In the kitchen. My father hates singing. I never knew why until this week." She smoothed the next plate flat. "He grew up hearing her sing songs from the Row."

No one answered. There was nothing to add. Some truths you carry home yourself or not at all.

* * *

The candles burned down twice. Elias replenished them from a box he'd brought "for census fieldwork," and I understood that he had planned for a long night long before he knocked on our door — that the man had probably been planning since the day he found a razored register page and couldn't rest until he'd learned what the town had cut away. He sewed badly and knew it, so Brida moved him to pasting, and he pasted with the thoroughness of a man who believed every surface deserved accurate documentation.

Around four, my hands finally stopped shaking.

I want to record that honestly, because it felt less like calm than like dissolution — the moment when repetition wore through grief the way water wears through limestone. Needle in, thread along, needle out. Kettle stitch. Square the section. Pass it on. The rhythm took me the way the river's rhythm takes the ferry, and somewhere inside it I stopped being a woman rebelling against her history and became simply a craftswoman doing the only honest thing her hands knew: making a book true.

Master Hollis had taught me that a map is a sentence about the world, and every sentence has an author. For twenty years I'd believed him to mean that authorship ended at accuracy — draw the world rightly and you've said all there is to say. Lying in the gutter of every atlas that passed beneath my needle were eleven lines proving him both right and wrong. Accuracy was not neutrality. Accuracy was the argument. Someone had authored a county without the Row; tonight, stitch by stitch, we were authoring it back.

"Forty-one," Brida announced near dawn. She'd been keeping count with knots in a string. "Forty-one done. Two hundred nineteen to go."

"We won't finish," Merrit said flatly. It wasn't despair; it was arithmetic. "Not at this pace. Not before the inquiry reconvenes."

"Then we finish after," Ada said. "Or we finish enough."

"Enough for what?"

"For Maren." Elias looked up from his paste brush. "She doesn't need all three hundred. She needs to hold one copy in her hands and see that the truth was bound into the trade edition itself — that it wasn't a pamphlet, wasn't a protest, wasn't something separable. She needs evidence of intent. Show her one finished atlas, show her forty, and the question changes from *what happened in 1831* to *who has been publishing a lie under seal since the spring.*" He smiled, tired and crooked. "Census logic. You don't count every soul in the nation. You count the household, and the household tells you what the street contains."

"So we work until the boat docks," I said, "and whatever's bound by then is bound."

"Whatever's bound by then," Brida agreed, "is covenant. The rest is follow-through."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin through the frosted windows, and none of us had slept, and none of us stopped. The soup pot emptied and was refilled. Brida's knot string grew heavy. My shoulders burned; my fingertips were pricked to lacework; the smell of hide glue had entered my skin and hair and I suspect will enter my dreams for years. And slowly, stack by stack, the rack filled — pale new-spined atlases leaning together like a congregation, each carrying in its belly a map the founders had killed a man to keep unborn.

Hollis hadn't died for the atlas, I understood then, watching the light strengthen over the workbench. He had died leaving it unfinished. Deliberately. A finished atlas is a closed argument; an unfinished one is a question handed to whoever loved him enough to pick up the graver. He had made sure that finishing it would require choosing. And this — the glue, the thread, the seven of us swaying with exhaustion around a bench at dawn — this was the choice, made flesh, made thread, made permanent.

At half past six, Joss threw the last log on the furnace and said, "Boat smoke on the river."

We went to the window. Far downstream, where the ice had rotted into leads and pans, a smudge of dark rose against the brightening east. The steamer. Two days early, or on time by a clock we hadn't been shown.

Maren would dock within the hour. Corven would meet her at the wharf with his historians' welcome and his sealed affidavits and his warehouse of clean, unmutilated atlases — every copy innocent, every copy complete, every copy a lie sewn shut with professional stitching.

But on the rack behind us stood forty-one books with the truth bound into their spines, flexible, breathing, impossible to remove without leaving a wound anyone could see.

I took the first one off the rack. I wrapped it in oilcloth, the way I'd wrapped the plates in the cellar, and I held it against my chest.

"Come on," I said. "We've got a hearing to attend — and a book to hand her before Corven finds his tongue."

Brida snuffed the lamps. Ada wiped the chalk labels away. And the six of us went out into the frozen morning, down toward the river, carrying the covenant.
