## 38. Publication Day

**Brida Ferris**

The ferry rope was stiff with frost when I came down to the landing, and the river ran brown and fast beneath the last of the ice, carrying winter out to sea like a debt being paid. I had crossed that water ten thousand times and never once thought about what lay under it. Now I thought of nothing else.

The bookshop had opened before dawn. I knew because I'd seen the lamplight from the water — a yellow square in the dark, and Ada Quill's silhouette moving behind it, arranging things on a table with the same care she'd once given her slate. By full light there was a queue. A queue, in Marlow Bend, for a book of maps.

I tied off the rope and stood a while watching them come. Farmers' wives in their good shawls. The tanner's boys, sent by their father, jostling. Old Mother Casson, who could not read, buying one anyway because she said a house ought to hold the truth even if it couldn't speak it. They paid their coins and carried the atlases away flat against their chests, the way you carry something living.

Some of them, I will say it plain, bought it to burn it. You could tell those ones by their faces — tight, quick-eyed, not meeting anyone's gaze. Corven's people. There were more of them than I'd hoped and fewer than he'd promised himself, I think.

And some bought it the other way. A young man I didn't know stood at the back of the queue with his cap in his hands, and when Ada handed him the atlas he opened it right there on the counter, past the fine engraved map of the town with its tidy streets and its tidy nothing where the laborers' row had been, to the second plate. To our plate. He looked at it so long that Ada touched his arm, gentle as touching a bird, and asked if he was all right.

"My grandmother," he said. "Her name is on it. I never knew why my mother wouldn't talk about this town." He closed the book carefully, like closing a door on a sleeping child. "Now I know."

I stayed by the landing most of the day. Word travels faster over water than road, and every crossing brought somebody new who wanted to ask me — Brida Ferris, sixty-one years old, keeper of a rope and a boat — whether it was true. Whether there had really been a row of houses by the mill. Whether eleven children had really been scattered like seed corn across three counties.

"It's true," I told each one. Forty years I hadn't said it aloud. It took less than a day to wear the word smooth.

**Ada Quill**

I sold books for nine hours and did not sit down once, and I would do it again tomorrow without complaint, though my feet have lodged a formal protest.

There is a particular thing that happens when a person opens an atlas to a page they did not expect. I watched it happen perhaps two hundred times today. First the confusion — the second plate bound in after the county map, unnumbered, unsigned except for eleven names and a flood line drawn in Tamsin Ashgrove's sure hand. Then the reading. Then the stillness. That stillness is the thing I cannot put out of my mind. People went quiet the way a congregation goes quiet, and several of them — grown men, women older than myself — put their fingers on the paper and left them there, as though checking that the ink was real.

One woman tore hers.

She was well-dressed, Corven's sort, and she did it standing right at the counter, ripping the supplementary plate out along the gutter with a sound like cloth tearing, crumpling it into her muff. "There," she said, as though she had accomplished something. "That's better."

"Madam," I said, "you have just paid four shillings for half a book."

She stared at me. I confess I enjoyed it.

"You may keep the remainder with my compliments," I continued, "but I should tell you that we printed more plates than books. Every torn page will be replaced within the week, and the man who engraves them has very steady hands."

She left without her change. Elias Vane, who had come to stand witness in his quiet census-taker way, picked up the coins she'd abandoned and set them in the poor box.

By afternoon we had sold through the first printing entire. Joss Renner appeared at dusk with a handcart and an expression suggesting he had carried heavy things all day and intended to be bitter about it forever, which is simply Joss's face. He stacked the empty crates and said, gruffly, "Framed one already. Saw it through a window on Chapel Street. Framed, Ada. Like a picture of a saint."

"Good," I said.

"Framed," he said again, as if the word offended him, and then he almost smiled, which for Joss is a religious experience, and went home.

**Alderman Pike**

I signed the retraction at half past ten in the morning, on the steps of the courthouse, before a crowd larger than any I have addressed in twenty years of public life.

I had written it out four times the night before. The first draft blamed Corven. The second blamed the times. The third was honest but cowardly, all passive voice — mistakes were made, records were mislaid — and I burned that one in the grate and was ashamed of how long it took me to do it. The fourth said only what was true:

*I, Nathaniel Pike, alderman of Marlow Bend, do hereby retract the affidavit sworn by me in the year 1831 and reaffirmed since, wherein I denied the existence of the laborers' row upon the mill lands. The row existed. I lived in it. My mother died in the flood of that winter, and her name — Ruth Pike — appears on the supplementary plate of the county atlas now published. I denied her for thirty years to keep what I had built on the ground where she drowned. I will not deny her another day.*

Reading your own mother's name aloud on courthouse steps is a strange sensation. The crowd made a sound I have no word for — not a gasp, not a cheer. Something between grief and relief, shared out among strangers.

Corven was not present. His windows were shuttered, I am told, and his clerk turned away all callers. But his daughter stood at the edge of the square, and she did not look away from me, and when I finished she nodded once — a small nod, private, the kind you give across a room to someone who has finally stopped lying to themselves. She of all people knows what the signature cost. She knows better than anyone living, perhaps.

Afterward a reporter from the capital — arrived on yesterday's boat, one of Maren's people, dry as chalk — asked me why now. Why, after thirty years, after everything.

I told him the truth, or the shape of it: "Because a man can live comfortably inside a lie the way you live inside a warm house. And then someone shows you the wall is paper, and beyond it is your mother's voice, and you understand you were never warm at all. Only insulated."

He wrote it down. I doubt he'll print it. The truth is rarely quotable; it only survives, which is more important.

**Elias Vane**

I keep records. That is the whole of my profession and, I have come to believe, the whole of my character, and so I record the following.

By nightfall, of the two hundred copies printed: one hundred and forty-four sold intact. Eleven returned for refund by persons who described the second plate as "seditious," "indecent," and, in one memorable instance, "badly centered." Nine copies purchased and defaced — plates torn, burned, or in the case of the mill owner's son, soaked in the horse trough outside the Bell. Thirty-two remain unsold in the shop.

Against this: four framed copies observed in street-facing windows by nightfall. One copy carried openly to church by the Widow Marsh, who is nearly deaf and does not care who knows it. One copy sent by post to the capital, addressed to Professor Ilsa Maren herself, with a note in Tamsin's hand that I did not read and do not wish to, some correspondence belonging to its sender alone.

And one copy, I learned at dusk, placed anonymously on the step of the Corven house. No note. Just the atlas, weighted with a stone against the wind, open to the second plate, the eleven names facing the door.

Merrit, I would wager anything I own. And I would further wager that her father found it there this morning and has not yet decided whether it is an accusation or an inheritance.

The town talked all day in the low constant way towns talk — at the well, at the forge, over fences. Not shouting. That was the remarkable thing. After the inquiry, after Merrit read the names aloud and Corven walked out rather than hear his own history spoken by his own daughter, I had expected fury, faction, torchlight. Instead there was this steady murmur, like thaw water under ice, going everywhere at once and refusing to be directed. Truth loose in a town behaves exactly like that. You cannot recall it. You can only decide, house by house, what to do with it.

Some nailed their doors shut. Some opened their windows.

**Joss Renner**

They wanted me to write something for this and I told them I don't write, I fix things, so this will be short.

Thirty years ago men put me on a ship because it was cheaper than feeding me. Today a man I've known all my life — a deacon, no less — stopped me in the street, looked at the calluses on my hands, and asked if I was the one from the book. The Renner boy. The tenth name.

I said yes.

He shook my hand. Shook it, like I'd come back from the war instead of the hulks. And I stood there wanting to hit him and wanting to thank him and doing neither, because here's the thing nobody tells you about coming home: the town doesn't change. You do. And then you spend the rest of your life deciding whether the difference is yours to forgive.

I fixed the shop door this morning — it sticks, always has stuck, Hollis never could abide it and neither can I. Tamsin was at the bench with her sleeves rolled, cutting a replacement plate for the woman who tore hers, and she didn't look up when I came in, just said, "Hand me the burnisher," the way you talk to family.

So. That's my account. The book is out of our hands now. It's in theirs — all of theirs, the framers and the tearers, the ones who'll read it and the ones who'll burn it. Can't guard a published thing. Can't take it back.

Turns out that's not a loss. Turns out that's the whole point.

**Tamsin Ashgrove**

I did not go to the shop. I could not have borne to watch the book leave me one copy at a time, so instead I sat in Master Hollis's workshop — my workshop, I may as well write it, though the lease is only mine until Michaelmas — and I cut the replacement plates, one after another, until my shoulders burned and the candle had burned down twice.

It is a strange grief, finishing something. For a year the atlas was a wound I was working at, and now it is done and loose in the world, and I find I miss even the dread of it. This morning the book belonged to me, to Brida and Ada and Joss, to the eleven. By tonight it belongs to everyone — to the woman who framed it on Chapel Street and the woman who tore it into her muff, to Professor Maren's dry annotations and to whatever use the capital makes of us next. A map, once published, is an argument you no longer get to referee. I wrote that in the cartographer's note. I am only now beginning to feel its weight.

Near midnight there came a knock, soft, and Merrit Corven stood in the doorway with snow in her hair and no lantern, and behind her, at the gate, the lights of the Corven house burning in every window — every window, which I understood at once to mean the house was awake, and arguing, and possibly ending.

"He's leaving," she said. "In the spring. The capital, or somewhere farther. He says a magistrate cannot preside over a town that has read itself truthfully." She smiled, and it was not a happy smile, but it was an honest one, and I have learned the difference matters more. "He asked — God help him, he actually asked — whether the atlas might be dedicated to the town's founders. As they were."

"As they were," I repeated.

"Yes. Flood and fire and all. He wants to be in it, Tamsin. Accurately. I think it's the only penance he can imagine — to be remembered correctly."

I looked at her a long moment, this woman who shares my origin and chose a different armor, who stole a copybook to keep two truths from ever touching and then read it aloud on the inquiry floor to end the war inside herself. Then I went to the shelf and took down the fair copy of the dedication page, still uninked, waiting.

"There's room for one line," I said. "Help me choose the words."

Outside, the river ran loud and free, and the last ice went out with it, and spring — real spring, the kind that comes after — was three days away.
