## 39. The Orchard in Bloom

The ground gave that morning the way it always does in late April — all at once, grudgingly, as if it had been holding its breath since November and could finally exhale. I stood at the orchard cottage gate with a spade over my shoulder and watched steam rise off the turned earth where Brida had been digging since dawn, and I thought: this is what soil remembers. Not words. Not ink. Warmth.

Eleven holes. We had measured them out the evening before with Ada's schoolroom string, pegged at even intervals along the south wall of the cottage garden, where the light came longest. Eleven holes for eleven trees, one for each parent of the laborers' row — the row that had no name on any map until we cut one into copper, the row that now existed twice in every atlas bound in Marlow Bend: once as absence, once as argument.

"You're standing there like a woman waiting for a funeral," Brida said, not looking up from her hole. Her hands were bare despite the cold; they were ferry hands, rope hands, hands that had spent forty years arguing with the river and winning often enough to keep going. "It's not a funeral."

"It's a memorial."

"A memorial is stone. This is planting." She drove the spade in and levered up a dark slab of earth, and the smell of it rose between us — wet loam, old leaves, the sweet rot of winters composting into something usable. "You don't mourn a tree, girl. You argue with it. You tell it how tall to grow and it ignores you, and twenty years on you stand under it in July and think, well. Perhaps it knew better."

I laughed, and the laugh surprised me. I had not laughed much since the inquiry. Grief, I was learning, did not leave all at once like ice out with the current; it drained slowly, finding low places in me I hadn't known were hollow.

* * *

They came through the gate one by one through the morning, and each arrival was its own small weather.

Ada Quill first, of course, punctual as a bell she'd rung herself for twenty years. She carried her slate wrapped in sacking against her chest like an infant, though the slate's work was done — its true names read aloud, copied into the record, argued over by Professor Maren's dry voice until they were no longer secret but simply *fact*. She unwrapped it anyway and set it on the garden wall, facing outward, so that anyone passing the lane might see eleven names chalked in a schoolmistress's careful hand. "Habits," she said, when I raised an eyebrow. "One does not stop keeping records because the danger has passed. One stops when the forgetting has passed, and that, Tamsin, will take longer than either of us."

Joss Renner came next, and Joss came sideways, the way he did everything — down the lane from the direction of the mill ruins, hands in pockets, scowling at the pegs and string as if they'd insulted him. He'd been back in the town five months and still walked it like a man casing a prison yard. But he carried saplings. Four of them, balanced across his shoulders, their roots balled in wet canvas, and he had walked eleven miles before dawn to the nursery at Hallow Ford to get stock good enough for the purpose, because — he said, dropping them by the wall harder than necessary — "if you're going to plant dead people, you don't plant them in scrub."

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. It's bad husbandry, thanking a man for doing a job right." But he wouldn't meet my eye, and his ears had gone red, and I understood that this was Joss's whole vocabulary of tenderness: complaints delivered at volume, gifts left where they couldn't be refused.

Brida's granddaughter came with the last of the water buckets. The alderman did not come at all — but a cart arrived mid-morning from Pike's own yard, driven by his gardener, loaded with manure and bone-meal and a note in Pike's small, ruled hand: *For the roots. P.* Two words and an initial. It was not retraction. It was not apology. It was a man who had signed a blank line once learning, very slowly, to put something truer in its place. Ada read the note and sniffed, but she spread the manure herself.

And Merrit —

Merrit did not come. That was the shape of the morning's one absence, and everyone felt it the way you feel a missing tooth. She had taken rooms above the draper's shop in town, estranged from her father's house since the day she stood on the inquiry floor with the copybook open in both hands and read our names — *her* name, the ninth child, Merrit Corven née nothing, born of the drowned row and given away to the magistrate's household as a ward, an act of charity so convenient it had functioned for thirty years as a kind of theft. Corven had not spoken to her since. Town said he took meals alone behind a closed door. Town said a great many things about Corven now, most of them quiet, all of them watchful.

But at noon, when we broke for bread and cheese on the cottage step, a boy came up the lane with a covered basket and would not say who sent it. Inside: seed potatoes, onion sets, a jar of honey, and a folded paper. On the paper, in a hand I knew too well to mistake, was drawn — not written, *drawn*, in surveyor's precision — a plan for the garden. Trellis positions. A drain line where the ground sloped toward the river. And in the corner, small, a sketch of an apple tree in bloom rendered so finely it could have come off one of my plates.

No signature. No word. Just the drawing, and the honey, and the knowledge that Merrit Corven could not walk through that gate yet but had measured its garden from memory and wanted it to feed people.

"She'll come," Brida said, taking the honey. "Not today. Maybe not this year. But she drew you a drainage line, girl. That's a woman planning to be here when the rains come."

* * *

We planted through the afternoon.

I had thought I knew what the ceremony of it would be — solemn, ordered, Ada perhaps reading the names, the rest of us bowing our heads. It was nothing of the sort. It was argument and laughter and dirt under every fingernail in the county. Joss quarreled with Brida about hole depth until Ada threatened to make them both write lines. The ferry-woman packed earth around her tree with a tenderness she showed no living creature, then stood and cursed the frost for a full minute on principle. Ada chalked each parent's name on the slate and propped it beside its hole while the sapling went in, so that for one afternoon every grave in Marlow Bend's unmarked graveyard was briefly, gloriously labeled.

I planted the seventh tree. Rowena Ashgrove — no. Not Ashgrove. Rowena Hale, lacemaker's daughter, drowned in the flood of '31 with her husband Daniel, leaving one child aged two years, carried across the ice by persons unknown, recorded thereafter under a false surname by a mapmaker drowning in guilt.

My mother. My father. My tree.

I knelt at the hole a long time with the sapling in my hands, its roots pale and naked in the light, and the others moved away down the row without being asked, because grief has a sound to it and they had all made that sound themselves. I thought of the initials in the cellar wall, eleven small carvings, and mine among them in a hand I didn't remember owning — *T.A.*, scratched by a two-year-old guided by older children, proof that I had belonged somewhere before anyone told me where. I thought of Hollis's letter, and the unfinished atlas as a test, and how I had passed it without knowing I was sitting the examination.

Then I set the roots in the earth the way Brida showed me — spread wide, never cramped, given room to become what they would — and I packed the soil down with my palms, and I said her name out loud to the ground. Rowena Hale. And then I said mine. Tamsin Hale Ashgrove. Both of them. All of them. The name I was assigned and the name I recovered and the name I am still becoming, planted together in one hole with one tree, because a person is not a corrected map. A person is both plates, bound.

When I stood, Joss was watching me from the end of the row, and something in his face had unclenched.

"They'll take," he said. It was not comfort. It was a craftsman's verdict on materials and conditions, and it was the kindest thing he had ever said to me.

* * *

Evening came down gold and slow. The others had gone — Ada home to her schoolroom, Joss walking Brida to the ferry landing, the granddaughter carrying the empty buckets — and I stayed to water the new trees, moving down the row with the can, saying nothing, needing to say nothing. Eleven small trees against the darkening sky, staked and tied, ridiculous and immortal. In twenty years they would be an orchard. In fifty, someone would sit beneath them eating apples and never know why they stood in that exact pattern, unless we had done our work — unless the atlas held, unless the second plate kept turning in readers' hands, unless remembering became ordinary instead of dangerous.

That was the thing no one tells you about truth. You do not win it once. You plant it, and stake it, and water it, and every spring you go out to see whether it survived the winter.

A figure stood at the gate.

Merrit. Dressed for town, gloves still on, as if she had set out meaning only to pass by and been stopped by her own feet. Behind her the lane ran silver toward the river and the lights of Marlow Bend, where her father's house sat dark behind its closed door.

"I saw the drawing arrived," she said.

"It did. The drain line was exactly right. We used it."

She looked past me at the row of trees, counting, and I watched her arrive at eleven and understand it — one for each parent, hers among them. Her mouth did something complicated. Thirty years of calling another woman *Father* had taught her face to hold still, but the trees undid her a little.

"Which one is mine?" she asked, barely above the wind.

"The fourth. Your mother grew flax, according to Ada's slate. I'm told the tree suits her." I hesitated, then stepped aside from the gate entirely, opening it wide. "There's a spade with your name on it whenever you want it. No ledger, no record, no affidavit required. Belonging here isn't granted, Merrit. It's just chosen. You already chose, on the inquiry floor. I'm only telling you the gate agrees."

She did not come in. She stood a moment longer, gloved hand on the gatepost, and then she reached out and touched the fourth sapling — one finger, brief, the way you touch something you're afraid will prove to be a dream — and turned back down the lane toward town.

At the bend in the road she stopped, and called back, her voice carrying clean across the water-meadow:

"Tamsin. The atlas. Everyone keeps asking me which map is true — the founders' or yours." A pause, and then, in a voice that was almost laughing: "Tell them neither. Tell them the truth is the binding."

Then she was gone, a silhouette shrinking down the silver lane, and I stood alone in the young orchard with her words ringing in me like a struck plate.

Because she was right, and I knew it, and I also knew it wasn't the ending. Somewhere in the workshop lay the fair copy of the dedication page, still uninked, waiting three days now for its single line. And tonight, watering eleven trees in the last light, I finally knew what it must say — and knew too that once inked and bound into every copy, it would go out beyond my hands forever, into parlors and libraries and years I would never see, to be read by strangers who would decide what my life's work meant.

Every map is an argument, Hollis had written in his letter. Tomorrow I would write the last line of ours.

I went inside to sharpen my pen.
