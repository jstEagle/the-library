## 40. Cartographer's Note

The workshop smelled of beeswax and cold iron, and for the first time in a year, of morning.

I opened the shutters at dawn because I wanted to see the light fall across the press the way it used to fall across Master Hollis's shoulders — slantwise, patient, the way light falls on a man who believes the day will be long enough. It would not have fallen on him today. He had been dead fourteen months, and the press had been broken and mended and broken again, and the town of Marlow Bend had learned, slowly and unwillingly, to say the words *laborers' row* out loud as though they had always known them.

I set the last bound atlas on the bench and turned to the final leaf. Blank paper. The only blank paper left in the whole enterprise, and I had saved it deliberately, the way a mason saves the keystone, because I had known for weeks that the book was not finished — that it could not be finished — until I wrote the page that explained all the others.

A cartographer's note. Hollis would have called it presumptuous. He believed maps should speak in lines and elevations, that a map which needed explaining had failed at its one task. But Hollis had also razored a page out of a parish register and sealed a letter to a child he could not bring himself to name, and I had learned from him — from his silence more than his instruction — that the honest map is never the whole map. The whole map does not exist. What exists is the argument, and the hands that made it, and the price those hands paid.

I dipped my pen. My right hand had healed poorly from the engraving weeks — the middle finger still stiffened in cold weather, and it was cold this morning, the last cold of a spring that had come late and grudging — but the stiffness was a kind of signature too, and I did not resent it. I had earned every crooked joint I carried.

*To the Reader of this Atlas,* I wrote.

And then I stopped, because the first sentence of a thing is a door, and I wanted to open it properly.

Below me, in the yard, Brida was arguing with the ferry rope. I could hear her voice through the floorboards, low and scolding, the same tone she had used on the ice the day she told me to ask about the winter of '31 and then decide if I wanted the answer. She had never decided for me. Not once in all the months since had any of them decided for me — not Brida, not Ada with her slate of drowned names, not Joss with his scarred hands and his terrible patience, not even Merrit, who had betrayed her father on the inquiry floor and then refused, with a pride I came to admire, to let me call it a gift. They had laid the truth out like tools on a bench and let me choose which to pick up. That was the whole of what a family was, I understood now. Not the people who tell you who you are. The people who leave the tools where you can reach them.

I wrote:

*To the Reader of this Atlas:*

*You will find two maps of Marlow Bend in this volume. The first shows the town as its founding families have recorded it for sixteen years. The second shows the town as it stood on the night of the ninth of January, 1831, before the ice dam broke and the mill burned and eleven children of the laborers' row were carried away and renamed.*

*Both maps are accurate. That is the first thing you must understand, and the hardest. The first map is accurate to what the town wished to be. The second is accurate to what the town was. I have bound them together because I have come to believe that a map which shows only one is not a map at all. It is a verdict.*

*Every map is an argument. This I learned too late for my master, who taught me that paper is neutral, that ink records and never changes the world. He was wrong, and he knew he was wrong, and the proof is in your hands. Someone drew the laborers' row off these plates before I was old enough to hold a graver. Someone decided that a flood and a fire and eleven orphaned children were obstacles to a land claim, and the way you remove a thing from the world — the only reliable way — is to remove it from the record. The world forgets what the paper does not say. I have watched it happen. I was one of the things it happened to.*

*My name, on the title page of this atlas, is Tamsin Ashgrove. That is the name I was given at seven years old by a mapmaker who took me in out of guilt and love in equal measure and never told me which was which, because he could not have answered the question himself. It is a good name. I do not return it.*

*But it is not the whole of my name. The whole of my name is this: Tamsin Ashgrove, called in the winter of 1831 Tamsin Row, daughter of Eliza Row, laundress of the laborers' row, drowned in the flood of the ninth of January, and of a father whose name is on Ada Quill's slate and nowhere else in the world. I was the eleventh child. There were ten others. Their names are engraved on the second plate of this atlas, and I will not set them down here a second time, because a name engraved in copper outlives a name written in ink, and I have spent enough of my life on things that wash out.*

I set down the pen. My hand was shaking, not from the cold.

It is a strange thing, to write your own name and have it be an act of cartography. All winter I had thought of myself as the engraver — the one who stood apart from the map and cut the world into it. But the second plate had undone that. You cannot engrave your own childhood onto a plate and remain the engraver. Somewhere between the first line of the flood contour and the last letter of Eliza Row's name, I had become territory. I had been mapped. And the strange and terrible freedom of it was this: a territory can be surveyed honestly or dishonestly, but it cannot be unmade. Whatever they had taken from me — the smoke, the singing, the woman's arms on the ice — they had not taken the ground itself. The ground remembered. The wall in the orchard cellar remembered. Eleven small sets of initials cut into brick by children's hands, and mine among them, in a hand I recognized as my own the moment I saw it, the way you recognize your own reflection in dark water: with a jolt, and then with a strange calm, because of course it is you. It was always going to be you.

* * *

Ada came at midmorning with bread and a slate under her arm, out of habit, though there was nothing left to hide on it. She set the bread down, read over my shoulder the way she had once read over the shoulders of children she was not supposed to be teaching, and was quiet a long time.

"You've written *argument*," she said. "Corven will hate that word."

"Corven is gone to his club to explain his withdrawal from the inquiry," I said. "He can hate it in comfort."

"He'll hate it longer than that. Words outlive magistrates." She touched the page, just below the line where I had written *verdict*. "You know what I keep thinking? Twenty years I kept that slate. Twenty years, and I told myself I was keeping it for the children — so they'd know their parents' names someday. But it wasn't only that. I kept it because I could not bear for the paper to be on their side. You understand? The registers were on their side. The deeds were on their side. Every document in this town was a door closed in my face, and the slate was the one door I held open, and I held it alone, and I would have held it until I died if you hadn't come along."

"You didn't hold it alone," I said. "Brida held the ferry. Hollis held the letter. Joss held—" I stopped, because what Joss had held for nine years in the penal colony was a rage that had nearly killed him twice, and it was not mine to soften. "Everyone held something. That's the finding of this whole inquiry, if it has one. They couldn't erase the people. They could only erase the paper. And people keep paper. It's the one thing they couldn't stop us doing."

Ada looked at me with her schoolmistress's face — the one that means *you have said something true and I am going to make you say it again, better, in front of the class* — and then she only nodded and cut the bread, and we ate standing up like laborers, which is what half of us were, once.

* * *

In the afternoon, Merrit came.

She came the way she had come all spring: at odd hours, without knocking, with her father's carriage left at the corner so the town would not see it and her father would not know. She stood in the doorway with the copybook in her hands — the child's copybook, eleven names, ten struck through and one initial standing, *T.A.*, the mirror I had not been ready to look into and had looked into anyway — and she turned it over and over as though the answers were on the back cover.

"Professor Maren's steamer left this morning," she said. "Elias saw it off. She took depositions from Brida and Ada and Joss, and the plates, and the slate. She said—" Merrit's mouth did something complicated. "She said the second plate would be cited in the capital's survey for the next fifty years. She said, *this is how it is done.*"

I had heard it too. I had stood on the dock and heard the dry woman with the capital accent say *this is how it is done*, and I had felt nothing I could name — not triumph, not relief, something more like a joint setting back into place after a long dislocation, painful and correct at once.

"You could have gone with her," Merrit said. "She asked. Elias told me she asked you to come as her engraver. The capital. A real press. A name."

"I have a name," I said. "I spent a year finding it. I'm not giving it to a city."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." I took the copybook from her hands, gently, and set it open on the bench between us, to the page with the eleven names. "Look at it. Really look. Ten of us scattered like seed, and every one of us grew into whatever soil we landed in. A schoolmistress. A ferrywoman. A convict. An alderman. A magistrate's ward. And me, a mapmaker's ghost. You know what we have in common, Merrit? Not the row. Not the flood. We all learned the same lesson before we were eight years old: that the story you are told about yourself is a map drawn by someone else, and you are allowed to redraw it. Pike drew himself rich. Joss drew himself hard. You drew yourself a Corven. I drew myself an Ashgrove. None of the drawings were lies, exactly. They were arguments. And now the evidence is in, and we all have to live in the corrected edition."

Merrit looked at the names a long time. "I still can't say mine," she said. "The ninth name. I read it out loud on the inquiry floor and it was like reading a stranger's debt."

"You don't have to say it."

"Then what am I supposed to do with it?"

"Plant something on it," I said. "That's what I'm doing. Come in the spring and see."

* * *

I finished the note at lamplight, with the furnace ticking and the river running loud and free beyond the levee, the ice gone three weeks now, the water carrying boats and news and the first cargo of the open season. The last lines went down clean, without crossing-out, which for me is a kind of miracle and which I attribute to the fact that I had stopped trying to write the truth and had started, finally, simply writing.

*A final word on why two maps.*

*You may ask which map is the town. I will tell you what I have come to believe, as a cartographer of nineteen years' training and one year's hard education. The town is the argument. The town is what it argues about. Marlow Bend argued, for sixteen years, that the laborers' row had never stood, and for sixteen years that argument was the town, and it was built on drowned ground and it knew it. Now the town argues something else, and the argument is not finished — it will not be finished in my lifetime — because eleven people remember, and their children will remember differently, and their children's children will argue again, and every generation will redraw the map, and the map will be wrong, and then it will be corrected, and that correction is the only honest thing a map can hope to be.*

*I used to believe that finishing my master's atlas was a duty owed to the dead. It was not. It was a question the dead had left for the living, and the question was this: when you find that the record has been cut with a razor, will you cut it back? I have cut it back. It cost the workshop its lease and Brida her license and Ada her post and Joss his peace and Merrit her father, and I would pay all of it again, and I am not certain that is virtue. I only know that the alternative was to live inside their map, in a town without tragedy, in a cottage with no cellar, as a child with no before — and I have lived there, and I am telling you it is a small country, and the borders are guarded, and no one who lives there is ever allowed to grieve.*

*Grieve. That is the word the first map left out. A map without a flood line is a map that forbids mourning, and a town that forbids mourning is a town of ghosts pretending to be living men. I have engraved the flood line. Mourn if you need to. The paper will hold it.*

*This atlas is published with both maps or it is not published at all. That was the ruling of Professor Ilsa Maren, and I have never been so grateful to a stranger in my life, and I hope never to see her again, because the best arbitrators are the ones you hope never to require.*

*The plates are in the capital now, and the book is in the shops, and it is no longer mine. That is what publication means, I have learned. You carve the truth into copper, and then the copper belongs to everyone, including the people who would rather melt it down. Some will tear out the second plate. Some will frame it. I cannot control what they do, and I have stopped wanting to. A map is an argument, and an argument belongs to whoever picks it up.*

*History happened to me. I was seven years old and the water took my mother and the town took my name, and for twenty years I did not know it, and the not-knowing was the cruelest part, because you cannot answer a question you have never been asked. But I have been asked now. And I have answered — not with testimony only, though I gave it, and not with tears, though there were those — but with a graver, and a plate, and a book.*

*That is my answer. It is the only kind I know how to give. I am a cartographer. When the world is misdrawn, I redraw it.*

*Signed on the last day of April, in the year 1848, in the workshop above the river at Marlow Bend, in the presence of witnesses who need not be named here because their names are already engraved in copper, which is better:*

*Tamsin Ashgrove,*
*called Tamsin Row,*
*daughter of Eliza Row,*
*the eleventh child of the laborers' row,*
*engraver of this atlas,*
*and of the other one too.*

I blotted the page and read it through once, standing, the way Hollis had always read his proofs — aloud, in a low voice, to hear where the sentences caught. None caught. I sewed the leaf into the last copy myself, four stitches through the gathering, the needle pulling the thread with the small singing resistance I have loved since I was nine years old and allowed, for the first time, to hold a book together with my own two hands.

Then I carried it to the window and set it on the sill, open to the note, and let the last light of the day lie across the page.

Below, in the orchard cottage garden, the eleven trees stood in their new-planted rows — one for every parent, eleven trees for eleven names, Joss's tree already stubbornly budding out of spite, Brida's leaning the way she leaned, toward the river. Merrit had said she would come at planting and had not come, and had come anyway, late, in a plain dress, and had stood at the fence until Ada went and fetched her in. That was Merrit. The map of her was still being argued, and would be for years, and I had stopped needing it settled.

The river ran. The light went gold, then grey, then out. Somewhere in the town, in a shop or a parlor or a schoolroom, someone was opening the atlas for the first time, turning past the official map, past the flood line, past the eleven names, and arriving — as every reader arrives, as I arrived, as you have arrived — at the blank page that is never blank, and reading the argument, and deciding.

That was the whole of it. That was all I had ever wanted, though I had not known the shape of the want until it was filled: not to be believed, not to be forgiven, not even to be remembered. To be answered. To have the question asked — *who are you?* — and to put down, in ink that will outlast me, in a hand that is my own:

*Here is the map. Here is the other map. I was here. I drew it.*

I closed the shutters against the dark and left the atlas on the sill until morning, because the morning should be the first thing to read it.

And in the morning — this I will set down as the last true thing, the last entry in the record, though no plate will ever hold it — in the morning, the first tree in the orchard came into bloom.
