## 1. The Last Road Out

The pass had a name once, Coral supposed, though no map she carried bothered to print it. On the republic's surveys it was marked only as *Route 9, seasonal, unmaintained above the treeline*, and that was as good a name as any for a road the world had agreed to forget until spring. She had been walking it since before dawn, and the sun was still only a rumor caught in the ice-glaze on the eastern crags when she stopped to count again.

Nine days. Perhaps ten, if the weather turned.

She counted the way other people breathed — without deciding to, without being able to stop. Nine days until the first hard freeze closed the river at Tamsin's Ford and the ferryman tied up his boat for the winter. Ten days until the bridge timbers came down, if they hadn't already; the mountain towns pulled their crossings apart early now, ever since the war ate the timber convoys and everyone learned that a bridge was an invitation written in wood. After that there would be nothing between the Ford and the lowlands but rock, white water, and four months of snow.

And after that, whatever answer she carried would be worth exactly nothing to anyone.

Coral flexed her left hand inside its glove. The chain gave a small cold whisper against her wrist bone, the sound it made every time she moved, so familiar now that silence itself felt like a warning. The dispatch case rode against her ribs under her coat, a flat leather slab the size of a prayer book, and it weighed what all such cases weighed, which was more than iron. Three documents inside, sealed in wax and cipher. One alliance, one set of terms, one last chance for a republic that had been dying politely for two years and was now dying quickly instead.

*You are not a person,* Sef had told her, fifteen years ago, in a courtyard smelling of wet stone and pigeon droppings, when she was nineteen and still flinched at raised voices. *You are a channel. Water doesn't remember the shape of the pipe.*

She had gotten very good at forgetting the shape of the pipe. She had gotten so good at it that sometimes, alone on a road like this one, she would try to recall what she herself wanted — not the mission, not the route, not the cover — and find the question dissolving in her hands like snow held too long.

Below her, through a ragged seam in the cloud, the valley opened.

Tamsin's Ford sat where the river bent north around the shoulder of Mount Corren, a scatter of slate roofs and woodsmoke beside a wide gray water. Even from this height — even with the wind peeling tears from her eyes — Coral could see the town was wrong in ways that interested her. Too many chimneys for its size. A millrace that fed not one wheel but three. And along the near bank, long low buildings with windows spaced like teeth, glowing faintly amber in the last of the morning: workshops, working by lamplight at midmorning, which meant either prosperity or desperation, and in her experience the two wore the same coat.

She did not go down toward it. Not yet. Instead she turned off the track into a fold of granite screened by wind-bent pines, shrugged out of her pack, and went through the ritual that kept her alive.

First, the back trail. She knelt and studied the path she'd broken across the last stretch of scree — frost had already softened her prints, which was good, but frost also meant anyone following would be walking on the same blank page, and she couldn't read it any better than they could. She listened. Wind in the pines, the creak of ice expanding somewhere below, a bird — some kind of chough, she thought — ticking indignantly overhead. Nothing that sounded like boots.

Second, the self. She unpinned her hair and rebraided it duller, tucking the bright strand of gray she'd earned at thirty-one back under the plait where it wouldn't catch light. She rubbed a little ash from her cookfire into the collar of her coat, dulled the brass buttons with grease, and smeared mud along the hem where a careful woman would have shaken it off. Her traveling papers said Marta Ilves, widow, seamstress, going up-country to winter with her late husband's cousins. The papers were excellent. Sef had taught her that the best papers were ninety percent true — Marta Ilves had existed, had died childless in a cholera summer, and dead women made the finest travel companions because they never contradicted you and no one missed them.

Third, the case. She checked the chain, checked the seals beneath her shirt, checked the false bottom of her pack where a folded letter from her sister lay — the one true document on her body, and therefore the most dangerous. She had considered leaving it behind in Vessany City. She had not been able to. That, she suspected, was the kind of fact about herself she was better off not examining.

Then she ate. Half a heel of black bread, a smear of rendered fat, three dried apples counted out like coins. She chewed slowly, watching the town, because eating was the hour when careless couriers got found, sitting with their backs to a trail and their eyes on their own hands. Her eyes stayed on the track below, and on the road beyond it, switchbacking down out of the pass toward the river — empty, white-rimmed, silent.

Empty roads were good. Empty roads were what she paid for. But an empty road nine days before the closing of the season also meant that anyone else coming up this track had come deliberately, and Coral had spent enough years in the republic's service to know that in the ninth week of a collapsing autumn, deliberate travelers were rarely bringing good news to anyone.

* * *

She reached the outskirts as the light went thin and pewter-colored, the short afternoon already leaning into evening. The town announced itself gradually: first a boundary stone, older than the republic, carved with a symbol she didn't recognize — a stylized bridge over a river, or perhaps a yoke, depending on how you looked at it. Then a fence of split chestnut, mended in a dozen places with wood that didn't match, each repair neat as a sentence corrected by a schoolmaster. Then the smell.

It stopped her for a moment, that smell. Sawdust and linseed oil and hot glue and woodsmoke, layered over the green cold smell of the river, and underneath it all something sweeter — curing pine, maybe, or beeswax. It smelled, absurdly, of *making*. Coral had spent the last six weeks passing through villages that smelled of ash and fear and unswept chimneys, villages where the men had gone to war or fled conscription and the fields stood in stubble like unshaved chins. This place smelled like someone intended to be here next year.

A boy of perhaps twelve was stacking cordwood outside the first house, and he watched her come up the lane with the frank, unhurried stare of a child who has never been taught to be afraid of strangers, which told her something, and then called over his shoulder in a carrying voice, "Mama! Traveler!" — which told her something else. In the border villages, children had learned not to announce arrivals. Announcements got people taken.

"Marta Ilves," she said, when the mother appeared in the doorway wiping flour from her hands — a broad, tired-faced woman with sawdust in her braid, because apparently everyone here breathed the same air. "Seamstress. I'm looking for my husband's people. Ilves. They're said to winter at the Ford."

"No Ilveses." The woman said it without apology and without suspicion, which was almost worse. Suspicion she knew how to work with. "There's a Vant family does tailoring-adjacent work — awnings, sailcloth, heavy canvas. You might ask at the workshop hall. Council meets evenings." She paused, and her eyes did a quick professional inventory — boots, pack, hands, face — the same inventory Coral performed on everyone, and it was unsettling to be on the receiving end of it, like hearing your own voice played back. "You'll want lodging. There's the ferry-house, but the ferryman's drinking his own stock this time of year. Or the common room at the joiners' hall takes coin."

Coin. Coral had coin, republican scrip that was worth less every week, and a small store of honest silver sewn into her belt. She thanked the woman and moved on, and behind her heard the boy say, not quietly enough, "Her boots are army boots," and the mother say, "Half the world's boots are army boots now, love," and Coral filed both sentences away where she filed everything, in the cold archive at the back of her skull that never slept and never forgave.

*Army boots.* Sloppy. She should have dyed them. Exhaustion making sloppiness, and sloppiness making graves — Sef's third lesson, delivered the day she'd nearly walked a checkpoint with a courier's stride, shoulders square, pace metronomic. *Walk like your feet hurt, girl. Everyone's feet hurt. Nobody remembers a woman whose feet hurt.*

Her feet hurt now. That, at least, required no performance.

* * *

The joiners' hall stood at the center of the town like a heart, if a heart could be made of mortised oak and lit with forty windows. Coral circled it twice before going in — once along the riverbank, counting boats (two dories hauled up, one flat ferry barge chained to a mooring post, and a rope-ferry platform half-dismantled already, its planks stacked and numbered in chalk), and once along the back, counting doors (four, plus a loading bay, plus a cellar grate she could probably fit through and definitely shouldn't think about fitting through unless it became necessary). Old habits. The pipes must know their shapes.

Inside, the hall was warm and loud with industry. Long benches ran its length under hanging lamps, and at them worked perhaps thirty people of every age from graybeards to a girl no older than eight who was sweeping shavings with the solemnity of a high priestess. The noise was extraordinary — planes hissing, mallets knocking, someone singing a counting song in a language Coral didn't know — and threaded through all of it ran a rhythm she recognized with a start: the whole room worked in time, unconsciously, like oarsmen. She had seen that rhythm before only once, in a printworks in the capital running illegal pamphlets the night before a raid, and those workers had been terrified. These people were simply working.

"Looking for someone?" The voice came from her elbow, and Coral controlled her flinch — barely, and hated that there had been a flinch to control. The speaker was a tall woman in her fifties with forearms like dock rope and gray going to white at the temples, a marking knife tucked behind one ear like a carpenter's pencil. Her eyes were the pale, patient gray of weathered oak, and they went over Coral with the same unhurried completeness the flour-woman's had, but slower, and worse, the way a surveyor reads land.

"Traveler," Coral said, giving the truth because it was the least memorable lie available. "Looking for lodging, and for family. Ilves."

"No Ilveses," the woman said. Same words, different weight. Where the flour-woman had been ending a conversation, this one seemed to be beginning one. "I'm Odile Vant. I keep the hall." She didn't offer a hand; her hands were full of a half-assembled chair back, spokeshave resting in the crook of her arm. "Where from?"

"Downriver. Ashford way." A real town, burned in the second year of the war, which made it conveniently hard to verify anything about it.

"Ashford." Odile Vant said the name the way people say the names of the dead. "We took three families from Ashford two winters back. Good hands among them." She set the chair back down on the bench and looked at Coral directly, and Coral felt the strange sensation, familiar and always unwelcome, of being *read* — of standing in front of someone whose trade was understanding what things were made of and whether they would hold. "You look like a woman who's walked a long way on short rations. Common room's through there. Supper's at the second bell, and it's a copper, and nobody eats free because free is how debts start ugly. You can pay it?"

"I can pay it."

"Good." Something in Odile's face eased, very slightly, as though the correct answer had confirmed a hypothesis she'd been sorry to hold. "One thing, Marta Ilves." She picked the spokeshave back up, and the blade caught lamplight along its edge, bright as a held breath. "Whatever it is you're carrying — and everyone who comes up that pass this late is carrying something — it stays yours while you're under this roof. We don't open letters, we don't search packs, we don't ask after papers. Those are the Regiment's rules. But the second rule under the first rule is that whatever a guest carries must not bring soldiers to our door. So." The pale eyes didn't move. "If what you carry is the kind of thing that brings soldiers, you'll understand why I mention it now, while it's cheap to mention."

The hall's noise went on around them — planes hissing, mallets knocking, the child singing her counting song — and Coral stood in the middle of it with the case pressing its flat weight against her ribs and the chain whispering its tiny cold note against her wrist, and she smiled the smile of a tired widow whose feet hurt, and said:

"Madam, the only thing I'm carrying is a change of clothes and my grief. If soldiers come for either, I'll hand them over myself."

Odile Vant looked at her for three seconds longer than comfort allowed. Then she nodded, once, as a judge nods when evidence is admitted, and said, "Second bell, then," and turned back to her chair.

Coral walked to the common room on legs that ached honestly, sat with her back to the wall out of habit she no longer questioned, and counted again, because counting was the only prayer she had left.

Nine days to the closing of the river. Three documents against her ribs. One town that smelled of next year, full of people who read strangers like timber and kept rules like scripture, and one formidable woman who had just told her, in the politest possible language, *I will know what you are before you leave this valley.*

Through the hall's high windows, far downstream where the river ran dark between the mountains, Coral thought she saw — for only a moment, before the clouds sealed the view away — a light moving on the water. A lantern, low on a hull, riding upstream against the current.

The last ferry of the season, she told herself, coming up before the freeze. Boats did that. Boats had schedules, and passengers, and innocent cargo.

She told herself that, and ordered supper, and did not believe a word of it.
