## 2. A Town That Keeps Its Own Books

The road down from the pass was the kind of road that made a person grateful for every switchback, because each one meant the river was closer and the wind a little less interested in killing her. Coral came around the shoulder of the mountain in the gray of late afternoon and saw Tamsin's Ford for the first time: a scatter of slate roofs cupped in the valley's palm, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, the river running broad and quick below it all with a wooden bridge strung across like a stitch.

She stopped where the trees thinned and let herself look, which was itself a discipline — Sef had taught her that too. *See a place whole before you see it as an obstacle.* A town was a terrain like any other. There would be ways in and ways out, doors that locked from one side and not the other, eyes that watched the road and eyes that never did. She catalogued what she could from this distance: the bridge, obviously, spanning the river at its narrowest point; a second crossing downstream where the water spread shallow over gravel — the ford itself, then, the thing the place was named for, usable perhaps in summer and suicide in autumn; a mill with its wheel turning slow; and on the near slope, larger than any building had a right to be, a long-roofed structure with open bays along its flank, from which came a sound she felt through her boots before she heard it properly. Rhythmic knocking. Dozens of mallets, not quite together, like rain on a roof.

A workshop town, then. She had passed through three on her way north and knew their general shape: insular, proud of their craft, indifferent to borders because borders changed and joinery did not. Good. Indifference could be worn like a cloak over anyone's shoulders if you cut it to fit.

The dispatch case rode against her ribs under her coat, strapped where a satchel might plausibly sit, and the chain — she touched it once through the wool, confirming what she already knew, that it was there, that it had been there for eleven days — ran from the case's hidden ring to a cuff beneath her left sleeve. To anyone watching, she was a woman with a bag. Only she knew she was a woman chained to her work.

She walked the last mile with the gait she had chosen for herself two passes ago: tired but unhurried, a traveler at the end of a long road who wanted nothing more than soup and a bed and to be forgotten by morning. It was not entirely a performance. Her feet ached genuinely. Her shoulders carried genuine weight. The best disguises, Sef said, were built on true foundations — lie with the material you have.

* * *

The first building inside the town's edge was a bakehouse, and the smell of it nearly undid her. Bread and woodsmoke and, beneath both, something resinous — pine pitch, cut timber, the sweet dust of sawing. A woman was scrubbing the bakehouse step with such vigor that Coral half expected the stone to apologize. The woman looked up.

"Road's closed behind you, I'd wager," the woman said. Not unkindly. Assessingly.

"So I've heard." Coral let her voice come out roughened, a little flat, the accent of the central provinces softened by distance. "I heard it from the drover at the last farm. He seemed pleased about it."

"He would be. Fewer sheep going south means better prices staying north." The woman stood, wringing her rag. "You'll be wanting the Ford Inn, then. Or—" and here something flickered across her face, a small recalculation, "—or the workshop hall, if you've a trade. We house travelers with trades."

"I'm a seamstress," Coral said. It was the cover she had worn since the pass, sewn into her papers with real thread: Maren Ellery, seamstress, late of Vessany's middle districts, traveling to her sister's family before winter shut everything. The papers were excellent. Sef had taught her that forgery was mostly patience — age the paper with tea, wear the fold lines with your thumb, get the ink right, and above all make the boring parts boring. Nobody forged an interesting life and got away with it.

"A seamstress." The baker looked at her hands. Coral kept them loose and visible, calloused at the fingertips where a needle leaves its mark — she had given herself those calluses years ago, patiently, with a real needle and real cloth, because Sef had insisted that a cover story lived or died in the details a body couldn't help telling. "Well. Hands that have worked, anyway. Hall's up past the mill, the long roof you can hear from here. Ask for Odile Vant, and tell her Petra sent you, though mind she'll pretend that means less than it does."

"Thank you," Coral said, and meant it, and filed the name away: *Petra, bakehouse, garrulous, watches the road, sends people toward the council.* In a republic town the baker would have sent her to the magistrate. Here they sent her to a woodworker. Interesting.

The main street ran along the contour of the valley, cobbled in places and packed earth in others, lined with buildings that had been repaired rather than replaced — she noticed patch after careful patch, new timbers scarfed into old beams, shingles of slightly different ages overlapping like feathers. Nothing wasted. Everything maintained. And everywhere, nailed to doorposts and lintels and the corner posts of fences, small brass plaques no bigger than a hand, each engraved with a number and a word. She read them as she passed without breaking stride, the way Sef had drilled into her until reading became breathing: *4 — Looms. 11 — Coopers. 17 — Wheelwrights & Carters. 23 — Dye House.* Numbers and trades. She understood within a street's length what she was looking at: the town had registered itself. Every workshop numbered, every trade declared, a census written in brass for anyone to consult.

And anyone consulting it would learn exactly how many people here could make things worth taking, and exactly where they slept.

It was either remarkable openness or remarkable confidence, and Coral suspected the latter, and found it unsettling in a way she couldn't immediately name. Towns that hid their wealth expected thieves. This town hung its inventory on its doors like a challenge.

Children ran past her carrying armloads of offcuts, shouting about something to do with the kiln. An old man sat on a bench outside the coopers', sanding a stave with the serenity of a monk, and nodded to her as she went by — a nod that included her, that assumed she belonged to the day's ordinary traffic. No one asked her business. No one followed her. She counted three alleys between the bakehouse and the mill, noted which ones dead-ended and which connected through, marked the position of the well, the position of the watch-house — except the building she took for a watch-house turned out to hold, judging by the sign, the town's lending library, open evenings, and she had to revise her map on the spot.

There was no watch-house. She walked the length of the high street twice and found no watch-house, no garrison, no office bearing any republic's seal. Just the plaques, the workshops, the smoke, and somewhere ahead the ceaseless patient knocking of mallets.

*You are being watched*, she reminded herself. *You simply can't see by whom.* A town with no soldiers was not a town with no order. Order here was somewhere else, wearing an apron.

* * *

The workshop hall swallowed sound the way a cathedral swallows whispers. Inside, the long roof rose over a floor the size of a threshing barn, and the floor was alive: thirty benches in ranked rows, and at each bench a worker bent to some stage of making, and the air itself was thick with a golden haze of sawdust that caught the light from the clerestory windows and held it suspended, so the whole space seemed lit from within, underwater, timeless. The mallet-knocking she'd heard on the road resolved into its components — chisels driven by mallets, tenon after mortise after tenon, and between the blows the rasp of planes and the hiss of drawknives and underneath it all the deep voice of the great rip-saw singing in its pit at the far end.

Coral stood just inside the door and let the noise cover her while she counted exits. Main doors, double, behind her. Two smaller doors flanking the sawpit, likely to the yard. A loft running the length of the east wall, reached by a stair — and at the loft's rail, a rope-and-pulley arrangement for hoisting stock, which meant a hoist door in the gable, which meant a drop of twenty feet to soft ground. Four exits, five counting the hoist. Windows too high and narrow to matter. She had the shape of the room memorized before she had taken her fourth breath in it.

"You'll want to wipe your boots," said a voice near her elbow, "but honestly it hardly matters, the floor eats everything."

Coral turned. The speaker was a girl of perhaps sixteen, sawdust in her dark braid, a marking gauge in one hand and flour — actual flour, from the bakehouse, still on her sleeve — and she was looking at Coral with the frank, total attention of someone too young to have learned discretion. "You're the seamstress. Petra sent you. Everyone already knows." She said this not as gossip but as weather report. "I'm Tamsin. Odile's my mother. Well — my foster-mother, which is a longer story than you want at the end of a road. You look like the end of a road."

"I look like a seamstress," Coral said mildly.

"You look like someone who's been sleeping in ditches and calling them inns." The girl — Tamsin — tilted her head. "Your seams are good, though. Whoever taught you didn't waste time." And before Coral could decide whether that was compliment or probe, Tamsin had raised her voice across the din: "MOTHER. The traveler. The one Petra sent."

What came toward them out of the golden haze came with the unhurried certainty of a tide. Odile Vant was not tall, but she occupied space the way load-bearing walls do. She wore a leather apron gone white at the edges from years of sharpening, her forearms bare and scarred along a map of old nicks, and her eyes — gray, level, set in a face weathered to the color of oiled walnut — performed the same survey of Coral that Coral had performed of the town, except faster, and Coral strongly suspected, more accurately. Master joiner. Councilor. The formidable one. Coral had known the type in every district of the republic: the person whose approval was currency and whose disapproval was architecture.

"Maren Ellery," Coral offered, before being asked, and extended the papers.

Odile did not take them. She looked at them — at Coral holding them — for a long moment, then said, "Put those away. If I need to know your grandmother's parish I'll ask." She turned to Tamsin. "Is the guest room aired?"

"It will be in an hour."

"It will be in half of one. Go." Tamsin went, but backward, unwilling to stop watching, and Coral felt the girl's gaze on her all the way to the stair. Odile turned back. "Seamstress."

"Yes, mistress."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"'Yes, mistress.'" Odile's voice wasn't sharp; it was precise, like a plane set fine. "Nobody in this hall is anybody's mistress. We're the Regiment. You'll have heard the word and thought of soldiers, and you shouldn't. A regiment is a body that holds formation when it would be easier to break. That's all we've ever meant by it. Now — Petra says you've a trade, so you'll eat at the common table and pay in labor or coin, whichever you have more of. Which is it?"

"Labor," Coral said truthfully, and then, because the silence invited it and because a harmless traveler volunteers harmless facts, added, "Coin's short. The roads ate it."

"They usually do." Odile studied her a moment longer, and Coral endured the study the way she'd learned to endure all scrutiny: by becoming slightly boring. Let the eyes slide off. Be the woman who is exactly what she appears, whose story has no hooks in it, whom you will forget by supper. "Can you sew sailcloth? Canvas? Heavy work?"

"I can sew anything that doesn't argue."

Something moved at the corner of Odile's mouth — not quite a smile, but the ground where one might build. "Good. The ferry awnings need re-stitching before the freeze, and everyone who claims to sew in this town is secretly a joiner who hates needles." She jerked her chin toward the benches. "You'll start tomorrow. Tonight you eat, you sleep, and you don't wander the hall after hours — we keep our own books here, and I'd rather you weren't a line in the incident column."

"Of course."

"And Maren." Odile had already turned away, but she paused, half in profile against the sawdust light, and said it without heat, the way one remarks that rain is coming: "Whatever you're carrying under your coat — and I'm not asking what it is — this town has survived four armies, two famines, and one extremely determined tax assessor by knowing exactly what crosses our threshold. So whatever it is, decide soon whether it makes you a guest or a problem. We feed guests. Problems we handle differently." She resumed walking, apron strings swinging, already calling measurements to someone at the far benches, and the great room absorbed her back into its rhythm as though she had never stopped.

Coral stood alone among the noise and the gold-lit dust, and felt, distinctly, the case against her ribs seem suddenly loud — absurdly, impossibly loud, as if the whole hall had gone quiet just to listen for it.

*Decide soon whether it makes you a guest or a problem.*

Her heart was doing something quiet and traitorous. Because Odile Vant had looked once at a stranger's coat and seen the strap, or the stiffness, or simply the way Coral held her left arm a half-inch too carefully — fifteen years of training, and this woman had read her in the time it took to decline a document — and had said nothing, demanded nothing, only drawn a line in the sawdust and waited to see which side of it Coral would stand on.

Nobody in the republic would have done that. The republic would have arrested her, or used her, or both.

This town fed its guests and handled its problems, and had looked at her and declined, for now, to say which she was.

Coral breathed the sweet dust and thought: *Exits. Five of them. Bridge closes soon. Count the days.* But beneath the counting, uninvited and unwelcome, another thought surfaced, small and warm and dangerous as a coal dropped in shavings:

*Nobody here has lied to me yet.*

She found the common table by following the smell of onion and barley, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only hunger. Outside, far off toward the pass, the light was failing earlier than it should have, and the wind had turned, and somewhere in the bones of the mountains the first snow of the season was deciding whether to come early.
