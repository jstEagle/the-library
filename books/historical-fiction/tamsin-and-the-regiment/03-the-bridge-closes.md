## 3. The Bridge Closes

The snow began an hour before dawn, and Coral knew it before she opened her eyes, because the sound of the town had changed. Snowfall did not fall so much as it *settled*, a soft hushing over the rooftops of Tamsin's Ford, swallowing the creak of the workshop shutters, the distant ring of someone's hammer, the ordinary morning argument between a dog and a cart wheel. She lay in the narrow bed of the ferry-house loft — the ferryman's wife had taken her coin readily enough for two nights, and asked nothing, which Coral had noted as either courtesy or habit — and listened to the world being muffled, and felt the first cold thread of something that was not quite fear.

Fear could wait. Fear was a luxury, like grief. She had learned that from Sef, in another country, in another life: *Count what you have, girl, before you count what you've lost.* So she counted. She had a room, a roof, a body that ached from the pass but functioned. She had coin enough for a month, if she was careful, and she was always careful. She had the case, chained to her wrist, warm from her own skin, holding the one thing in the world that mattered to anyone but herself.

And she had, by her own reckoning, eleven days. Eleven days until the seasonal crossing closed — the ferryman had told her so the first evening, a broad, wind-burnt man named Hask with hands like root vegetables, who had said it cheerfully, the way men say things they have said a thousand times. *Eleven days, maybe twelve if the weather holds. After that the ice takes the river and nobody crosses till thaw, and the thaw's late some years.* He had laughed when he said it. She had smiled the small, forgettable smile of a traveling seamstress going to visit a cousin, and inside she had done the arithmetic of empires: eleven days to cross, four days downriver, six more to the relay station at Corve, where the winter dispatch would be sealed and sent on by the fast riders before the passes closed behind them. It could be done. It had been done before. She had done harder things in worse weather with less sleep.

That was before the snow.

* * *

She went down into the ferry-house kitchen, where Hask's wife, a small iron-grey woman called Berta, was frying eggs in a pan black with years. The window over the sink was a white blur.

"Early," Berta said, not turning. "That's what it is. Early."

"Early for what?" Coral asked, and made her voice the voice of a woman making conversation about weather, which was the easiest voice in the world, the one that asked for nothing and promised nothing.

Berta glanced at her then, and there was something in the look — not unkind, but weighing, the way the whole town had weighed her since she'd walked in two days ago with dust on her boots and a story about a cousin in the lowlands. "For the ice," Berta said. "Snow up top means snow in the high valley, and snow in the high valley means the river drops cold and slow, and cold slow water makes ice that means it. Hask'll be down at the landing already. He always is, first snow. Counting the timbers."

"Counting the timbers?"

"The bridge." Berta flipped the eggs with a spatula worn thin as a knife blade. "Seasonal bridge. You've seen it — the timber span upstream, the one they pull the planks off every autumn and stack in the boathouse. They put it back in spring. While it's down, the ferry does the work. Hask's ferry." She said it with a small, plain pride, the way she might have said *my garden* or *my mother's recipe*. "He'll be down there now, seeing what the snow's done to the stacks."

Coral's hands, wrapped around the chipped clay mug Berta had given her, went very still. "The bridge closes early some years?"

"It closes when it closes." Berta set the eggs in front of her, unasked, uncharged. "Town council decides. There's a mark on the almanac — first hard snow, they pull the rest of the planking, and that's that. Ferry stops when the ice stops the ferry. After that, Tamsin's Ford is Tamsin's Ford and the rest of the world can wait till thaw." She said it without malice, almost without emphasis, the way one states that winter follows autumn. "You'll want to make your crossing today, I'd think. Last day it's likely to be safe, with the water dropping the way it will."

Today. Coral ate the eggs because it would be strange not to, and because a courier who lost her appetite in front of strangers was a courier who got remembered. The eggs were good. She tasted none of it.

Eleven days had become one day, and one day had become a morning, and the morning was already half spent.

* * *

The landing was a quarter mile downstream of the ferry-house, where the river bent wide and slow between grey stone banks. The seasonal bridge — she had seen it the day she arrived, noted it automatically, the way she noted every exit from every town: *timber span, seasonal, disassembled in winter, chokepoint, avoid* — lay along the bank in great stacked tiers of planking and beam, most of it already under a foot of new snow. The boathouse beside it stood open, and a dozen townsfolk were moving in and out of it with the unhurried purpose of people doing a thing they had done all their lives. Planks came out on shoulders. Ropes creaked. Somewhere a man was calling numbers, and other men were answering them, and the whole operation had the rhythm of a dance rehearsed for generations.

Hask stood at the water's edge, hands on hips, staring at the river as though it were a debtor who had come to him with a story.

Coral approached the way she had practiced approaching everyone for fifteen years: sideways, unhurried, a seamstress out for a walk in falling snow, hands in her pockets, face arranged into mild, forgettable interest.

"Master Hask," she said. "Your wife says the crossing might be made today."

Hask didn't look at her at once. When he did, his eyes were the flat grey of the water. "Might," he said. "Might's doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"I need to be downriver. Family."

"Everyone needs to be downriver." He said it without cruelty. He nodded at the river. "You see that?"

She looked. The river had changed overnight. It was lower — she could see it in the exposed band of dark stone along the banks, a hand's width of stone that had been under water yesterday — and slower, the current gone thick and sullen, and riding on it, turning slowly as they came down from the high valley, were the first floes. Not ice yet, not really. Panes the size of dinner plates, then the size of doors, white and grey and glassy, spinning lazily in the slack water near the banks.

"Floe ice," Hask said. "Comes down ahead of the real cold. Ferry can work in a little of it. Can't work in much — a floe the size of a table hits a hull at that current, it's like a hammer. And it's coming down thicker every hour. By tonight the channel'll be a millpond of it, packed edge to edge, and by tomorrow it'll be frozen in solid, and that's the river done till thaw." He spat, with the precision of long practice, into the water. "I could put you across now. Maybe. Probably. If the wind holds off and the floes stay scattered and nothing goes wrong in the next ten minutes. But I'll tell you what I told the Vellum family yesterday when they wanted to get a wagon across — I don't gamble with the river. The river always collects."

Coral heard herself say, in the seamstress's mild voice: "And if I paid well for the gamble?"

Something moved behind Hask's eyes — not anger. Something older and wearier. He turned and looked at her fully for the first time, and she understood, in one cold drop of clarity, that she had made an error. The mild voice, the forgettable face — those worked on people who wanted something from you, who had a use for a harmless traveler. But Hask was a man whose whole trade was knowing exactly what the water would bear and what it would not, and he had spent his life being offered money to bear more than it should.

"Miss," he said, not unkindly, "I've ferried this crossing thirty years. I've carried brides and coffins and one year a whole troupe of players with their painted cloths. And every year, somebody offers me good money to do the stupid thing, and every year I say no, because the ones who offer are always the ones who can't swim." He nodded back up the bank, toward the town, where the snow was coming down thicker now, fat flakes with weight to them. "Bridge comes out tomorrow. Council's already voted — Odile Vant called the session last night when the snow started. You're welcome to stand on the bank and watch. It's a fine thing to see, if you've never seen it. Whole town turns out. Takes the span apart in an afternoon, neat as a clock coming apart. And in spring they put it back, and the road opens, and everyone who's been waiting goes where they were going." He shrugged, a great rolling shrug that seemed to involve his whole body. "That's the bargain here. The river keeps the time. Not me. Not the council. Not you."

*The river keeps the time.* Coral stood in the falling snow with her wrists chained — invisibly, inside her gloves, to a case that weighed less than a dead bird and more than a mountain — and felt the trap close around her with the soft, unhurried certainty of the snow itself.

She had counted on eleven days. She had counted on the road, on the relay at Corve, on the fast riders, on the arithmetic that had never yet failed her. She had not counted on a river with opinions.

"Is there another crossing?" she asked. "Downstream? A ford, a ferry, anything?"

"Not till Marlow's Deep, forty miles down, and Marlow's Deep closes a week before we do. It's already closed." Hask said it gently, the way one tells a child there is no more candy. "You could go back over the pass. If you left today. Maybe."

The pass. Three days' climb back the way she'd come, through country that had already tried to kill her once, in weather that was now, visibly, burying the trail behind her. And even if she made it — even if her body held, even if the case held, even if the snow held off — the pass would close behind her within days, and she'd be on the far side of it with the same winter between her and Corve, and no town at her back, and no roof, and no Berta frying eggs for strangers.

The trap had not one door but two, and both were closing, and she had walked in through the gap between them like a fool, like a courier with her arithmetic and her practiced face, and now the arithmetic was snow.

"Thank you, master ferryman," she said, and her voice did not shake, because fifteen years of practice is fifteen years of practice. "You've been very clear."

Hask looked at her a moment longer. Then, unexpectedly: "Berta'll keep your room. She likes the coin, and the truth is the loft's warm and you don't snore." A pause. "Whatever it is you're carrying — and I'm not asking, because it's not my business and I don't want it to be — you'd best make your peace with it keeping till spring. The river doesn't care what's in your bags. Nobody's is so important the ice will open for it."

He turned back to the water. The floes came down, turning, turning, patient as everything else in this town.

* * *

She walked back up through the town without deciding to, her feet carrying her along the main street because that was what feet did when the mind had stopped giving orders. The snow was thicker now. It lay on the workshop roofs in a smooth white foot, on the signboards, on the great timber crane by the joiners' yard. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys, straight up, unhurried. Somewhere a woman was calling a child in to dinner. Somewhere a saw was going, steady as a pulse, because in Tamsin's Ford, apparently, the saw went on regardless of empires, rivers, and the end of the world.

Coral stopped in the lee of the grain store and did the thing she had not allowed herself to do at the landing: she let the panic come up, all the way up, for exactly ten breaths, no more. She stood with her back against the cold boards and her eyes closed and let it move through her like a flood through a channel — *trapped, trapped, trapped, the dispatches, the deadline, the council, the war, the case, the case, the case* — and then, at the count of ten, she shut the floodgate, and opened her eyes, and was Coral the seamstress again, mild and forgettable, standing out of the snow like any sensible woman.

But the panic had left something behind, the way floods leave silt. A thought, small and cold and clear, sitting at the bottom of her:

*The dispatches are not going anywhere. And neither are you. So the question is no longer how you get the case to Corve. The question is what happens to the case, and to you, in a town that keeps its own books, for five months.*

Five months. A winter. A winter among these people, with their almanac marks and their pulled-up bridges and their unhurried saws. A winter in which the republic she served — the republic she had served since she was a child, since Sef had found her picking pockets in the Corve fish market and taught her that silence was a trade — might win the war, or lose it, or simply cease, the way a candle ceases, and no one in this town would notice or care.

And the case. The case with its coded orders, its sealed instructions, its *buy the militia charter, secure the rolls, by any means* — she had not yet decoded the second seal, the one beneath the first, the one her handlers had told her was only a formality, a confirmation of terms. She had told herself she would read it downriver, at leisure, at the relay station, where there was no danger in knowing.

There was danger in knowing everywhere. She had always known that. She had simply never before been *forced* to know.

She looked up the street, toward the council house, where a light was already burning against the early dark of the snowfall, where the formidable master joiner Odile Vant had called a session to vote a bridge out of the world. A town that decided things. A town that wrote things down. A town with archives — she had seen the archive-keeper's window, the second evening, noted it automatically, *records, likely complete, likely old* — and archives meant charters, and charters meant words, and words were the one country where Coral Okonkwo had never needed a road or a river to travel.

The snow fell. The floes came down the river, turning, turning. Somewhere behind her, the saw went on.

Coral pulled her collar up, and turned — not toward the ferry-house, not toward the warm loft and the safe, forgettable seamstress's life she had built in two days — but up the street, toward the light in the council house, and began, for the first time in fifteen years, to walk toward the thing she feared instead of away from it.

She did not yet know that Odile Vant was looking for her. She did not yet know that the master joiner had already asked Berta, that morning, exactly who the quiet cousin was, and where she had come from, and what she intended to do through a winter that had just closed around them all like a fist.

But the town knew. The town had known since the ferry-house took her in. In Tamsin's Ford, the books kept themselves.
