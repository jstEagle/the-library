## 5. The Regiment

The morning after the bridge timbers came down, Coral woke in the loft above the ferryman's boathouse and did what she always did first: she counted.

Three windows in the loft, one of them shuttered from the outside. A ladder, not stairs — defensible but slow. The boathouse below held two skiffs, both hull-cracked for the season, their oars hung on pegs like sleeping birds. The ferryman, a broad old woman called Hesk with forearms like mooring rope, had told her plainly at supper that nothing crossed the river now until the ice went out, and she had said it the way people say *the sun will rise*, without apology or interest in argument. So: no road east until spring. No road west but the pass, which was already white to the knee and closing by the hour. North lay rock and scree where even goats filed complaints. South, downriver, lay the republic — and everything the republic would send looking for her if her handlers decided a courier who failed to report was a courier who needed finding.

Coral lay on the straw tick and performed the arithmetic she had performed every morning for fifteen years. Days of food on her person: four, if she stretched it. Coin sewn into her coat hem: enough for a month in a town, less than a week in a city. Names she could credibly wear: six, though only three still fit her face. And beneath all of it, steady as a pulse, the weight on her wrist — the dispatch case, chained, hidden this morning under her shirt where it rode her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She had been trained never to stay anywhere longer than necessary. Staying was how couriers died. Staying gave a face time to become familiar, gave questions time to accumulate answers, gave a town time to remember you. Sef had drilled it into her before she was old enough to understand why: *You are a letter, Coral. Letters do not unpack.*

And yet here she lay, unpacked, in a loft that smelled of pitch and fish, listening to a town wake up below her window with the unhurried confidence of a place that had never once been burned.

* * *

Tamsin's Ford did not wake the way garrison towns woke, all shouts and bugles and boots on stone. It woke the way a workshop wakes: gradually, in stages, by craft. First came the bell — a single low note from the meetinghouse tower, struck seven times, which Coral learned later meant simply *the day has begun, no more significance than that*. Then smoke rose from chimneys in an order that seemed almost choreographed, the bake-ovens first, then the forge, then the kilns at the river's edge. By the time full light came over the eastern ridge, the sound of the town was the sound of making: saws in long slow strokes, the tap-tap-tap of mallets on chisels, someone singing a counting song in a voice that carried across three rooftops without any apparent effort.

Coral walked through it with her hands in her pockets and her eyes working.

She had spent one day already mapping the town — a day, because Tamsin's Ford rewarded attention. It was built like a thought rather than a plan. Streets bent where the terrain demanded and not otherwise; houses leaned together at the corners as if conferring; and everywhere, everywhere, were the marks of the Regiment's trade. Shavings drifted in the gutters like pale snow that had come early. Door lintels bore carved dates and initials. A bench sat outside the smithy with its legs worn concave by generations of waiting men. Even the well-head had joinery about it, a little roof of cedar shakes renewed so recently it still wept resin smell into the cold air.

And there were no soldiers. That was the thing she kept circling back to, the absence that itched. In every town she had ever passed through between the capital and the mountains, there had been soldiers — billeted, marching, drunk, deserting, hanging a deserter, burying the men who hanged the deserter. Here: militia rolls kept in a chest, Hesk had mentioned, and a drill on the green once a month that was half festival. The town governed itself by council, kept its own books, settled its own quarrels, and had done so, according to the innkeeper's wife, since before anyone's grandmother was born.

*A town that keeps its own books,* Odile Vant had said yesterday, when Coral had asked, in her careful traveler's voice, who collected taxes here. The master joiner had looked at her for a long moment — that look again, the one that made Coral feel like a joint being tested for play — and said: "We keep our own books, mistress. Everything else follows from that."

Now, on the morning after the bridge closed, Coral stood outside the workshop hall where the council met, telling herself she was there for one reason only: the archives. Every self-governing town kept records — charters, tolls, correspondence with the crown, with the republic, with the enemy's governors, with each other. Records meant information, and information meant leverage, and leverage was the only currency a trapped courier could spend. If the republic's war swallowed this valley in the spring, she needed to know what this town had in writing that might keep it — and her — alive.

That was the story. She told it to herself twice, the way one checks a knot.

The door of the workshop hall stood open, as doors seemed to do here, unguarded, as if the town had never heard of thieves. Inside, the great room smelled of oak dust and linseed oil and something sharper — glue, hot, animal. Along one wall ran benches scarred into topography by a century of blades. Along another, shelves of drawings rolled and tied like scrolls of scripture. And in the middle of the floor, surrounded by apprentices, stood Odile Vant, turning a chair leg slowly in the light from the high windows.

Coral had met perhaps four hundred officials in fifteen years — magistrates, quartermasters, colonels, ministers' secretaries — and she had developed an ear for power the way a tuner develops an ear for pitch. Power spoke loudly when it was nervous and softly when it was sure. Odile Vant spoke softly. She was not tall; she was broad through the hands and shoulders the way a tree is broad at the root, and her hair was iron-grey and pinned with what appeared to be an actual nail. When she turned the chair leg, eight apprentices turned with her, silent, watching the grain the way sailors watch weather.

"Mistress Okonkwo." She did not look up. "Come in or shut the door. Drafts ruin glue."

Coral stepped inside and shut the door. "I've come to thank the council for the bed last night—"

"The council didn't give you the bed. Hesk did. Hesk gives everyone a bed the first night; she says it's cheaper than arguing. Thank her." Odile set the leg down and wiped her hands on her apron, and only then gave Coral the whole weight of her attention, which was considerable. "You're here because the bridge is out and you want work."

It was not a question, and Coral felt the floor shift slightly beneath her practiced footing. "I hadn't—"

"You'd asked Hesk the price of passage south within an hour of arriving. You asked me yesterday whether the town paid tolls. You watched the council chamber door through supper the way a cat watches a mousehole. You need coin for a winter you didn't plan for, and I need hands, because two of mine are down with lung-fever and winter doesn't wait for the healthy." Odile folded her arms. "So. Can you use a plane?"

"I can learn," Coral said, which was the truest sentence available.

"Hm. Everyone says that. Most of them are lying." The master joiner studied her hands — Coral resisted, barely, the instinct to hide them; they were a courier's hands, hard-palmed, knife-scarred along the thumb where a lock had bitten her once in Vessany City. "Strong enough. Patient enough, maybe. I'll know by week's end if you're a liar, because wood tells on liars faster than people do. Room over the shop, meals at the common table, wages against spring. In exchange—" and here her voice dropped half a tone, not softer exactly but closer, "—you leave the council chamber alone. Whatever you came here thinking to find in our archives, you'll find it when the town decides you should, and not one page sooner. We read strangers slowly here. It keeps us alive."

The chill that went through Coral then was entirely professional — admiration, even, of a kind. The woman had seen through her in a single evening, or near enough, and was naming the terms aloud with the same unhurried precision she'd use on a mortise. Coral ran through her options in the space of a breath. Refuse, and spend winter in the inn burning coin she'd need in spring, under the eyes of a town already reading her too well. Accept, and be inside the workshop, inside the rhythms of the town, close to the very rooms she'd been warned away from — close enough, given patience, to learn everything.

"Terms accepted," she said.

Odile nodded once, as if closing a joint. "Then pick up those shavings before you trip an apprentice."

* * *

The work, in the first days, humiliated her.

She had not expected that. Coral had crossed the Ashen Pass alone in November with a case chained to her wrist; she had talked her way past checkpoints whose guards shot first and read papers never; she had memorized forty pages of cipher in a single night by candlelight and recited them back flawlessly while hung-over on purpose-drugged wine to prove she could. Her mind was a weapon kept honed by fifteen years of use. Her hands, it turned out, were useless.

The plane chattered and dug. The chisel wandered. She split a board of good quarter-sawn oak along its whole length because she had set the gauge wrong and then, worse, tried to force it, and the sound of the split went through the workshop like a scream through church. An apprentice named Pell — fourteen, freckled, merciless — picked up the ruined board, examined it with theatrical gravity, and announced to the room, "She's invented a new joint. She calls it the broken one."

Laughter, warm, unmalicious, and Coral stood in the middle of it feeling something she had no name for. She had been laughed at before — she had engineered it, deliberately, playing the fool at border crossings, the tipsy widow, the dull-witted peddler's sister, because laughter made you invisible. But this laughter was different. This laughter included her. It assumed she would still be here tomorrow to be laughed at again.

She did not know what to do with that assumption, so she did what she knew: she worked. She stayed at the bench after the others left, planing scrap until her arms shook, learning the grain of pine the way she'd once learned the grain of a city street — by failure, repetition, and total attention. She learned that wood had moods, that it moved with the weather, that it remembered every injury done to it and showed the memory forever in its figure. She learned that a sharp tool forgave nothing and a patient hand was worth more than a strong one.

And she noticed — because noticing was the one skill that transferred entire — how the workshop ran.

There was a forewoman called Dagny who assigned tasks by some system no one explained and everyone obeyed. There was a boy who swept, and sweeping, it emerged, rotated weekly among every member including Odile herself, who took her turn with the broom with the same gravity she brought to anything else. Disputes went to the bench of elders on Thursday evenings. Wages were entered in a ledger anyone could ask to see — Coral asked, casually, on her third day, and was shown, casually, pages of neat columns going back decades, births and debts and timber bought and timber owed, the whole economic pulse of the town in ink.

"It's all in there?" she asked the archive-keeper, a dry small man named Master Fenwick who smelled of dust and vinegar.

"All of it that matters," he said. "Which is most of it. Empires keep secrets; towns keep accounts. Secrets rot. Accounts just get settled." He smiled, showing tea-stained teeth. "You'll get used to it. Strangers always think the openness is a trick."

"And is it?"

"Of course," Fenwick said cheerfully. "But it's an honest trick. Come back Thursday."

* * *

On her fifth night in the workshop loft, Coral dreamed of the pass.

In the dream she was crossing it again, snow to the waist, and the case on her wrist had grown heavy as an anchor, dragging her backward off the trail, and below her in the valley the lights of Tamsin's Ford burned gold and warm and utterly unreachable. She woke with her heart hammering and lay in the dark doing the arithmetic again, because the arithmetic calmed her, because the arithmetic was the shape her fear took when it wanted to be useful.

Days since the bridge fell: five. Days of food in the town's stores, per Hesk's gossip: enough till thaw, if the thaw came on time, which it might not. Days until her handlers in the capital expected a confirmation signal: eleven. After that, they would assume her lost or taken. After that, they would send someone to find out which.

Eleven days. She turned the number over like a coin worn smooth. Eleven days to decide what to do about a message she had not yet dared open — really open, beyond the surface cipher, beyond the routing marks. Eleven days to decide whether she was still a letter, or whether she had become, somewhere on the frozen pass, something else.

Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the last sounds of the workshop settling: a bar dropping into its brackets, Dagny's voice saying goodnight to someone, the banked stove ticking as it cooled. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Sounds that assumed tomorrow.

And threaded through them, faintly, from the direction of the master's quarters, a violin — no, a fiddle — playing something slow and minor, played not for an audience but the way a person talks to themselves when they think no one listens. Odile Vant, it seemed, played after hours. Coral lay still and listened to it longer than was prudent, feeling warmth seep into a place she had boarded over years ago, and distrusted the warmth thoroughly, the way you distrust ice that looks thick and isn't.

*Warmth is a tactic,* Sef used to say. *When they're kind to you, ask yourself what they want.* But Odile wanted nothing from her that Coral could name — only labor, honestly priced, and obedience to rules posted openly on a wall. Kindness with no visible hook in it. Either it was a deeper game than any Coral had ever run, or—

Or the town was simply like this. She couldn't decide which possibility frightened her more.

The fiddle stopped. The house slept. Coral drew the dispatch case out from under her shirt and held it in both hands in the dark, feeling its weight, its promise, its threat. Eleven days.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would begin on the cipher properly. Tomorrow, while the workshop rang with mallets and the town went about its confident, bookkeeping life, she would find out what she had carried across the mountains into the middle of it.

The case's lock yielded to her pick with a soft click, loud as a shout in the sleeping loft.

Down the corridor, a floorboard creaked — once, twice — and went quiet.
