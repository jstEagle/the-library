## 6. Tamsin

Tamsin Vant had been planing a length of ash when the stranger first walked past the workshop window, and she ruined the cut entirely.

The board had been going well. That was the trouble with ash — it forgave nothing, but it rewarded attention, and Tamsin had been giving it her full attention for the better part of an hour, the plane singing that low sweet note it made when the iron was set just right and the wood was in the mood to cooperate. Shavings curled up off the blade and dropped to the floor in pale ribbons, and the smell of them filled the shop the way bread smell fills a kitchen: so familiar it stopped being a smell at all and became simply the air of being alive.

Then a shadow crossed the window, moving wrong.

Everything in Tamsin's Ford moved in ways she could predict with her eyes shut. The miller's boy took the corner by the tannery at a dead run every morning and a saunter every afternoon. Old Piet walked the river path at the same hour regardless of weather, his dog trotting three lengths ahead as though the dog, not Piet, had somewhere to be. Market day drew the outlying farms in from the east road, carts creaking, in ones and twos, all morning. Tamsin had grown up inside this choreography the way a fish grows up inside water, and she had never once thought of it as a choreography until the day a shadow moved against the current of it.

This shadow moved like someone reading the street. It stopped at the corner. It went on. It stopped again — not the stopping of a tired traveler, which is a slump, a sag, a letting-down, but the stopping of someone counting. Counting doors. Counting roofs. Counting, Tamsin thought, watching through the glass with her plane forgotten in her hands, the distance between one thing and the next.

She set the plane down and went to the door.

Up close the stranger was younger than the walk suggested — a woman, travel-stained, with the kind of face that took effort to make forgettable. Tamsin had grown up around faces. The Regiment was a town of joiners, wheelwrights, coopers, and carvers, and every one of them could read a surface: the grain of oak telling you where it would split, the paint of a door telling you when it was last opened, the set of a person's shoulders telling you what they carried. This woman's shoulders said she carried nothing, and her boots said she'd walked a very long way, and her hands — her hands were wrong. Soft in the wrong places. Calloused along the inside of the fingers, not the palm. Tamsin had never seen hands like that on anyone who wasn't a scribe, and scribes did not come to Tamsin's Ford in the last week of the season, alone, on foot, looking at the town like a map they were memorizing.

"Mama says we're to call you the widow's cousin," Tamsin said, before she had decided to say anything at all.

The woman turned. Her face did the most remarkable thing, which was nothing. A blink, a small polite tilt of the head, the ghost of a smile — a whole performance of mild incomprehension, as though Tamsin had addressed her in a foreign tongue. "I'm sorry?"

"The widow Sarelle. You're staying at her house. She's telling everyone you're her cousin from downriver." Tamsin crossed her arms. "I've known Sarelle my whole life. She hasn't had a cousin from downriver. She hasn't had anyone from downriver. She once refused to speak to the ferryman for six years over a disputed goose, so the idea that she's quietly harboring family all this time —"

"I see." The woman's voice was level, warm even, and completely uninformative. "You're a very direct person."

"I'm a very bored person. It's nearly winter. There's nothing to do but work and gossip, and I've already worked today."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes then — not a crack, exactly, but the suggestion that a crack existed somewhere and was being carefully papered over. "And you are?"

"Tamsin. Tamsin Vant. My mother owns this workshop, which means I own nothing and do everything." She jerked her chin toward the woman's boots. "You walked the high pass. Alone. In the last week before the snows. Either you didn't know the bridge closes, or you didn't care, and I've been trying all morning to decide which is more interesting."

The woman regarded her for a long moment. Snowmelt had dried in white lines on the leather. The straps were worn through at the heel, mended with a stitch pattern Tamsin didn't recognize — not the Regiment's way, not the valley's way. Something from farther off, from the flat country, from the world that existed mostly in the ledger-books and the occasional downriver peddler's complaints.

"I knew it closed," the woman said at last. "I hoped I'd beat it."

"And did you?"

"I'm standing here."

"You're standing here because the bridge is a pile of kindling and the ferryman won't put a boat on that water till the ice goes out. You didn't beat it. You arrived at the exact moment it closed. That's not luck, that's arithmetic, and I don't believe in that much bad arithmetic in one person." Tamsin unfolded her arms. "So. What were you hoping to do here that was worth the pass?"

For the first time, the woman hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a half-breath, and Tamsin would have missed it in anyone else. In this woman, whose every gesture was sanded smooth, it rang like a cracked bell.

"Rest," she said.

"Liar," Tamsin said cheerfully. "Well. You'll get rest whether you want it or not. That's the one thing this town is good at forcing on people. Welcome to Tamsin's Ford, cousin-from-downriver. I'm sure you'll be exactly who you say you are for the entire winter."

She went back inside before the woman could answer, and stood with her hand flat against the door, heart going like a drum, feeling — though she could not yet have said why — that the winter had just become the most interesting one of her life.

* * *

Her mother noticed within the day, because Odile Vant noticed everything, and said nothing about it for three days, which was its own kind of commentary.

On the fourth evening, as they closed the workshop — Tamsin sweeping, Odile oiling the good chisels and putting them away in their felt-lined slots with the tenderness she showed no living creature — her mother said, "You spoke to the Sarelle cousin."

"I did."

"And?"

"And she's a liar." Tamsin leaned on her broom. "A good one, though. The best I've ever seen, and I've watched Brann lie to this council for six years about the granary accounts."

Odile's hands did not pause on the chisels. "What kind of liar?"

"The kind who lies with her whole body. Her boots say one thing, her hands say another, her face says nothing at all. She walked the high pass in the last week of the season knowing the bridge was going out from under her, and when I asked why, she said rest." Tamsin swept a pile of shavings into the bin with unnecessary force. "Nobody walks a mountain pass for rest, Mama."

"Nobody," Odile agreed. She set the last chisel in its slot and closed the case. "Your grandmother walked one to escape a war. People walk passes for all kinds of reasons they won't say out loud."

"That's different. Grandmother was running from something. This one's —" Tamsin stopped, hunting for the word, and found it with a small cold thrill. "This one's going toward something. She was counting doors, Mama. On the street. I watched her. She wasn't looking at the town like a traveler looks at a town. She was looking at it like a — like a surveyor."

Odile was quiet for a while. The stove ticked as it cooled. Outside, the early dark of the season pressed against the windows, and somewhere down the street a door banged and someone shouted the ordinary goodnights of an ordinary town.

"Your grandfather," Odile said finally, "kept a saying on the bench. *Measure twice, cut once.* It's on the beam over your head. Do you know why the Regiment keeps its own books, Tamsin? Not its accounts — its books. Its record of who owes what, and to whom, and since when?"

"Because the Republic's books are lies."

"Because any book can be a lie. Ours too, if we're careless. The keeping is the thing. Watching. Writing down. Not deciding too fast what a thing means." Odile hung the chisel case on its peg and looked at her daughter, and in the stove-light her face was the face Tamsin had known all her life: patient, shrewd, and entirely without mercy for sloppy thinking. "The woman is a question. You don't answer a question in the first week. You keep her in the book. You watch what she does when she thinks no one's watching. And you don't, under any circumstances, let her know how interesting you find her, because interesting is a weakness, and clever people can smell a weakness the way a dog smells fear."

"I wasn't going to —"

"You asked her, to her face, what she was doing here."

Tamsin flushed. "She was counting our doors."

"Yes," Odile said. "She was. Which is why you'll be polite, and dull, and unremarkable, and you will spend the winter finding out everything about her without ever once letting her see you look." She picked up the lamp. "That's the joiner's way, Tamsin. The grain tells you everything, but only if you don't start cutting before you've read it."

Tamsin said yes, Mama, in the tone that meant she had heard and filed and not necessarily agreed, and Odile, who had raised her and knew exactly what the tone meant, sighed the sigh of mothers everywhere and blew out the lamp.

* * *

Being dull, Tamsin discovered, was the hardest work she had ever done.

The trouble was that the woman — Coral, Sarelle's supposed cousin, though Tamsin had already extracted from Sarelle, over an afternoon of pie and relentless friendliness, the information that the cousin had arrived with no letter, no bundle, no luggage beyond a single case, and a story that changed in small unimportant details each time she told it — the trouble was that Coral kept doing interesting things.

She attended the council meeting on the second Thursday, sitting in the gallery with the other unenfranchised townsfolk, and she watched the debate over winter rations with an attention that no bored cousin had ever given anything. Tamsin watched her watch. Coral's eyes went to the tally-keeper when the counts were read. They went to Brann when he objected, and stayed on him a beat too long, the way you look at a piece of wood you suspect is rotten under sound paint. They went to the door twice — twice — in an hour, and each time they came back to the room as though pulled on a string.

She came to the workshop on the sixth day, at Sarelle's suggestion — Sarelle, Tamsin was now certain, had been set to the task by Odile herself, the whole town a net and Coral walking into it smiling — and asked, in her mild uninformative voice, whether the workshop took on winter help.

"We take on anyone with hands and patience," Tamsin said, sanding a drawer-front and not looking up, because looking up was forbidden. "Do you have hands and patience?"

"I have hands."

"We'll see about the rest."

Odile set her to sweeping. It was, Tamsin recognized with reluctant admiration, a masterstroke: the dullest possible work, in the middle of the room, where the stranger could be seen by everyone and could see everything, and where her hands — those wrong, soft, scribe-calloused hands — would be on open display eight hours a day. Coral swept. Coral swept with a competence that told Tamsin she had done manual labor somewhere, sometime, and hidden it since. Coral swept and watched and asked no questions, which was itself an answer, because everyone who came to the Regiment asked questions; the town was strange and people were curious, and only a person with a reason not to know things declined to wonder aloud.

On the ninth day, Coral picked up a offcut of walnut from the scrap bin, turned it over in her fingers, and said, to no one in particular, "This is good stock."

"It's scrap," Tamsin said.

"It's scrap here." The woman set it down with a care that Tamsin noted and filed. "Where I'm from, this would be a table leg."

"Where are you from?"

"Downriver."

"You've said. Downriver is a hundred miles of country and forty towns. Which —"

"Tamsin." Odile's voice, from the far bench, without heat. "The walnut."

Tamsin went back to the walnut. But she caught the look that passed between her mother and the stranger — two careful people acknowledging each other across a room, the way two chess players acknowledge a board — and something in her chest went tight and bright, the feeling she got from a difficult joint that was finally going to fit.

* * *

It was on the twelfth evening that Tamsin stopped being merely suspicious and became, in the privacy of her own skull, genuinely fascinated.

She had gone down to the river at dusk — the frozen-edge river, the river that had eaten the bridge, walking the bank the way she always walked it when her head was too full, watching the ice plates grind and shudder against the ferry landing. And she had not been alone. Coral was there, standing at the end of the ruined bridge's approach, looking across the black water at the far bank, where the road went down and away toward the flat country and whatever it was she had been carrying when she came.

She was carrying it still. Tamsin saw it now plainly: the case, a flat courier's case, worn at the strap, and — the detail that made the back of her neck prickle — chained. Chained to her wrist. The woman had worn it into the workshop, had worn it sweeping, had worn it to council, and everyone had taken it for an eccentricity of downriver fashion, and here at the river's edge, in the last light, with no one watching — or so she believed — Coral stood looking at the far road with an expression that Tamsin had never seen on a human face before, because it was not one expression but all of them at once, held perfectly still: longing and dread and duty and a kind of grief, all pressed flat behind glass.

Tamsin should have withdrawn. Odile's instructions were clear, and Odile's instructions were usually right. But Tamsin had inherited her mother's eyes and none of her mother's patience, and besides, the ice was groaning, and Coral turned at the sound.

For a moment they looked at each other across ten feet of frozen mud and dying light, and Tamsin saw the performance come up over the woman's face like a visor — the mild smile, the polite tilt — and heard herself say, before the visor could fully close:

"Whatever's in that case, it's not worth the pass, is it? That's why you look like that. You got what you came for, or you know what you came for, and now the bridge is gone and you have to sit in a town full of strangers holding a thing you can't put down and can't use." She took a step closer, hands in her pockets, heart hammering. "I'm not going to tell anyone. I'm the worst gossip in this town — ask anyone, I never tell anyone anything — which is exactly why you can believe me. Everyone knows I keep things. It's the one reputation I have."

The visor did not come down. But behind it, for the first time, Tamsin saw something move — something real, startled, almost frightened, and underneath the fright, so faint it might have been the ice groaning, something that looked horribly like hope.

"You should go inside," Coral said quietly. "It's getting cold."

"You should put that thing down," Tamsin said. "Whatever it is."

And she walked back up the bank toward the lamplit street, not looking back, because looking back was forbidden, but listening — listening hard — and hearing behind her, for a long time, nothing at all: no footsteps, no departure, just a woman standing alone at the edge of the broken bridge, holding whatever it was, in the dark, in the cold, in the town that had closed around her like a book.

Tamsin went home and did not sleep. She lay in her loft under the eaves watching the moon move across the window, and thought: *she's afraid of something in that case, or for it, and she's afraid of us, or for us, and she walked into the one town in the valley that watches everything and writes it all down.* And underneath the thought, growing like warmth from a stove she hadn't lit yet, another thought, simpler and more dangerous: *I want to know her. Not the cousin-from-downriver. Her.*

In the morning there was frost on the inside of the windows, and Odile was already at the bench, and Tamsin came down the ladder and said, before her mother could speak:

"The Sarelle cousin. She's carrying a courier's case, chained to her wrist. She stands at the broken bridge every evening looking at the far road like a woman at a graveside. And I think, Mama — I think whatever she came here to do, she can't do it anymore, and she doesn't know yet whether that's a rescue or a disaster."

The workshop was very quiet. The stove ticked. Overhead, on its beam, the old saying looked down at them both: *measure twice, cut once.*

Odile set down her chisel, and Tamsin saw — for the first time in her life — something in her mother's face that was not patience.

"Then it's time," Odile said, "we found out what she's carrying."
