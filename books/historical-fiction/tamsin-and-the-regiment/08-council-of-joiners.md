## 8. Council of Joiners

The council met in the long room above the counting-house, and Tamsin Vant had been sneaking into its meetings since she was nine years old, which meant she knew exactly which floorboard creaked and exactly where the gallery bench sat in shadow behind the rail. It was not that anyone had forbidden her to come. It was worse than that: nobody had ever thought to mention it at all. She was Odile's daughter, and daughters of the Regiment were like the beams overhead — present, load-bearing, unremarked.

Tonight the gallery held her and two of the Ferrier boys, who were there for the same reason she was, which was to say none at all except that the fire was warm and the arguments were good. Below, eleven chairs stood in a crescent around a trestle table scarred by two hundred years of elbows, ink rings, and one famous scorch from the night old Marta Ferrier had set down her pipe during a dispute about the toll schedule. Eleven chairs, and on the wall behind them, no portrait of any prince or president — only the original Articles of the Regiment, written out fair in iron-gall ink on vellum gone brown as tea, beginning with the words *We who work here agree to be counted.*

Tamsin had read those words so many times they had worn smooth in her mind, the way a stair-nose wears underfoot. Tonight she found herself reading them again anyway, because the stranger was downstairs in the hall being shown up, and Tamsin wanted something steady in her head while she watched.

Coral — Mrs. Corla Ashen, widowed cousin to Widow Prewitt over the chandlery, if you believed the story, and Tamsin did not believe it further than she could throw the chandlery — came up the stair at half past seven precisely. Not a minute before, not a minute after. Tamsin noticed things like that. The townsfolk drifted; they arrived when the kettle called them or when their last joint of glue had set. The stranger arrived the way a letter arrives: exactly when sent, wearing exactly what was expected.

She wore gray wool today, mended but well-mended, the kind of mending you only see if you've been taught to look — and Tamsin had been taught to look since she could hold an awl, because joinery is nothing but looking. The stitches along the cuff were even as machine-work. Nobody's grieving-widow cousin sewed like that. Grieving widows let their cuffs go.

"Sit anywhere," said Councilor Brann, gesturing grandly, though there was really only one place left, at the table's end nearest the door. Brann always took the chair nearest the stove and always said *sit anywhere* as though the seating were free, which everyone knew it wasn't, because the seats meant seniority and the seniority meant your voice carried. He was a broad, comfortable man with a merchant's smile and a wool coat better than anyone else's in the room, and he had been kind to Tamsin all her life in the way of men who are kind to children they expect to grow up useful.

Odile did not look up when Coral entered. Her mother never looked up at anything until she had finished the line she was working. She was writing figures into the ration ledger, each numeral struck down like a peg driven home, and the scratch of her pen was the only sound in the room for a full minute after the stranger sat down. Tamsin felt Coral go still in that silence — not nervous-still, exactly. Measured-still. A person deciding how much stillness was appropriate and purchasing exactly that amount.

"The ledger first," Odile said at last, capping her pen. "Then the charter question stays off this table until I say otherwise."

"We agreed—" Brann began.

"You agreed. I said we'd hear it after rations, and rations take as long as they take." Odile turned the ledger toward the room. "Grain stores stand at four hundred and twelve sacks against three hundred and eighty mouths, counting the Ferriers' new twins and not counting waste. That's thin but honest. Salt is short. Lamp oil will carry us to thaw if we burn it at half-wick through February. And we have one more mouth than we planned for." Her eyes went down the table, not to Coral directly — Odile never aimed anything directly — but the whole crescent understood. "Mrs. Ashen. You'll take work at the workshop. Bench rates, winter terms, same as anyone. Do you have questions about the terms?"

"None, mistress," Coral said. Her voice was low and even, a voice like a drawer closing properly. "Thank you for the work."

"Thank the bridge for falling," said Odile, and there was a ripple of laughter, warm enough, and Tamsin saw the stranger laugh too — a small, correct laugh, arriving exactly on time like everything else about her — and Tamsin thought: *she counts her laughs.* Who counts their laughs?

* * *

The ration debate ran two hours, and it was, Tamsin thought afterward, the finest thing she'd ever seen done in that room, which was saying something, because she'd seen them argue a man's whole reputation over a miscounted toll.

It began with salt. Ferrier the elder wanted the salt reserve opened early for curing, because the hogs were heavy this year and every week of delay was meat going gray on the hook. Brann wanted the reserve held against "emergencies," a word he liked very much and used the way other men used brandy. Old Sela Quill, the archive-keeper, whose voice sounded like paper folding, observed that the emergency most likely to arrive was February, and February would not be argued with, only provisioned against. A young councilor named Doss, barely thirty, proposed buying salt downriver through the Ferriers' spring consignment, which was sensible and also impossible, because the river was already carrying ice at the banks, and everyone knew it, and he knew they knew it, and he said it anyway, because Doss was ambitious and ambition proposes impossible things so that later it can say it tried.

And then Odile did the thing she always did, the thing Tamsin had watched her do since childhood without ever naming it until tonight: she stopped arguing. She turned the ledger back around, dipped her pen, and wrote something — a single line — and slid the book to the center of the table, and waited.

The room quieted itself. That was the marvel. No gavel, no calling of order, no king's peace. Just eleven people and a book, and the understanding that whatever got written in the book became true, and whatever stayed unwritten was only noise. Brann leaned over and read the line. His jaw worked. Ferrier read it and grunted. Sela Quill read it and made a small sound like a stitch pulled tight.

Tamsin craned and caught it upside-down across the lamplight: *Open the reserve at half-measure now, full at midwinter if ice holds the ferry past New Year.* Half now, half held. Nobody won. Nobody lost face. The compromise was written where everyone could see it, signed by whoever chose to sign, and the signing was the law, because the Articles said so, and the Articles said so because the founders had decided that a town run by whoever shouted loudest would die of shouting inside ten years.

They signed it. All of them, even Brann, even Doss, even Ferrier who had gotten less than he wanted and knew it. Eleven names, eleven dates. Then Sela blotted the page and the matter was simply over, finished as cleanly as a dovetail, and they moved on to lamp oil.

Tamsin sat in the dark of the gallery with her chin on her fists and loved them all so much it was almost pain. *This,* she thought. *This is the thing. This is what the republic downriver will never understand, with its proclamations and its governors and its wars.* Power here didn't flow downhill from somebody's crown. It pooled. It was poured out onto a flat table and every hand at the table got a finger in it, and the only rule was that you wrote down what you poured. A town that keeps its own books answers to its own arithmetic, and arithmetic has never once been bought by an empire.

Across the table, the stranger was watching too. Tamsin had spent the evening watching Coral watch, and it was like watching someone read in a language they pretended not to know. When the vote-by-signature came, Coral's eyes went to the pen first, then to the hands that took it up — weighing each grip, Tamsin would have sworn it, the way you weigh timber before you trust it to bear. When Odile spoke, Coral listened with her whole body arranged into attention, but her eyes never once settled on Odile's face; they rested instead on her hands, her throat, the exits behind her chair. Counting. Always counting.

*What are you for?* Tamsin asked silently, across the lamplight and the ledgers. *Who sent you, and what are you counting us for?*

Because here was the thing Tamsin had turned over all night while the salt argument rolled: the stranger had arrived six days before the bridge fell. Six days. In a season when no traveler comes to Tamsin's Ford unless they must, and nobody *must* anymore, because the war had eaten the roads and the post hadn't run regular since summer. A woman traveling alone, with no trade goods worth mentioning, no family anyone could write to and verify, sewing stitches fine as a clerk's cipher — arriving days ahead of the freeze, asking careful, forgettable questions about the town's affairs at the chandlery counter where Widow Prewitt tells everybody everything.

Tamsin had mentioned none of this to her mother. Yet. She was keeping it the way you keep a board you're fitting: dry, close, ready. Because if the woman was a spy, Tamsin wanted to know for whom before she made noise about it. And if she was something sadder than a spy — a runner, a fugitive, a ghost of one of the fallen cities downriver — then noise would be the cruelest thing Tamsin could do, and she'd have to decide what mercy looked like, and whether mercy and the town's safety could fit in the same sentence.

* * *

The meeting wound down near midnight to the small matters: a dispute between the Hallow cousins over a boundary fence, a request from the schoolmistress for coal, the annual question of who would maintain the ferry winch through the freeze (nobody ever wanted it; it went to whoever lost the coin-draw, and Ferrier the elder lost again and took it with the grace of a man rehearsing his grievance for the spring). Chairs scraped. Lamplight guttered. And then, in the settling of things, Tamsin saw the small moment that she would turn over in bed for an hour afterward, unable to put it down.

Brann, gathering his papers, paused beside Coral's chair. Bent slightly — not far, just the courteous inch — and said something Tamsin couldn't hear. Three sentences, maybe four. Coral answered with a shake of the head and a widow's small sad smile, and Brann laughed, patted the table twice as men do when sealing an understanding, and moved off whistling.

Nothing in it. Nothing at all. A councilor being neighborly to the newcomer.

Except that Tamsin had watched Brann all evening, the way she watched everything, and Brann had looked at the stranger three times during the salt debate — quickly, each time, the way you check a clock — and his face each time had done a thing faces aren't supposed to do in public, which is calculate. And when the stranger gave him the widow's smile, his answering expression was not the warmth of a man charming a guest. It was satisfaction. The specific, greasy satisfaction of a man who has found a key he'd been told existed and has just confirmed it fits.

Down below, the room emptied. Odile banked the stove. Sela Quill chained the ledger into its cabinet — actually chained it, iron and padlock, though everyone teased her for it, and everyone was glad of it too. Coral rose, thanked the room in her drawer-closing voice, and went down the stair alone, her footsteps fading on the boards, measured and even, a metronome walking away into the snow.

"Tamsin." Her mother's voice, from below, without looking up. "You're visible in that gallery, you know. You have been since you were nine."

"I know," Tamsin said, coming down. "You never said to stop."

"No point. You'd have listened at the keyhole instead, and the keyhole distorts." Odile closed the cabinet and turned, and for a moment her mother's eyes were on her fully, and Tamsin felt the familiar sensation of being read grain-deep. "Well? You sat through two hours of it. Tell me what you heard."

"The salt compromise. Brann wanted to hold the reserve so he'd have something to bargain away later, probably to whoever asks next. Doss was performing. Ferrier will complain about the winch until April. And—" Tamsin hesitated, and then decided, because lying badly to Odile Vant was not a skill anyone in that family possessed. "And our guest counted the room tonight. Doors, hands, voices. Like an auditor."

Odile was quiet a moment. Outside, the wind worried the eaves, and somewhere below in the yard the snow kept its soft business of covering everything.

"An auditor," her mother repeated. "Or a debtor. Or a widow, which is its own kind of counting." She took the lamp from the table. "People come to mountains to disappear, Tam. Sometimes it's crimes chasing them. Sometimes it's grief. Sometimes it's only the world being too loud. The Regiment doesn't ask which, on arrival. We ask at the right time."

"And when's the right time?"

"When the cost of asking wrong is smaller than the cost of not knowing." Odile lifted the lamp, and the light swung their shadows huge up the plastered wall. "Go to bed. Workshop at seven. If Mrs. Ashen is going to learn joinery at her age, someone will need to sweep up behind her, and I'm told that's you."

Tamsin climbed to her room under the eaves, where frost ferned the glass and her breath made ghosts. She should sleep. She had a full day of teaching a probable liar to cut a mortise.

But she lay awake a while longer, thinking of the ledger chained in its cabinet below, all those names and debts and signatures going back past her grandmother's grandmother — *we who work here agree to be counted* — and thinking how strange it was that the town's whole freedom lived in a book that trusted people, while the woman downstairs traveled chained to something she wouldn't show anyone, and counted doors instead of friends.

Two ways of keeping accounts, Tamsin thought, drifting off. One of them was going to break.

She intended to be in the room when it did.
