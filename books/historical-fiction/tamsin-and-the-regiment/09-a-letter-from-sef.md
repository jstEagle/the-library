## 9. A Letter from Sef

The letter came with the tallow delivery, which was the first strange thing about it.

Coral had learned, in the workshop's second week, that everything in Tamsin's Ford arrived by the same indifferent logic: the tallow man came up from the low farms once a month with candles and salt and whatever the river had seen fit to carry him, and he left parcels on Odile Vant's bench without ceremony, because a parcel was only another material to be worked. So when Odile held out a grimy square of oilcloth toward her one grey morning — "This has your name on it. Well, not your name. Some name." — Coral took it with hands that did not shake, because her hands never shook, because she had trained them not to for fifteen years.

The name written on it in Sef's cramped, backward-leaning hand was *Marla Fen*, a widow of Kessel Row who had died of fever nine years ago. It was an old dead-drop designation, retired before Coral had even finished her apprenticeship in courier craft. No living handler should have used it. No living person should have known it at all.

She waited until evening. She waited until Tamsin had gone home across the yard with lamp-oil smudged to her elbow and a running argument about drawer runners still half-finished on her lips; until Odile had banked the great hearth and gone up the stairs with the particular heavy tread of a woman who trusted her house to keep itself; until the workshop was nothing but settling wood and the tick of cooling iron. Then she sat on her cot in the loft above the finishing room, turned the oilcloth over twice, and opened it the way Sef had taught her — from the seal side first, so that if the letter proved to be a trap she could claim she'd found it unsealed among the tallow.

It was sealed with plain wax and a thumbnail pressed into it. Sef's thumb. She knew the whorls of it better than she knew her own reflection, because she had watched those thumbs for six formative years: turning cipher wheels, folding paper into birds, tapping out the rhythm on table-tops that meant *you are being watched, smile*.

*Marla,* the letter began. *If this reaches you, then the winter roads held and you are further from me than I can imagine, which is the only comfort I have.*

She read it standing, though her knees wanted otherwise.

*I am writing this in the last week of Thaw-Month, in the capital, which you will have heard is unwell. I will not dress it. The purge has come to our floor. They took Pellis on Tuesday. They took the twins on Thursday, together, as if separation would have been mercy. On Friday a very polite young man sat at my own desk and asked me, with great courtesy, whether I had ever handled correspondence for persons unknown, and I understood that my life was now measured in the time it took him to finish his tea.*

Coral lowered herself onto the cot's edge. The loft was cold. She had not noticed the cold until now, and could not seem to notice anything else either — not the smell of linseed rising through the floorboards, not the distant dog, not the river muttering under its first skin of ice.

*I burned what I could. You know which things. But there are letters in motion that cannot be recalled, yours among them, and so I write this instead: the truth, plainly, because plainly is the one cipher they never suspect.*

*You were told your dispatches serve the Republic's survival. That was true once. It stopped being true some years ago, and I did not tell you, because telling you would have endangered you, and because I told myself the work was still worth doing, and because I was a coward in all the small ways that matter most. Forgive me. Or don't. Both are honest responses and I have earned them equally.*

*The handlers you answer to now are not the handlers we served. Director Veyne's office answers to no ministry. The dispatches you carry are instruments, Marla, and the places they are aimed at are not enemies of the Republic. They are inconveniences to certain men. Learn the difference before it is too late to matter.*

Her eyes caught on the word *instruments* and refused to move past it for a long moment. Outside, wind worried the eaves. Somewhere below, the workshop's frame gave one of its deep wooden sighs, the sound of a building breathing in its sleep.

She thought of the case chained to her wrist for eleven days over the pass. She thought of how she had slept with it, washed beside it, memorized the weight of it against her ribs the way other people memorized a lover's shoulder. She had believed herself the republic's faithful servant, invisible, essential, clean. Now the letter lay in her lap saying otherwise in her mentor's handwriting, and the worst of it was not the accusation. The worst of it was that some part of her, some cold back room of herself, had already suspected, and had chosen not to look, because looking would have cost her the only identity she had.

*They will use you until you are used up,* Sef wrote. *Or until you see too much. Those are the two exits from their service, and I have watched colleagues take both.*

Then, near the end, the cramped hand grew smaller, as if the writer were leaning close:

*Last thing. I taught you every rule I had, and you learned them better than I did, and I am proud of you in a way I was never permitted to say aloud. But forget the rule about silence if you must forget any of them. Silence kept you alive. It will not keep you good. There is a difference, and I am afraid you are only now old enough to learn it, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry, because you deserve the truth more than you deserve comfort.*

*Trust no handler. Not Veyne, not the ones under him, not the friendly voice that gives you your next route. If a handler tells you a thing serves the Republic, ask who profits, and how soon, and whether anyone downriver will bleed. That is the whole of the craft, Marla. Everything else was just folding paper into birds.*

*If I do not send another letter, it is because the polite young man finished his tea. Burn this after reading. And find yourself one place, one bench, one set of hands that know you — not your cover, you — before the roads close forever.*

*S.*

There was no signature beyond the initial. There didn't need to be. She would have known the hand anywhere; she had copied it for practice as a girl, tracing his letters until her own writing bent backward like his, a habit she'd never entirely broken.

He was dead. She understood it the way one understands weather coming — not stated anywhere on the page, but present in every line, in the way the letter was both a confession and a farewell, in the fact that it had taken four months to reach her because whoever carried it had been running, or hiding, or dead themselves along some stretch of road. He had written it knowing she might read it over his corpse, so to speak, and had wasted none of it. No sentiment except where sentiment was instruction. That was Sef. That had always been Sef.

She sat in the dark loft until the moon moved off the window, and she did not cry. Fifteen years of training held. Her face stayed composed, her breath stayed level, her hands stayed quiet in her lap like folded paper. And somewhere inside, behind the composure, something enormous and silent shifted its weight — the way a bridge shifts before it decides.

* * *

In the morning she was at her bench before the others, working a length of ash into drawer sides, badly.

She had discovered, over these weeks, that bad joinery was a kind of honesty. The plane bit where she didn't intend it to. The grain ran contrary to her plans. Wood did not care about her covers or her ciphers; it simply required attention, total and undivided, and so for an hour each morning she was permitted to think of nothing but the next stroke, and it was the closest thing to rest she had known since Kessel Row.

But this morning the plane kept slipping, and her thoughts kept returning to the letter, which she had not burned.

That was itself a transgression. Sef had said burn this, and she had folded it instead into a false bottom beneath the lining of her sewing basket, sewn shut, invisible to any casual search — old habit, old cage. A courier who keeps evidence is a courier who hasn't yet decided whose side evidence serves. She turned that thought over while the shavings curled up around her feet.

"Your tenons are crying," said Tamsin, appearing at her shoulder with the suddenness she reserved especially for Coral, whom she seemed to regard as a puzzle box that might eventually open. "Look at this. You've got blowout on both cheeks. Were you planing drunk?"

"It's early for drinking."

"Not in this town during the dark months." Tamsin picked up the ruined stile, turned it in the light, and frowned — not at the wood, Coral realized, but at her. "You look terrible. Not insult-terrible. Ill-terrible."

"I slept poorly."

"You slept poorly," Tamsin repeated, in the tone of someone filing the phrase away in a drawer to be examined later. She was seventeen and had her mother's gift for making evasion feel like a physical object being handed over. "Well. There's porridge. Mother says eat or don't, but she made extra, which means eat."

She went. Coral listened to her boots cross the yard, and thought: *one place, one bench, one set of hands that know you.* Sef had written it as advice, and here she stood in a workshop full of benches, surrounded by hands that knew her only as a widowed seamstress's cousin named Marla Fen — a woman assembled from spare parts, a dead drop given flesh. The town had offered her a shape to inhabit. Sef's ghost was asking a harder question: whether she had anything underneath the shape.

*Silence kept you alive. It will not keep you good.*

She looked down at the ruined tenon. Blowout on both cheeks. Fifteen years of flawless tradecraft, and a piece of ash had exposed her in a morning.

* * *

Odile found her at midday in the archive room.

The town's archive was not impressive to look at — a stone-floored room behind the council hall, shelves of ledgers bound in oiled canvas, boxes of contracts and charters going back past the founding. But Coral had spent three weeks finding reasons to be in it, dusting, copying, re-shelving, and Odile Vant missed nothing that happened in her orbit.

"The Kessel Row woman," Odile said, without preamble, setting down a lamp. "That letter."

Coral kept her face pleasant and empty, the traveler's face. "I don't know what you—"

"I've run this shop thirty years. I know what grief looks like holding a chisel." Odile pulled a stool over and sat, heavily, the way she did everything — as if furniture should be grateful for the opportunity. "I'm not asking what was in it. I'm asking whether it changes what you're doing in my town."

The directness of it knocked something loose. In the capital, in the service, questions were never asked straight; they were asked sideways, in tea, in errands, in silences with edges. Odile simply sat there, forearms on knees, waiting, as though truth were a normal currency between two people in a room.

"My teacher died," Coral heard herself say. It came out flat, factual, the report-voice. Then, unexpectedly, less flat: "In the capital. In the purges. I don't know exactly when. The letter took months to reach me. He—" She stopped. Started again. "He taught me everything. And the letter says the work I've been doing isn't what I was told it was."

Odile nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that unsurprise frightened Coral more than anger would have.

"And the dispatches you carry," Odile said. "The ones in that case you sleep with like a child sleeps with a knife. Are they what you were told they were?"

The question hung in the lamplight. Coral felt the old reflexes rise — deflect, minimize, perform harmlessness — and for the first time in fifteen years she watched herself decline to obey them. Not fully. Not yet. But she let the silence stretch one beat past safe, and in that beat she said:

"I don't know anymore. That's the truth. I need to decode them properly, and I haven't dared, because—" Because decoding them would make it real. Because as long as the case stayed closed she could remain a courier rather than a conspirator. Because ignorance had been her last shelter and she wasn't ready to leave it.

"Because you're afraid of what you'll find," Odile finished for her. She rose, joints cracking, and took up the lamp. "In this Regiment we keep books. Every debt, every credit, every lie a neighbor told a neighbor about a fence line — it goes in the book, because a thing unwritten becomes a thing unsolvable. Your secrets are debts, girl. They accrue interest." At the door she paused, backlit, formidable. "When you're ready to open the ledger, come to me. Not the council. Me. I've been owed apologies by governments before. I know how they're collected."

She left. Coral stood alone among the shelves of the town's accumulated memory, and understood that she had just been seen — not her cover, her — for the first time since Sef had died, possibly for the first time since Sef had first found her starving in a warehouse district and said, *you have quick eyes; they can be used for hiding or for seeing, choose.*

* * *

That night she took out the letter again, along with the dispatch case.

She set them side by side on the cot: the oilcloth packet and the black lacquered case, chained still to the old bracelet she hadn't removed in eleven weeks. Two objects. One from a dead man who had loved her badly and honestly; one from a director's office that had used her cleanly and without love. Between them, the whole architecture of her life, and it suddenly looked flimsy — paper and lacquer, wax and chain. Things that burned.

*Ask who profits, and how soon, and whether anyone downriver will bleed.*

She thought of Merrick Hale, expected downriver any week now on the last ferry before the freeze — the handler's polite eyes, the courteous voice. She thought of Tamsin arguing about drawer runners. Of Odile's ledger. Of the bridge timbers groaning under early snow, the ferryman's refusal, the trap of mountains and winter that had seemed, weeks ago, like the end of her mission and now felt more like a door she had walked through without knowing.

Her thumb found the case's lock. The cipher wheel was hidden in the false bottom of her sewing basket, three turns from its working position. She had decoded dispatches in stagecoach jolts and latrine stalls and once, memorably, in a confessional booth. She could do it here, tonight, in an hour.

She didn't. Not quite yet. Instead she did the thing Sef had actually asked: she fed the letter to the candle flame and watched it curl, his handwriting blooming orange and vanishing — *find yourself one place, one bench, one set of hands* — until nothing remained but ash in the dish and the words printed permanently behind her eyes.

Then she picked up the dispatch case and held it in both hands, feeling its true weight for the first time.

Tomorrow, she promised the dark. Tomorrow I'll open it.

And far downriver, on a ferry beating upstream against the last open water, a man in a merchant's coat checked his papers and smiled at the boatman, and asked, casually, how the roads above the falls had been this season — and whether many strangers had come through before the snows.
