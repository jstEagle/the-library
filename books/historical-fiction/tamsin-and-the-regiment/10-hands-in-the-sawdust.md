## 10. Hands in the Sawdust

Tamsin had decided, by the third morning of the stranger's presence in the workshop, that Coral was a fraud.

Not a dangerous fraud — she wasn't sure yet what kind she was — but a fraud all the same. A woman who claimed to be a seamstress's cousin, down from the hill towns for the winter, and who could not so much as thread a needle without stabbing herself. Tamsin had watched her do it twice. The second time, Coral had sworn — quietly, under her breath, in a language that wasn't Vessany's trade-tongue or any dialect Tamsin knew — and then smiled at her as though nothing had happened, and asked, with perfect pleasantness, whether the workshop ever took on mending work.

Nobody came down from the hill towns anymore. Nobody crossed the pass this late in the season. And nobody with hands like that had ever sewn anything but lies.

So when her mother said, over breakfast, "The cousin can work the bench beside you today. We're short since Pell went up-valley," Tamsin said nothing and ate her porridge and thought: *Good.* Because there were two ways to learn what a person was, and asking questions got you nowhere with this one. You had to put a chisel in her hand and watch what happened.

The workshop smelled the way it always did in deep winter — resin and cold iron and the tallow lamps' faint animal sweetness, the stove ticking as it warmed. Frost ferns still feathered the inside of the north windows at this hour, and the light came through them blue-white and thin, laying pale bars across the long bench where six people could work shoulder to shoulder. Odile Vant ran the morning the way she ran everything: by saying almost nothing. She set out stock, set out tools, pointed once at the pile of drawer fronts that needed dovetailing, and the day began, because everyone in the Regiment understood that being pointed at by Odile was not an instruction so much as an inheritance.

Tamsin took the seat at the far end. She left the space beside her open, and made sure Coral noticed it was open, and made sure Coral noticed *her* noticing.

"You'll want to mark your lines first," Tamsin said, when Coral stood over the drawer front the way a person stands over a sleeping animal they've been asked to groom. "Pencil. Light. You can sand out pencil."

"I know," Coral said.

"Mark them anyway."

A pause. Then Coral picked up the pencil and marked her lines, light as breath, exactly right — and Tamsin felt something shift in her chest, some small door swinging on its hinge. Because that was not how a fraud held a pencil. That was how someone held it who had been *taught*, once, carefully, and had let the teaching rust.

"Where'd you learn to measure?" Tamsin asked, too casually.

"Nowhere," said Coral, and began sawing.

* * *

It went badly from there, which suited Tamsin fine, because watching it go badly told her more than an hour of conversation ever would have.

Coral sawed the way she walked — controlled, economical, silent — and that was the whole problem. Joinery was not silent work. It was argument and apology, wood talking back, blade wandering, grain turning against you mid-cut. You had to listen and adjust and swear and start again, and Coral did none of those things. She cut one long confident stroke straight off her line and through the tail of the first dovetail as though the wood had no opinion worth consulting.

"That's waste," Tamsin said.

"I see that."

"You cut on the waste side of the line. Always the waste side. The line stays."

Coral looked at her. For just a moment — less than a moment — something moved behind her eyes, something tired and sharp, like a bird shifting in a dark room. Then it was gone and the pleasant traveler's face was back, and she said, "Show me again?"

So Tamsin showed her. She took Coral's hand — it was the fastest way, and besides, she wanted to — and set it on the saw's grip and drew the first stroke slow, slow enough to feel the teeth bite, saying, "Feel it? When it bites true you'll feel it pull itself along. When it wanders it goes quiet. Sawing is listening." And Coral's hand under hers went very still, stiller than a hand should go, and for a heartbeat Tamsin thought she might pull away and run — actually run, out the door into the snow, over whatever cliff this woman kept inside herself.

She didn't. She breathed out, once, long and careful, and said, "Again," and cut the next one on the waste side of the line, and it was nearly true.

"There," Tamsin said, and heard her own voice come out warmer than she'd planned, and covered it by adding, "Don't get proud. You'll ruin the next four."

She ruined the next four.

By midmorning the floor around Coral's station was drifted with shavings and spoiled offcuts, and Marta from the wheel-bench had begun glancing over between pulls of the bow, and old Sennet had abandoned all pretense of his own work entirely and sat sharpening the same chisel he'd been sharpening since breakfast, watching over his spectacles with the naked delight of a man at a puppet show. Tamsin felt the laughter building in her own chest and tried to hold it down, because her mother's rule was that you didn't laugh at learners — everyone in the Regiment had been a learner once — but Coral looked up from her fifth ruined tail, sawdust in her black hair and a red blister rising on her palm, and caught Tamsin's expression, and said dryly:

"Go ahead."

"I'm not—"

"You are. I can hear it rattling in you like a loose peg. Go ahead."

And Tamsin laughed — a real one, helpless, bent over the bench — and the workshop laughed with her, kindly, the way the Regiment laughed, and to Tamsin's astonishment Coral laughed too. Not the polite traveler's laugh she'd been wearing all week, the one fitted like a borrowed coat. Something shorter, rougher, surprised out of her. It changed her whole face. It made her look, for one unguarded instant, about Tamsin's own age.

Then it was gone, shuttered away, and Coral bent back to her ruined drawer front, and Tamsin thought: *There you are. Whoever you are.*

* * *

They broke for bread and small beer at noon, sitting on the benches with their feet on rails, and Tamsin used the hour the way she used all hours: asking questions.

"You hold the pencil like a clerk," she said. "And your hands are soft except the blisters. And yesterday you read the tally board from across the room, which half our apprentices can't do at arm's length. So. Seamstress's cousin."

"Is that a question?"

"It's the shape of one."

Coral turned her bread over in her fingers. Around them the workshop ate and talked — Marta arguing freight rates with Sennet, two of the younger boys reenacting a sled disaster with escalating casualties — the ordinary noise of the Regiment, which Tamsin had grown up thinking every place made, and which she was only now, with a stranger in the room, beginning to understand was rare.

"My mother's family kept accounts," Coral said at last. "Before."

"Before what?"

Another pause, longer. The lamp flame leaned in the draft. "Before things ended," Coral said. "Where I come from, things end. Here it seems things get repaired instead." She glanced up, and there it was again, that flicker behind the shutters — not quite defiance, not quite appeal. "That must seem strange to you."

"It seems sad," Tamsin said plainly, because plainness was the only tool she'd found that made a dent in this woman. "You say it like it's weather."

"It is weather," Coral said. "For some people it's the only weather there is." And she ate her bread and asked no questions in return, which was itself an answer — couriers and spies never asked questions, Tamsin's mother had taught her that much, because questions were debts and these people paid nothing back — and Tamsin sat there feeling the shape of the wall around Coral, high and smooth and load-bearing, and thought not *how do I climb it* but, unexpectedly, *who built it, and what were they keeping out?*

* * *

The afternoon went better. Or differently, anyway.

Odile passed their bench twice without comment, which Tamsin knew for a form of approval — her mother's silences were graded, and this one was mild — and on the third pass she stopped, looked at Coral's sixth attempt at a tail, now nearly square, and said only: "Your saw is fighting you because you're holding it like a weapon. It's a conversation. Loosen your wrist." Then she moved on, and Coral stared after her with an expression Tamsin couldn't read at all, something almost like grief.

"She scares you," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question either.

"No one here scares me," Coral said. Too fast. Too flat. And then, hearing it land wrong, she added, quieter, "She reminds me of someone."

"Sef," Tamsin said, before she could stop herself.

Coral went very still. "What did you say?"

"Sef." Tamsin shrugged, suddenly aware she'd stepped somewhere she hadn't been invited. "The archive-keeper mentioned him. He wrote to my mother years ago — letters, when I was little. Some courier man downriver who taught her cipher-work back before the Regiment closed its rolls, back when the republic still pretended to like us. She burned most of them. But I remember the name." She watched Coral's face with the particular hunger she'd learned to hide from everyone but herself. "Funny. Same look you just made."

"The look of a woman remembering a dead man," Coral said, and picked up her saw, and that was the end of the conversation — but not, Tamsin noted, the end of the tremor in her hands, which took three cuts to settle.

*Dead man.* Filed away. Tamsin had her mother's habit of keeping books, even in her head.

* * *

Near dusk, when the lamps were lit and the frost was creeping back across the windows from the outside, Tamsin found Coral standing at the scrap bin, picking through the spoiled offcuts of her own disastrous day. She'd salvaged three pieces — poor ones, split at the edges — and was looking at them the way a person looks at evidence.

"You keep the failures," Tamsin said from the doorway.

"They're mine," Coral said simply. "I made them. It seemed wrong to throw them away." Then, catching herself, as if the sentence had said more than she meant: "Waste is waste."

But Tamsin had already seen it — had already seen the whole strange shape of the woman, standing in the lamplight holding her ruined wood like a soldier holding letters from home. And something in her chest that had been suspicious for eleven days decided, not entirely, not safely, but a little, to be warm instead.

She crossed the room and took the worst of the three scraps out of Coral's hands and set it on the bench, and pinned it, and handed her a chisel.

"One more lesson," she said. "Last one today. You're going to make that piece into something on purpose. A mistake you chose is called design."

"And what would I make?"

Tamsin grinned. "A dove. For your first drawer front. Every joiner in the Regiment carves one into their first finished piece — it means *I was bad once too*. Mother's rule. Even she has one, and she'll deny it."

Catal looked down at the chisel in her blistered hand. The lamp threw her shadow long against the wall, and for a moment she looked like a woman standing at a door deciding which side of it to live on.

"All right," she said finally. "Teach me the dove."

So Tamsin stood behind her — closer than strictly necessary, though she'd have denied that under oath — and guided her through the first cuts, the shallow v of the wing, the little notch of the eye, and Coral worked slowly, tongue between her teeth, sawdust settling gold in the lamplight, and the stove ticked, and somewhere across town a dog barked at the frozen river, and Tamsin thought: *whatever you're carrying, cousin, whoever you're running from — you're learning our hands now. And hands remember.*

They carved until the lamps burned low. The dove came out crooked, one wing thicker than the other, its eye a blind white knot.

"It's terrible," Coral said, turning it over.

"It's yours," Tamsin said. "Keep it."

Coral kept it. Tamsin saw her slip it into her pocket, into the place where a person keeps the thing they'd grab first if the house caught fire, and said nothing, and walked her to the door of the widow Anselm's house where she lodged, and stood in the snow a moment after the door closed, breathing white air, happier than she could account for.

Behind her, up the street, a lantern was coming down from the ferry landing through the falling snow — a stranger's lantern, moving fast, and Tamsin stood a moment watching it, because strangers arriving at night in early winter were rare as doves, and the Regiment kept its own books on such things.

She didn't know yet whose lantern it was. But she remembered, later, exactly how the snow had sounded that night: soft, and patient, and falling on everything equally, on the honest and the hidden alike.
