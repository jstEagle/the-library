## 11. The Visitor from Downriver

The river told them first.

Coral heard it in the way the town spoke that morning — a loosening of voices in the workshop, heads turning toward the water whenever the door swung open to admit a knife-edge of cold air. By midmorning the word had traveled the length of the long room and back again three times: *the ferry's coming up one last time before the freeze.*

She was sanding the inside curve of a cradle rocker when Tamsin said it directly to her, leaning close over the bench so the shavings between them wouldn't hear.

"Ferryman Vetch is running the crossing today. Last run." Tamsin's breath fogged even indoors; the workshop's great stove fought a losing war against November. "He only does that when someone's paid him enough to risk it. Nobody from here would."

Coral kept her hands moving along the grain. Fine grit, even pressure, no hurry. Fifteen years of practice had taught her that the body could lie beautifully if the hands were given honest work to do while the mind ran its calculations.

"Perhaps a merchant," she said.

"Merchants come in autumn, when the prices are known. This is past autumn." Tamsin watched her with those quick grey eyes that missed less than any child Coral had ever met. "It'll be someone who wants something badly."

*Or someone sent to want something badly,* Coral thought, and felt the old cold open in her chest like a door onto winter.

* * *

She had counted the days, as she always did. The bridge had closed eleven days ago; the river needed perhaps another week of hard nights before it would bear a man's weight, two weeks before it would bear cargo. That gave her a window — narrow but real — in which she was sealed into Tamsin's Ford with her dispatch case hidden beneath the loose hearthstone in Widow Anselm's back bedroom, and sealed out everyone who might come asking after it.

A courier's arithmetic was simple: distance divided by speed, subtracted from time remaining. She had run the numbers every morning since the bridge fell and found them comforting. Whatever moved against her would have to move by water now, and the water was closing like an eye.

She had not accounted for a ferryman's greed.

"Vetch is a coward," Odile Vant said at the noon table, saying aloud what the whole room was thinking. She sat at the head of the long trestle where the masters took their meal, and though there was no crown in Tamsin's Ford, the chair at that end might as well have been a throne for all the deference that gathered around it. "He won't cross for coin alone. He'll cross because whoever's paying him frightened him worse than the ice does. I'd know who that is before supper."

Coral ate her bread and beans and said nothing, which was easy, because saying nothing was the one craft she had truly mastered.

Around her the talk went on — rations, roof timber, whether the kiln could be fired one more time before deep cold — and she let it wash over her while she performed the interior work that no one at the table could see. If a republican agent had bought the last crossing, he had done it because the republic believed its courier was still reachable. Reachable meant south of the river. South of the river meant the search had narrowed to the Ford itself, or the villages below it, and Merrick Hale — if it was Hale; if the handlers had spent their best hound rather than a common tracker — did not narrow searches idly.

She looked down the table at the faces she had come to know across five weeks of sawdust and shared bread: the archive-keeper Pell with his ink-stained cuffs; the smith Doss arguing cheerfully about iron; Odile, reading the room the way Coral read coded text, line by line, for what lay under the surface. Five weeks. Long enough to learn the weight of a drawknife. Not long enough, she realized with a small cold shock, to stop caring how these people fared if trouble came looking for her among them.

That was the danger of shelter. It softened the edges you leaned on.

"You're quiet today, Mistress Cora," Odile said, without looking at her.

"I'm always quiet, mistress."

"Aye, and mostly it's a comfortable sort of quiet." Now Odile did look at her, and her eyes were the brown of seasoned walnut, patient and unhurried and impossible to fool for long. "Today it has corners in it."

Coral smiled the smile she'd worn through checkpoints and customs houses and one memorable interrogation in a customs shed at Vell's Landing: apologetic, slightly dull, forgettable within the minute. "I slept poorly. The widow's cat takes the warm side of the bed."

Someone laughed. The moment passed, the way moments passed when you were good at moving them along. But Odile held her gaze a heartbeat longer than comfort allowed, and Coral understood that the master joiner had filed something away in whatever ledger she kept behind those walnut eyes.

* * *

They came up from the landing at midafternoon, and the whole town found reasons to be outdoors.

Coral stood at the workshop's open door with a broom in her hands — a prop, a reason, an ordinary woman sweeping sawdust into the cold air — and watched the horsemen climb the switchback road from the water. There were four of them: Vetch the ferryman riding sullen and hunched, two drovers' men hired for muscle, and a fourth rider who did not need an escort to be dangerous.

Merrick Hale had changed nothing about himself in the eighteen months since she'd last seen him, and everything about himself since she'd first known him. He rode a good grey horse badly, deliberately, the seat of a man who wished to be taken for a clerk. His coat was good wool gone soft at the elbows. He wore his thinning hair combed forward over a broad, mild face built for trustworthiness, and he smiled at the children who ran alongside the horses the way a man smiles when he genuinely likes children and wants you to know it.

Only his hands betrayed him. They were very still. Clerks' hands fidgeted; couriers' hands checked; hunters' hands rested, because they had learned that reaching is a thing you do exactly once per career.

Coral swept. Her pulse, she noted with detached professional interest, had not risen above resting rate. That was Sef's training holding, fifteen years deep: *fear is fuel, girl, burn it slow or it burns you.* But underneath the stillness she felt the cage drawing in around her, bar by bar. The last road out was closing. The hunter was climbing the hill. And between them stood an entire town of people who had fed her and paid her and taught her the difference between pine and spruce, none of whom knew they were standing inside her problem.

Hale reined in at the square before the meeting hall, dismounted, and stood a moment surveying Tamsin's Ford with the frank appreciation of a traveler. Then he produced — Coral's stomach tightened — a small ledger book, opened it, and made a note.

He was writing down the town. Names, numbers, roofs, boats, whatever a man cataloguing a place about to be used would write. She had seen officers do it in the war's early years, before the retreats began. It was the gesture of ownership preceding possession.

"He's noting our chimneys," Tamsin said at her shoulder. Coral hadn't heard the girl approach; she made a mental note of her own failure and turned a mild face toward her.

"What for?"

"How should I know? Counting what we've got, same as crows count lambs." Tamsin's chin was up, her arms crossed, her whole body radiating the particular indignation of the young watching strangers appraise their home. "Vetch looks ashamed of himself. Good. He ought to."

"Careful," Coral said quietly.

Tamsin glanced at her. "Of what?"

"Strangers who smile at children." Coral returned to her sweeping. "The ones who do that are usually the ones you should count first."

* * *

Hale presented himself at the meeting hall within the hour, and by evening the story of him was all through the town, retold and reshaped like timber passed hand to hand. He called himself an agent of the Republic's Bureau of Supply — plausible, Coral conceded; the Bureau's remit had grown fat and grasping in the war's last years — and he came, he said, with an offer so generous the council would think him mad to make it. Protection, subsidy, guaranteed contracts: in exchange for certain considerations regarding the Regiment's militia charter and its rolls.

He was staying at the inn. He was charming at supper. He asked each person he met their name and wrote it in his little book, and people gave it, because names are gifts this world teaches us to hand over freely.

Coral learned all of this secondhand, from the safety of Widow Anselm's kitchen, where she helped salt pork and listened to the widow's running commentary on the town's gossip with the attention of a scholar consulting primary sources.

"Handsome enough," the widow allowed, "in a soft way. Soft men are either harmless or the worst kind. No middle." She pressed the salt down into the brine barrel with both fists. "Odile Vant gave him ten minutes and no more. Pell wouldn't let him near the archives without the full council sitting. Brann, though—" A sniff. "Brann walked him round the granary like they were sweethearts. An hour and more."

Coral's hands went still over the pork. *Brann.* Councilor Brann, big-voiced and ambitious, who had argued at the ration debate for selling surplus downriver at profit rather than storing against hunger — the only voice in that hall that had sounded, to her trained ear, like a voice already halfway sold.

"Brann's ambitious," she offered carefully.

"Brann's hungry," the widow corrected. "Different animal. Ambition climbs. Hunger bites whatever's nearest." She looked at Coral with sudden directness, and for a moment Coral felt seen straight through, the way one feels seen by cats and dying people. "You watch yourself too, girl. Strangers bring weather, and you're the newest stranger this town's had."

"The newest," Coral agreed, "excepting the gentleman at the inn."

"Excepting him." The widow went back to her salting. "And I'll thank you both to be gone before spring, if it's all the same."

It was not all the same. That was the trouble. Somewhere in five weeks of benches and bread and Tamsin's relentless, unguarded curiosity, *gone by spring* had stopped being the plan and started being the threat. Coral turned that fact over in herself and found it alarming, the way a joiner finds rot alarming — not because of what it is, but because of how far it has quietly spread.

* * *

He came to the workshop on the third day.

She was at the bench — her bench now, the one Odile had grudgingly assigned her between the old turner Wace and the window — working a mortise with a chisel older than the republic, when the door opened and the temperature of the room changed. Not literally. But she felt it, the way animals feel weather: a stillness passing through the joiners, a fractional straightening of backs.

"Master Vant." Hale's voice was warm, unhurried, pitched to carry just far enough. "Forgive the interruption. I'm told the Regiment's workshops supply half the valley's joinery, and I confess I couldn't leave without seeing it."

"You could," Odile said, without turning from the frame she was fitting. "Plenty do."

"But I'm weak." He laughed, and several of the younger joiners laughed with him, caught off guard by his charm. He drifted down the aisle between benches with his hands clasped behind him, admiring, exclaiming softly, and Coral understood that the tour was cover for the true errand, as it always was: he was reading faces. Looking for the flicker. Every agent knew that somewhere in any room there might be one person whose pulse answered to his presence, and the whole art of the hunt was walking slowly enough to catch it.

So she became wood.

It was a discipline Sef had drilled into her until it lived below thought: *you are not hiding, hiding draws the eye; you are furniture, furniture is invisible because it belongs.* She bent to her mortise. She blew shavings from the cut. When his shadow crossed her bench she let her chisel pause — naturally, the way any apprentice pauses when a stranger leans over her work — and lifted her face into the mild, slightly anxious blankness of Cora the seamstress's cousin, the widow's house-guest, the woman who swept sawdust and was learning, badly, to cut a joint.

Hale looked at her.

His gaze rested on her for two seconds, maybe three — long enough that Wace beside her shifted and muttered. In those seconds Coral did the hardest work of her life, which was nothing at all: no swallowed breath, no darted glance, no tremor at the corner of the mouth. She was a tired woman in a borrowed apron with sawdust in her hair, faintly embarrassed by her crooked mortise, waiting for the important people to finish talking so the day could go on.

"New to the trade?" Hale asked pleasantly.

"She's winter help," Odile said, arriving at the bench like a wagon arriving, taking up all the space in the aisle. "Learning which end of a chisel to hold. Cora — show the gentleman your joint, since he's interested."

It was a test dressed as a joke, and Coral took the gamble of honesty. She turned the rail so its gappy, splintered mortise faced upward, and said, ruefully, "It wants another month. Possibly two. Possibly a different trade."

Hale laughed — a real laugh, or a perfect copy of one — and moved on. At the end of the aisle he paused, looked back once along the row of bending heads, and made a small note in his book.

Coral put her chisel back to the wood and cut the mortise clean through to the waste side, ruining it entirely, and did not care.

* * *

That night she climbed to Widow Anselm's loft and lay beneath the rafters listening to the wind come down off the pass, and made herself do the arithmetic she had been avoiding.

Hale had come up on the last ferry. Therefore he had left downriver before the freeze was certain — therefore he had been dispatched days ago, before the bridge fell, when the handlers still believed their courier might be anywhere along the northern road. Which meant his arrival here was not proof he suspected the Ford. It was diligence. He was sweeping the map, town by town, the way you sweep a floor: thoroughly, patiently, without assuming where the dust lies.

But he had looked at her three seconds too long. Or she had imagined it. Both possibilities required identical responses.

She got up in the dark, eased the hearthstone aside, and drew the dispatch case out of its nest of rag. The chain was still there, cold as riverbed stone, coiled where she'd wound it. She did not light a candle. She sat with the case on her knees and let her fingers learn it in the blackness — the worn leather, the brass lock, the seam along the bottom where the false papers lay over the true ones — and she asked herself the question she had been refusing to ask.

If he knocks on this door tomorrow and says *Courier Okonkwo, I've come to take you home*, what will she do?

Fifteen years ago the answer would have been instant: give him the case, give the case's contents, vanish downriver and be forgotten by spring. That was the covenant. The republic protected her invisibility; she protected its secrets; neither party ever had to be human to the other.

But the republic had burned villages to slow an enemy's advance. The republic had let Vell's Landing starve rather than divert grain. And the dispatches chained to her wrist — she still didn't know all of what they contained; the second cipher layer had defeated her twice — were heavy in a way paper should not be heavy, and growing heavier every night she lay under a stranger's roof eating a stranger's bread.

Below her, through the floorboards, she heard the widow turn over in her sleep, mutter something to a husband twenty years dead, and settle. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a life that had never once required a code name.

Coral slid the case back beneath the hearthstone, pressed the stone home, and dusted her palms.

In the morning, she decided, she would find a reason to walk past the inn. You did not defeat a hunter by hiding from him; Sef had taught her that too. You learned his habits, his contacts, his doubts. You got close enough to see which way his eyes moved when he lied.

She did not yet know that tomorrow morning, before she could take a single step toward the inn, Merrick Hale would save her the trouble — that he would be standing in the workshop doorway at first light, hat in hand, smiling his trustworthy smile, asking Odile Vant a question that stopped Coral's blood:

*"The widow Anselm's cousin — the one learning joinery — I wonder if I might have a word with her. We may have mutual friends downriver."*
