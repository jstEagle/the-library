## 12. Invisible by Design

The seamstress's name was Marta Ellery, and she had buried a husband, two sons, and — by the look of her parlor — every bright color she'd ever owned. Her house sat at the crook of the lane where it bent toward the millrace, and its windows were hung with finished work: shirtsleeves drying on the line like pale flags of surrender.

"You'll be my cousin's girl," Marta said that first morning, not looking up from her basting. "Come down from Vessany-side after your man died. Grief made you quiet. That part won't need much acting."

Coral stood in the doorway with her one bag and her hands folded, and understood that Odile Vant had already spoken to this woman, and that whatever Odile had said, Marta had agreed to it without asking why. That was the Regiment's way, she was learning. Questions were currency here, spent carefully, and Odile Vant did not waste money.

"I'm grateful," Coral said. "For the room."

"Gratitude doesn't hem a skirt." Marta bit her thread. "You can sew?"

"A little."

"Then you'll learn more. Sit. Watch my hands."

So began the strangest fortnight of Coral's life: hiding inside the most visible room in town. A courier's instinct was to find the dark corners, the alleys, the shuttered upper floors. But Sef had taught her better than that, once, in a lesson she'd half-forgotten until now. *The best place to hide is where everyone can see you and no one looks.* A woman sewing in a window all day became furniture. Furniture was never interviewed.

She learned the work badly at first and then less badly. Her fingers, trained for cipher wheels and wax seals and the delicate business of lifting a seal intact with a heated knife, proved surprisingly willing to learn a running stitch. Marta corrected her grip forty times a day and praised her once, obliquely, by handing her a whole shirt to finish alone.

"Grief made you quiet," the old woman repeated sometimes, apropos of nothing, and Coral would nod, and the lie would sit between them like a third person at the table. It bothered her more than she expected. She had lied to border guards, to innkeepers, to a colonel's wife who'd wept on her shoulder for an hour about a son lost at sea while Coral memorized the son's regimental number off the mantel photograph. Lies were tools; she kept them sharp and didn't mourn them. But Marta fed her barley soup and complained about the price of indigo dye and left the good chair near the fire for her without ever saying so, and the lies started to feel like coins stolen from a specific pocket rather than picked up off an anonymous street.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You've been warm before. Warmth is weather. It passes.*

* * *

Merrick Hale arrived on the ninth day, though Coral didn't see him arrive. She saw him the way the town saw him: gradually, then everywhere.

He lodged at the ferryman's sister's house — the last lodging available before the freeze — and presented himself in the workshop square on a bright cold morning as what he appeared to be: a surveyor's clerk, downriver-born, traveling to assess bridge timber for his employers. He wore a good coat worn carefully, boots resoled twice, and the smile of a man who found everything mildly delightful. He was perhaps forty, sandy-haired going grey at the temples, with a face built to be forgotten — which was how Coral knew, before he said ten words, exactly what he was. Nobody worked that hard at being forgettable by accident. It was her own craft walking around in another man's body.

She watched him from Marta's window while pretending to match thread colors. He moved through the square unhurried, stopping to admire the joiners' racks, laughing at something the cooper said, buying a twist of tobacco he probably didn't use. Making purchases. Making change. Making sure people held things he'd touched, looked at his face, answered his questions — small ones, harmless ones, each one a hook baited with nothing.

*How long has the bridge been closed? Early this year, was it? And the ferry still runs? Until when? Who decides?*

By evening, three separate customers had mentioned the pleasant downriver clerk to Marta, and Coral had assembled his route through the town from their chatter like a birdwatcher plotting flight paths. He'd begun at the council hall. Then the archive — the *archive*, her stomach clenched — though the archive-keeper, a dry stick named Pellows, reportedly told him the records were closed to outsiders until spring. Then the mill, the smithy, the tavern called the Ferry Bell.

"He's asking after travelers," Marta said, snipping a seam. "New faces. Says his employer lost a shipment and thinks a carrier went off with it."

"What do people tell him?"

"That the only new face in Tamsin's Ford is mine." Marta's needle paused, just for a beat, and resumed. "I told him about you myself. Cousin's girl, widowed, come over the pass before the snows. Sad creature, half-deaf in the left ear since a childhood fever — don't ask me why I added that, it came out natural."

Coral's breath went out slowly. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Deafness is useful. People forgive a deaf woman anything — not answering, staring, standing too close. My mother was deaf in one ear and heard everything worth hearing." Marta bit her thread and spat it. "Now. When he comes here, and he will, because he's working his way through every house like a man counting fence posts — you'll be mending in the back room. You'll come out when I call you, you'll answer what's asked, and you'll be duller than dishwater. Can you be dull?"

Coral thought of fifteen years of being dull. Of practicing a limp she didn't have, a stammer she didn't have, a dead husband she'd invented so many times she'd caught herself leaving flowers. "I'm told I'm very good at it."

* * *

He came on the fourth day, in the blue hour before supper, when the light through the west window turned the parlor the color of weak tea.

"Mistress Ellery." Hale filled the doorway with practiced harmlessness, cap in hand, snow melting on his shoulders. "Forgive the hour. I'm making calls before the freeze shuts us all in together — thought I'd pay my respects while there's still a road."

"There's no road," Marta said comfortably. "There's a river and a bad idea. Sit, if you're sitting."

He laughed, sat, accepted tea he didn't drink — Coral noted that, the untouched cup, a man keeping his hands free and his head clear — and performed ten minutes of small talk about timber prices and the ferryman's stubbornness. Coral sat in the back room with the door ajar, working a seam she could have done blind, listening with the whole surface of her skin.

Then Marta called, "Niece! Bring the mending basket — the gray coat wants its buttons seen to."

And Coral walked into the room carrying her own cage on her arm.

Up close, Hale was unremarkable in the way of well-made weapons. His eyes did the thing she'd known they would: passed over her face once, catalogued, dismissed. Brown hair going grey, brown eyes, a widow's plain dress, chapped hands with a thimble-callus forming. Nothing. Less than nothing. She let her gaze rest slightly past his left ear — thank you, Marta — and gave him the shy, apologetic half-smile of a woman who found strangers tiring.

"This is my cousin's girl," Marta said. "Widowed in the autumn. From over the pass."

"Ah." Gentle sympathy, expertly dosed. "My condolences, mistress. These are hard years to lose anyone." He turned back to Marta as if she were the interesting one. "You'll have seen strange traffic on the roads this season, I imagine. With the war moving about the way it does."

"People move about," Marta said. "War or no war."

"Of course. Of course." He warmed his hands at the fire he hadn't been offered. "It's only — my employer lost a courier. Dispatches, you understand, government business, terribly dull but terribly important to the men who care about such things. A woman, as it happens. Clever with codes, they say. Last seen heading for the high passes." He said it lightly, a story told for entertainment, and watched the fire while he told it, and watched Marta's reflection in the window glass while he told it, and Coral sat with her buttons and felt the old familiar cold slide up her spine and settle in behind her breastbone like a tenant paying rent.

"Fancy," Marta said. "A woman with codes. What would she want with our little town? We've got nothing here but sawdust and opinions."

"All the better reason to check, surely. A clever woman hides where nobody expects cleverness." He smiled at his own wit, then at Coral, kindly, including her the way one includes a child or a chair. "What do you think, mistress? Would you know a fugitive if you saw one?"

Coral let the silence stretch a half-second too long — the deaf ear, the gathering of attention — then looked up with mild, empty eyes.

"Beg pardon?" she said. "I wasn't attending."

"Would you know a fugitive," he repeated, louder, patient, *kind*, "if you saw one?"

She considered the question with the gravity of someone considering weather. "I suppose they'd look frightened," she said. "I was frightened enough, coming over the pass. Everyone looks frightened this time of year." She returned to her buttons. "Is your courier frightened, sir?"

Something flickered behind his eyes — not suspicion; appraisal. The appraiser's pause. Then he laughed, warm as mulled wine. "Probably freezing, wherever she is. Well." He rose, thanked Marta for tea she hadn't drunk, complimented the shirts on the line, and paused at the door just long enough to say, "If anything odd turns up — papers, strangers, a case chained to anybody's wrist — the ferryman's sister knows where I lodge. I'd hate for trouble to find this town unannounced."

The door closed. The snow outside creaked under his boots, going away.

Coral sat very still and finished the button she was on, and the next one, and the next, because her hands knew what to do even when the rest of her had gone somewhere white and screaming. Fifteen years of practice, and it came down to this: sew the button. Sew the next button. Terror managed through performance, performance worn smooth as river stone, and underneath it all the same girl Sef had trained in a cellar in Vessany City, learning that fear was a thing you folded small and put away in an inside pocket.

"Coral." Marta's voice, low. "Look at me."

She looked. The old woman's eyes were sharp as awls.

"You knew that man's trade before he got his boot in the door. I saw your face."

"Did you?"

"I'm old, not blind." Marta took the coat from her lap and set it aside. "I won't ask. But hear me: that clerk asked questions in the Ferry Bell last night about whether the Regiment keeps written rolls of its militia. Asked Brann, of all people, and Brann talked his ear off, which tells you what Brann's worth. Whatever game you're playing in my back room, girl — play it fast. Men like that don't knock twice out of politeness."

* * *

That night Coral lay in the narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the millrace muttering under its new skin of ice, and took stock the way Sef had taught her: assets, liabilities, exits.

Assets: the town believed her. Marta believed her, or had chosen to act as if she did, which amounted to the same protection. Hale had looked at her and seen furniture.

Liabilities: Hale had looked at her and seen furniture, and some cold, honest corner of her mind whispered that he had also seen *exactly* what she wanted him to see, which meant either she was still the best courier the republic had ever trained — or he was better, and was letting her sew her buttons in peace while he tightened the net elsewhere. He'd asked about militia rolls. He'd asked about the archive. He'd planted his need at the ferryman's sister's like a seed in furrowed ground, waiting for some anxious soul — a Brann — to water it.

Exits: none. The river was skimming over with ice that wouldn't bear a cat for another month. The pass was a white grave. She was sealed into Tamsin's Ford with her dispatches hidden in the false bottom of Marta's flour chest, with a hunter counting fence posts fifty yards away, and with a decoded order in her head that grew heavier every night: *if the town refuses the charter, let the enemy have it.*

She had spent fifteen years learning to be invisible, and she had been proud of it, in the way of a swordsman proud of a blade he'd never examined too closely. Now she lay in the dark and understood the shape of the thing at last. Invisibility wasn't freedom. It was a cell with transparent walls — she could see warmth everywhere, the lit windows, the families at supper, Tamsin's laugh carrying up the lane from the workshop — and none of it could reach her, because reaching required being *someone*, having a name that could be called, a self that could be caught.

Sef had promised her safety. He'd never promised her anything else.

Below, in the parlor, Marta banked the fire. Somewhere across town a dog barked twice and stopped. And Coral made herself do the exercise she hated most, the one Sef called *the other side of the glass*: *If I were Hale, what would I do tomorrow?*

Answer: stop searching houses. Searching houses was for amateurs. She would find the one person in town whose ambition outran his loyalty, buy him cheaply, and wait for the council to come to him. She would attend the charter debate wearing his surveyor's-clerk smile. And she would keep a list of every soul who spoke against the republic, because lists outlasted winters, and regimes remembered.

Which meant the clock was no longer the season. The clock was Brann.

Coral closed her eyes and began, methodically, to plan — not her escape, she noticed, with a small internal jolt, but the town's defense. Somewhere between the buttons and the dark, without her permission, the calculation had changed sides.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would find a reason to be in the workshop square when Hale made his morning rounds. Not to run. To watch him watch them — and to learn, before the ice thickened and the trap closed fully, precisely what he already knew.

Because there was one question she could not sew away, one that sat up with her past midnight like a second insomniac in the attic bed:

Why had a man that good at forgetting looked at her — just for half a heartbeat, just at the door — with the expression of a man checking a memory against a face?

She told herself she'd imagined it.

She was a professional liar. She knew exactly how well she could do it.
