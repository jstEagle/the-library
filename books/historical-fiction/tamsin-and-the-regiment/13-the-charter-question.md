## 13. The Charter Question

The council chamber had never been built to intimidate anyone, and that was precisely what made it formidable. Tamsin had grown up in that room the way other children grew up in kitchens — underfoot, half-listening, learning the smell of lamp oil and beeswax and old pine the way she'd learned her mother's voice. The benches were plain. The table was long and scarred with forty years of knife-marks, compass-points, and one deep gouge nobody would explain to her until she was twelve, at which point Odile had said only: *Brann's first year as a councilor. He wanted a bigger chair.* The windows looked out on the workshop yard, so that whatever was decided inside could be measured against the saws and lathes still turning outside, as if to remind every speaker that words were cheap and timber was not.

On the morning of the third of Deepmonth, the room was fuller than Tamsin had ever seen it.

Word had gone round the night before — the way word always went round, by way of the bakery, the wash-house, and Maretta Kell's talent for hearing everything twice before it was said once — that the gentleman from downriver had asked formally to address the council. Not to trade. Not to buy hinges or commission a cabinet, which was what downriver gentlemen did. To *address*.

Tamsin arrived early with her mother and helped carry the extra benches in from the meeting hall, and by the time the light came level through the east windows there was standing room along the walls and people spilling into the corridor. She counted faces out of habit: the full council of nine at the table — Odile Vant at the head, archive-keeper Pellum Sarn to her right with his ledger open like a shield, Brann Ostrey two seats down looking scrubbed and important, the ferryman Doss with river-mud still on his boots because he'd come straight off the ice-line inspection, weaver Anselm Grey, smith Cora Ives, tanner Wren Halloway, miller Josiah Fen, and old Marta Quill who had been on the council longer than anyone could remember and spoke perhaps four times a year, each time like a nail going in. Then the gallery: half the town behind them, and Coral Okonkwo seated quietly near the back with her hands folded, wearing the expression of a woman attending a lecture on drainage.

And at the table's foot, in the visitor's chair that had been dusted for the occasion, sat Merrick Hale.

He had dressed for them, Tamsin noticed. Not grandly — that would have been a mistake, and he was clearly no fool — but carefully. A good coat, dark wool, brushed clean of road-dust. Boots polished but not mirror-bright. His papers were squared on the table before him in a stack so neat it looked machined. He had the kind of face that was easy to look at and hard to remember afterward, which Tamsin found herself disliking on principle, because she had grown up in a house where *remembering* was the whole of the craft.

"Master Vant." He inclined his head to Odile. "Councilors. People of the Regiment." He said *people* warmly, as though the word were a gift he'd brought wrapped. "I thank you for the floor."

"You have it," Odile said. "For now."

There was a small ripple of appreciation along the benches. Tamsin felt a fierce pride and pressed it flat, because her mother had taught her that pride was a tool like any other and you didn't wave tools about while working.

Hale smiled as if Odile had been gracious rather than grudging, and began.

"I'll be brief, because I know your time is measured in daylight this season, and I respect that." He touched the paper stack without picking it up. "You will have heard that the Republic of Vessany is at war. You may have heard — rumors travel even up here — that the war goes badly. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. The republic is fighting for its life against the Coalition armies, and in such a fight, a republic learns quickly what it can and cannot spare."

He paused. Tamsin realized he was pausing for effect, timing his silences like a joiner timing glue-sets, and she hated how well it worked.

"What the republic cannot spare," Hale continued, "is uncertainty on its own roads. And what it has never learned to spare — what no government ever learns — is gratitude. This town sits above the Vessany gorge. It holds the only bridge across the high water between Ironhollow and the coast road. Your militia rolls name sixty-one able-bodied members trained in arms. Your workshops produce, by conservative estimate, three hundred fittings a season that end up in military wagons whether you intend it or not. And your charter — your precious, self-written charter, older than the current republic itself — exists on sufferance of governments that have mostly forgotten you exist." He let that sit. "Mostly."

"Get to the offer," said Cora Ives, who had never in her life been accused of patience.

Hale turned to her with genuine-looking pleasure, as though she'd asked exactly the question he'd hoped for. "The offer is simple. The republic formally recognizes the Regiment's charter — its laws, its council, its exemption from conscription — in perpetuity. Not at the whim of a ministry. In writing, sealed, witnessed. In exchange, the town places its militia rolls at the republic's disposal for the duration of the war. Not your people marched away — I know better than to propose that, Master Vant would laugh me out of the gorge. Your *rolls*. A registered company, mustered locally, defending the bridge and the pass if it should ever be threatened. Sixty-one names on a page, and in return, a government guarantee that no page of any ministry can dissolve what you've built."

He spread his hands. "Protection," he said, "for paper."

The room was quiet in the particular way Tamsin knew meant everyone was thinking hard and nobody wanted to be first. She stood by the wall beside Pellum Sarn's apprentice, close enough to see her mother's hands resting flat on the table — the pose Odile took when she was listening with her whole body.

It was Brann who broke the silence, and Tamsin watched him do it with a discomfort she couldn't yet name. "That seems reasonable," Brann said. "Frankly, it seems more than reasonable. We've spent forty years wondering when some governor downriver would remember we're sitting on his bridge. Here's a man offering us a lock on our own door."

"A lock," Odile said mildly, "with a key that stays in his pocket."

A few laughs. Hale laughed too, easily, showing he could take a joke — another thing done well, Tamsin noted, another thing done *deliberately*.

"Master Vant, may I ask what troubles you? Because something plainly does, and I'd rather hear it plainly than watch it circle."

Odile considered him for a long moment. Tamsin knew all her mother's silences the way she knew grain directions — this one was the slow, appraising silence, the one that preceded either a compliment or an amputation.

"What troubles me," Odile said, "is the word *duration*. Wars end. Charters, you say, are perpetual. But a guarantee given during a war for services rendered during that war has a way of expiring with the last shot fired. I've read history, Master Hale. I keep books. So does Pellum." She nodded at the archive-keeper. "Pellum, what does the republic's record look like on guarantees?"

Pellum Sarn adjusted his spectacles with the air of a man who had been waiting all morning for permission. "In the last ninety years," he said, "the Republic of Vessany has issued written guarantees of autonomy to eleven communities. Four were dissolved within a decade. Three were taxed into compliance without formal dissolution. Two were simply ignored until they ceased to function. One — Copperfield Ford — had its guarantee honored faithfully." He looked over his spectacles at Hale. "Copperfield Ford burned in the second Coalition campaign. So one might say the record is perfect either way."

Another ripple, less amused this time. Hale absorbed it without flinching.

"I won't argue with your archivist," he said. "I'd be a fool to. But I'll point out what his numbers actually show: communities that *held* their charters kept them through the republic's strength and lost them in its weakness — except those whose usefulness outlasted the emergency. Usefulness, Master Vant, is the only immortality any document ever conferred. And I am offering to make you useful in the cheapest possible way. Names on a roll. Men who drill in your own yard, behind your own walls, for your own bridge." His voice dropped slightly, and the room leaned in despite itself. "Or consider the alternative, which I'm authorized to mention only because I'd rather you hear it from a friend. If the regiment's rolls are not placed at the republic's disposal, then the republic cannot guarantee how its officers interpret this town's status. There are those in the ministry who would call an unregistered armed body on a strategic crossing something other than a militia. Something with a shorter word attached."

"*Rebellion,*" said Marta Quill.

Everyone turned. Old Marta had spoken her fifth sentence of the year, and it landed in the room like a dropped chisel.

"That is the word," Hale agreed quietly. "I would fight against its use. But I am one man, and winter is long, and ministries are frightened, and frightened ministries reach for short words." He gathered his papers, squaring them again with those precise fingers. "I don't require an answer today. I'll be in town some weeks yet — the river sees to that. Consider it as you consider everything: slowly, together, and in your own book." He rose, bowed to the table, and added, almost as an afterthought, "Your hospitality has been exemplary. Whatever you decide, I hope the Regiment and I part as friends."

He left through the corridor door, and the room exhaled.

* * *

They did not vote that day, and they did not debate openly while the galleries were full — Odile ruled it so, saying decisions of this weight belonged to the council first and the assembly after, and half the town grumbled and half the town approved, depending on which half liked which outcome. But the corridors and kitchens of Tamsin's Ford debated anyway, all afternoon and into the evening, and Tamsin moved through the sound of it carrying firewood and opinions she mostly kept to herself.

At the bakery, Doss the ferryman was holding forth: "Rolls on a page. That's all he wants. We drill anyway — we drilled last month, half of you were there. Sign the paper, take the seal, and when the war's over we're still us."

"And when the war's over," Anselm Grey's wife countered, flour to her elbows, "some new ministry reads the paper and finds out we promised ourselves to them *in perpetuity*, and perpetuity turns out to mean as long as we're convenient. My grandmother fled a town that was convenient."

At the workshop, the apprentices argued in whispers over their bench-work until Odile told them their dovetails were suffering for their politics. And at supper, Tamsin finally said what had been sitting in her chest since morning.

"He never once said what happens to us if we say yes and the republic *loses*."

Odile looked up from her plate. "Go on."

"The Coalition comes through, finds a town that signed itself to Vessany in writing, sealed and witnessed — he said those words himself, he wanted them remembered. We'd be a marked town. A loyalist town. The charter wouldn't protect us from the people who actually win." Tamsin turned her cup. "He offered us protection from one army by painting a target for the other."

Her mother was quiet for a moment, and then something moved across her face that Tamsin rarely saw there — not approval exactly, but the acknowledgment of good work, the look she gave a joint that closed clean on the first try.

"Pellum said much the same, after," Odile said. "As did Ives. As did I, in fewer words, because I was busy watching him instead of listening to him." She set down her fork. "Do you know what I was watching for?"

"His hands?"

"His hands, yes, but also his pauses. A man telling the whole truth doesn't need rehearsed silences." Odile stood and began stacking dishes, which meant the conversation wasn't over, merely relocated. "He wants an answer slowly. Did you notice that? A man in a hurry wants answers fast. This one wants us to turn it over and over until we've worn our own objections smooth. That's not the technique of someone selling a bargain. That's the technique of someone who needs the *yes* more than he lets on — and needs it before spring."

"Before the thaw," Tamsin said slowly. "Before anyone can check his story downriver."

"Before anyone can check anything." Odile glanced toward the window, toward the guest quarters where the downriver gentleman lodged, and beyond them, toward the corner of the workshop loft where a certain quiet woman with careful hands had been mending sacking and asking gentle questions about the archive for six weeks now. "Which means," her mother said, "that we are not the only ones in this town doing arithmetic about the spring. And I'd very much like to know what the others have concluded."

Later, banked fire, house asleep, Tamsin lay awake doing her own arithmetic. Sixty-one names. One bridge. Two armies. One gentleman with rehearsed silences lodging by the ferry landing. And somewhere in the gallery that morning, a courier-shaped silence she couldn't stop thinking about — because Tamsin had watched Coral Okonkwo through the whole of Hale's speech, and she had seen something she doubted anyone else in that room had caught.

When Hale had said *rebellion*, when the word dropped into the chamber like cold iron — Coral's hands, folded so neatly in her lap, had tightened. Just once. Just enough.

People's hands told truths their faces were paid to withhold. Tamsin had learned that at her mother's bench before she'd learned her letters.

She stared at the ceiling beams and thought: *she knows him. Or she knows what he is. And tomorrow I'm going to find out which.*

Down in the workshop loft, unaware that she was being counted among the town's arithmetic, Coral Okonkwo sat late over a candle stub with the dispatch case open before her, and began, alone, to decode the second message.
