## 14. Night Work

The candle stub had perhaps an hour left in it. Coral had chosen it deliberately — a small flame for small work, nothing that would throw her shadow large against the loft's paper-thin window. Below her, the workshop had gone quiet hours ago: the lathes still, the glue pots cold, the great sawdust smell of the place settled into the beams like snow into a field. Once, that smell would have meant nothing to her. Now she noticed it the way she noticed weather — as something that would go on without her, indifferent and patient.

She unrolled the second dispatch across her knee.

It was thinner than the first. That should have warned her. In fifteen years of carrying other people's intentions folded into cipher, Coral had learned that the weight of a message bore no relation to its consequence; the lightest papers were often the ones that moved armies. But some animal part of her — the part Sef had never managed to train out of her, the part that flinched before thunder — had hoped that thinness meant triviality. A supply requisition. A change of contact protocols. Anything but what the first dispatch had already shown her to be true: that Tamsin's Ford, with its careful books and its unhurried councils, was not a refuge on her route at all, but a *target*.

She set her decoding frame across her knees — two slats of pine, hinged, that she'd made herself years ago in a rented room in Corvel, whittling by candlelight while the innkeeper's dog scratched at the door below. The key phrase from the first dispatch still lived in her head where no search could find it: *the river remembers*. She worked the letter-groups through the grille methodically, transcribing plaintext into the margin of her own notebook in a hand so small it might have been the scratching of an insect.

The first words came slowly, as they always did, the cipher resisting like frozen ground giving way to the spade. Then the pattern took hold and the sentences began to assemble themselves, and Coral felt the old courier's trance come over her — that state in which the self recedes and only the work remains, hands moving, mind empty of everything but the next group, the next substitution. It was, she had long ago admitted to herself, a kind of mercy, that trance. Decoding was easier than reading. The hands could do it while the heart stood aside.

*...should the council decline the charter terms presented by the envoy...*

Her hands kept moving. Her heart did not stand aside this time.

*...the Regiment is to be publicly designated as being in a state of rebellion against the lawful authority of the Republic. All protection, provisioning, and diplomatic standing shall be withdrawn effective upon such designation. No relief expedition will be authorized. The military situation downriver being what it is, the town's disposition may be expected to resolve itself by spring.*

Coral stopped.

The candle flame leaned in some draft from under the eaves, righted itself, leaned again. Down in the sleeping workshop, a beam ticked as the frost outside worked at the timbers.

*May be expected to resolve itself.*

She read the sentence again. Then again, a third time, because the first two readings refused to become sense. It was not that the language was obscure. The language was perfectly clear. That was the horror of it — the flat bureaucratic clarity, the phrasing of men who signed things. *Resolve itself.* As if the town were a knot in thread. As if the enemy army now wintering at Callow Ford, forty miles downriver — the army whose outriders she herself had slipped past in the dark of October, the army that burned what it did not want and requisitioned what it did — were merely a solvent into which Tamsin's Ford would be introduced, politely, officially, with all proper documentation.

They would not even send soldiers. They would send *paperwork*. A declaration, read aloud perhaps by Merrick Hale before the assembled council, and then the republic would simply look away, and let the Vellish columns come up the valley in their own time, and when the smoke cleared there would be no Regiment left to argue about militia charters or debts owed or any of the quiet stubborn machinery of self-government that Odile Vant guarded like a banked fire. And the republic's hands would be clean. Cleaner than clean — they would be *righteous*. A town in rebellion. A town that chose defiance over loyalty. Who could pity it? What court, what clerk, what future historian would ever ask whether the rebellion had been invented for the purpose?

Coral became aware that her breath was fogging in the cold air of the loft, and that she had been sitting motionless so long her knees ached against the decoding frame.

She thought about the first dispatch, decoded three weeks ago in this same loft, and how she had managed it then — how she had told herself, in the fluent interior voice of the practiced coward, that a charter purchase was not so terrible, that towns sold such things, that the council would weigh the offer and refuse it and life would continue, that her duty ended at delivery and the choosing belonged to others. She had built that argument brick by brick in the nights since, mortared it with Sef's oldest rule — *never be remembered, never be responsible, carry the letter and not the consequence* — and it had held, more or less, the way a dam holds, which is to say until you looked too closely at the water behind it.

There was no damming this one. She understood that with a clarity that felt almost physical, like cold water finding the crack in a boot seam.

Because here was the thing she had known since the ferry, since Hale stepped off it with his soft boots and his soft questions, and had refused to know: the second dispatch was not a contingency plan. It was the *plan*. The charter offer was theater. Hale's patience, his courtesies, his careful courting of Brann — all of it existed to give the council a fair chance to refuse, so that the refusal could be entered into the record. The republic did not want the militia rolls. The rolls were worthless; every competent officer downriver knew the Regiment's muster was a hundred joiners and potters with fowling pieces. What the republic wanted was a *precedent* — a self-governing community, famous for its independence, formally declared traitor, and destroyed. Every other town in the valley would understand the lesson. Pay your levies. Sign your charters. Do not keep your own books.

And she had carried the instrument of it into the town on her own wrist, chained to her like a bride's bracelet, and walked it past Odile's bench, past Tamsin's sharp grey eyes, past the kiln and the ledger room and the children learning letters in the archive — carrying it the whole way inside the fortress of her own harmless face.

*Never be remembered.* Sef's voice in her memory, gentle as ever, his hand on her shoulder in the schoolroom of the courier house, fifteen years ago. *Remembered means blamed, little wren. Blamed means dead. Be the wind, not the knife.*

But the wind carries the fire as surely as the knife does. The wind is how the fire gets where it's going.

*

She got up because sitting still had become unbearable, and crossed the loft to the window, pressing her forehead to the cold pane. Below, the town lay under its first serious snow, roofs softened white, chimney smoke standing straight up in the still air. At the far end of the square she could see the dark bulk of the council hall where the charter question had been argued that afternoon, and beyond it the black seam of the river, not yet frozen, moving in the dark with a sound she could just barely hear — that low ceaseless commentary of water over stone.

Somewhere downriver, past the ferry crossing, past the ruined bridge, the Vellish army sat in its winter quarters doing whatever armies do in winter: sharpening, waiting, eating through other people's stores. Somewhere south of that, the capital was eating itself — the purges, the arrests, the lists. Sef had died on one of those lists. She had learned that from a letter delayed four months, a letter whose last line she had read so many times the ink had begun to feel warm: *trust no handler, little wren, least of all the ones who are kind.*

Merrick Hale was kind. He remembered names. He asked after Tamsin's mother's cough. He had complimented Coral's mending, the first week, in the easy tone of a man who notices everything and threatens nothing, and she had smiled her widowed-cousin smile and said thank you, and both of them had known exactly what the exchange was, and neither had blinked.

He knew who she was. She was nearly certain of it now. He had known since the ferry, probably — had let her settle into her disguise like a bird settling into a snare, because a courier in hiding was more useful than a courier arrested. Arrested, she'd be searched, and the case might pass to someone careless. Left alone, she'd decode everything, verify everything, and eventually — if her nerve broke, if her conscience woke — she might be turned. He was farming her. Patiently. The way the town farmed its fields: tending, waiting, expecting yield.

And the terrible thing, the thing that made her press her forehead harder against the glass until the cold hurt, was that the farm was working. Not because she wanted to betray anyone. Because she had done nothing. Because silence, in a place like this, in a season like this, was not neutrality. Silence was a vote cast daily, by absence, for whichever side needed the quiet.

She had told herself for fifteen years that invisibility was innocence. Standing at the window of a town she had not yet admitted she loved, holding a decoded death warrant in her notebook, Coral Okonkwo finally understood that invisibility was simply guilt with better posture.

*

She went back to the desk because there was nothing else in the world to do with the truth except finish reading it. The rest of the dispatch came in fragments, each one worse than the last.

*Designation to be published in the Gazette within ten days of refusal.* Ten days. The midwinter assembly was nine days off — the whole town, gathered to vote on the very offer that had been designed to fail.

*Envoy is authorized full discretion in establishing the evidentiary record.* Which meant Hale could manufacture whatever "rebellion" he needed — an arms cache planted in a cellar, an insult to a republican official, a forged letter in the council's hand. Or he could simply wait for Brann, whose hunger had been visible from the second week, whose copies of council minutes were already traveling downriver in the lining of a coat. Coral had seen Brann looking at her once across the square — a long, measuring look, the look of a man pricing a horse — and had understood that Brann suspected her too, and had decided to sell the suspicion rather than act on it. Everyone in this valley was trading everyone.

*Courier Okonkwo to confirm receipt by the usual channel and proceed as instructed.*

There it was. Her name, or the name the republic used for her, sitting in the plaintext like a hook sitting in water. *Proceed as instructed.* Proceed meaning: deliver the confirmation that the town has been approached. Proceed meaning: remain in place, remain invisible, remain silent while the declaration is drafted and the evidence assembled and the columns march.

Or proceed meaning nothing at all, because there was no usual channel anymore, because the capital had fallen into its own teeth and the handlers were purge-ash, and the dispatch might be the last order the republic ever issued, signed by men who would be dead before spring. That possibility should have comforted her. It did not. Dead men's orders still killed; paper outlived its authors the way fire outlived the match. And even if no signature anywhere remained to enforce it, Hale was here, Hale had discretion, Hale had Brann and pitch and time, and the army downriver did not require the republic's permission to burn a valley. The dispatch was not a threat that needed enforcing. It was a description of what was already going to happen unless someone stood in front of it.

Someone. She laughed quietly, without humor, at the word. Fifteen years of training against exactly that word. Someone else. Anyone else. Never the courier; the courier is furniture; the courier is the wind.

Below her, faintly, she heard the workshop door open and close — someone checking the fires, probably; the night watchman making his rounds, counting the banks of coals the way Odile counted everything, twice, in ink. The sound of it undid her a little. This was what the town was. A place where a man walked the floor at midnight not because a lord commanded it but because the benches mattered, because the shared thing mattered, because tomorrow a dozen apprentices would need warmth to work by. Nobody had ordered him. Nobody would remember him. He did it anyway, and slept well, presumably, and would never appear in any dispatch, any gazette, any history written downriver.

That was the country she had been sent to help destroy.

*

Coral sat back down and forced herself to do the hardest part, the part Sef had called the reckoning: after the decoding, the arithmetic. What did she know. Who needed to know it. What would knowing cost them, and what would not-knowing cost.

What she knew: the offer was a trap; the refusal was scheduled; the destruction was pre-written; Hale held discretion; Brann was bought; the timeline ran through the midwinter assembly.

Who needed to know it: the council. Odile first. And —

She stopped there, pen above the page, because the list would not stop where she wanted it to. *And Tamsin*, it insisted. *And Tamsin, who has watched you for weeks with those eyes that count hands; who half-confessed her own suspicions at the kiln and offered you trust you didn't deserve; who will learn anyway, because she always learns, and will forgive you never if she learns that you knew and let her walk blind into a room where the trap was already sprung.*

And there was a further item, one she had been circling for days the way her tongue circles a broken tooth: the town's leverage. Odile's secret ledger — the debts the republic itself owed the Regiment, decades of unpaid contracts, advances taken, promises recorded in the town's own meticulous hand. A town with receipts. A town that could prove, in ink, that the republic was the debtor and the Regiment the creditor. If the republic wished to declare Tamsin's Ford in rebellion, the Ford could declare itself *defrauded*, and produce documents, and make noise in whatever courts and chancelleries survived the war. It was not a sword. But it was a shield, maybe. And shields required someone to hold them up, and say aloud, in front of witnesses: I know what is written here, and I choose.

Coral blew out the candle stub, because it had guttered to nothing, and sat in the dark with the decoded pages in her lap and the cold deepening around her.

Fifteen years ago, in a border town whose name she still could not make herself think fully, she had kept silent — had delivered her dispatches, worn her harmless face, walked out ahead of the soldiers — and she had told herself every version of the story there was to tell, and none of them had kept her warm since. She had been given, by accident of weather and a collapsed bridge, a second chance at the same crossroads, and this time the town had fed her and paid her and taught her badly-learned joinery beside a girl who laughed at her mistakes, and this time there was nowhere to walk out *to*, because the republic she served was a ghost and the road home was a rumor.

Silence now meant complicity. There was no other translation. She had spent the evening proving it in cipher, character by character, and the plaintext did not lie.

In the dark, Coral did something she had not done since she was nineteen years old. She wept — briefly, silently, furiously, the tears freezing cold on her face almost before they fell — and then she dried her face on her sleeve, took up her pen one more time, and began, in the smallest hand she owned, to draft the note she would slip beneath Odile Vant's door before the household woke:

*Master Joiner — we must speak privately, and soon, and away from the hall. Ask me nothing yet. Trust me once more than is reasonable. There is a second document, and it does not say what the envoy says it says.*

She read it over twice. Then she folded it small, sealed it with plain wax, and held it in her closed fist, feeling her own pulse knock against the paper — and downstairs, faint through the floorboards, she heard the workshop door open again, and footsteps on the stairs, and a voice she knew saying her name softly into the dark.
