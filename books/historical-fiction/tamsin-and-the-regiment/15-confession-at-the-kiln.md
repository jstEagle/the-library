## 15. Confession at the Kiln

The kiln stood at the edge of the pottery yard like a sleeping animal, breathing heat through its iron mouth. Tamsin had been feeding it since noon — split birch first, then the denser oak that burned slow and even — and by the time the light went copper over the western ridge, the firebox glowed the color of a held breath. Firing night was her favorite of all the workshop's nights. It was the one job in the Regiment that could not be interrupted, could not be rushed, could not be left to be finished tomorrow. You fed the fire until it was done being hungry, and you stayed with it, and you did not sleep. There was something honest in that. Most things in her life could be put down and picked up again; the kiln demanded the whole of you for as long as it wanted you.

She had not asked Coral to help. That was the strange part, the part she kept turning over while she stacked the wood. She hadn't asked, and Coral had come anyway — appeared at the yard gate at dusk with her sleeves rolled and her hair tied back in a way Tamsin had never seen before, as if she'd dressed for work instead of for disappearing.

"I thought you might want company," Coral had said. "Long nights go shorter shared."

It was the most unguarded sentence Tamsin had ever heard from her. So now they sat on the low stone wall beside the kiln shed, passing a jug of cider between them, watching the fire through the viewing slot, and Tamsin found herself studying the other woman the way she studied a joint she couldn't get to sit flush — looking for where the grain ran wrong.

Coral Okonkwo had been in Tamsin's Ford since the bridge closed, going on seven weeks now, and Tamsin knew almost nothing true about her. She knew what the town knew: that she was a widow's cousin from downriver, that she'd been traveling to see family when the season shut, that she had quick hands and a patient manner and an accent that slipped — river-flat some days, highland-lilted others, once, memorably, carrying a vowel shape Tamsin had only ever heard from a Vessany naval officer who'd come through buying timber six years ago. She knew what Odile knew, which was more: that the woman counted exits, that she read upside-down documents without seeming to, that she watched council sessions the way a cat watches a bird cage — not the birds, but the latch.

And she knew what she herself had noticed, which was perhaps the most: that Coral flinched, very slightly, every time someone said the word *republic*.

"You're staring," Coral said, without turning her head.

"I'm working," Tamsin said. "You're just in the way of my view."

"The view is a brick wall."

"A beautiful brick wall. I laid that course myself, two years ago. It took me three tries because I kept getting the mortar too wet." She passed the jug. "Drink. You've earned it. Nobody's ever fed a fire with me past midnight before."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't ask." Tamsin pulled her knees up onto the wall, boots finding purchase in the old mortar gaps. "Firing night is when I do my thinking. Mother says I brood. I say I plan. Either way, I don't much want witnesses."

"And yet here I am."

"Here you are." Tamsin let that sit a moment. The kiln ticked and settled; somewhere inside, a shelf of green pots was beginning the long transformation, clay to stoneware, soft to permanent. "Funny thing about witnesses, though. Sometimes you want one. Just one. Somebody who isn't going to run off and repeat it at assembly."

Coral's hand paused on the jug. Small thing. Tamsin caught it anyway. She caught everything about this woman; it was becoming a problem.

"That sounds like a warning," Coral said lightly.

"It sounds like an offer." Tamsin turned to face her fully. The firelight did half the work, painting one side of Coral's face amber and leaving the other in blue dark, so that she looked like two different people meeting at a seam. "I'm going to tell you something nobody in this town knows except me and the river, and then you can decide what you owe me back. Fair trade. That's how we do things here — everything goes in the ledger eventually."

"Tamsin—"

"Not yet. Let me get through it or I'll lose my nerve, and I don't lose my nerve twice."

* * *

What Tamsin told her was this:

When she was fourteen, the year before she apprenticed properly to her mother's bench, there had been a man named Doss Merrow. He was a cousin of the ferryman's wife, come upriver for a summer, and he was charming the way rot is sweet — you didn't notice until the fruit fell apart in your hand. He'd taken a liking to the town's grain accounts. The Regiment kept its books openly; anyone could read them; that was the whole point of keeping them. Doss had understood that openness differently. He'd understood it as an unlocked door.

He'd altered the ledgers. Not much. A fraction here, a rounding there, siphoned into a false account under a name that sounded like a real farm up the valley. And he'd done it cleverly, in his own neat hand, matching the clerk's ink. By the time the discrepancy surfaced at autumn tally, the trail pointed at old Master Ellery, the archive-keeper, whose eyes were failing and whose hand had begun to shake.

Ellery didn't defend himself. That was the thing Tamsin had never been able to forgive or forget. He simply went quiet, the way a man does when he knows the town needs a culprit more than it needs a truth, and offered to make restitution from his own savings. And the council — good people, careful people, her mother among them — had been *inclined to let him*, because the alternative was months of forensic misery over accounts nobody wanted to relitigate, and winter was coming, and Ellery was old, and old men made convenient endings.

Tamsin had been fourteen and furious and certain. She'd spent eleven nights going through the tallies by candlelight, comparing hands, matching slants, until she found the page where Doss Merrow's *g* betrayed him — the same lazy open tail he used in the ferry log, in letters, everywhere, because a man's hand is a man's hand no matter how hard he tries to wear another's gloves. She'd carried the page to the council floor at the autumn assembly and laid it down in front of everyone, fourteen years old, voice cracking, and said: *Look at the g.*

They'd looked. They'd looked, and seen, and Doss Merrow had been gone over the wall before dawn, and Ellery had wept openly in the yard, and Odile Vant had put her hand on her daughter's head in front of the whole assembly and said nothing at all, which from Odile was a parade.

"But here's the part I don't tell people," Tamsin said now, and her voice had gone low, and she found she was gripping the wall's cold stone. "Afterward, everybody kept saying how brave I was. How sharp. Little Tamsin, saving the day. And I ate it up. I was proud for years. And then one night — I must have been nineteen, twenty — I woke up and understood that I hadn't saved anything. I'd gotten lucky. If Doss had been careful with his g's, if he'd been a better liar, Ellery would have paid for it. Would have died owing a debt he never incurred, and the town would have called it justice, and moved on. My truth-telling wasn't a system. It was a coin toss. And I've been terrified ever since — actually terrified — of how easily good people believe the tidy story. Including me. *Especially* me."

The kiln roared softly. Tamsin drank, and her hand wasn't quite steady, and she let Coral see that it wasn't.

"So that's my confession. Not that I lied — I don't lie, it's a character flaw — but that I know exactly how fragile the truth is in a room full of decent people, and I love this town enough to be frightened of it." She set the jug down. "Your turn. Or not. But I'll tell you what I've noticed, cousin-from-downriver, and then you can decide whether to keep lying to me, which is your right, or to give me one true thing, which would be a kindness."

* * *

Coral was very still. Stillness, Tamsin had learned, was her native language; motion was the translation, and the translation always came late.

"One true thing," Coral said at last. Her voice had changed — flattened, stripped of the pleasant downriver roundness, and underneath it was something older and wearier, an accent from nowhere at all. "All right. Here's one."

She looked into the firebox rather than at Tamsin.

"I have not told the truth to anyone in fifteen years. Not wholly. Not once. Do you understand what that means? It doesn't mean I lie. Lying is easy; lying is nothing. It means that every word I say is chosen — weighed against what it reveals, what it costs, what it buys. I have spent fifteen years being a person made entirely of answers to questions nobody asked. I have a name I use, a history I use, a widowhood I use, and none of it is mine. When your mother asked me at supper where I learned to mend netting, I gave her a story about a fishing village, and I chose that story in the space of half a breath, and I have told it four times now, identically, because consistency is survival." A short, terrible laugh. "I could tell you my real name tonight and by morning I'd have to decide whether you knowing it was a debt, a danger, or a mistake. That's what my life is. Decisions like that, forever, in half-breaths."

"What's your real name?" Tamsin asked quietly.

Coral's jaw worked. For a moment Tamsin thought she'd pushed too far, that the woman would fold herself back behind the pleasant traveler like a fan snapping shut. Then—

"Coral," she said. "That one's true. Coral Okonkwo. My mother named me for a thing the sea makes out of ten thousand small deaths, which she thought was funny. Everything else you know about me is scaffolding around that one true word." She finally turned her head, and her eyes in the firelight were wet-bright and absolutely level. "There. One true thing. Now you know as much about me as any living soul, which is to say: almost nothing, but honestly almost nothing."

"Why are you telling me this?" Tamsin asked. "Tonight? Why now?"

"Because you told me about the g." Coral's mouth twisted. "Because you sat there and told me the most dangerous thing a person in your position could say — that you know how easily the truth loses, and you're afraid of it — and you trusted me with it. And I sat here doing the arithmetic, Tamsin. I sat here calculating what your confidence cost me, what exposure it risked, whether it was a trap, whether your mother put you up to it, whether the ferryman's wife talks in her sleep. Fifteen years of arithmetic, running automatically, like a heart. And then I realized I was doing it to *you*. To a girl who fed a kiln with me and told me about the worst night of her childhood for no reason except that she wanted to be known." She looked down at her hands. "I'm tired of the arithmetic. That's the second true thing. I didn't plan to give you two."

The fire shifted; a knot popped in the firebox and spat a spark through the slot, and neither of them moved to catch it.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Coral went on, softer now. "The honest words feel like this. Like standing outside in winter after a fever breaks. Everything hurts and everything is clear. I'd forgotten. I genuinely forgot that talking could feel like anything other than a transaction."

Tamsin found she couldn't speak immediately. There was a tightness in her throat that had nothing to do with smoke. She had spent her whole life in a town where everyone said precisely what they meant, where the ledger was open and the doors were unlocked, and she had never once imagined what it would cost to live the other way — to hold every sentence up to the light and check it for fingerprints before letting it leave your mouth. Fifteen years. Coral had been younger than Tamsin was now when it started. Whatever had built those walls had built them young, and built them well, and the fact that she was standing outside them right now, shivering, on purpose —

"Thank you," Tamsin said. It seemed insufficient. She said it again anyway. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Coral's voice steadied into something wry and sad. "You should understand what you've accepted. Because here's the third true thing, and it's the expensive one: I'm not what I said I am. I'm not a widow's cousin. I'm not downriver. I'm here on business, and the business isn't finished, and when it finishes, it will either mean nothing to this town or it will mean everything, and I don't yet know which, and I am — God help me — no longer sure I want to find out the way I was sent to find out." She met Tamsin's eyes. "I can't tell you more. Not because I don't trust you. Because the more you know, the more danger you're in, and I have already decided — sometime in the last seven weeks, without noticing when — that you and your mother and this ridiculous, beautiful, bookkeeping town are not going to be the price of anything I do. But that's all I can say tonight. Half a confession. Will that hold you?"

Tamsin considered. The kiln breathed. Down in the dark yard, frost was forming on the woodpile, and above the ridge the first stars were out, indifferent and sharp.

"My mother says a half-truth is worse than a lie," she said slowly, "because a lie you can catch, but a half-truth you build on."

"Your mother is right."

"But my mother also says a person is a ledger, and you don't judge the book by one page." Tamsin hopped down off the wall and stood close, close enough that the kiln's heat touched them both equally. "So here's mine, and it's a full one, no halves: I believe you. I don't know what you are, and I believe you. And when whatever's coming comes — and it's coming, isn't it? I've watched you watch the road for a month — when it comes, you come to me first. Before my mother, before the council, before anyone. You promised me the worst night of my life tonight; I'm promising you the first knock. Deal?"

For a long moment Coral said nothing. Then she extended her hand — the courier's grip, firm and brief, but her fingers lingered half a heartbeat past the bargain, and Tamsin pretended not to notice, and treasured it anyway.

"Deal," Coral said.

They fed the fire together until dawn, saying little, needing to say little, and when the first gray light came and Tamsin cracked the kiln door to check the cones, the pots had fired true — every one, whole and hard and permanent.

It was on the walk back through the frosted yard, breath steaming, that they both saw it at the same moment: hoofprints in the new snow by the landing stage, deep-shod, heavy, and the ferryman already standing at his door staring downriver at a figure waiting on the far bank, coat collar up, patient as winter itself.

"Who crosses before the freeze?" Tamsin murmured.

Beside her, Coral had gone very still — the stillness Tamsin now understood at last, the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic at the speed of fear.

"Somebody important," Coral said quietly. "Somebody who pays for crossings."
