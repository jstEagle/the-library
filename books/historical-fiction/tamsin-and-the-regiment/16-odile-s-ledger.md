## 16. Odile's Ledger

The hoofprints were gone by morning, buried under four inches of fresh snow, and with them any easy answer to the question of who had crossed. The ferryman — old Doss, whose silences were as long as his river — would say only that a gentleman had paid triple rate to be put ashore at dusk and had walked up into the town without asking directions, as if he already knew where everything stood.

Coral spent that first day after doing what she did best: nothing that could be remembered. She mended a chair seat in the workshop's back room, badly enough that Tamsin laughed at her, and let the laughter cover the counting she was doing behind her eyes. A gentleman who pays for crossings. Who arrives before the freeze, when no one else can follow him and no one else can leave. Who walks unasked through a town that prides itself on asking.

She had known men like that. She had *been* a person like that, in a smaller key.

It was Odile Vant who came for her at the end of the day, when the workshop lamps were being trimmed and the apprentices were shaking sawdust from their aprons. Odile did not ask if Coral had a moment. She simply stood in the doorway of the finishing room with her arms crossed, a woman built like the furniture she made — all load-bearing lines and no ornament — and said:

"Come and see something."

Tamsin looked up sharply from her bench. "Mother—"

"Not you." But there was no cruelty in it, only the flat economy of a woman who rationed words the way she rationed walnut. "You'll be wanted soon enough. Go home, Tam. Bank the stove."

Tamsin went, though not gracefully, and not without one long look back at Coral — a look that had become, over these weeks, its own kind of language. *Be careful,* it said. Or possibly, *remember everything.* With Tamsin the two were often the same instruction.

* * *

Odile led her out of the workshop entirely, down the lane past the kilns, past the tannery with its winter-smell of bark and lime, to the archive house — a low stone building that Coral had catalogued on her second day in town and had been trying to find an excuse to enter ever since. It sat against the hillside like a thing grown rather than built, its roof weighted with river stones against the wind, its single door iron-banded and older than any republic.

The keeper was a stooped man named Ellery Foss who had ink stains to the elbow and eyes that measured people the way Coral measured exits. He looked at Coral for a long moment when Odile spoke her name, and whatever he saw — or decided to see — he stepped aside and lit them through.

Inside, the air was dry and cold and smelled of paper and tallow and time. Racks of document boxes rose to the ceiling, each labeled in a small precise hand: *Mill Rights, 3rd Survey. Bridge Contracts. Apprenticeship Rolls. Water Shares, East Race.* The Regiment kept its own books; Coral had understood that from the beginning. What she had not understood, until Odile walked past all of it to a plain cedar chest set into an alcove behind the survey racks, was how far back the keeping went — or what else the books contained.

"Lock the door, Ellery," Odile said.

Foss locked it. Then, to Coral's mild surprise, he stayed — settling onto a stool by the lamp with the proprietary calm of a man who had watched this ritual before.

"You're wondering why I'm showing you this," Odile said. She knelt — heavily, joints complaining — and drew the chest toward her.

"A little," Coral admitted.

"You're wondering a great deal more than a little, and you've been wondering since the night you arrived, because I've watched you look at doors." Odile lifted the lid. Inside lay ledgers — perhaps twenty of them, bound in hide, their spines numbered not in dates but in some older sequence. "I'm going to show you this for three reasons. First, because a woman who counts our exits has earned the right to count our accounts. Second, because someone crossed my river yesterday who makes me think the season for secrets is shorter than winter. And third—" she drew out the oldest ledger and laid it on the table between them, opening it with both hands, gently, the way one handles a wound "—because I think you may be carrying something heavier than luggage, Coral, and I'd rather know now than learn it the way towns usually learn such things. From the ashes."

Coral did not flinch. Fifteen years of practice held her face still, held her breathing even, held her hands loose at her sides. But inside, where no practice reached, something turned over slowly, the way ice turns over in a river before it goes under.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Odile agreed. "You say that very well."

She turned the ledger toward the lamp.

* * *

The pages were dense, ruled in faded iron-gall ink, and written in a hand so regular it might have been typeset. At the top of each entry: a date. Below it: a name, an office, a sum. Below that: what the sum was *for.*

Coral read. And as she read, the cold in the archive stopped being the room's cold and became her own.

*Paid to Deputy Quartermaster Aris Vole, 40 crowns, for silence regarding timber tonnage at the spring inspection.*
*Paid to Captain-Inspector Ren Halloway, 120 crowns, for amendment of the militia rolls to show forty fewer names.*
*Paid to the office of the Provisional Assembly, 300 crowns, for continued exemption from requisition — renewal, third year.*
*Paid to Sef Ademar, courier, 60 crowns, for delivery of documents to the capital unexamined.*

That last one stopped her heart for exactly two beats. She counted them, because counting was what she had instead of screaming.

"Sef Ademar," Odile said quietly, watching her. "Your teacher. Yes?"

"You know that name."

"Everyone in this ledger is a name somebody knows," Odile said. "That's rather the point of it." She turned pages backward, decades deep. "When the Republic of Vessany was young, it came to this valley hungry. We had timber they needed, waterpower they needed, and later, when the wars started, we had something they needed even more — a militia charter, signed by every household, that made us legally a free company under our own command. They couldn't conscript us. They couldn't quarter soldiers here. They hated it, but they needed us too much to break it. So instead they bought around it. Every year, some new clerk or captain or minister with a clever idea about how to get what the law wouldn't give them. And every year, my grandmother's grandmother wrote down what they paid, and to whom, and for what."

"Why?" Coral asked. It was the honest question, and she let it be heard honestly.

"Because a debt is a weapon that keeps," Odile said. "Because signatures rot faster than ink. Because half the men in this book rose to be ministers, and ministers can be ruined by a page." She tapped the ledger with one blunt finger. "The Republic thinks it owns the valley's patience. This book says the valley owns sixty years of the Republic's shame. That is the bargain underneath the bargain, courier. We have never once threatened anyone with it. We have only kept it. But it exists. It is real. And it is the reason the Republic has always, always negotiated."

Foss spoke from his stool, mildly, the way a man remarks on weather. "There's a page missing from the third volume. Torn, not removed whole. Someone wanted one entry very badly and couldn't take the book."

"We've wondered about that for years," Odile said. "Now we wonder harder." Her eyes came back to Coral and stayed there, patient and terrible. "So. You are a courier for a state that is dying — don't bother denying the dying part, we trade with the river towns, we hear things — and you carry a case chained to your wrist, and you have been reading our council's faces at supper like a cardplayer reads a deck. I am not accusing you. I am telling you what you're standing in. There is leverage in this room. There has been leverage in this room since before your mother was born. The only question worth asking tonight is which side of the ledger *you* intend to stand on."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, wind moved across the stone roof and was gone.

Coral thought of Sef's letter, folded flat against her ribs these many weeks. *Trust no handler.* She thought of the dispatches sleeping in their case upstairs, coded in a cipher only half of which she had cracked, the half that had already taught her the mission was not what she'd been told. She thought of Tamsin's laugh in the sawdust, of the town voting by lamplight, of Doss the ferryman refusing a crossing on principle and being obeyed by everyone, including the river.

And she understood, with the clarity that comes only once or twice in a life of careful lies, that she was standing at a fork with snow filling both roads, and that whichever way she walked, she would walk it as herself — not as nobody, not as the harmless traveler, not as Sef's invisible girl. The invisibility had been a coat. It was wearing out. Underneath it she was only a woman, tired, and the question Odile had asked was not a test she could fail safely.

"If I told you what I carry," Coral said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, roughened at the edges like unfinished wood, "you would have to decide what to do about me. And I'm not certain yet that knowing would keep this town safe. I'm not certain it wouldn't do the opposite."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Whatever she found, she seemed to find sufficient, or at least familiar.

"That," she said, closing the ledger with care, "is the most honest answer I've had in this room in twenty years." She returned the volume to the chest but left the lid open, deliberate, a door left ajar in both senses. "Keep your case. Keep your silence, for now — I know what silence costs, and I won't cheapen it by rushing you. But understand this, Coral, whoever you truly are: the Regiment does not sell its people, and it does not spend its people, and it does not abandon them to make an accounting come out neat. If the Republic's last act is to try to turn this valley into a line item, they will find we have been keeping books longer than they've been keeping power."

"And if I'm the instrument they sent to do the turning?" Coral asked. "What then?"

"Then you'll bring the instrument to me," Odile said, "before you use it. That's all I ask. That's everything I ask." She straightened, joints cracking like green wood in fire, and for a moment her granite composure shifted and showed something underneath — weariness, and a fierce, banked tenderness. "My daughter likes you. That should frighten you more than anything I've said. It frightens me considerably."

* * *

Foss unlocked the door and let them out into the blue dark of early evening, stars hard as nailheads over the ridge. Odile walked Coral as far as the workshop lane and then stopped beneath the eaves of the cooperage, breath smoking.

"One more thing," she said. "The gentleman who crossed yesterday. Doss says he gave his name freely — Merrick Hale, an inspector of charters, he called himself, down to review the militia rolls for the winter assembly. Very courteous. Bought wool. Complimented the bridge." She paused. "He asked Ellery today whether the archive allowed visitors. Ellery told him the archive allows no one, which is true, and also told him the third volume was complete, which is not. I'd like to know, when you're ready to tell me, why those two facts seem connected in your face."

Coral felt the blood leave her hands. Merrick Hale. Even the name sounded like a man who paid triple rate.

"I'll tell you soon," she said. "I promise you that."

"Do," Odile said. "Soon is a currency too. Spend it well." And she turned away up the lane, a broad dark shape against the snow, unhurried, as though the republic were merely weather and she had seen worse seasons.

Coral stood alone in the cold a while, watching the windows of the Vant house glow gold down the street — Tamsin's shadow moving behind the glass, banking the stove, waiting up. Behind her, the archive sat sealed against the hill, holding sixty years of other people's sins in cedar and ink. Above her wrist, beneath her sleeve, the dispatch case hung quiet as a coal.

Two ledgers, she thought. Two sets of debts. The town's were written down and therefore survivable.

Hers were still burning holes in her pocket — and somewhere across the frozen yard, in the guest quarters by the meetinghouse, Merrick Hale had just lit his lamp.
