## 17. Hale Makes Friends

Tamsin noticed the change in Brann before anyone else did, because noticing was what she did. She had grown up watching her mother's hands — the way a joiner's fingers telegraphed a mistake before the wood ever showed it, a hesitation at the plane, a half-second of doubt before the chisel fell — and she had learned to read people the same way, as grain and figure, as something that would tell you its intentions if you only paid attention.

And Brann's grain had shifted.

He came to workshop suppers now when he had never come before, or hardly ever — Brann Odulfar, councilor for the mill ward, who had spent twenty years making himself too important for sawdust on his boots. He sat on the long benches between the apprentices and the masters and laughed at jokes that weren't funny, and his laugh had a new sound in it, a bright hollow ring like a bucket struck against stone. He wore his good coat on days with no occasion. He had begun carrying a small silver-topped walking stick that Tamsin was almost certain he hadn't owned at midwinter.

"He's courting someone," she told Coral quietly, one evening in January, as they scraped glue from the bench by lamplight. "But I can't work out who."

Coral didn't look up from her scraper. Her hands had gotten better over the weeks — still clumsy by the Regiment's standards, but no longer shameful — though her eyes had never changed. They moved over everything the way water moves over stones, touching all, settling nowhere. "Maybe it's nobody," she said. "Some men dress up because they want to be somebody."

"Brann already thinks he's somebody. That's not the problem. The problem is he wants everyone else to agree."

That earned the smallest flicker of a smile from Coral, there and gone like a match in wind. Tamsin had catalogued Coral's smiles the way other people collected river-glass: rare, irregular, each one slightly different, and worth keeping.

* * *

The trouble, when it showed itself, showed itself gently, which was how Tamsin would later understand it had been designed to do.

It began with the ferryman's daughter, Ilse, who kept the little bake-oven by the mill and sold rolls on market mornings. On the second Saturday of the thaw-months-that-weren't, Ilse mentioned — laughing, scandalized, delighted — that Councilor Brann had bought six of her honey rolls and carried them down to the ferry landing, where the republican agent Merrick Hale was still lodged in the ferry-house, waiting out the ice like a man waiting for a letter.

"Hale?" Tamsin's mother said, when Ilse reported this at the supper table. Odile Vant could make a single syllable weigh as much as an anvil. "What business has Brann with Hale?"

"No business anybody knows," said Ilse. "Just friendliness. Hale asked after the mill ward, and after the winter stores, and whether the young men were restless cooped up so long. Brann answered him very civilly. They drank a flask of plum brandy together, right there on the landing, like old friends."

"Restless," Odile repeated, in the tone she used for a knot she'd found in a board marked clear.

Tamsin watched her mother across the table, and watched Coral pretend not to watch anything at all, and felt the first cold thread of something go down her spine that she couldn't yet name.

* * *

She began, then, without quite deciding to, to keep an eye on Brann.

This was easier than it should have been, because Tamsin had errands everywhere. The Regiment's children knew her; their mothers sent her on messages; the archive-keeper, old Master Ferren, liked her enough to let her shelve folios when her hands were too cold for fine work. A girl of nineteen with a basket on her arm could stand in any doorway in Tamsin's Ford and be furniture. She and Coral were alike in that, though she'd have been stung to hear it said — both of them watchers, both of them invisible in different keys. The difference, she told herself, was that Tamsin watched because she loved the town, and wanted nothing from it but its continuance. She tried not to examine too closely whether that distinction would survive scrutiny.

On the last Thursday of the month, following a rumor that the council had been called into special session — unusual in itself, since the regular assembly was barely a week off — she took the long way home past Brann's house on Mill Row, and found Hale's tall grey figure just leaving it.

They stood a moment in the blue dusk, the agent and the councilor, and the distance between them was wrong. It was the distance of men who had shaken hands but didn't want to be seen doing it. Hale said something low; Brann laughed, that new bucket-ring laugh; and then they parted, Brann up the hill toward his warm house and his silver stick, Hale back toward the landing, unhurried, a man with no enemy in the world.

Tamsin stood in the shadow of Ferren's woodshed with her heart going hard, and understood, in the wordless way one understands a joint cut badly — everything sound on the surface, everything about to fail — that something had been given and something promised, and that neither thing would appear on any ledger.

* * *

"You're certain it was his house," Coral said.

They were in the loft above the workshop, where the drying lumber made the air smell sweet and resinous, and where Tamsin had taken to bringing her questions because the rafters kept secrets the way the kiln kept heat. Coral sat cross-legged on a plank of seasoned cherry, sanding a drawer-front with slow strokes, and her stillness while she listened was total. It always unsettled Tamsin a little, that quality Coral had of going quiet all the way through, like deep water under ice.

"Certain. I've delivered to Brann's house my whole life. And it wasn't the first time, either. Marta Voss saw them at the tannery steps two weeks ago and thought nothing of it. Everyone thinks nothing of it. That's what frightens me — he's *practicing* being unremarkable."

"Or you're practicing being remarkable." But there was no mockery in it. Coral set down the sandpaper and looked at her, and in the lamplight her face was older than her years, worn smooth in places faces shouldn't be worn. "Listen to me, Tamsin. If Brann is talking to Hale — truly talking, trading talk — then it matters less *what* he says than *what he is*. A man like Hale doesn't buy information. Information is cheap; he could get the store-counts from any idle tongue at the bake-oven. What he buys is *access*. A voice inside the room. When the vote comes on the charter, he doesn't need the town to say yes. He needs one man inside the council who will keep saying maybe, and keep the town divided, and keep telling Hale exactly how close the town is to saying yes." She turned the drawer-front over. "Corruption doesn't kick the door. It's invited in for supper."

"And we've been feeding him honey rolls."

"We've been feeding him whatever he asks for. That's what hospitality is. That's also what makes it dangerous." Coral picked the sandpaper up again, and her voice went lighter, deliberately, like a hand smoothing a wrinkled cloth. "It may be nothing. Men do drink brandy. Don't go building gallows out of a walk past a woodshed."

But her eyes, when they lifted briefly from the work, were not the eyes of a person reassuring herself.

* * *

The assembly came, and the charter question came to the floor again, and Tamsin sat in the gallery beside Coral and watched Brann perform.

He performed well. That was the terrible thing. He rose during the debate on whether the Regiment should even entertain Hale's offer — protection, roads reopened, the militia rolls formally enrolled under republican command — and he spoke for a full quarter hour in the old rolling cadence the mill ward loved, about caution, about sovereignty, about how the Republic of Vessany had been a good neighbor for sixty years and a neighbor's hand extended deserved at least the courtesy of examination. Not yes. Never yes. Just *examination*. Just *patience*. Just let us not be hasty, friends, in a season when haste has cost other towns dearly — and here he named two towns downriver whose names made the hall go cold and quiet, towns that had refused the republic's terms and had, come the war's shifting fortunes, been left to the enemy's mercy while the republic's garrisons withdrew.

He didn't argue for surrender. He argued for delay. And delay, in a town where the river was frozen and the road out closed and spring a rumor, was a weapon dressed as wisdom.

When the vote was taken on whether to send a formal refusal now or wait until the thaw, the hall split nearly down the middle, and the deferral carried by eleven voices, and Tamsin sat with her nails bitten half away counting the yeas and finding, again and again, that she could account for every bloc in the Regiment except the mill ward, which had voted together, solid and strange, behind its councilor.

Eleven voices. She counted them twice. If four of them had crossed the floor, the refusal would have carried.

Afterward, in the dispersing crowd, she saw Brann accept congratulations from both sides — from those who'd wanted refusal and thanked him for his stirring words, and from those who'd wanted acceptance and murmured that at least the man was reasonable. He received it all with the modest smile of a servant of the whole town, and his silver stick tapped softly on the flagstones, tap, tap, like something measuring.

* * *

It was Ilse who gave her the next piece, and gave it unwillingly, which was how Tamsin knew it was true.

They met at the washhouse on a grey morning, steam rising off the copper kettles, and Ilse worked her washing and talked the way she always talked, in a stream with eddies, until suddenly the stream went shallow and careful.

"I shouldn't say," she said, wringing a sheet hard enough to crack her knuckles. "Truly I shouldn't, Tamsin, it isn't mine to say. But it's sitting on me like a stone."

"Then put it down."

Ilse glanced at the door, at the empty benches, at the steam. "My da keeps the ferry-book. Landings, cargoes, fees — the Regiment requires it, every crossing written down, who came, who went, what was carried. It's how the town keeps its own books, your mam always says." She lowered her voice until the kettle-noise nearly ate it. "Hale's been sending things across the ice. Not often. A runner comes up from the south bank every week or so, meets him at the landing at first light when nobody's about. Small packets. And here's the stone, Tamsin — twice now, my da's seen Brann there too. At first light. Before the book opens for the day. Once Hale handed him a packet, flat, wrapped in oilcloth, and Brann put it inside his coat like a man hiding a loaf."

"What kind of packet?"

"I don't know. Flat. Paper-sized." Ilse stared into her wash-water. "My da won't write it in the ferry-book. He says if he writes it, it becomes real, and if it becomes real, he has to do something about it, and he's got daughters, Tamsin, and the republic's still the republic till it isn't." Her chin trembled, anger and fear fighting in the same face. "So now *I'm* carrying it, aren't I? Same as the packet. Same as Brann. Everybody passing the weight along so nobody owns it."

Tamsin reached across the wet boards and took her friend's red hands. "You've put it down now," she said. "I'll carry the rest."

* * *

She carried it straight to the loft, and Coral listened with that total stillness, and when Tamsin finished — the packets, the first-light meetings, the oilcloth — Coral was silent for so long that the workshop sounds below rose up around them like water filling a hold.

"Papersized," Coral said at last. "Wrapped in oilcloth. Carried across the ice weekly." She was looking at the middle distance, somewhere beyond the wall, beyond the mountains. "That's courier traffic. That's how I moved things, in the years you don't know about. Small, regular, deniable. Which means either Hale is reporting to the capital—" she stopped herself, and Tamsin watched her stop herself, saw the sentence die behind her teeth — "or he's receiving instructions. And Brann is in the chain. Not a recipient. A link."

"A link to what?"

"To whatever happens next." Coral stood, abruptly, and went to the little window that overlooked the yard, and stood with her back to Tamsin in the grey light, and when she spoke again her voice had a flatness in it that Tamsin had learned to hear as the sound of Coral protecting someone — usually Tamsin. "A man in Brann's position is useful for three things only. To stall a decision. To pass documents. Or, when the time comes, to open a door." She turned. "Which door, and when, and for whom — that's what I intend to find out. But Tamsin—"

"Don't tell your mother. Yes, I know. You think she'll confront him and burn the whole thread."

"I think Odile Vant does nothing halfway, and right now halfway is the only safe place to stand." Coral came back to the cherry plank and sat, and picked up the sandpaper, and for a moment her hands wouldn't start moving, and Tamsin saw that — saw the hesitation at the plane, the half-second of doubt before the chisel falls. "There's one more thing. If Brann is passing papers for Hale, then sooner or later he'll want papers worth passing. Council papers. Store counts, charter drafts, the militia roll itself." Her eyes lifted, dark and level. "Keep your eyes on the archive. And on Ferren. And Tamsin — trust your reading of the grain. You're better at it than you know."

It was the kindest thing anyone had said to her in months, and it frightened her more than the packets did.

* * *

Three nights later, coming home late from the kiln-yard where she'd banked the fires for the morning's firing, Tamsin took the shortcut past the tannery and stopped dead in the shadow of the smokehouse.

Below her, at the foot of the lane where it met the frozen towpath, two figures stood talking. One was broad, familiar, silver stick planted in the snow like a standard. The other was tall and grey-coated, and even at forty paces and by moonlight she knew the patient set of his shoulders.

She couldn't hear the words. She heard only the tone — Brann's voice running quick and eager beneath the wind, Hale's answering rarely, evenly, the way a man speaks who has already decided how the conversation ends. Then Hale extended his hand, and Brann took it, and held it a moment longer than a handshake needs, both of his own hands wrapped around the agent's one, bowing slightly over it, the way a vassal holds a lord's hand in the old pictures in Ferren's archive.

Then they parted, and Brann walked up the hill whistling — actually whistling, a tuneless happy little run of notes — and Hale stood alone on the towpath a moment, looking north, toward the workshop roofs, the council hall, the sleeping town. Even at that distance Tamsin felt the look go through her like a cold draft under a door.

She pressed herself into the smokehouse shadow and did not breathe until his grey coat had faded into the dark toward the landing.

Her heart was hammering, and beneath the fear was something harder and colder taking shape, the way ice takes shape on still water, spreading from the edges inward. Brann Odulfar had eaten at their table. His mother had taught Odile's mother. His name was in the ledger on the same page as hers.

And tomorrow — tomorrow she had promised to help Ferren rebind the council's winter minutes, and she had seen, just yesterday, the key to the document chest hanging on its hook by the archive door, and she had seen, too, that the hook stood empty tonight when she passed, and she had thought nothing of it, and now she thought of nothing else.

She stood a long time in the cold, watching the hill where Brann's window still burned yellow and steady, a man awake late with his papers and his plum brandy and his future.

Then she turned and ran for the loft, because Coral had to know, and because some part of her already suspected what Coral would say when she heard it — that the empty hook was the answer, that the chest had already been opened, and that whatever Brann was carrying across the ice in oilcloth had been theirs all along.
