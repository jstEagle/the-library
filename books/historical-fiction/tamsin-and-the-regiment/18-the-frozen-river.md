## 18. The Frozen River

The cold came down from the pass like a debt being called in, and on the ninth morning of the freeze, Coral woke to a silence she had not heard before.

She had learned the town's sounds by then — she had learned them the way she learned everything, involuntarily, the courier's curse of noticing. The workshop's first hammer before dawn. The scrape of Odile's boots on the stair, two steps, pause, two steps more, her bad knee announcing itself. The kettle in the common kitchen that whistled a half-tone flat because someone had dented it years ago and no one had mended it, because in Tamsin's Ford even the flaws were kept, catalogued, loved in their way. She had learned the river most of all: its mutter under the ice at the shallows, its low throat-sound at the bend where the current still ran open, the groaning and cracking in the deep cold of night that had frightened her the first week until Tamsin laughed and said the river was only turning over in its sleep.

But this morning there was a new sound, or rather an absence of one, and Coral lay in her narrow bed in the seamstress's loft — the widow Maren's cousin from downriver, that was who she was, that was who she had been for two months now — and she understood what it meant before she had even risen to the window.

The ice had gone quiet.

A river that groans is a river still moving beneath its skin. A river that has fallen silent is a river that has frozen through, top to bottom, bank to bank, a road of grey glass where last month there was only water and death.

She dressed without hurry. That was the discipline of it, the old craft rising in her like water into a wick: no hurry, no hurry ever. She laced her boots, wound her shawl, and went down the stair past Maren's closed door with the care of a woman who had spent fifteen years learning to pass through other people's houses without leaving a mark on them.

Outside, the world was white and hard and utterly still. Her breath hung in front of her like a small ghost she was reluctant to release. She walked down through the town — past the workshop with its lamps already lit, past the kiln sheds breathing their thin smoke, past the council hall where the shutters were still closed — and out along the ferry path to the water.

The river was a road.

Not a metaphor. Not a poet's fancy. A road, wide and flat and grey-white, running from bank to bank, from the town's edge all the way downriver toward the lowlands, toward the ferry crossing at Marrow's Bend, toward the post road, the capital road, toward everything she had spent a winter pretending she did not want.

Coral stood at the edge of it and did not step on.

* * *

She tested it first, of course. She was who she was. She walked the bank until she found a stone the size of her two fists, and she threw it out onto the ice with a courier's flat, economical motion, and the stone skipped once, twice, and lay there, and the sound it made was the sound of a hammer on a church door. She threw a bigger one. It boomed. The ice rang like a bell and did not so much as powder beneath it.

She knew ice. She had crossed frozen rivers in the north country in her third year of service, with Sef's voice in her ear — *ice that sings will hold, ice that hisses will not, and ice that says nothing at all, girl, that's the ice you trust least, because you can't hear what it's thinking* — and this ice said nothing. This ice was two months of hard cold laid down in layers, a foot thick at the bend if it was an inch, and the old men of the town had already been walking it for three days to check the fish traps at the downstream weir. She had watched them go out and come back with their lines of silver fish and their breath streaming, casual as men crossing a street.

It would hold her. It would hold her and her boots and her small bag with its spare shift and its knife, and it would hold her all the way to Marrow's Bend, and from Marrow's Bend the post road ran south and the war ran everywhere, and no one in Tamsin's Ford could stop her, and no one in Tamsin's Ford, when it came to it, would even know which way she had gone until the light failed.

She could be gone by noon.

She stood there a long time. The cold worked through her shawl and into her shoulders and she let it, because the cold was honest in a way almost nothing else in her life had been. It did not flatter her. It did not pretend. It simply said: *this is what you are, a body, alone, on the edge of a road that leads away.*

The dispatch case was not on her wrist. That was the first thing, the strange thing, the thing she kept circling back to. She had burned the chain off — no, not burned, Odile had cut it, weeks ago now, with a jeweler's saw and no questions asked, and Coral had worn the faded red stripe around her wrist like a scar ever since, touching it the way other people touched saints' medals. The case itself lived now under the third floorboard in the workshop's archive room, under the floorboard with the knot in the shape of a fish, because Coral could not bring herself to carry it and could not bring herself to be far from it, and so it lay there in the dark under the town's records, this small leather weight that contained, as far as she knew, the death warrant of everything she had come to — no. Not everything. She corrected herself, standing on the bank, breath steaming. It contained the ruin of a town. The town was not yet hers. The town was something she was only borrowing, the way she had borrowed Maren's loft and the workshop bench and Tamsin's sharp, suspicious, gradually warming friendship, the way she had borrowed every room she had ever stood in, because a courier owned nothing, a courier *was* nothing, a courier was a hole in the shape of a person that messages passed through.

And the river was open. The river was a road. And the hole in the shape of a person could walk out of Tamsin's Ford this morning and never be filled in at all.

* * *

She went back up through the town without deciding anything, which was itself a decision, and she hated herself a little for the relief of it.

The workshop was warm and loud. Winter work was interior work — joinery, turning, the glue pots going, the long benches crowded — and the smell of cut pine and hot hide glue was so familiar to her now that she caught herself, some mornings, breathing it in before she was awake enough to stop herself. She hung her shawl on her peg. Her peg. There was a peg, and it was hers, and someone had burned her initial into it — Tamsin, obviously, with her knife, in that quick sly way she had, doing kind things so fast you couldn't catch her at it — and Coral looked at the peg and thought: *I could walk out of here and this peg would still have my letter on it for a month before anyone took it down.*

"Cousin Maren's been asking after you," said Pell the turner, not looking up from his lathe. "Says you were out on the bank at first light. Says you were standing there like a heron."

"I wanted to see the ice."

"Mm." Pell turned his spindle. Wood curled off it in a ribbon like something being unmade. "Old Doss says it'll take a wagon by week's end. First time in six years it's gone this early, this hard. Ferryman's already talking about running sleds down to Marrow's Bend for salt and iron." He glanced at her then, just once, the way people in this town glanced — briefly, without appetite, without the hungry measuring look of the capital. "Roads open, I suppose. For those that want them."

Coral said nothing. There was nothing to say that was not a confession.

* * *

She found Tamsin at the midday meal, in the long common room where the town ate together in winter, because in Tamsin's Ford a cold hearth was a shared shame and no one ate alone if it could be helped. Tamsin had saved her a place, which she did every day now without comment, shoving her own bowl an inch to the left and not looking up, and Coral sat down in the saved place and ate barley soup and did not taste it.

"You've got your going-away face on," Tamsin said.

"I don't have a going-away face."

"Everyone has a going-away face. Mam says she can spot one across a room. It's the face of someone counting doors." Tamsin tore her bread in half and put the larger piece, wordlessly, on Coral's plate. "You've had it for two days. Since the ice went quiet. Don't think nobody noticed."

"Nobody noticed."

"I noticed." Tamsin said it plainly, without triumph, which was somehow worse. She was seventeen that winter and she had her mother's forearms and her father's — whoever her father had been, the town kept that book shut — unblinking way of looking at a thing until the thing confessed. "So. Are you going?"

Coral set down her spoon. Around them the common room roared on — arguments about salt, about the mill share, about whether Brann's new scheme for a tannery was ambition or folly — and the noise closed over them like water.

"If I asked you," Coral said slowly, "what you would say to someone who was leaving."

"Depends why they were leaving."

"Suppose they had to. Suppose staying was — dangerous. For them. Suppose the thing they were carrying was going to hurt people if they stayed near it, and going was the only way to put it down somewhere far away."

Tamsin chewed. Considered. "Suppose," she said at last, "the thing they were carrying was the reason it would hurt people. Suppose the thing doesn't hurt anyone sitting under a floorboard. Suppose it's the person who knows what's in it who does the hurting, by carrying it somewhere it can be used." She wiped her mouth. "Mam says a tool's never dangerous in the chest. Only in the hand."

"And if the hand has to carry it somewhere eventually. If not carrying it is not an option. If there are people who will come for it."

"Then," Tamsin said, "they'd better make sure that when the people come, what they find isn't what they were promised." She shrugged, and went back to her soup as if she had said nothing, as if she had not just, in her flat, seventeen-year-old way, walked straight through the middle of Coral's entire life. "Anyway. You're not going. You've been here since the bridge fell and you haven't left, and there's been a hundred mornings you could have, and you're still eating my bread. People who are leaving don't learn the names of all the chisels."

Coral looked at her. "I don't know all the chisels."

"You know eight. You knew four in autumn." Tamsin stood, gathered both bowls, and paused behind Coral's chair just long enough to say, quietly, in her ear: "Whatever it is you're counting, cousin — count this town in it."

* * *

That night she went out onto the ice.

She told herself it was to test it, and it was, in the way that all her reasons were true and also not the truth. She walked out from the bank in the blue dark with the stars hard overhead and the town's lamps behind her, and the ice held her with a sound like a struck drum, deep and sure, and she walked until the town was a smudge of gold at her back and the river ran away before her, black under white, all the way down to the lowlands and the world.

This was the moment. She knew it was the moment. Somewhere behind her, under a floorboard with a fish-shaped knot, lay the dispatches that could buy a militia or burn a town, and somewhere ahead of her lay Marrow's Bend and the post road and the handlers she no longer trusted and the republic she no longer believed in, and between the two lay this road of singing ice, open for the first time since the bridge fell, and every year of her training said *go now, go fast, go light, never be remembered* — and her boots stood on the ice and did not move.

She thought about Sef. She thought about him more than she had let herself all winter — his dry voice, his hands always half in his pockets, the way he'd taught her to walk through a market so that eyes slid off her like rain off oilcloth. *You're a shadow, girl. Shadows don't want things. Wanting is what gets you remembered.* And she had wanted nothing for fifteen years, had made an art of wanting nothing, had hollowed herself so thoroughly that the handlers had called her the best courier they had, and now here she stood on a frozen river wanting — what? Not the capital. Not the road. Not even safety, exactly.

She wanted to know the ninth chisel's name.

The thought arrived so plainly, so absurdly, that she laughed out loud, and the laugh cracked away across the ice and came back to her from the far bank like an answer. She wanted to finish the little box she was making for Maren's spectacles, badly, with its crooked dovetails that Tamsin had examined and pronounced "not shameful, which is a compliment, don't get proud." She wanted to see Odile's face when the spring timber came upriver. She wanted to sit in the gallery at the next assembly and watch a room full of joiners and fishermen decide their own lives, badly sometimes, slowly, but theirs — she wanted, she understood at last, standing alone on a road that led everywhere, to be *somewhere*.

And the dispatches. The dispatches would die with her out here, or worse, they would live — she would carry them downriver and hand them to some handler and some other town would burn instead of this one, some other gallery of strangers, and she would stand in the shadow of it and be a shadow, and the shadow would be all that was left of her when she died, because shadows leave nothing.

Or.

Or she could walk back.

She stood on the ice a long time, until the cold had worked through every seam of her, and the stars wheeled, and the river said nothing at all beneath her, and she found — she checked, the way she checked everything, twice, three times, because belief was a thing you verified — that she was not tempted. That was the surprise. Not that she resisted the road. That the road had stopped calling. Somewhere between the bridge falling and this frozen night, the arithmetic had changed without her noticing: the town was no longer the place where she was hiding the dispatches. The dispatches were the thing hiding in her town.

She turned back. The lamps of Tamsin's Ford glowed at the head of the river like a hearth seen from far off in a dream, and she walked toward them, and her boots rang on the ice, and she counted the doors of the town as they came into view — the workshop, the hall, the kiln shed, the loft — and for the first time in her life she was not counting them as exits.

She was counting them as rooms she would defend.

She was halfway up the ferry path, breath steaming, when she saw the lantern coming down through the trees from the direction of the council hall — a lantern she knew, swinging at the pace of a man who walked like the ground owed him money. Brann. And with him, a second figure, taller, wrapped in a traveling cloak, and even at that distance, even in the dark, Coral knew the walk of the second man the way she knew her own hands.

Merrick Hale had not waited for the thaw.

He had come over the ice.
